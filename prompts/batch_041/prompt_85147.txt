The Common Prior Assumption as a Modeling Discipline

The common prior assumption (CPA) is often introduced as a claim about rational agents: that differences in belief must trace to differences in information rather than to differences in prior. On this reading, agents who share a prior and are rational cannot agree to disagree; persistent disagreement is a sign that something has gone wrong. I want to argue that this reading conflates two things. The CPA is better understood as a modeling discipline. It is a way of representing a situation so that all disagreement is informational. Whether that representation is illuminating or distorting depends on the application, and in many applications—especially multi-agent AI systems—it is a substantive and possibly false assumption rather than a harmless normalization.

First, some distinctions. A common prior can mean at least three different things. It can mean a single prior distribution over states of the world. It can mean a single prior over types, in the standard representation of incomplete information. Or it can mean that the prior is common knowledge among the agents. These are not equivalent. The first is compatible with agents having different information and therefore different posterior beliefs. The second is a modeling convention that allows us to represent hierarchies of belief with a single ex ante distribution. The third is a much stronger epistemic condition. Much confusion in discussions of the CPA comes from sliding between them.

Second, the agreement theorem. If two agents have a common prior and their posterior beliefs are common knowledge, then their posteriors must be equal. This is a striking result, but its assumptions are strong. Common knowledge of posteriors is rare in practice, and the common prior is an assumption, not a theorem. The theorem does not show that disagreement is irrational; it shows that a certain package—common prior plus common knowledge of posteriors—is inconsistent with disagreement. If we relax common knowledge of posteriors, disagreement is possible even with a common prior. If we relax the common prior, disagreement can be common knowledge. So the CPA is doing real work, and its role should be made explicit.

Third, and this is the part I am least sure about, the CPA may have less empirical content than it appears. If we are allowed to enrich the state space—adding dimensions that represent the agents' beliefs, information, or psychological quirks—then it is sometimes argued that any pattern of beliefs can be represented as arising from a common prior. If that is right, then the CPA is not a testable hypothesis about the world but a choice of representation. It becomes substantive only when the state space is fixed in advance by the application. The interesting question is not whether the CPA is true in general but whether it is appropriate for a given state space.

Fourth, the application to AI alignment. In multi-agent AI systems, we often model agents as having a common prior over task distributions, reward functions, or other agents' policies. This is convenient for mechanism design and for results about when agents should not trade or should converge in belief. But if the agents are trained by different processes, on different data, with different inductive biases, then the common prior assumption may fail in ways that matter. Two language models fine-tuned on different corpora may have different effective priors over ambiguous prompts. If we model them as sharing a prior, we may predict convergence or no trade where in fact there is persistent disagreement or strategic trade. This is not merely a theoretical curiosity. It affects how we design debate, amplification, and oversight protocols that rely on agents converging on a common answer.

The alternative I want to suggest is to treat the CPA as a null model. Use it to ask what disagreement would look like if it were purely informational. When the null fails, that is evidence of different priors, different inductive biases, or different information structures. But do not reify the null as a normative ideal. The CPA is a tool for isolating one source of disagreement, not a requirement of rationality.

I can imagine two objections. The first is that if the CPA is just a modeling choice, then the critique is trivial: of course a modeling choice can be inappropriate. My reply is that the critique is not that the CPA is false but that it is often used as if it were a substantive constraint on rationality, and that this usage has costs. It can hide genuine prior disagreement and make mechanisms look more robust than they are. The second objection is that if we are allowed to enrich the state space enough, the CPA can always be imposed, so there is no substantive issue. My reply is that this may be true for some purposes, but it comes at the cost of a very rich state space; the CPA then loses predictive bite. The cases that matter are those in which the state space is fixed by the application.
