For the past couple of years I've been sitting in on a joint seminar between my department and a reinforcement-learning group across campus — I'm a microeconomist, my own work is on revealed preference and the measurement of welfare — and I keep having the same frustration. The problems the RL people call inverse reinforcement learning look, from where I sit, like a very old problem wearing new notation, and I want the translation done properly rather than gestured at. So: take the central commitments of that literature — that there is a reward function which a behaving agent is optimizing, that it can be recovered from demonstrations of the agent's behavior, that it is recoverable only up to certain invariances (the one that interests me is that you may add to the reward any function of the state whose contributions telescope along a trajectory without changing which behavior is optimal, and alongside it the whole industry of selecting a canonical member of the consistent set by maximum entropy or by a prior over rewards), and the competing views about what the recovered object is — the agent's actual objective, a mere predictive summary of its behavior, or something that licenses claims about what is good for it — and restate all of it in the vocabulary I actually use: choice functions and budget sets, the weak and strong axioms of revealed preference, rationalizability, ordinal versus cardinal utility, and the older dispute over whether preference is a summary of choice or a cause of it. Be as faithful as you can; a loose analogy is worse than useless to me. To fix ideas: a colleague once showed me a demonstration in which an agent navigated a grid and the recovered reward glowed brightest at the goal cell, and when I asked whether the agent preferred the goal or merely chose it, nobody in the room thought the question was well-formed. I want to know what my question looks like from inside their framework, and what their answer looks like from inside mine.

Then the residue report. Which parts of the inverse-RL picture survive translation without loss, which survive only if you let yourself distort something — I suspect the cardinality of the reward and the role of the environment's dynamics are the places to watch — and which claims have no counterpart at all in choice-theoretic terms. Two specific things I can't place: the assumption that the agent is maximizing a single scalar, and the notion that there is a fact of the matter about the reward which the data underdetermine. I'd also like you to say something about the counterfactual dimension, since my framework lives on variation in the feasible set while theirs seems to observe one policy in one fixed environment; is that a real difference, or does the transition structure of their environment already supply the counterfactual variation my budget sets supply for me? For each item of residue, tell me whether it marks a defect of my framework — something choice theory cannot express because it deliberately gave up mental states — or a sign that the inverse-RL claim is less clear than it looks. And say which of the two frameworks is more committed to the existence of a determinate object being inferred, and whether that commitment is doing any real work in their arguments or is just a manner of speaking.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each part of the translation and single out the one point where you are least sure you have got it right; I would much rather have a flagged uncertainty than a smooth answer.
