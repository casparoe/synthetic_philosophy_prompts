I was at one of those after-work meetups for people in ML and a guy there who does AI safety research (I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, so half of it went over my head) gave me an argument I keep chewing on because I'm pretty sure it's wrong but I can't find where it breaks. It's about the whole family of "control" style techniques, where instead of just hoping the alignment training took, you assume the model might be quietly working against you — sandbagging the evals, scheming when it thinks no one's watching — and you design your pipeline around that. His argument, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) A control measure is only worth having if it lowers the chance of something catastrophic versus just running the model bare.
(2) Every control measure leans on some component you're taking on faith: the monitor model that flags bad behavior, the sandbox that's supposed to stop the code from escaping, the human who's supposed to actually read the alert at 3am.
(3) Taking a component on faith means you're assuming it won't betray you — which is exactly the assumption we're refusing to make about the model being controlled.
(4) So no control measure can do what it claims, since each one just relocates the assumption one step outward.
(5) Push it and the regress never terminates: the monitor watching the monitor watching the monitor.
(6) Therefore "assume the model may be misaligned and design controls anyway" is incoherent — either you trust something unconditionally (and you're back to hoping) or you don't (and you can never ship anything).

I think (6) is nuts. Keeping the model off the internet, or not handing it the prod credentials, obviously reduces risk — that seems like the whole point of not-being-an-idiot engineering, and I'd fight to keep (1), which reads to me like basic decision theory. But (2) through (4) feel airtight every time I go back through them, and I can't find the seam. So: which step do I cut, why did it look so good, and what does cutting it cost me? Does something in the neighborhood — sandboxing, monitoring — turn out to be doing less work than I assumed, or more? Lead with the single step you'd reject, then price the alternatives in order of how much it'd hurt to give them up. Max ~350 words, no need for citations, I just want the argument taken apart.
