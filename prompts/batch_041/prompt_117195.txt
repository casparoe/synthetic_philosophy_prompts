I chair a municipal heritage and commemoration board in a mid-sized city. We meet monthly with a docket of eight to twelve items — proposals to erect a plaque or statue, to rename a park or a school, to remove or relabel an existing monument, to fund a small local museum's display or its storage, to let a derelict site fall down, and occasionally to commission a public history project. Each item gets about fifteen minutes. We have no professional historian on staff; I have a master's in history, but the other six members are council appointees, and our recommendations go to the city council, which can and does overturn us. The decisions are public, they are contested, and they are durable — a statue outlasts the council that approved it.

I keep finding that we argue in the wrong vocabulary. Someone says a figure is inspiring and should be honored; someone else says the past is the past and should be left alone; someone else says the figure did terrible things and should be torn down. These are all gestures at something, but we have no way to make them answerable to each other. I want to run our docket through Nietzsche's account of the three ways history can serve life — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and through his warnings about what each becomes when it is overdone. Please turn that into a procedure we can actually run, item by item, in the fifteen minutes we have.

I need the following from you.

First, a decision procedure in words — a checklist or ordered set of questions, not an essay. It should begin with a step that identifies which mode or modes the proposal is actually invoking, and it should not assume the proposer knows the terminology; give me a short set of plain-language diagnostic questions for that step. Then, for each mode, the procedure should ask what service to life is being claimed and what would count as a sufficient answer, and it should include a separate check for that mode's characteristic pathology: monumental history's tendency to falsify the past by analogy and to console the weak rather than equip the strong; antiquarian history's tendency to mummify, to revere indiscriminately, and to make letting go impossible; critical history's tendency to judge the past by present standards and to forget that we are also its products. The procedure should say explicitly where it stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value choice rather than a fact about the past.

Second, build in a step I care about more than the rest: a forgetting test. Before we decide to commemorate, preserve, or destroy, ask what would be lost if this thing were simply allowed to pass out of public memory, who would bear that loss, and whether the proposal is really a way of avoiding some present action by substituting a gesture toward the past. Nietzsche's contrast between the historical and the unhistorical, and his worry about a life that cannot forget, should be doing work here, not just decorating the procedure. Also include a check for the opposite failure: a proposal that wants to cut itself off from the past it is actually made of, as though we could start from zero.

Third, apply the procedure, in full, to three items from a docket like ours. (a) A proposal to erect a statue to a nineteenth-century industrialist who founded the city's main employer and who also broke a strike violently; the proposers want him as an example of local greatness, and the opponents want no monument at all. (b) A proposal to keep funding a small collection of domestic objects and local ephemera that almost no one visits, on the grounds that it is ours and would be lost; the alternative is to deaccession and use the space for a display about a recent wave of migration. (c) A proposal to remove a colonial-era monument from the main square and replace it with a critical exhibit explaining what it commemorated; the removal has broad support, but a small group argues that the exhibit is a self-congratulatory way of avoiding any actual change. For each, show the procedure running, including the answers that are not available to us and what we should do when they are not.

Fourth, make one of those three come out in a way I will not like. I am not asking you to rig it; I am asking you to follow the procedure where it leads and to tell me plainly when it tells me that the thing I want is the thing that fails the test. Do not soften that outcome.

Fifth, tell me what this procedure cannot do. Which decisions on our docket will it leave unresolved, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where will it produce a confident answer that a better-informed board would overturn? I would rather know the failure modes now than discover them in a public meeting.

Write the procedure so that a board member who has never read Nietzsche can apply it, but do not flatten the distinctions to the point where monumental, antiquarian, and critical history become three words for good, bad, and controversial. If the procedure has to drop something from the theory to be usable, say what you dropped and why.
