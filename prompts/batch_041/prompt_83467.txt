I'm in a seminar on punishment and I've been assigned to present on the capital-punishment chapter of an eighteenth-century English translation of Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (the original is 1764; the translation I have is a 1775 London edition). The prose is period English and I keep losing the thread of the argument, so I'd like help reconstructing what is actually being claimed and how the pieces are supposed to fit together. Here is the stretch I'm stuck on:

"It is not the intenseness of the pain that has the greatest effect on the mind, but its continuance; for our sensibility is more easily and more powerfully affected by weak but repeated impressions, than by a violent but momentary impulse. The power of habit is universal over every sensible being. As it is by that we learn to speak, to walk, and to satisfy our necessities, so the ideas of morality are stamped on our minds by repeated impressions. The death of a criminal is a terrible but momentary spectacle, and therefore a less efficacious method of deterring others, than the continued example of a man deprived of his liberty, condemned, as a beast of burthen, to repair, by his labour, the injury he has done to society."

A little later the chapter says: "A punishment, to be just, should have only that degree of severity which is sufficient to deter others." But it also says: "The severity of a punishment should be just sufficient to excite compassion in the spectators, as it is intended more for them than for the criminal."

What I'd like is a patient reconstruction: first, what the psychological premise about duration versus intensity is supposed to establish, and whether the whole case against execution rests on that premise or only leans on it; second, how the two sentences about severity are meant to coexist, since one seems to set the ceiling of punishment at what deters while the other seems to make the spectators' compassionate response the measure — I can't tell whether these are two criteria, one criterion stated twice, or a genuine inconsistency in the argument. I'm also trying to figure out how much of the chapter is really an empirical deterrence claim and how much is a claim about legitimacy, since the earlier part of the chapter seems to deny that anyone could have handed over a right over their own life, and I don't see how that denial connects to the habit-and-repetition psychology. Finally, a classmate pressed me on whether this whole framework has anything to say about the risk of executing someone who is innocent; my instinct is that the argument as written doesn't address it, but I'd like you to say whether the passage contains any resources for that worry or whether it would have to be imported from elsewhere, and to flag anywhere I'm reading a modern deterrence debate back into an eighteenth-century text.
