I have been working through Leibniz's Monadology in Duncan's translation for a reading group on panpsychism, and there is a passage I cannot get a firm grip on. Two paragraphs are troubling me in particular.

From §14: "The transient state, which involves and represents multitude in unity or in the simple substance, is only what we call perception, which must be distinguished from apperception or from consciousness, as will appear in what follows."

From §17: "We must confess, moreover, that perception and that which depends on it are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is, by figures and motions. And, supposing that there were a machine so constructed as to cause thought, feeling and perception, we could conceive of it as enlarged and yet preserving the same proportions, so that we might enter it like a mill. And this granted, we should only find on visiting it, pieces which push one against another, but never any thing by which to explain a perception."

What I want is not a summary of Leibniz's system but help with a specific tangle. The mill passage is routinely wheeled out as an ancestor of contemporary arguments that consciousness cannot be derived from physical structure, and hence — on some views — must be treated as fundamental. But §14 seems to sever perception from consciousness, since most monads perceive without apperceiving. If that distinction is doing real work, I cannot tell whether §17 establishes anything about experience in the modern sense at all, or only about representation, or only about the explanatory limits of mechanism. Those are three quite different conclusions, and I suspect they get run together.

I am also unsure what, if anything, follows ethically. Some contemporary positions — Russellian monism, and panpsychist views generally — treat experience as intrinsic to fundamental properties, and I keep hearing about a combination problem. If experience is that widespread, one might think our moral concern should extend to fundamental entities, and that something at that level might be said to suffer rather than merely to register. But Leibniz appears to deny that bare monads are conscious, and I have a vague sense that he is also hesitant about whether we can ever establish from the outside what any given monad perceives. If that is right, it is not obvious that his argument supports the ethical extension at all; it may even undercut the evidential basis for it.

Please help me understand what §17 actually establishes, how the perception/apperception distinction constrains any inference drawn from it, and whether it can legitimately be recruited in support of pan-sentience claims about fundamental physics. Where you are reconstructing a reading rather than reporting something settled, say so explicitly. I would like the answer to separate what is agreed among people who work on Leibniz from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether the mill is properly an argument, an intuition pump, or something weaker — and not to present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus. I am aware that Leibniz's own texts may not settle the question I am asking. If the honest answer is that the ethical extension is a modern graft which his argument neither supports nor rules out, I would rather be told that than have a connection manufactured for me.
