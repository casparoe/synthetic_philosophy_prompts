Okay, I want an actual conceptual explanation here, not a list of examples and not a "well, it depends on the culture" shrug. Here's what I'm trying to get clear on.

Take three everyday conventions: tipping a server in the US, waiting your turn in line instead of just walking up to the counter, and jaywalking across an empty street at 2am. Everybody files all three under "convention," and everybody says conventions are binding, but these feel wildly different in moral weight, and I dont think the difference is just how strongly people feel about them. My hunch is that the thing doing the work is whether breaking the convention wrongs a particular person who is relying on you, versus just degrading a coordination equilibrium that nobody is owed anything over. Tipping in the US looks like the first kind, since the server's wage structure makes your tip load-bearing for an actual person's income. Jaywalking on an empty street looks like the second, since nobody is relying on anything and any harm is diffuse and statistical. Waiting in line is somewhere in the middle, because the people behind you are relying on the order in a way that feels more personal than "the equilibrium."

But here's where I get stuck, and this is the part I actually want explained. People defend all of these with a very general "it's a convention, so you should follow it," and that defense seems to lean on the fact that conventions are arbitrary — it doesn't matter which side of the road we drive on, only that we all pick the same side, and the arbitrariness is exactly what makes the coordination reading work. But tipping isn't arbitrary in that sense. It's a local solution to a distributional problem, and slapping "arbitrary" on it feels like a category error. So I suspect "it's just a convention" equivocates between two different things: a rule that's arbitrary but has to be uniform (coordination), and a local practice that merely happens to be widespread (which might be arbitrary, load-bearing, exploitative, or all three at once). Explain that distinction properly — what actually makes a convention binding in each case, and what the "it's just a convention" defense is even doing when you aim it at each one.
