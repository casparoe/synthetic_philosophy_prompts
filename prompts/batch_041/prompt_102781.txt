I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in ethics; the group is 16–20 students, mostly philosophy majors but with no prior exposure to the moral-luck literature. I want to open that unit with a structured activity rather than a lecture, so that the students run into the phenomenon themselves — the pull toward judging people by what happened to them rather than by what they controlled — and only encounter the vocabulary for it in the debrief. Please design the activity.

The design I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve: students begin as agents who make a single consequential choice under partial information, and their own small reward (I can offer a real, trivial stake — priority in choosing from a box of snacks at the end, or a chocolate) is determined by the quality of their choice multiplied by an outcome drawn by chance after the fact. A subset of them then sit as a review board that assigns praise and censure to the agents, first on the basis of the choice alone, then again after outcomes are revealed, and the board is permitted to revise its verdicts. The facilitator records the drift between the two rounds. Build in two further layers: participants should have their available options and their dispositions partly dealt to them at random rather than chosen, so that they feel how much of what they are judged for was never up to them; and there should be at least one moment where an agent who acted well is left with less than an agent who acted badly, and the group has to decide whether the reward tracks desert at all.

Requirements. Do not name moral luck, or any of the standard categories, until the debrief; the point should be carried by the rules and incentives, not by anything I say beforehand. Include the full rules, a materials list with instructions for preparing the cards or profiles, the payoff scheme in enough detail to run it without improvising, a minute-by-minute timeline, and any roles needed. The debrief should map specific moments of the activity onto the components of the phenomenon — luck in outcomes, in circumstances, and in constitution; the asymmetry between how we treat good and bad luck; the thought that desert requires control — and should say where each mapping is strained.

I also want the debrief to press on two things that go past the usual summary: the difference between an agent's own retrospective feeling about what they did and the third-person regret that things turned out badly, and the question whether the lesson of the activity should move us to revise our judging practices rather than our metaphysics. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
