I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and the human sciences, and I'm about six months into a feature about the way people tell each other they have no reason to do things — the whole "thats not a reason, thats an excuse" genre of argument, which it turns out is a live fight in philosophy and not just something my relatives do at dinner. I've got the cartoon version of the internal/external reasons split: internalists think a reason has to hook onto something the agent already cares about, externalists think it doesn't. What I want is the version that isn't a cartoon, and especially the part I never see laid out properly, which is that there are three or four quite different theses all called "internalism" that get used interchangeably in the secondary stuff I've been reading. There's the Williams-style claim about what reason-statements mean, tied to an agents existing motivational set and whatever sound deliberation could get out of it; there's the metaethical claim that sincerely judging something right necessarily motivates you (or does so unless you're depressed, or a psychopath, or just very tired); and there's the Humean claim about motivation always needing some prior desire behind it. I'm fairly sure these are supposed to come apart, but every time I draft the paragraph that distinguishes them I end up writing something that collapses two of them into each other.

So: can you walk me through what actually separates them — what each thesis is a claim about (the semantics of reason-talk, the psychology of moral judgment, the mechanics of action), which ones entail which and which are merely neighbours, and a concrete case where someone plainly satisfies one and plainly violates another? And then the thing I really can't get straight: the internalist formulation that says an agent has a reason to X if she would be motivated to X were she to deliberate soundly starting from the motivations she already has. That counterfactual seems to be doing something sneaky — either the soundly-deliberating agent isn't quite the same person anymore, or the deliberation can only ever shuffle around desires she already had, so the thesis comes out either false or empty — but I can't put my finger on the exact problem, and I have a suspicion philosophers have a name for it. No need for a reading list, I'm not going to cite anybody, I just want the conceptual map and the case.
