I'm a high school student doing an independent research project on interpretability, and I'd like a careful conceptual explanation rather than a survey. I don't have journal access, so please don't organize the answer around who has said what; I'm interested in the ideas themselves.

The project is on mechanistic anomaly detection, which I take to mean roughly this: you use circuit-level analysis on a model's training distribution to form a hypothesis about the mechanism the model is using, and then you flag an input as anomalous when the model's behaviour on that input isn't what the hypothesized mechanism would predict. My advisor keeps pressing me on something that comes before any of that, and I can't answer her: when do two episodes of a model's computation count as instances of the same mechanism at all?

Here is the case I keep coming back to. I train a small transformer on formatted multi-digit addition. On inputs that look like the training data, one pair of attention heads plus an MLP does the work: ablating them destroys accuracy. On a new family of inputs — say, addends with leading zeros, or ones with unusually long carry chains — the model is still accurate, but the information is now routed through a different set of heads; ablating the original circuit barely hurts performance, while ablating the new heads wipes it out. So is this the same mechanism or a new one? Three criteria I've tried give me three answers. (1) Same components and wiring: no, plainly not. (2) Same algorithm — still digit-wise addition with carry propagation, just implemented elsewhere: yes. (3) Same role in the larger computation, i.e. the same thing is being computed and handed onward: yes, and this is the one I actually want, but I can't say why it should win over the other two.

A friend suggested that a mechanism should simply be defined as an equivalence class of computations under the set of interventions we care about, so that sameness just means invariance under those interventions. That seems to make sameness depend on what we happen to care about, which sounds circular — unless the notion really is interest-relative and that's simply the truth of the matter.

Please explain how these criteria come apart, and what, if anything, settles which one is right for the purpose of calling something an anomaly. If my framing assumes something that makes the question unanswerable as posed, tell me that plainly instead of answering the nearest well-formed question — I would rather find out that my question is confused. And if the honest answer is that it always depends on your purposes, please say what work the purposes are actually doing rather than leaving it as a slogan.
