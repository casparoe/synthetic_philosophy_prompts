I was a bystander in a reading group where the topic was consequentialist cluelessness — the thought that the consequences of any action ramify indefinitely, so that we cannot know whether the act did more good than harm on the whole. Two other participants, Ana and Ben, went several rounds on whether this is a serious problem for consequentialism or a pseudo-problem. I have set out the exchange below as faithfully as I can. I have no stake in the outcome; I would like you to adjudicate it point by point rather than simply declaring a winner.

(1) Ana: Consequentialism says the right act is the one with the best total consequences. But the total consequences of anything run on indefinitely: the child you save grows up and alters other lives, and so on without limit. No one can trace that, and no one can know whether the sum is positive or negative. So the consequentialist criterion is one we can never know whether we have met. A theory of right action that guarantees we can never know whether we have acted rightly is at best useless as a guide and at worst self-undermining.

(2) Ben: That proves far too much. Every theory is applied under uncertainty and the future is opaque to everyone. A deontologist who keeps a promise also cannot know whether keeping it had better or worse effects overall. If ignorance of distant consequences refuted a theory, it would refute the possibility of acting for reasons at all. And in any case we do not need the total; we need to know which of the options open to us ranks highest, and that comparison is often robust even when the totals are unknown. Buying the bed nets beats burning the money whether or not the world ends in 2200.

(3) Ana: The parallel fails. For a non-consequentialist, the feature that makes an act right — that the promise was kept, the truth told, the person treated as an end — is something you can check at the time; the unknown consequences are downstream of an act whose rightness is already settled. For the consequentialist the unknown thing is the right-making feature itself. And your robustness reply treats the unknown as a perturbation on a known quantity. What is unknown is not a margin of error on a ledger but whole categories of effect — political, institutional, effects on people who do not yet exist — that we cannot even enumerate, let alone estimate. When the space of possible effects is not known, "the comparison is robust" is not a claim anyone is in a position to make.

(4) Ben: You are running together two complaints of very different force. One is that we lack specific empirical facts about the future; inquiry could in principle supply them. The other is that the causal structure is such that no feasible inquiry would settle the matter. Grant that the second is real. Two things follow. First, it afflicts any view that gives weight to the distant future, including non-consequentialist views that tell us to care about future generations; it is not a defect of consequentialism as such. Second, if we really are clueless in that deep way, then the far-future terms are a wash — no option beats any other at that range — and the decision should be made on the terms we can see. That is not a refutation of consequentialism; it is a derivation of a modest, near-term consequentialism from consequentialist premises.

(5) Ana: That derivation is exactly where it breaks. If the criterion is "maximize total value," truncating the calculation to the near term is not licensed by the criterion; it is an extra premise — a discount rate on the future, or a restriction of the moral community to those already alive — smuggled in to save the theory. So the consequentialist faces a dilemma. Either the criterion is applied as stated and we are clueless, or it is truncated and the truncation is not consequentialist. Either way, cluelessness has done real damage: what survives is not the theory we started with.
