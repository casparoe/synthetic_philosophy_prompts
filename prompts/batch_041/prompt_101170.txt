I'm presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on biological function next month, and I need a question-and-answer preparation sheet for the discussion period. The thesis I'll be defending is this: the long-running dispute between selected-effects (etiological) accounts and causal-role accounts of function is not, as both sides assume, a dispute about the extension of the term "function," nor a disagreement about which of two rival analyses is correct. It is a disagreement about which property our function ascriptions actually track. I argue that the property doing the explanatory work in most function ascriptions is neither selection history nor present causal capacity taken alone, but what I call a trait's maintenance profile: the set of ongoing processes -- selective, developmental, and niche-constructive -- that keep the trait reliably produced and retained in the population or system. On this view selection is one way a trait can come to have a maintenance profile rather than a necessary condition for having a function at all; a trait's function is the contribution that figures in its maintenance profile; and malfunction is the failure of the trait to play that role, so the normative dimension survives without being anchored in selection.

The room will be mixed, and I know roughly who will be in it. About half are philosophers of biology working squarely in the selected-effects tradition; for them a trait's function is fixed by what it was selected for, and they will be suspicious of anything that loosens the tie to natural selection -- they tend to hear "maintenance" as "just another word for current causal role." Two or three work on evo-devo and niche construction; they are likely to be sympathetic to the idea of non-selective maintenance but will press hard on whether it can carry normative weight, and on whether developmental constraint is really "maintenance" of a function or merely a constraint on form. One is a comparative physiologist who uses function talk constantly and has no patience whatsoever for the etiological tradition; she will want to know why history is in the picture at all. And one is a philosopher of neuroscience who will want to know what any of this buys for function ascriptions to neural representations, where there is no selection story to speak of.

Please draft the prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room -- not the stock objections from the literature that this particular audience would not bother raising -- ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it, each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me explicitly what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and where I should retreat to a weaker claim rather than defend the stronger one. Finish with the single question I am hoping nobody asks. I have roughly twenty minutes of discussion, so the sheet needs to be prioritized rather than exhaustive.
