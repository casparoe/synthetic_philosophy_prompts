I sit on the interpretation advisory board of our regional museum, which is opening a permanent gallery on the ends of settlements this spring, and I am the only philosopher on it. The board must approve the label copy at Thursday's meeting; the fabricator needs final wording by Friday, so the memo I circulate beforehand is our last real chance to change the text. Please draft that memo for me.

The panel in dispute describes a river-valley town whose excavation I have followed for years. The stratigraphy is not in question: the occupation layers stop a few centimeters beneath a volcanic ash fall, and tree-ring and lake-sediment series place a decade of cold, dry summers in the same window. The town's granaries, however, drew on a distant imperial provisioning system that was contracting in those same years; the storehouse was emptied before the ash fell, and the river trade that had supported the town was already shifting downstream.

The draft label reads, in full: "The eruption ended the town." The gallery's introductory wall also lists the things that "act" upon settlements, and the volcano is on that list. Our education staff will build the school-group materials out of whatever wording we approve.

Three positions have hardened. The chair holds that the label says exactly what the evidence shows and that softening it is capitulation to sentiment. A historian on the board calls it environmental determinism and wants the volcano demoted to a line of background. A third member, an epidemiologist, argues for what she calls a recipe model: an outcome is produced by a set of conditions that jointly suffice, no one of which is necessary by itself, and the same outcome may have more than one recipe — so the eruption is one ingredient in one recipe and the emptied storehouse is an ingredient in another. She wants the label rewritten accordingly.

Write the memo. Recommend a specific disposition of the label — approve as written, approve with the wording you supply, or return it — with a deadline the board can hold to, and give reasons the members can argue against rather than merely assent to. Give the historian's objection an honest hearing: say plainly whether the label is determinist, and if it is, what is actually wrong with determinism here rather than merely that it is unfashionable. In correcting that, do not let the social explanation quietly strip the eruption of causal weight. Say whether her recipe model is the right instrument for two sentences of wall text or too much machinery for the purpose. And say whether the gallery should keep the volcano on its list of things that "act," distinguishing causal efficacy from agency if you think the distinction earns its keep.
