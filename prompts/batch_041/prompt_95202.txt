A colleague defends AI safety via debate by likening it to an adversarial trial. On his picture, two advocates argue before a judge, each has an incentive to expose the other's errors, and the verdict tends to track the truth. He concludes that a weak judge can supervise a much stronger model, because the adversarial structure does the epistemic work. I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a simple endorsement or rejection.

Please set out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the prosecution and the defence, the judge, the jury, the rules of evidence, the burden of proof, the appeal, and the enforceable verdict. Then identify where the analogy breaks, and say how far it holds overall. Give particular attention to obfuscated arguments, that is, cases in which one debater can win by making the judge unable to follow the reasoning rather than by being right. Suppose the judge is a model of much lower capability than the debaters, has a fixed query budget, cannot inspect their internal reasoning, and may itself have been trained on the debaters' outputs. Ask what corresponds, in a trial, to a valid but impenetrably complex proof that the judge cannot check, and which procedural safeguards of a trial have no analogue here.

Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion — that debate can scale oversight to superhuman models — from those that do not. Finally, state whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy is available. Please answer in British English.
