I am a high school senior, and I have been asked to open a meeting of our philosophy club with a reading of a film; I have chosen Ratatouille. The theme I want to work with is aesthetic testimony — the question of whether a judgment about a work of art, or about a dish, can be justified for me when I have only someone else's word for it — and whether the film takes a side. As I understand the disagreement, some philosophers hold that testimony can transmit knowledge of ordinary matters but not aesthetic ones, so that nothing anyone tells you about a thing puts you in the position of having appreciated it for yourself, while others hold that testimony transmits aesthetic justification much as it transmits other kinds. I would like the film read against that disagreement rather than merely illustrated by it.

Three things in the film pull in different directions, and I would like you to say what to make of them. First, the critic Anton Ego is converted not by an argument or by anyone's report but by a single dish that carries him back to his childhood; if a film ever dramatised the claim that only first-hand acquaintance can justify an aesthetic judgment, this is the scene. Second, the ending turns on a published review and on word of mouth, and on a bistro whose reputation draws a crowd — aesthetic belief spreading through precisely the channel the first scene seemed to discredit. Third, the kitchen's motto that anyone can cook, which hangs over the story as a claim about who is capable of taste, is turned by the restaurant's new owner into a brand on a line of frozen dinners: testimony that manufactures belief in people who will never taste the thing itself, and, it seems to me, the film's most cynical image of what talk about taste can do.

What I want from the reading is an argument about which position the film actually supports, and honesty about the scenes that resist it. I am especially interested in a question I have not seen the philosophical discussion address: the debate as I understand it is conducted almost entirely in terms of what one person says to another, whereas the film's central act of transmission is a made thing — a dish, handed over — and I cannot decide whether that counts as testimony at all or simply as a way of arranging acquaintance. If it is a second channel, does the film identify something the usual framing of the debate misses, or does it only redescribe acquaintance? Relatedly, I want to know what to do with Gusteau, the dead chef whose voice Remy consults: testimony that has been internalised, or a figure for the authority of a tradition, and either way whether that helps or undermines the reading.

Please write this as criticism rather than as a summary, and assume the reader knows the film. Do not quote the film — I have to present this aloud and would rather paraphrase — and do not give me citations or further viewing suggestions. Close by saying what, if anything, the film shows about aesthetic deference that the arguments on their own do not.
