You are a philosopher who works on moral responsibility in the tradition of P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment: the reactive attitudes, the contrast between the participant and the objective stance, and the further contrast between excusing a particular action and exempting an agent from the moral community altogether. Work carefully and in stages. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm: set out every rule you can think of that might generate the pattern of verdicts below, then test each rule against each verdict and discard the ones that cannot survive, and only then turn to the new case. If every rule you end up with delivers the same verdict on the new case, you have not stated them precisely enough; go back and sharpen them. Do not tidy away the disagreements among the surviving rules — the point of the exercise is to locate them and say which way the evidence points.

I have been keeping a record of the verdicts of a colleague, whom I will call Nadia. She works on moral responsibility in the Strawsonian tradition, she is careful, and her judgements have been consistent. The following are the facts as she took them, each followed by her verdict.

1. A man, in a fit of envy, deliberately humiliates a colleague in front of their department. He knows exactly what he is doing, does it because he wants to, and would do it again. Nadia: blameworthy.

2. A man with no psychiatric history, in the grip of a sudden first psychotic episode, attacks a stranger under the delusion that the stranger is a demon about to kill him. Nadia: not blameworthy, and not merely excused; she says that while the episode lasts we should take the objective attitude to him, and that what he does then is something to be managed rather than answered.

3. A year later the same man, now well, with medication and appointments available to him, stops taking the medication and stops attending appointments because he dislikes the side-effects, and relapses. In the relapse he attacks someone. Nadia: blameworthy, even though she agrees that at the moment of the attack he was as deluded as he had been the first time.

4. A woman who was abducted at the age of three and raised in isolation by her captor, who taught her that the outside world is a conspiracy and that doing harm to certain people is a duty. At twenty-five, having never met a contrary view she could take seriously, she acts on that teaching, wholeheartedly and with no flicker of doubt. Nadia: not blameworthy; she says the woman is exempt, not excused.

5. A man raised in an ordinary town where nearly everyone, including his parents and his teachers, held a demeaning view of a local minority. He absorbed the view without ever examining it, had access to contrary views the whole time, and never took them seriously. At thirty he acts on it. Nadia: blameworthy.

6. A man in his fifties whose personality changes abruptly when a tumour is found; previously gentle, he becomes contemptuous and violent, and injures a neighbour. Nadia: not blameworthy; exempt again, not excused.

7. A woman who, through an ordinary and entirely blameless error, forms a false factual belief — she takes a container to hold sugar when it holds poison — and so harms someone she was trying to help. Nadia: not blameworthy, but she insists that this is an excuse for the action and not an exemption of the agent: the woman remains a full participant in our practices.

The new case. A man of ordinary intelligence who, from earliest childhood and for no discoverable reason — no injury, no illness, no deprivation, no trauma — has never been able to be moved by the interests of anyone he has not met. He understands the moral rules as well as anyone; he can state, accurately and without hesitation, the reasons why the suffering of the stranger he later defrauds would count; he is neither deluded nor coerced; he is warm and loyal to his friends and family, and has never harmed anyone he knows. He simply cannot be brought to care about people he has not met, and no argument, drug or therapy has made the slightest difference. Last year he defrauded a stranger of her life savings, having weighed her ruin against a modest gain to himself and found the comparison obvious. He is not remorseful. Nadia is asked for her verdict.

Give me the following, in this order. First, at least two rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts and that are stated precisely enough to disagree with one another; for each, say exactly what it implies about the new case. Second, your best guess at the verdict Nadia will give, which rule you think she is really running, and why you prefer it — including whatever in the record tells against the rivals. Third, one further case that would separate your leading rule from its closest rival, with the verdict each rule would give in that case. Fourth, an honest statement of what would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong.

End with a single final line in this exact machine-readable form: \answer{VERDICT | RULE}, where VERDICT is one of blameworthy, excused, or exempt, and RULE is a short label for the rule you take her to be running.
