I've been kicking around the expected-value fanaticism thing for ages, ever since a friend in my rationalist book club told me that anyone who refuses a 1-in-a-googol chance of infinite bliss is just being irrational, and I realised I couldn't tell whether I disagreed with the maths or with the ethics. What I want from you is a thought experiment built to order, not a summary of the usual Pascal's wager or infinite-utility cases. The spec: construct a decision problem in which expected value maximisation recommends an action that almost any sane person would find monstrous or absurd, but where the fanaticism is not produced by an obviously silly probability assignment, by infinity, or by an appeal to a literal god. Instead it should fall out of a structural feature of the case that a reasonable person could actually face, such as uncertainty about the probability itself, a payoff that is finite but enormous, or the fact that the same expected-value reasoning applies to a whole series of small independent choices. Keep it realistic enough that the reader could imagine being in the situation, no longer than a paragraph, and make sure the case is one where a rival view such as risk-weighted expected utility or a maximin-style rule gives the opposite verdict. After the scenario, walk through what expected value theory says and why, what the rival theory says and why, and tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then do the honest bit: say what happens if you remove or tweak that feature, and how a committed expected-value maximiser would try to defuse the case without just denying the arithmetic. The point of the exercise is to show that fanaticism is a feature of the structure of expected value reasoning rather than a bug that only shows up when someone starts talking about infinite heaven, so please make the case do that work rather than stipulating that expected value is fanatical.
