I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, midway through a chapter on the ethics of intervening in prices, and I've hit a problem that is less about the literature than about my own judgments. I keep reacting to cases, and the reactions don't obviously come from one standard. Here are the ones I've been carrying around. A coastal town, the day after a storm knocks out the water system, forbids anyone from selling bottled water above the price it fetched the week before. My reaction: obviously right, and I don't even feel the tug of the objection that the cap will mean less water gets trucked in. The same town forbids hotels from raising room rates for the week after the storm. Same reaction, a little weaker. A city with severe restrictions on new construction forbids a landlord from raising the rent on a sitting tenant by more than inflation. My reaction: fine, and I'd say so knowing that the landlord will let the building run down. Now the ones that go the other way. A state sets a floor of fifteen dollars an hour on wages, and a sixty-two-year-old with no other offers says he'd rather work at twelve than not at all. My reaction: not fine — the law overrides a bargain both parties wanted, and I can't shake the thought that it's the man himself who pays for it. A state caps the price at which tickets to a concert may be resold, at the price printed on them. My reaction: not fine, even though no one is desperate here and the reseller did buy the ticket, though I notice the landlord also bought his building. A state caps what a wedding photographer may charge for a Saturday in June. My reaction: absurd, and not merely because it's pointless — I think it wrongs her. A state caps the price of insulin near its cost of production. My reaction: fine, with no tug at all. And a town during a drought caps what golf courses may pay for water while also barring anyone from filling a private pool. My reaction: fine, though the golf course is the least desperate party in any of these stories and the water is going to something nobody needs.

The trouble is that I can't get the water case and the wage case into the same frame. Both are ceilings on the price of something a buyer urgently needs from a seller who holds most of the cards; in one I think the ceiling is plainly required, in the other plainly wrong. My best attempt so far runs like this: a price may be capped when the seller's margin comes from the buyer's lack of any alternative rather than from anything the seller made or did, and when the buyer would otherwise lose something she cannot do without. That gets the water, the hotel, the insulin, and, stretching, the rent, since the tenant's alternatives were foreclosed by the same city's zoning; it gets the photographer and the tickets by denying that anyone cannot do without them. But it fails on the wage case, because the sixty-two-year-old's alternatives are as thin as anyone's and the employer's margin comes from exactly that thinness; and it fails on the golf course, where the buyer is the least desperate party in the story and the case still strikes me as clearly fine. If I add a clause about diversion — the water would otherwise go to other users, the golf green would not — the golf course comes back, but then I can't explain why I don't equally want to cap the rents in that city on the ground that a tenant's dollars divert housing from other tenants. And if I confine the whole thing to goods necessary for a decent life, the wage case still doesn't come back, and I lose the ticket verdict too, since concert tickets aren't necessary for anything.

What I'd like from you is to tell me what I actually think, or what I would think if I were consistent. Please state the principle, or the small set of principles with a priority rule between them, as precisely as you can, and say for each clause which of my cases motivates it and which of them it strains. Then apply it to three cases I haven't mentioned, so that I can see whether it earns its keep: a landlord forbidden from ending a tenancy in order to rent to someone who will pay more; a state that sets a minimum price for milk in order to keep small dairy farms alive; and an employer and a union agreeing, with no state involvement, on a wage floor for a single factory. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate and what I ought to give up, and whether the tidier principle that costs me a verdict is preferable to the clumsier one that saves them all. I'd rather see the account worked out on the page than a survey of what other people have said about it.
