A colleague and I attended a seminar at which the speaker remarked, almost in passing, that Skolem's paradox shows that uncountability cannot be an absolute property of sets. Two other members of the audience, Ines and Marcus, carried the argument on afterwards. I followed most of it but could not decide it, and neither of them would give ground. Here is the exchange as fairly as I can reconstruct it.

To keep the dispute self-contained: ZFC is a first-order theory; the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem states that a first-order theory in a countable language, if it has a model at all, has a countable model; and ZFC proves that uncountable sets exist — the set of subsets of the natural numbers, for instance. So there is a countable model M containing a member that M regards as the set of all subsets of its own natural numbers and that M proves uncountable, while we, standing outside M, can enumerate every member of M, and therefore every member of that set.

1. Ines: That is the whole of Skolem's paradox, and it shows that uncountable is not a property of a set but only a property of a set-relative-to-a-model. Inside M the set is uncountable; outside it is countable; both are true, and there is nothing further to be right about. The realist who thinks there is a model-independent fact about whether a given collection is uncountable is positing a God's-eye fact that no one could ever state.

2. Marcus: That is an equivocation. Uncountable means, precisely, that there is no bijection between the set and the natural numbers. Inside M there is no such bijection, and there is not one in reality either, unless we help ourselves to bijections that lie outside M and are not members of M. So the set really is uncountable, full stop; what is countable is the model, not the set. The theorem tells us that models can be impoverished — that they can omit functions which exist — not that cardinality is relative.

3. Ines: But that reply assumes we can already identify which sets are the real natural numbers and which functions are the real bijections. The very fact that M satisfies every axiom of ZFC means that everything you want to say about the real reals you can say equally inside some countable model of your own. If the realist's facts are facts statable inside a countable model, then by the same reasoning they are countable facts. There is no real uncountable set, only the set as counted from where you happen to stand.

4. Marcus: That slide, from every axiom being satisfied in some countable model to there being no fact of the matter, is precisely what I am denying. The theorem is conditional: if a theory has a model, it has a countable one. It does not say that every model is countable, nor that the universe of sets is a model of the kind the theorem quantifies over. And the difference between a bijection that is in M and one that is not is a perfectly ordinary difference: a function is a set of ordered pairs, and a set of pairs either is or is not among the members of M. M's failure to contain it is not a confusion about counting; it is what failure to contain means.

5. Ines: Then the realist owes an account of how finite creatures like us determinately refer to the intended uncountable sets at all. No first-order axioms pin down the full power set of the natural numbers: whatever axioms you write down, if they have a model they have a countable one, in which your words all subsets receive a non-standard interpretation. And consider the regress: whatever model you take to be the real one, if it is a set model of ZFC it is countable from a larger vantage point, and so on without end. There is no last level at which uncountable is said absolutely. The most economical reading is that the word is indexical — it means uncountable relative to the collections available here — and the realist's absolute reading is the extra hypothesis.

6. Marcus: That is a claim about the limits of first-order axiomatisation, not about cardinality. It shows that no first-order theory is categorical for the power set of the naturals; it does not show that no collection is uncountable. The same argument would prove that natural number, or finite, is indeterminate, since there are non-standard models of arithmetic — yet nobody concludes that finite and infinite are relative. And the regress is real but benign: at each level the facts at that level are genuine facts. Relativity-to-a-model is not the same as indexicality; here shifts with the speaker, but not a member of M does not shift at all. You are turning a theorem about what some models omit into a claim about what there is.

That is the exchange. Please adjudicate it point by point. For each of the six rounds, say what is right and what is wrong in that particular move, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what each of them is missing that neither has stated, whether the paradox is genuinely paradoxical or merely looks so, and who is closer to right overall — distinguishing, where you need to, the mathematical theorem from the philosophical reading placed upon it.
