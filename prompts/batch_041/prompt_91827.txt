I've spent about a decade doing release engineering — branching models, tag namespaces, semantic versioning, cherry-picks, force-pushes, the occasional emergency history rewrite — and one side effect is that I now find it hard to think of any timeline as something you discover rather than something a team authors and maintains. I've been reading up on the Anthropocene debate, and it has started to feel less like a geology question than like a release-management question wearing a lab coat: a working group spent roughly fifteen years converging on a boundary in the mid-twentieth century, keyed to plutonium fallout from atmospheric bomb tests and anchored in the sediments of a lake in Ontario, and the proposal was voted down by the relevant subcommission and then confirmed as rejected by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences — and yet the word keeps being used by everyone, including the bodies that rejected it. Meanwhile the alternative dates on offer run from the beginnings of agriculture through the Industrial Revolution to the mid-century acceleration, plus at least one proposal tied to the early-modern population collapse in the Americas.

What I want is not an analogy but a translation. Take the whole dispute — the competing start dates, the machinery of who has standing to ratify a boundary, and what it means that ratification failed while the term flourished anyway — and restate it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary: commits and tags, branches and forks, main versus release, semantic versioning and breaking changes, retroactive tagging, rewritten or squashed history, maintainers and contributors and who holds commit access, deprecation, backporting. Go position by position rather than in one blob: for each candidate start date, say what it becomes in my terms, then what has to be distorted to make it fit, then what simply has no expression at all. Assume I know version control cold and that my geology is at the level of a curious amateur — don't pad the answer with geological background I didn't ask for, but flag clearly whenever the translation is leaning on something stratigraphic that I might be getting wrong.

Finally, the part I actually care about: the residue report. Where my vocabulary cannot carry the geologists' claim, is that a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was vaguer than it looked? My working hunch is that the requirement of a single, globally correlatable, physically synchronous marker in the rock record is the piece that will not translate — in my world a boundary exists because whoever holds the tag namespace says so, and "when it started" is downstream of "who decides" — so I suspect the real disagreement is about authority rather than about time. Tell me whether that hunch survives translation or whether I have imported a maintainer where the geologists were looking for a fact, and be explicit about any point where you had to assume something on my behalf in order to keep the translation running. I don't need sources, citations, or a reading list; I want the translation and the residue.
