I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent the better part of two years thinking about the brain-in-a-vat scenario on my own, and next month I am leading a session on it at the monthly philosophy discussion group that meets in the back room of a pub in my city. I would like you to prepare me for the question period.

My position, in brief: the vat story is usually run together with the older idea of a deceiver who deliberately misleads us, and that conflation is what gives it its force. I think these are two very different scenarios. A deceiver is an agent who can lie; a vat is a machine, and a machine cannot lie, only produce. So a vat can at most leave me ignorant of which physical world I am in — it cannot make my beliefs about the world systematically false in the way a deceiver can. I have also worked out a variant to use as an illustration: a vat whose program contains, as one of its own subroutines, a complete and accurate description of the vat and of the program itself, so that an inhabitant who reasoned carefully enough could in principle discover the truth from the inside. If that scenario is even coherent, then "being in a vat" stops being a special predicament and becomes an ordinary question about which world one is in.

The audience: about twenty-five regulars. Two are current philosophy PhD students, one in epistemology and one in philosophy of mind, who come because the venue is near campus and who I expect to be impatient with skepticism as a dead issue. Three or four are software engineers who take the simulation argument seriously and will likely press me on probability. A small contingent from a local church attends these debates and tends to be interested in whether a mind can exist without a body. The rest are curious non-specialists who will want to know how this differs from The Matrix and whether any of it matters.

Tell me the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each group would put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. I do not need the textbook objections that any undergraduate would raise; I need the ones this particular room will raise. Tell me what I should concede at the outset and what I should refuse to give up. And tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

I would rather have a straight answer than a flattering one: if the distinction I am relying on is one that people who work on this have already made and set aside, or if I am wrong about what this audience would actually ask, or if the framing is confused from the start, say so plainly rather than producing the prep sheet anyway.
