My teacher said the interesting part isn't the yes or no answer, it's all the moves people make to get *out* of having to answer it, so she made us write down every move we could think of. Here's my list, in my own words: (a) just do the math — if the expected lives saved is bigger than the expected harm of torturing him, do it, no special rules needed; (b) rights are absolute up to some catastrophic line, and past that line you can override them; (c) never, full stop, partly because the scenario basically never happens in real life and partly because once it's permitted at all the institutions rot; (d) it's a genuine tragedy — you should do it and you're guilty of doing it, both at once, and the good outcome doesn't wash the guilt off; (e) you don't actually have to hurt him, you just have to make him believe you will, so the real question is about lying and bluffing, not about pain; (f) the scenario is rigged, because it hands you certainty you'd never have (that there's a bomb, that he knows where it is, that he'll crack in time, that torture even works for this), so the real question isn't "may I" but "what rules should exist in advance", which is a completely different question; and (g) if you're in this situation at all, everything upstream already failed — intelligence, policing, whatever — so it's not a moral dilemma, it's a screwup with a body count.
