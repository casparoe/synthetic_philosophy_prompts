Im a third-year in the phil/CS joint program and I help run the student reading group attached to our alignment seminar. Next week our guest is a PhD student in the alignment group here who's been defending a line I find interesting and also kinda suspicious: she thinks corrigibility is an epistemic problem rather than a value problem. Her story is roughly that if an agent is honestly uncertain about what we want, then the fact that we press the shutdown button is itself evidence about the objective, so an ordinary Bayesian agent will let itself be turned off without anyone having to hard-code a special 'don't resist shutdown' rule. I have 40 minutes with her in front of the group and I dont want to burn it on her restating the argument. Can you draft 9 or 10 interview questions that actually bite, with a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer she's most likely to reach for (I'd bet money she says something like 'well, the agent should be uncertain about its model of the operator' whenever she's cornered).

Here's what I need covered, roughly one question each. Whether the uncertainty does any work once the agent is confident and the stakes are large -- when the agent is 99% sure what we want and the upside is enormous, does the button still have pull, or does her kind of corrigibility quietly evaporate as capability goes up? The manipulation worry: if a button press is evidence, the agent has a reason to manage the evidence, to time its disclosures, hide things, or nudge the operator into not pressing. Permanence: in the toy cases the button is basically a one-hour pause, so I want to ask whether the view only works because shutdown is cheap there, and what happens when it's irreversible. Fallible operators: we press it by mistake, or drunk, or out of spite. And I want to inject a scenario of my own to see what her framework does with it -- suppose the operator sometimes hits the button purely as a loyalty check, to see whether the agent complies, and the agent knows the rate; does it comply and die, or treat the press as unreliable evidence and stay on, and if the latter, is there any observation left that would count against her view? Then one on whose values are being tracked: the person who wrote the objective at t1 and the person pressing the button at t2 disagree about what we want, so whose uncertainty is the agent supposed to be tracking? And one on the failure mode in the other direction -- does her picture predict an agent that's too eager to be switched off, one that treats every ambiguity as a reason to stop and never does anything useful, and how would she tell that apart from the good case. Finish with a single question about what a failed implementation would look like: what she'd have to see to admit that the epistemic route doesn't buy you corrigibility after all. No 'how did you get into this' stuff and please don't have me ask her to define corrigibility from scratch, she'll just do that unprompted.
