I am a high-school student, and for my philosophy club's year-end project I have been playing the self-amending game Nomic with my classmates — the game in which players change the rules during play, including the rules that govern how rules may be changed — and the club has split into two camps that keep talking past each other about what this shows. I would like you to draft the joint statement that the two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The first camp, which I will call the grounding camp, holds that self-amendment is not paradoxical: a valid rule always derives its validity from a higher rule, and the hierarchy ends in a foundational rule — a basic norm or rule of recognition — that is not itself validated by any rule but is instead a social fact or a presupposition, so a rule can authorize its own amendment without circularity. The second camp, the reflexivity camp, holds that this only relocates the problem: if the foundational rule validates every rule, then a change to it is validated either by itself (a circle) or by nothing (arbitrariness); and if it cannot be changed, the order is not genuinely self-amending. On this view the paradox is real, and a legal order rests on an extra-legal act.

The club is stuck on two concrete puzzles, which I would like the joint statement to address. (i) A ruleset consisting of a single rule: "This rule may be repealed by a simple majority." A simple majority votes to repeal it. Was the repeal valid? (ii) A ruleset with two rules: (A) "Any rule may be amended by a two-thirds majority," and (B) "Rule A may not be amended." A two-thirds majority votes to amend Rule B. Is the amendment valid?

Please produce the document they should have produced. I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps accept about the structure of self-amending rules; about what would have to be true for something to count as a paradox rather than a merely surprising feature; about the difference between amending an ordinary rule and amending the foundational rule; and about what the Nomic-style cases do and do not establish.
2. The precise remaining disagreements, each written in wording that the camp holding it would accept as its own, not slanted toward the other camp.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp.
4. For each crux, a case, argument, or thought experiment that both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about it written down in advance.
5. An explicit verdict on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — whether the camps mean different things by "rule," "validity," or "foundational," and would agree if those terms were fixed — and if so, which part.

Please take a definite position rather than surveying the options: where you must choose between two ways of stating a camp's view, choose the one you can best defend and say why; where you think a disagreement is verbal, say so outright. Do not hedge by offering several possible drafts.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the crux on which the whole disagreement turns.
