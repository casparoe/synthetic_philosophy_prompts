The paragraph below is from an explainer a colleague is drafting for a general readership. He sent it to me because he knows I write about philosophy of mind, and I have been using a looser version of the same framing in my own column for about a year. Before I take it any further, I want to know how much of it is defensible:

'Functionalism in the philosophy of mind says that a mental state is whatever plays a certain causal role in a system — what makes a state a pain is the job it does, not the material it is made of. That is why functionalists think a silicon chip could be in pain, or an octopus, or a Martian: the same mental state can be realized in different physical hardware, which is what philosophers call multiple realizability. This is the argument that killed the identity theory, the view that pain is just C-fiber firing. If pain were a particular brain state, then an octopus, whose nervous system looks nothing like ours, could not feel pain, which is absurd. So functionalism is really behaviorism with better manners: both say that what makes something a pain is what it makes you do. In short, functionalism is physicalism done properly — the mind is what the brain does, described at a higher level. And the moral for our purposes is that if you get the software right, the machine is conscious, which is why the whole debate about whether AI could be conscious is really just a debate about functionalism.'

Please audit it claim by claim rather than as a whole. For each claim, tell me what a philosopher of mind would sign off on, what would make them wince, and what they would reject; and separate the places where the prose is merely loose from the places where the summary has quietly changed the substance. Three things I would especially like addressed: whether the argument against the identity theory does what the paragraph says it does, and what the strongest reply from an identity theorist would be; whether the closing claim about software and AI consciousness follows from the parts of the paragraph that survive correction; and a remark from a philosopher I interviewed, that the multiple realizability argument 'cuts both ways', which I did not understand at the time and have been quoting ever since. I am not after a reading list. I need to know which sentences I can keep, which need qualifying, and which I should cut, and I would like one replacement paragraph that is both accurate and still usable in a general-interest column.
