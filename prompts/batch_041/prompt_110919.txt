Okay, so my political philosophy course is on Nozick this week and I've been arguing with myself about it in the shower, so I'm going to dump my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and see if you can find a principle that actually covers them. I have a feeling they dont all fit together and I want to know which one I should give up.

Start with the famous one, the basketball star who takes a cut of every ticket. Start from whatever distribution you like, the fans hand over their money freely, and he ends up with a mountain of cash while everyone else has exactly what they had before. My gut: completely fine. Unequal, sure, but nobody was wronged, and I don't think the resulting pattern is anyone else's business.

Now change one thing. Someone quietly buys up every well within a week's walk of a desert town, one seller at a time, every seller agreeing freely, no threats, no lies, full information, and then charges whatever people will pay for water. Every single transfer is voluntary, same as the tickets. My gut: this is wrong, and the townspeople have a real complaint against him. But I can't find the step where anyone's rights got stepped on, and if I say the outcome is the problem, I don't see how I get to keep saying the basketball case is fine.

Next, two cases about history. A family has a farm that was grabbed from another family three generations back, by a law that was legal at the time but was obviously a land grab. The current owners just inherited it, they did nothing, they bought nothing. My gut: the land is tainted, but you can't just take it off them now. If the grab happened last year instead, I think you can. And then: a guy inherits a fortune his great-grandfather made running a plantation with enslaved people. My gut: he owes something to someone, even though he personally did nothing wrong and the actual victims are long dead and unidentifiable.

Then a pair that's supposed to feel like the water case but doesn't, for me. Someone signs on to be another person's live-in servant for ten years for a lump sum, fully informed, no coercion, and regrets it in year three. Someone sells a kidney for cash and regrets it. My gut on both: unwise, maybe gross, but not unjust. Yet the water buyer was in the same spot as the servant's employer — he set the price and the other side had nothing to bargain with. So why is one wrong and the others not? I honestly can't tell you.

So here's the half-baked principle I've been trying on: a holding is just if (a) every actual step in its history was voluntary — no force, no fraud, no deception — and (b) at each step the people on the losing end had a genuine alternative they could have taken instead. Test it on all of the above. My worry is that (b) is secretly doing all the work and is really just a claim about the pattern, which is the thing the whole historical view says is irrelevant. And (b) gets me the water case but it also condemns the servant contract, which I called fine, and probably the kidney sale too. So either I flip my verdict on the servant, or I lose the water case.

What I want: the best principle, or the two or three best candidates, stated precisely, with the specific case that forces each clause. Then tell me which of my verdicts each candidate makes me give up, and don't be nice about it. Also run whichever you favor on a case I haven't listed: a company trains a model on a few million people's writing without asking and it then generates value for them. Nobody was mugged, the writers weren't deprived of anything they had, and I genuinely don't know what my gut says. And one more: a factory owner buys his land and his labour freely, but the smoke ruins the neighbouring farms, and the neighbours never agreed to anything at all.
