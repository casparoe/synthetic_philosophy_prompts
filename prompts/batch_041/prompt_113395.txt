I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy — I listen to a philosophy podcast on my commute and argue about it with a colleague during code review, which is the extent of it — and for a while now I've been collecting a pattern that I can describe precisely but cannot name. The pattern is this: we say of some thing that it would produce a certain outcome under certain conditions, the statement seems obviously true, and then it fails to hold for reasons that don't feel like the same reason in each case. Four specimens from the past few months. First, our staging load balancer runs a health check that pulls a node out of rotation the instant it starts returning errors, so a node carrying a known bug never actually serves a failing request; a month ago I would have said with confidence that such a node would return errors to users, and I am no longer sure what I should say now. Second, a payment service is set to retry three times on timeout, and that setting is correct and untouched, but a circuit breaker sits in front of it and swallows the timeouts before they arrive, so the retry logic is intact and simply never gets to run. Third, a cache holds a stale entry whose shape happens to match the real response, so callers keep getting healthy-looking answers while the service behind it is down — nothing here has any tendency to answer correctly, and yet correct-looking answers keep appearing. Fourth, and this is the one that made me write this down, a colleague is meticulous in exactly the way you would want, but the moment you make him formally responsible for reviewing production configs he becomes anxious, rushes, and starts missing things; the job that ought to set his care in motion is what dissolves it. The everyday versions bother me in the same way: a friend who is genuinely generous but whose generosity evaporates the moment you ask her for money, so that the asking seems to destroy the trait rather than merely block it; a man whose courage is not in any doubt and who nonetheless faints at the sight of blood; a house that would stand up to a gale because a hill shelters it, though the framing was never actually stressed. My rough sense is that some of these have been discussed under separate headings, and that what I've read either treats one case as the whole story or lumps everything together under a heading too broad to do any work.

What I want is not a label but a carving, so let me be specific about what would satisfy me. Is there an established term, or a small set of established terms, for the sub-patterns I've described, and is there a term for the family as a whole — or is the family itself a mistake, that is, are these really several unrelated things that only look alike because of how I happen to be describing them? For each term you give, tell me what it attaches to: the thing and its tendency, the interfering condition, or the mechanism that does the interfering. Then tell me how my specimens differ from the dull cases that don't interest me — where the conditions simply never arose, where the thing never had the tendency in the first place, and where we were just wrong about it. I'd also like the boundary cases adjudicated. Is the circuit breaker in my second specimen an outside interference or just part of how the system is built, and does that distinction matter? Is the fourth specimen the same phenomenon as the first, given that in one the interfering condition is designed into the machinery and in the other it is a person's anxiety? What do we say about a tendency that never gets to show itself because it is known — a trader's edge that vanishes once others discover it, a person whose honesty has never been tested because nobody has ever asked? And is there a case in the same family where the interfering condition is a matter of how we describe the situation rather than a fact about the world, since the house and the hill feel like that to me? Finally, if the general family has no name, coin one and define it, and say explicitly which of my specimens fall under it and which don't. Please explain whatever you mention well enough to stand on its own; I can't look anything up while I read your answer, and a list of names and authors would be useless to me.

Please write in American English, and end with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing only the term or terms you settled on — one per specimen, in my order, followed by your name for the family as a whole (or "none" if you think there isn't one) — with nothing else on that line.
