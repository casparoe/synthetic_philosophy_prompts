I run a biweekly reading group on decision theory for a mixed crowd of graduate students in economics and philosophy, none of them specialists in the foundations, and for three sessions running we have gotten stuck on the same pair of puzzles. The first is the standard counterfactual mugging: a perfect predictor flips a fair coin, and in one branch of the outcome he asks you for $100, while in the other branch he hands you $10,000 only if he predicted that you would have paid in the branch where he asks. The second is the blackmail case in which an honest and perfect predictor tells you that he has sent you this message if and only if exactly one of two things is true — your house is infested with termites, or you pay him $1,000 — where the infestation would cost you $1,000,000. Every session ends the same way: someone says the answer is obviously no in the first case and obviously yes in the second, someone else says the two cases are really one case, and we never reach the question I actually want answered, which is what the disagreement is about. Please take on the role of a decision theorist with a firm command of this literature and build me a decision tree — a classification instrument for the debate, not an essay about it. Begin by spending a few paragraphs mapping the logical space: list the commitments that can vary independently here and the combinations they permit, and only then draw the tree. The internal nodes should be the questions that actually split the space, each phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to (something like "does what makes an act worth doing include facts the act cannot cause?" rather than "are you a causal decision theorist?"), and the leaves should be positions, named where a standard name exists and described by their commitments where it does not. I would rather have a leaf described than labeled wrongly, so do not stake anything on a term of art you are not sure is standard.

I want these distinctions represented as nodes: whether an act's value depends on anything outside its causal reach; if it does, whether the relevant outside-the-reach relation is probabilistic (the act raises the probability of something) or logical or functional (the act is the output of a procedure that shows up elsewhere in the situation); whether the thing being evaluated is the act, a policy over acts, or a whole decision procedure; whether the evaluation ranges over branches in which the coin fell the other way and you never got to deliberate at all; and whether an adversary who is deliberately trying to exploit your policy changes the verdict. Beyond the two puzzles above, use these cases, which we invented and which you should treat as the cases for the exercise even if a textbook would set them up differently: the mugging with a predictor who is right only eighty percent of the time, so that if the answer is not simply the same as before it has to be argued for rather than asserted; a version in which you are asked for the $100 but not told which way the coin fell; a version in which the counterfactual payoff is controlled by an adversary who wants you to pay; and a version in which you are allowed to commit before the setup is described. I need leaves for at least the causal family, the evidential family, the updateless or policy family that pays in both puzzles, a split view that pays the mugger while refusing the blackmailer, a view on which the puzzles have no verdict until a prior over the predictor's reliability is fixed, and a view on which only prior commitment matters. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, say so and diagnose it in a sentence — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves are distinguished only by a case nobody has actually run, flag that rather than papering over it, and where a leaf's occupant turns on an unresolved technical question, say which.

Keep it to a dozen leaves or fewer, one sentence each naming that position's most awkward commitment, and skip the reading list — I want the positions described, not cited. Finish by telling me how confident you are in the tree as a whole, and single out the one step you are least sure of: a node whose placement you would treat as provisional, or a leaf you suspect is in fact empty.
