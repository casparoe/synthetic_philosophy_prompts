I am a transactional lawyer — fifteen years of practice, mostly commercial agreements, with a substantial amount of agency and fiduciary work — and I have lately been reading about the alignment problem in AI. I do not understand the technical literature well. I understand contracts and agency doctrine very well. I would like you to put the two into contact, and then tell me what fails to survive the trip.

Here is the distinction as I have come to understand it. On the narrow reading, the problem is to build systems that reliably do what their operators intend: systems that pursue the objective they were given rather than some nearby proxy that happens to score well, that do not conceal what they are doing from the people running them, and that can be corrected or shut down. On the broad reading, the problem is to build systems that promote what people actually value — including what they would endorse on reflection, and including people who are not in the room — which is not the same as what they say they want or what their instructions specify. A colleague who works on AI policy insists that only the narrow reading names a real problem, and that the broad reading either collapses into the narrow one or dissolves into aspiration with no operational content. I would rather test that claim against my own materials than against his.

Restate each reading, separately and as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of contract and agency law: offer and acceptance, express and implied terms, the objective theory of contract, incomplete contracts, the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, fiduciary duties of loyalty and care, the principal's best interests, agency costs, monitoring and bonding, capacity, termination at will, remedies such as damages, specific performance, and disgorgement, and the enforcement apparatus all of it presupposes. Treat those materials as your working vocabulary. If a claim will not fit, say so rather than forcing it; and do not quietly substitute the vocabulary of the alignment literature and call the result a translation.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims about alignment translate cleanly into my vocabulary? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly is distorted? Which have no counterpart at all? I am most interested in the third category, and in your judgment about what it signifies: whether my vocabulary is missing something that the alignment debate has, or whether the residue shows that some of that debate is less well defined than it appears when stated in its own terms. Finally, tell me whether the translation supports my colleague's claim, undermines it, or shows that he and I are not disagreeing about the same thing. Be direct; if the exercise exposes an equivocation on either side, say so.
