I'm in a small Discord with a bunch of game-design and philosophy people and we've been arguing for like three weeks straight about whether the characters in games matter morally or whether it's all just pixels, and the fights keep going in circles because everyone actually means something different by "matter" — matter to the fictional person who doesn't exist, to the player's own character and habits, to the social practice of the game, to the real people downstream (the writers, the other players, whoever the character is modelled on, the person in the permadeath save you just reloaded). Someone always says it's just pixels and someone else says they'd never play a game where you hurt a child even though they'll happily mow down a hundred pedestrians, and nobody can say whether those two are consistent. So I want to stop arguing and build an instrument: act like a philosopher of fiction and imagination who also actually plays games, think carefully, and before writing anything brainstorm the logical space out loud — what are the candidate answers to where the moral weight sits, and which combinations of those answers are even coherent — then turn that into a decision tree or sorting quiz that takes a person from their gut reactions to a named position.

Six to nine branch nodes, each a concrete case or a yes/no question about a case, phrased so the person answering has no clue which position it's steering them toward, so no nodes like 'do you think fiction has moral content?'. The cases that split our server, and that I want in there: reloading a save to undo an NPC death you caused; mowing down procedurally generated pedestrians for fun; an NPC that begs you to stop and says it's in pain, where you know it's a script; a character modelled on a specific real person (a dead relative of the player, or someone who never agreed to it); a shared-world NPC that other players have talked to and remember; your own avatar customised to look like you versus a default one; deleting a character as a glitch exploit in a speedrun; playing as a real victim in a game about a real atrocity; and the friend who's fine with virtual murder but refuses the child-abuse game — the tree needs either a node that catches that asymmetry or an explicit note that it can't. Each leaf carries the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really holds it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment, i.e. the case it handles worst. Build it questions-first and place the positions into the resulting space afterwards rather than just listing schools of thought, flag any two leaves the tree genuinely can't separate, and for each empty combination say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Audience is smart non-philosophers, so no jargon without a half-sentence gloss, and please no reading list or citations, I cant use them and it just makes the thing untestable for me. Finish with one line on which node you think is doing the most work.
