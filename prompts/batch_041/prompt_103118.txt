I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and I'm trying to write a chapter section on the normative presuppositions of sex-work law. I've been staring at a local policy package that my supervisor calls a Nordic-plus regime, and I can't tell if its a coherent position or just a pile of compromises. It borrows from the Nordic model (criminalise buyers, not sellers), from the German and Netherlands legalization approach (registered indoor work, health checks, zoning), and from New Zealand decriminalization (no specific sex-work offences, general labour and safety law applies). But the actual bylaw does this: buying is a summary offence; selling is not an offence; anyone selling from a fixed indoor premises must register with the health authority, do quarterly STI checks, and carry a compliance card; street-based sellers are subject to anti-loitering fines that in practice fall almost entirely on them; and police run a diversion scheme that sends repeat street sellers to an NGO for exit counseling but does not charge them. The preamble says the aims are gender equality, reducing trafficking, and not punishing the vulnerable.

I want you to act as a philosopher of law and social policy who knows the sex-work debates but isn't just going to recite teh usual positions. Take a breath, brainstorm the candidate commitments first, and then give me the reconstruction. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Does it treat the buyer as a wrongdoer because of the act, the demand, the exploitation, or the symbolism? Does it presuppose that sex is special in a way that other labour is not, or does the health-and-safety apparatus presuppose the opposite? What view of equality is at work when the preamble says gender equality but the fines and registration burden fall on migrant and street sellers?
