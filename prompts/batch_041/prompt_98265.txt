Ok so I'm in a moral philosophy seminar this term and we've just done Scanlon's What We Owe to Each Other, and I'd rather test the aggregation objection out loud than just read about it. Could you play a committed contractualist -- the real thing, someone who thinks an act is wrong just when it's ruled out by principles no one could reasonably reject, and who insists that justification has to be given to each person one at a time rather than by tipping everyone's gripes into a single pot? Sharp, not a strawman, and please don't cave at the first hard case; if you think my example is rigged, say why, and if you need a detail about the case you can ask me for it. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like an actual conversation rather than a lecture.

Here's my opening move. Take a health authority with a fixed pot of money. Option A spends all of it on surgery that stops one woman from going permanently blind in both eyes. Option B spends it on a screening program that will save 400 people from each losing a single finger. I think B is at least allowed, and probably what they should do -- 400 fingers seems to beat one pair of eyes, whatever the ledger says. But on your view each of those 400 has a complaint that's less serious than hers, and I thought you're not allowed to lump their complaints together, so the principle favouring A can't be reasonably rejected and A is fine. Ok. Now here's the bit that actually bothers me. You definately do let a single person add up her own complaints across time, otherwise a rule that costs you one finger a year for twenty years wouldn't register as one big complaint, it'd register as twenty small ones, and that seems to matter. But the second you allow that, you've admitted aggregation is a legitimate way of weighing reasons, and the person-by-person rule starts to look like a bookkeeping convention about where the plus signs go rather than a deep fact about justification. So which is it: can the 400 pool their fingers, or can the one woman not pool her twenty years?
