I'm running a five-week reading group for grad students in a philosophy of science program, and next week is my turn: Hobbes on geometry. The twist is that all of these people have read the political chapters of Leviathan and nothing else by him, so for them Hobbes is the social contract guy, and I've got fifty minutes to make them see that his account of what a demonstration is is doing real work in the background of everything else he wrote. I don't need you to explain Hobbes's philosophy of geometry to me, I've read enough of the secondary stuff — I need a plan for explaining it, in what order, and I need to know in advance where it will go wrong.

Here's the content I have to get across, roughly: for Hobbes, knowledge properly so called is knowledge of causes, and we can only know the causes of things we ourselves make; the geometer draws the circle, so the geometer knows its cause, which is why geometry is the one science that can be demonstrated rather than merely conjectured; that connects up with his nominalism about definitions, since the definitions of geometry are laid down by us and their truth is therefore not something we have to go out and discover; and it pays off at the end in his claim that civil philosophy is demonstrable too, for the same reason, because we make the commonwealth. What I need from you: the order these pieces have to go in so that the last step doesn't land as a cheap pun on making; whether to put the contrast with natural philosophy early, as a foil (we can't know the causes of bodies we didn't make, so physics is stuck with hypotheses), or save it for the end; and which single example should carry the whole session. I was going to use the circle — you draw it, so you know its cause — but I worry it's too easy and they'll just nod without seeing anything. I'm also tempted to hand out compasses and have them construct a figure and prove a property of it, so they feel the difference between a diagram they made and a diagram they were shown. Tell me whether that's worth ten minutes of a fifty minute session and exactly where in the sequence it belongs, or whether I'd be better off building the session around one of his botched quadratures instead, which is more dramatic but risks them deciding the whole philosophy is a rationalisation of a crank.

Misconceptions I can already predict: they'll hear the word demonstration and assume the point is deduction from self-evident axioms, i.e. that Hobbes is Euclid with extra steps, when the load-bearing idea is the makers knowledge point, not the axiomatic one. A couple of them have read somewhere that his nominalism about definitions makes mathematical truth arbitrary or merely a matter of convention, and I think that's a misreading, but I need the plan to head it off at the right moment rather than in a footnote at the end. They'll also want to file him under something they already know — intuitionism, or fictionalism, or Vico's later thought that we know best what we have made — and I'd rather they see the differences than the resemblance. And someone will bring up the fact that he spent decades insisting he'd squared the circle and treating the mathematicians who told him otherwise as either incompetent or complicit; my suspicion is that story and his theory of demonstration are connected, not one an embarrassing hobby and the other the philosophy, but I'm genuinely unsure how far the connection goes, so tell me where it's real and where I'd be over-reading. Finally: the one question I should ask at the end that would show whether they've actually got the maker's knowledge point rather than just memorised the bit about the commonwealth, and what I should openly tell them I'm not covering at this level.
