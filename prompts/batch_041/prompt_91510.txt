I'm applying for a small interdisciplinary fellowship (philosophy of biology + astrobiology people, six months, basically one paper's worth of money) and I want to pitch something about the definition of life -- but not the standard "is a virus alive" seminar paper, everyone's seen that.

My half-formed angle: people argue about which single criterion is the right one -- metabolism, replication, evolution by natural selection, autonomy, whatever -- as if the question were just which criterion wins. I think the debate is stuck for a different reason. The word "life" is doing at least three different jobs and the argument slides between them without noticing: (a) picking out a natural kind that's supposed to be out there in the world independently of us, (b) marking out a homeostatic property cluster where nothing is strictly necessary but a bunch of features tend to hang together and reinforce each other, and (c) working as a practical/decision-theoretic label -- the kind of thing we should protect, or study, or worry about building. On top of that I think there's a second confusion underneath: the question of what *makes* something alive versus the question of how we'd *detect* or recognize it, and those two pull the criteria in opposite directions (the detectability criteria want something measurable and operational, the metaphysical ones want whatever actually does the work). The borderline cases only look intractable because nobody says which of these jobs the proposed definition is answering.

Turn this into the actual proposal. I need: the central question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, or a determinate reason to think there isn't one; why the existing positions don't settle it; the method -- what I'd actually do, week by week over the six months, since this is mostly conceptual so I need to be honest about what the "work" even consists in; what a successful result would look like versus what would count as the project failing; and how to keep the scope from ballooning, because I know the borderline-case zoo (viruses, plasmids, prions, transposons, fire, crystals, weird synthetic cell-ish things, software agents) could eat the whole six months if I let it.

Couple of constraints: no citations, no reading list -- I'll add those myself, and please don't just tell me to go read the standard papers. I want the framing and the argument structure, not a bibliography. And don't lean on super obscure ideas a general reader wouldn't know; keep it to positions and arguments I can actually state in a sentence or two.
