I am presenting a paper at a joint session of a type-design conference and a cognitive-science workshop. The audience is split. The type designers are mostly from a foundry background and think in terms of skeleton, ductus, contrast, and spacing; two of them are committed to the idea that a letter has an underlying structural essence. The cognitive scientists are mostly categorization researchers, several of whom work on prototypes and exemplars, and one who works on reading and visual word recognition. My thesis is that there is no style-independent essence of the lowercase a. What makes a mark an a is not a checklist of geometric features, a prototype, or a Platonic form, but a role in a generative system of letterforms. More exactly: a shape is an a when it can occupy the a-position in an alphabet under the constraints that it be distinguishable from every other letter in the same style, that it be producible by the same constructional logic as its neighbors, and that it survive the optical corrections needed for spacing, scale, and context. Style, on this view, is not decoration added to a skeleton; it is the set of parameters that determines which skeletons count as solutions. My argument runs: no single feature is necessary and sufficient for a; the best explanation of that fact is role-based; roles are fixed by the whole alphabet and the practice of reading; so the essence of a letterform is systemic rather than intrinsic. I illustrate with a modular stencil alphabet whose a is three straight segments, a geometric sans whose a is a circle plus a stem, a double-story a, and a handwritten a that is illegible in isolation but read as a in context.
