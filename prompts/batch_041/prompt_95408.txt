I am a science-and-ideas journalist, and next month I am presenting a working paper to a philosophy of mind reading group at a research university. The paper defends a limited claim: epiphenomenalism is not refuted by the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The paradox, as I understand it, is that if phenomenal qualities are causally inert, then a judgment that I am experiencing red cannot be causally sensitive to the redness of the experience, so the judgment cannot be about the quality; epiphenomenalism appears to make its own avowals miraculous. My argument is that this inference depends on a causal theory of content for phenomenal judgments. I distinguish the phenomenal quality from what I call the phenomenal predicate: the predicate the brain deploys when it classifies a state as, say, red-ish. I claim that the quality and the predicate can be co-realized by the same neural state without the quality causing the predicate or the judgment. Call this co-realization without causation. On this view, the judgment tracks the quality because the predicate is nomologically tied to the state that realizes the quality, not because the quality exerts causal force. I need to distinguish this from two nearby views: the common-cause model, on which the quality and the judgment are independent effects of a third state, and the causal-inheritance model, on which the judgment's content is inherited from the quality's causal profile. On my view, the state is not a third thing; it is the realizer of the quality, and the predicate is a disposition of that same state. The view is meant to be a version of epiphenomenalism, not a backdoor identity theory or a causal theory with extra steps.

The audience is a philosophy of mind reading group: two illusionists who treat the hard problem as a meta-problem about why we think there is one; one higher-order thought theorist; one predictive-processing cognitive neuroscientist; one phenomenologist in the Heideggerian tradition; and three graduate students who work on attention and reportability. They are naturalists, allergic to homunculi, and generally impatient with anything that sounds like a nomological dangler. They are less interested in the hard problem as such than in reportability, attention, and whether my view can explain the epistemic warrant of phenomenal reports. They will have read a short draft, so they will arrive with prepared objections.

Tell me what I should concede up front, which objection has real force and should not be brushed aside, and the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the session. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need the argumentative moves, not the literature.
