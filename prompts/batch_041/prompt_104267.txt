Write a satirical press kit, in the deadpan register of a consumer-technology launch, for a company that has begun selling a wearable version of the ring of Gyges — a device that renders its wearer unobserved and untraceable. The kit should run to roughly 850–950 words and should consist of four short documents: a press release announcing general availability; a letter from the founder; a customer-facing FAQ; and a leaked internal memo from the company's Ethics & Compliance office. The voice throughout is bland, confident, and corporate; nothing should wink at the reader.

The satire should target a real feature of the social-contract picture of morality and of the modern compliance industry that inherits it: the idea that most people are just only because they cannot get away with being unjust, and that institutions make people good chiefly by removing the ring — through surveillance, audits, and reputation. The humor must come from following that logic straight-faced, not from inventing absurdities. A reader who has never encountered the ring of Gyges should still finish the piece sensing that something is being evaded.

Make the following work inside the documents, without naming any philosopher, school, or text, and without citing sources:

- The founder's letter should treat the ring as a neutral test instrument and report the company's own internal data on what wearers do with it, reading those results as evidence about human nature and as a reason the product is overdue. Let him appeal, without embarrassment, to the oldest question in ethics — whether anyone would stay just if they could do wrong with impunity — and then decline to answer it, on the grounds that the question is empirical and the data are still coming in.
- The FAQ should handle humdrum matters — warranty and resale, whether the ring works on one's spouse, what happens if you use it in a jurisdiction that forbids it — in a way that quietly stages the difference between being just and being thought just. Include at least one product tier or add-on sold to firms that want their employees to feel watched, and one that helps buyers preserve their good reputation while behaving badly.
- The Ethics & Compliance memo should concede, in the flat idiom of risk management, that every reason the company gives its customers for obeying the law is a reason about consequences — detection, reputation, repeat business, a tolerable self-image — and should flag this as a known limitation whose resolution is out of scope for this quarter. It should also register, as a legal exposure, the possibility that a wearer might do the right thing for no reason at all, and note that no one has yet been observed to do so.

No speaker in the kit may give the non-consequentialist answer to the question the piece raises. Every stated reason for acting well must be a reason about consequences; the one thing the company never says is that justice might be worth having for its own sake. Keep all quotations, names, statistics, product features, and internal documents invented. Do not mention real companies, real people, real research, or real publications.

After the kit, append a brief structured summary in the form of a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "target": a one-sentence statement of the position, practice, or discourse being satirized.
- "beats": an array of objects, each with "document" (which of the four documents), "moment" (the specific line, clause, or feature), and "argument_encoded" (the philosophical point that moment carries).
- "reasons_offered_for_acting_well": an array of strings, one per distinct reason for acting well that appears anywhere in the kit, each tagged with whether it is consequence-based.
- "answer_withheld": a sentence naming the non-consequentialist reason the piece deliberately never supplies.
- "residual_question": one sentence stating the question the reader is left holding.
