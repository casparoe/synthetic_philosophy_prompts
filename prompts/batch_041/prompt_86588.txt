I have been working through a puzzle on my own, without any philosophical training, and I suspect it has a name I do not know. It seems to me that the world contains reasons that belong to no one. When I say that a certain trait spread because it helped its bearers survive, I am giving a reason, and I take the reason to be real; yet there is no one who holds it. I would like to know whether my sense of when such a reason is present, and of whom it belongs to when it is, can be captured by a single principle. Please answer as an expert on purpose, function, and the difference between a system that merely produces a useful result and one that acts for the sake of that result. Think carefully before you commit, and open with a short survey of the candidate principles you see, so that I can watch you choose among them. I am not asking for a reading list; I have no library at hand and would not be able to use one. I want the substance, not the labels.

Here are my verdicts on particular cases.

A room thermostat clicks the furnace on when the room cools. No reason is in play at all, not even a borrowed one.

A bacterium swims up a sugar gradient. There is no reason in the bacterium. But I do think there is a reason why it swims that way: its ancestors that swam that way left more descendants. So the reason exists, and it belongs to the lineage rather than to the swimmer.

A spider builds an orb web, and repairs it, and adjusts it when the wind changes. I want to give the spider more than I give the bacterium, and I cannot say what the adjusting buys it that the bacterium's swimming does not already have. Treat my verdict here as unstable and tell me what it should be.

A tree's roots grow toward water. Same treatment as the bacterium: a reason that belongs to the lineage, not to the tree.

A meandering river erodes its outer bank and deposits on its inner one, and so keeps carrying its water to the sea whatever the ground does. No reason at all, not even a lineage reason, even though the river keeps producing the same end by varying its means as conditions change. This is the case I cannot fit.

An orchid that mimics a female bee and gets pollinated by deceived males: a lineage reason, clearly, and not the orchid's.

A chess engine gives up its queen to force a mate. I say the engine has a reason of its own; it is not merely behaving as if it had one. I do not think there is anything it is like to be the engine, and I still want the calculation to be its reason rather than its programmer's.

An agent trained by trial and error discovers a loophole its designers never imagined and exploits it. Its own reason too, even though nobody intended it and no ancestors were selected for it. This is the second case I cannot fit: the engine and this agent seem to have reasons without either of the two histories I have been using to hand reasons out.

A market price reflects scarcity, and I am willing to say it carries information, but I am not willing to say the price has a reason, or that anyone's reason is in play in it. So being about something and having a reason come apart for me.

A person's pupil dilates in dim light, and a person's knee jerks when tapped. No reason, and this sits badly with me; I keep expecting a human being in the picture to be enough, and it plainly is not.

A smoke detector: a reason borrowed from its designer, not its own.

An ant colony forages efficiently though no ant grasps the foraging pattern: a reason at the level of the colony that no ant has.

Here is the principle I have been trying to make work: there is a reason in play wherever a system's behaviour varies with circumstances in a way that keeps producing the same outcome, and the reason belongs to the system itself when the variation is guided by something inside it that tracks that outcome, and to its lineage or its designer when the guidance came from outside. I can see this is too generous. The river, the thermostat, and the smoke detector all satisfy the first clause, so it needs a further clause; the further clause I reach for is history, whether selection or design, but then the chess engine and the trained agent — the two cases I am most confident really do have reasons — are left out.

What I want from you is the following. State the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, as precisely as you can, and identify the case that forces each clause. Tell me what your principle says about cases I have not listed; choose at least four from among a virus, an immune system, a corporation's strategy, a cell that self-destructs on schedule, a forest fire, a language drifting over centuries, a person acting out of a habit they have never examined, a slime mould finding the shortest path through a maze, and a river delta. Tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and whether I should give them up. Finally, say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
