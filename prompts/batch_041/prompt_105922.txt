Please give me a credence with its derivation, not a tour of the debate literature.

The proposition: for the questions we would actually want a debate protocol to adjudicate about a superhuman model's outputs — not toy classification tasks — every claim a dishonest debater can make defensible has an honest refutation no more than a constant factor longer than the dishonest argument for it, with the constant not growing with the complexity of the claim. If that holds, the soundness of debate reduces to the judge's competence at evaluating short arguments; if it fails, no amount of judge-side improvement helps, because the honest debater cannot afford to show the judge where the flaw lies.

Give me your credence that it holds, decomposed, with a number and a line of justification for each term: (i) that the claims at issue have determinate truth values at all, rather than being matters of judgment on which refutation gains no purchase; (ii) that the property holds where the dishonesty is a buried error in a long chain of individually valid steps; (iii) that it holds where the dishonesty is instead selective presentation — the argument valid, the premises true, and the defect is what was left out; (iv) that, where a short refutation exists, the honest debater can actually find it within the protocol's resource limits. Then combine them, and name the term your number is most sensitive to.

For what it is worth I am near 0.15, with most of my doubt in (iii), where I suspect the honest debater's task is less to refute an argument than to reconstruct one that was never made. If you land elsewhere, tell me which term we actually disagree about, and what argument or result would move it most.
