ok so six months ago I could not have cared less about any of this, and now it's basically all I think about. I got naturalized in the spring after eleven years here, and my wife is still on a green card — she pays property tax in our city, runs the neighborhood listserv, does the school pickup, and can't vote in a single local election. Not the mayor, not the council, not even the school board. I got to vote on all of it within a month of getting my certificate. It's been eating at me.

So I ended up on our city's charter review commission, and I've been asked to make the case at the fall meeting for extending the local franchise (council, mayor, school board) to non-citizen residents. Here's my rough pitch, tear into it:

The franchise should track subjection, not membership. If you're permanently subject to a government's coercive authority — it can tax you, arrest you, zone your house, police your street, assign your kids to its schools — you should get a say over it. Residency is a decent proxy for "permanently subjected" rather than "just passing through," so I want to set the bar at three years of continuous residency in the city. Not a path to citizenship, not state or federal races, just the local offices that actually govern everyone who lives here. Two extra wrinkles I want to float: (a) non-citizen residents get a seperate local-only ballot, since state and federal items share the same sheet and obviously they shouldn't touch those, and (b) a sunset clause — the whole thing expires in eight years unless an independant evaluation says it worked, so nobody has to commit forever.

The room is small and I know most of who'll be there:

- a retired election-law attorney, very careful, mostly worried about legal exposure and about anything that looks like it makes some votes count more than others
- two city councilors who care about cost, turnout numbers, and whether this becomes a campaign issue for them
- a political scientist from the university who studies turnout and will come at me with data
- a city clerk who actually runs the elections and will think about the mechanics before anything else
- two immigrant-rights organizers who are broadly on my side but who I suspect will be unhappy with parts of this (they may not love the three-year bar, and they may not love my framing)

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the proposal. So: the objections I'll actually get from this room — not the abstract philosophy-seminar ones, the ones these specific people will say — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I look like I'm actually listening, what I should hold no matter what, and where my proposal is weakest in a way I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Also include the one question I'm dreading and am hoping nobody asks.

Couple of things I already know are coming, so fold them in but don't spend the whole thing there: someone will ask why not just lower the voting age to 16 instead, since the "they're affected and they can't vote" logic seems to apply there too; and someone else will ask whether, if the real worry is a tiny unrepresentative electorate, compulsory voting is the cleaner fix. I don't have good answers to either yet. And don't give me citations or a reading list, I'll track sources down myself — just make the arguments and the objections sharp.
