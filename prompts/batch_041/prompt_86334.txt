I retired some years ago after a career in the natural sciences, and I have spent the time since reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than judgement. This term I have been asked to mark the undergraduate paper on vagueness for the metaphysics course I sit in on. In my old line of work I could always check a claim against an instrument; here I can follow an argument while it is being made and still be unable to say, afterwards, whether the candidate has understood the distinction she was asked to draw. I should be grateful for your help with one script.

The paper set this question:

Explain the disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness, distinguishing throughout between (i) the semantics of vague predicates, (ii) the logic of vague discourse, and (iii) the epistemology of borderline cases. Which of the two theories, if either, better explains why the sorites paradox is troubling? Justify your answer.

The rubric for the question awards credit for keeping those three levels apart, for precision in the use of 'true', 'determinate' and 'valid', and for engaging with at least one standard objection to whichever theory the candidate favours. The paper is marked in the usual bands — First, Upper Second, Lower Second, Third, Fail — and reported as a mark out of twenty.

The candidate's answer is reproduced in full below.

Vagueness is the phenomenon whereby a predicate such as 'heap' or 'bald' admits of borderline cases, and the sorites paradox is its sharpest expression: a sequence of steps, each of which seems beyond reproach, carries us from an obvious truth ('a single grain is not a heap') to an obvious falsehood ('a pile of ten thousand grains is not a heap'). A theory of vagueness must say where the sequence goes wrong. The two leading candidates are epistemicism and supervaluationism.

The epistemicist holds that vague predicates do have sharp boundaries. There is a determinate number of grains at which a non-heap becomes a heap; classical logic requires that every sentence be either true or false, and the epistemicist keeps that requirement for sentences about heaps just as for any others. What she denies is that we know where the boundary lies. Vagueness, on this account, is a matter of our ignorance rather than a feature of the world. That ignorance need not be thought of as deep: it is the ordinary situation of a community whose classificatory practices are not fine-grained enough to have settled the question, and a more patient study of how competent speakers actually apply the word could in principle tell us where the line falls.

Supervaluationism, introduced by Fine, denies that 'heap' has any sharp boundary, on the ground that our use of the word never fixes one. Its central device is the admissible precisification: a way of drawing a line that is consistent with our actual usage. A sentence is supertrue when it is true on every admissible precisification, superfalse when it is false on every one, and neither true nor false when the precisifications divide. Borderline sentences therefore fall into the third class, but classical logic survives at the level of super-truth. 'Either five thousand grains is a heap or it is not' is true on every precisification, hence supertrue, and the familiar inferences remain valid. This gives supervaluationism a real advantage over three-valued logics such as Kleene's, which cannot respect penumbral connections: for some n the conditional 'if n grains is a heap then n+1 grains is a heap' comes out neither true nor false on a Kleene reading, whereas the supervaluationist secures it as supertrue, since no precisification makes the antecedent true and the consequent false.

Supervaluationism also escapes the difficulty of higher-order vagueness. That difficulty is that a sharp-boundary account seems to need a further boundary between the cases that are definitely heaps and the cases that are merely heaps, and then a boundary beyond that, and so on. Since the supervaluationist never claims that there is a boundary in the first place, the regress never gets started, and the theory is under no pressure to say where the line between the definitely definite and the merely definite falls.

Which theory explains why the sorites is troubling? Here I take supervaluationism to be clearly the stronger. The epistemicist tells us that exactly one of the steps is false, but she cannot tell us which, and she offers no account of why every step looks so compelling; if our position were merely one of ignorance, the paradox ought to feel like an unanswered question rather than a trap. The supervaluationist, by contrast, explains the pull of the argument: our language leaves the matter open, so there is no fact of the matter to be discovered, and we are not being unreasonable in declining to accept the conclusion.
