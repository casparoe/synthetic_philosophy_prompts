I serve on the science advisory board of a foundation that funds long-horizon social forecasting, and I have to circulate a written opinion before next week's renewal vote. The grantee models the interaction of population, food supply, and climate. For roughly twenty years it has predicted a sustained food-price shock followed by a fall in per-capita grain yields; the shock has not arrived, though several of its component mechanisms — soil salinization, aquifer depletion, the coupling of fuel and fertilizer prices — have behaved much as the model says. The team argues the theory is right but early, and asks us to renew for another cycle on a revised timeline. Its renewal proposal would reclassify the missed timing as a failure of auxiliary assumptions about trade and energy rather than of the model's core, and would commit in advance to a list of observations that, if absent by a stated date, would count against the core itself. Part of the board reads this as a legitimate refinement; another part calls it an unfalsifiable rescue and wants to terminate.

Draft the opinion: a definite recommendation — renew, terminate, or renew with conditions — the reasoning behind it, and an honest statement of the strongest case for the position you are rejecting. Say plainly what would have to be true for a claim of right-but-early to be more than a rescue, and propose one concrete condition, with a deadline, that the board could adopt whichever way it votes.
