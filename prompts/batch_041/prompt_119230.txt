Bit of an odd one, and I'm aware I may be making an idiot of myself.

I took early retirement from a physics department a few years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since — badly, mostly on trains. I've been going through the little philosophical peices Schrödinger published towards the end of his life, the ones where he bangs on about consciousness and the Upanishads. I'd allways assumed that side of him was a Sunday hobby, and that the serious business — consciousness and quantum mechanics — was the measurement problem. But he really seems to mean it: consciousness is one, the multiplicity of minds is an illusion, and he writes as though something follows from something.

So here's what's bugging me. If one of the people who built quantum mechanics, thinking as a physicist, ended up holding that consciousness is numerically singular, doesn't that count as real evidence for the Advaita claim (or is it Vedanta? I never get these straight)? Not proof, obviously — but the sort of thing you'd have to take seriously in a way you wouldn't take a mystic seriously. And I assume it hooks up with the measurement problem — the observer, Wigner's friend, that whole circus. Am I wrong to think the unity of consciousness has quietly become a scientific result rather than a religious one? And if I am wrong, what was his argument, exactly? He keeps saying something about how you can't count minds the way you count chairs, because counting needs things you can point at in space, and I can't tell whether that's an argument or a conjuring trick.

The other thing that confuses me: my own experience is plainly unified — I see the kettle and hear it boiling as one scene — and I can't work out whether that's the same claim as his or a much humbler one.
