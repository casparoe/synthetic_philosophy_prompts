I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, and next week I'm leading a seventy-five-minute session in a cross-listed seminar on concepts and creativity. The students are a mix of cognitive science and design graduate students; none of them has studied type design or the Letter Spirit project, and most of them think of categorization as either prototype-matching or feature-checking, because that is roughly what their intro course taught them. The session is on the question the Letter Spirit project was built around: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? I don't want you to explain the project to me or summarize its results. I want you to plan how I should teach the idea.

Give me the order in which the components have to come, and why each depends on the one before it, beginning from where the students actually are (prototypes and features) and ending somewhere they can state for themselves why a letter's identity and its style cannot be cleanly separated. Tell me the one carrying example that should hold the whole session together. I have been assuming it should be the lowercase 'a' itself, in particular the contrast between the double-story 'a' of most text faces and the single-story 'a' found in geometric sans-serif faces and in most children's handwriting. But tell me whether that is the right carrier, and if so, exactly what work it has to do at each stage, and what it must not be allowed to do. My worry is that it tempts them into thinking the letter is just a shape with optional decorations. If a different example would carry more weight, say which and why.

Then the misconception map. I expect the first to be that a category is a checklist of necessary and sufficient features, so that the 'a' is a bowl plus a stem plus a tail; the second, that the essence of a letter is a single canonical shape or prototype that all 'a's approximate; the third, that style is a separable overlay you add once the letter's identity is fixed; and the fourth, more subtle, that because letters are conventions, there is nothing there to explain at all. For each, tell me whether I have it right, when in the sequence the students will form it, and the precise move that heads it off. Tell me if there is a fifth I am not seeing. I would also like to know what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and announce that I am refusing, so that I am not dragged into, say, the whole perceptual-versus-conceptual debate or the details of any actual implementation.

Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end, something whose answer would show me whether they have actually grasped the distinction between what is essential to the letter and what belongs to its style, as opposed to having learned to repeat a slogan about it. Tell me what a good answer and a revealingly bad answer would each sound like, so that I know what I am listening for.
