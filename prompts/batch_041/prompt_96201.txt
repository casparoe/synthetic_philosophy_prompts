I'm inclined to think that the independence axiom — the requirement that preference between two lotteries not depend on what the two share — states a genuine requirement of rationality, and that the Allais preferences (the sure million over the mixed gamble in the first pair, the larger long shot over the smaller one in the second) are a mistake rather than a datum any adequate theory must accommodate. Please make the strongest case against this. Which of the standard defenses of the Allais chooser are live and which are merely verbal — does it matter whether the shared consequence is a fixed sum or a coin flip already settled but revealed only after the choice, and does the appeal to regret or to the resolution of uncertainty do any work that the sure-thing principle doesn't already absorb? Then tell me what I, as a defender of independence, should concede: is there a new lottery pair, not the textbook one, on which the independence-respecting chooser is forced to an answer that strikes us as plainly wrong, and what would that show? I would rather learn where the position is exposed than be reassured. Tell me how confident you are in each claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
