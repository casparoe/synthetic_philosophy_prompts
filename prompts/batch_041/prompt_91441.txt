I am a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I sit on the review board that signs off on production models at a company operating automated warehouses. Our item for Thursday is the training architecture for a new fleet of picking robots. The robots work in teams of eight, one team to an aisle, and each learns by reinforcement learning. The proposal before us assigns the reward to the team rather than the robot, so that an agent is credited with the throughput of its aisle rather than its own deliveries, and adds a periodic cull in which the worst team's policies are deleted and replaced by copies of the best team's, on the reasoning that this will select for coordination. Two members object. One says the free-rider problem will eat it alive: a robot's own contribution to its aisle's score is diluted, so shirking is individually cheap, and because we only ever choose between teams and never within them, the shirkers ride along with the good policies whenever a team is copied. The other says the whole idea rests on an error biology disposed of decades ago, when it was shown that selection between groups is too weak to overcome selection within them, and that we are dressing a dead theory in machine-learning clothes. Our chief scientist, who came to us from a biology department, disagrees: the old objection, she says, turned on facts about inheritance and reproduction that do not bind us here, since we set the reward and we decide when policies are copied or discarded. For what it is worth, in a four-week pilot team-level reward beat robot-level reward on throughput in six aisles out of eight, while in the two worst aisles nearly every robot settled into idling. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting: a recommendation with a clear time frame, any conditions I should attach to it, and an honest account of the strongest argument against it. Write in British English, in continuous prose — no headings, bullets, tables or bold text, the way one would write a letter.
