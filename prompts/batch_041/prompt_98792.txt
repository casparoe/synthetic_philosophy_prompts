I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of the infinite — sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session, no background beyond a first course in logic. I want a single in-class activity that makes the group feel, in their own hands rather than in a lecture, why a supertask's final state is not fixed by the steps that lead up to it: the same problem, as I see it, that runs through the vase-and-balls puzzle and the lamp-toggling puzzle usually called Thomson's lamp. I want them to run into the underdetermination themselves and only then hear it named.

Here is the seed I'd like you to build out, tighten, and improve on, keeping the spirit of it rather than the letter. Split the group into four teams of four. Give each team a shallow tray, which we'll call the vase, and a stack of index cards pre-numbered so that a team can always take ten fresh consecutive numbers. Each round, a team adds ten new numbered cards to its vase and then removes exactly one card of its own choosing. Points are awarded for the final contents of the vase at the end of a fixed number of rounds, and each team must commit in writing, before the rounds begin, to a prediction of what will be left and stake tokens on it. I expect the teams to converge on the same arithmetic — nine cards net per round, so the vase grows without bound — and then to discover that the choice of which card to remove, a choice that looks irrelevant, decides everything: a team that always removes its lowest-numbered card can end up with nothing, while a team that always removes its highest-numbered card keeps infinitely many, even though the two teams perform the identical add-ten-remove-one step every round. From there I want the session to turn to the lamp. Give me the whole thing as a runnable plan.

Structure the response under these headings, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated.

- Overview (about 120 words): the setting, the group size and time, the materials, and one crisp sentence naming what participants should come away seeing.
- Setup (about 150 words): how to prepare the trays, cards, tokens, and the prediction sheets; anything the facilitator must build in advance.
- Run sheet (about 200 words): a minute-by-minute timeline for the full seventy-five minutes, with the phases marked and the transitions explained.
- The activity: rules, rounds, and scoring (about 500 words): the full rules, how a round proceeds, how scoring and the token stakes work without any real money, how many rounds to run and how you keep the group from simply counting forever, and exactly what each team is asked to write down and when.
- The reveal (about 250 words): how to stage the moment the divergence appears, what the facilitator says and does not say, and how to present the two contrasting limits as something the group produced rather than something announced. Include, as a second beat, a variant in which the vase is replaced by a counter that must be incremented once per step, so that the limit value is not an integer at all, and let the group notice what that does to the whole setup.
- Debrief (about 350 words): map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical points — the difference between the finite steps and their limit, the clash between counting how many cards remain and asking whether any given card remains, the way the lamp's state at the limit is likewise undetermined by its toggling history, and the gap between an idealized description and any physical realization of it. Say where each of these actually shows up in what the students did.
- What will go wrong the first time (about 250 words): the predictable failures, the arguments the group will fall into, the temptation for the facilitator to resolve the puzzle and why that temptation should be resisted, and how to handle each failure without giving the game away.
- What the activity cannot reproduce (about 150 words): be frank about which feature of the idea the activity is unable to deliver and that the facilitator has to supply in words.

A few constraints. The activity must run entirely without internet access, so do not build in anything that asks students to look up a name, a date, or a source. Do not have the facilitator quote any text; describe and paraphrase instead. Do not ask the group to consult any reading. And please avoid leaning on contested attributions: describe the puzzles by their structure, and if you use the name Thomson's lamp, treat it as the familiar label for the toggling puzzle rather than as something the session depends on.
