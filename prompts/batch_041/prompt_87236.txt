I'm in a graduate seminar on the socialist calculation debate, and last week a classmate offered an argument I still can't decide whether to admire or dismiss. Her line is that the dispute between Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange on the other was never really about whether non-market allocation can work at scale, because we already have a standing example of it working: the modern corporation. A firm with a hundred thousand employees allocates capital internally, moves resources between divisions by fiat rather than by auction, and prices the flows between its units with transfer prices that its own head office simply sets; nobody calls a firm of that size irrational for failing to run an internal stock exchange, or for refusing to let its divisions bid against one another for the machine tools. If internal planning on that scale is unremarkable, she says, the burden shifts to the critics to show that planning the whole economy is a difference in kind rather than a difference in degree, and she reads Lange's trial-and-error market socialism as essentially the proposal that the state become the one firm, with the Central Planning Board occupying the role of head office and setting accounting prices the way a firm sets transfer prices.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the underlying debate. Start with the correspondence table — what plays the role of the chief executive, the divisional manager, the transfer price, the shareholder, the hard budget constraint, the customer, the bankruptcy — and then tell me which of those correspondences are load-bearing for her conclusion and which are merely decorative. I have a suspicion I'd like tested, namely that a firm's internal prices are on any account shadow prices of prices set outside the firm, and that a firm's very boundaries are drawn by the cost of using markets rather than by any independent proof that hierarchy scales; if that is right, the corporation may be less a demonstration that planning works than a device that lives off the price system it appears to supersede. I'd also like to know whether the ways a firm is actually disciplined — by its product market, by the market for capital, by the live possibility of failure — can be reproduced at the level of an entire economy, and what becomes of the analogy once there is no outside to trade with, no residual claimant, and no competitor against which to fail. Fold in, if they bear on it, two further things: that firms which have genuinely attempted internal markets have often retreated from them, and that the empirical literature on internal capital markets suggests large multi-division firms frequently misallocate capital across their divisions — which, if true, seems like evidence about the firm rather than against it.

Then tell me what the analogy can carry. Does it establish that large-scale allocation by authority is possible, that it can be efficient, or neither — and if the firm is the wrong comparison, what would be a better one? Throughout, say how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
