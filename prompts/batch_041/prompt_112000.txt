I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about whether beauty is objective, and I've ended up defending a position that I think is basically Hume's, though I doubt he'd phrase it the way I do. The position: there really are better and worse judgments of taste — some verdicts are correct and others are just wrong — but the standard that makes them correct isn't some mind-independent property sitting in the object; it's the joint verdict of ideal critics, people with genuine delicacy of taste who've practiced, cleared away prejudice, and compared works across times and cultures. So beauty is objective in the thin sense that it's answerable to a norm, but the norm bottoms out in refined human sentiment rather than in the world. I take this to be the sweet spot between "it's all just opinion" relativism and the Platonist picture where beauty floats around out there independent of any of us. My roommate thinks the whole thing collapses the moment you ask who counts as a true judge, and honestly I haven't had a good answer.

What I want is for you to stress-test it. What would count against this view — not the tired "but people disagree" objection, but the specific things that should actually move me: cases it can't handle, consequences it's committed to that turn out unacceptable, and results from neighboring areas (metaethics, the metaphysics of dispositions, maybe the psychology of aesthetic response) that would undercut it. Which of these would a sympathetic Humean themselves concede as a real cost, and which would they just wave away as confusion? I'm especially curious whether the view makes any predictions its rivals don't — is there anything it says about how ideal critics will actually converge, or about works no critic has ever encountered, that a straight relativist or a straight realist would deny? And there's a nagging issue I keep running into: no actual person is a true judge in the full sense, so is the standard just an idealization that never gets instantiated, and if so does that make the whole thing vacuous? Be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any disagreement just gets explained away as "those people aren't true judges" — and if it has, whether that's fatal or just the cost of doing business for a normative theory. I think I'd find it most persuasive if you could tell me, in principle, what it would take for me to have to give the view up.
