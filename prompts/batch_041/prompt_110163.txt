A colleague in the physics department settles the probability problem for the many-worlds interpretation with a single sentence: 'everything that can happen does happen.' When I ask why I should expect to observe the Born frequencies rather than anything else, he repeats the sentence and adds that outcomes with greater amplitude are simply the ones that occur in more branches, so nothing further needs explaining.

I suspect the sentence is doing several jobs at once, at least one of them illegitimate, and I cannot pull them apart. Set out the precise claims it could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, along three axes: what 'can happen' quantifies over — physical possibility, the branches of the formalism, my own future experience; what 'does happen' asserts — that a branch exists, that I have a successor in it, that the event is probable; and whether the claim is about the dynamics of the wavefunction or about the credences of an agent inside it. For each reading, say whether it is a truism, a substantive commitment of the interpretation, or something the interpretation denies; say which reading he most likely has in mind; and say which reading could actually carry the inference from the slogan to a conclusion about the Born frequencies — in particular, what would have to license the step from 'more branches' to 'more likely', and how the number of branches is supposed to be fixed when it appears to depend on how finely the outcomes are divided.
