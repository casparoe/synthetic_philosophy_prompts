I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and a paper drawn from my dissertation has just been awarded my field's annual graduate essay prize. The prize will be presented at the society's annual dinner next month. The citation has already been circulated; it will be read aloud to the room before I am asked to reply, and it describes my work as having shown that the adaptationist programme has been refuted, and that the traits we casually call adaptations are more often than not byproducts, constraints, or accidents. That is near enough to be flattering and far enough to be false, and I would rather not spend the evening being congratulated for a claim I spent four chapters denying.

What I actually argue concerns what the critique of adaptationism — the one associated with Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper — was a critique of. Read as a claim about nature, it says that there are fewer adaptations than we had assumed. Read as a claim about explanation, it says something sharper: that across a great deal of the literature, calling a trait an adaptation functions not as a hypothesis but as a default, so that the burden of proof falls on anyone who proposes a byproduct instead. I defend the second reading, which does not entail the first. A trait can be useful and tidy and still not be an adaptation, since byproducts and constraints can be useful and tidy too; whether something is an adaptation is a question about its history, not about how well it fits a story.

Please write the speech I should give. It should run under five minutes, accept the prize warmly, thank the right people — including the committee chair, whose textbook I take apart in my second chapter — and correct the record in a register that reads as gratitude rather than rebuke. The room will be split: perhaps half are philosophers and biologists who will hear at once whether I am hedging, and the rest are my parents, a few undergraduates, and the university's development office, for whom none of these words are yet doing any work. Give the non-specialists one ordinary example that carries the point, and not the Venetian spandrels themselves, which have been invoked at this dinner every year I have attended; and let the specialists hear that I have not softened anything in order to get there.
