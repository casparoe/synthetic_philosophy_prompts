You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of social conventions: on when a local custom generates a genuine obligation, and on what outsiders owe to practices they were not raised in. Please think the problem through carefully before committing to anything; you may sketch the competing positions for yourself first, but the answer itself should be clean prose.

Two colleagues of mine, Ines and Tomas, fell into an argument after a departmental dinner. A visiting researcher from a country where tipping is simply not done paid his share of the bill to the penny and left nothing on top. He is here for the term, not just the week. I was at the table and said nothing; they carried it on in the street afterwards, and I have set out the exchange below as faithfully as I can.

Ines: he did nothing wrong. Tipping is a custom, not a requirement, and nobody is obliged to observe a custom he was not raised in. It is unfair to hold a guest to an unspoken rate he had no way of knowing.

Tomas: that is too quick. In this city the tip is not decoration — the wage is set on the assumption that tips make up the difference. A diner who leaves nothing is not merely breaching etiquette; he is taking the meal at a price the local arrangement does not charge, and a particular person ends the evening worse off. The tip is part of the price, just collected separately. And he did know: the card reader offered him a percentage.

Ines: if there is a wrong here it belongs to the employer, not the diner. Making the worker's income depend on each customer's goodwill in turn is precisely what keeps an exploitative arrangement alive. And you cannot demand that a visitor pay to sustain a practice he may reasonably think unjust; that is asking him to be complicit in it.

Tomas: that proves too much. By your reasoning the visitor could ignore any local practice he happened to dislike — the queue at the till, the rule about waiting for the light. And note where the cost of his scruple lands: on the server, who designed nothing and can change nothing. If it is a protest, let it be a legible one. An unannounced shortfall at the table is indistinguishable from meanness, and it is read as meanness by the very person it hurts.

Ines: the queue and the crossing light are not the same case as the tip, and your own examples supply the distinction. Waiting your turn matters because someone is behind you; crossing against the light harms nobody and merely offends the locals; and the server is out of pocket only because her employer chose to make her wage depend on my decision. As for knowing — knowing the amount the machine suggested is not knowing that the amount is owed. And requiring the visitor to announce his politics to a stranger over a card reader merely relocates the burden you say you are worried about.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, mistaken, or right about something but for the wrong reason, and justify the rating briefly. Then say what both of them are missing, and give an overall verdict on whether the visitor was obliged to tip — and whether that verdict would change if he were a tourist passing through rather than a guest for the term. Keep the whole answer to 700 words at most. Tell me how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the one point you are least sure about. Write in British English.
