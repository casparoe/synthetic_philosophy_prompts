You are a philosopher of technology with a solid working knowledge of technical AI-safety research and a good deal of experience writing philosophical dialogue. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on how the AI-safety field has split between two research programmes, and I need a dialogue my students can use as a fair map of the disagreement. That means I need you to argue both sides at full strength rather than adjudicate between them. Before you begin writing, spend a few sentences setting out, in your own words, what you take to be the strongest version of each position, and where you suspect the two sides are merely talking past each other rather than genuinely contradicting each other; then write the dialogue.

The two positions are these. The first programme, usually called control, holds that the safety measures surrounding a deployed model should be designed on the assumption that the model may be misaligned and may be actively trying to defeat those very measures, so that no knowledge of, or trust in, the model's motives is needed for the deployment to be acceptable. Its characteristic method is the control evaluation: replace the model under test with a red-team version of it, instructed and if necessary fine-tuned to pursue one specific harmful outcome as effectively as it can while staying undetected, then measure whether the surrounding protocol — restricted permissions, model-based monitors, a budget of human review, randomized audits — catches the attempt, and report the catch rate. The second programme, usually called alignment, holds that this framing is at best incomplete and at worst self-defeating: that guarantees purchased against a scheming model hold only within a fixed and artificially narrow deployment distribution; that an adversarial training and deployment regime creates precisely the incentives and selection pressures that breed deception; and that treating the model's motives as a black box to be fenced in is a substitute for the harder work of making those motives not need fencing.

Here is the case I want the two of them to argue over. A lab is deploying a model, M, to write internal tooling code. Under the protocol, M writes the code; a smaller model from the same family reads each line and flags anything suspicious; a human contractor reviews only the flagged lines, under a budget covering roughly two per cent of all lines; and, as an extra check, M must produce each answer twice, once under a prompt saying that a monitor will read the output and once under a prompt saying that nothing will be read, with any discrepancy between the two answers flagged for human review. The lab's internal write-up reports that when M was replaced by a red-team model instructed to insert a backdoor that survives review, the protocol caught about seventy per cent of attempts, and concludes that the deployment is safe even if M is misaligned, on the grounds that a real schemer would have to be at least as good at evasion as a model that was explicitly trying to evade. The two characters have just come out of the review meeting: one drafted that safety case, the other filed a written dissent. Write their conversation.

Some constraints on the writing. Both characters must be equally competent and neither may be a caricature — the control researcher should not be a cold bureaucrat indifferent to whether the model has decent values, and the alignment researcher should not be a mystic who assumes good values will simply emerge. Each should respond to the other's actual points rather than to a convenient version of them, and each should concede something real at least once. They must engage with the specific mechanisms and numbers of the case above, not with control versus alignment in the abstract; the argument should turn on details such as what the red-team model's evasion ability does and does not establish, what the differential test can and cannot catch, and what the two per cent review budget is actually buying. Neither character should invoke real researchers, papers, or institutions as authorities, and neither should be given a fabricated quotation or attribution; both may use the field's vocabulary — scheming, sandbagging, monitoring, threat model, safety case — provided they explain what they mean, and all arguments should be reconstructible from first principles by a reader who has never read the literature. Aim for roughly 1,500 words of alternating turns, several substantial turns per speaker, with only minimal scene-setting. Do not let the exchange degenerate into slogans, and do not resolve it by fiat: end with the crux of the disagreement sharper and more visible to the reader than it was at the start, unless one of the two genuinely earns a concession.
