I sat in on a reading group at my local library a few weeks ago — I have no training in philosophy, I just read a great deal — and two of the regulars got into an argument about vagueness that I have not been able to put down since. One of them insisted that predicates like 'heap', 'bald' and 'tall' do have sharp boundaries after all: for any object there is a fact of the matter about whether the word applies, the boundary simply sits somewhere we can never discover, and our ignorance of it must not be confused with there being nothing to know. The other insisted that the boundary is not merely unknown but unsettled: the word is true of the clear cases, false of the clear non-cases, and for the items in between there is no fact of the matter at all until a context or a convention supplies one, so that some of those middling sentences come out neither true nor false. They spent the evening talking past each other, and I came away suspecting that each of them has a case the other cannot easily absorb. I would like you to write the dialogue they should have had: a genuine back-and-forth in which each of them takes up the other's actual point rather than his own, with both parties equally sharp and neither winning by being handed an easy concession.

There are two things I want put into their mouths, because they are exactly what leaves me unsure of both views. The first is my brother-in-law's workplace: he operates a machine that sorts fruit on a continuous ripeness scale, and the exact reading above which a peach drops into the ripe bin is fixed in the firmware as a definite number, though the workers are never told what it is. The second is a supply contract I once had to sign, which simply stipulated that, for the purposes of the agreement, a pile counts as a heap at five hundred grains and not one grain sooner. I would like the two of them to disagree about what these cases actually show. Does the undisclosed number in the machine suggest that all vagueness is just ignorance of a sharp line, or is it a special case with nothing to teach us about ordinary vague words? Does the contractual stipulation show that a boundary can be manufactured where none existed, or only that the parties have swapped a vague word for a precise one and thereby changed the subject? Have them press each other on the point where their own position becomes uncomfortable — my impression is that each of the standard views has somewhere it has to pay a price — and let each say plainly what he thinks the other's price is and why his own is smaller.

Finally, please close by separating for me what people who study this take to be settled from what is genuinely contested among them, and do not let a position that is in fact disputed be presented to me as though it were the consensus.
