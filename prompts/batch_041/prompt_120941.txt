I write about formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability for a general-readership science magazine, and I also run a small graduate reading group on Bayesian updating. I am putting together a teaching packet on the limits of conditionalization, and I want to include an exam question that gets at why updating on a conditional probability is not the same as conditionalizing on an event. The case I want to use is the Judy Benjamin problem, and I want the question to be answerable by a student who has never seen it before, provided they understand the probability calculus. Here is the setup I plan to give them. Judy Benjamin is parachuted into a territory divided between two armies, Red and Blue. If she lands in Blue, she is either in the Headquarters area (H) or the Second Company area (S); Red is undivided. Before any message, she is equally likely to be in Red or Blue, so P(R)=P(B)=1/2, and conditional on being in Blue she is equally likely to be in H or S, so P(H|B)=P(S|B)=1/2. She then receives a radio message: if you are in Blue territory, the probability that you are in the Second Company's area is 3/4. That is, the message tells her that P(S|B)=3/4. I want the exam question to ask how she should update her unconditional credences P(R), P(H), and P(S), and to require the student to work through the minimum-relative-entropy (Infomin) solution, to notice that it raises P(R) above 1/2, and to explain why that result is thought to be puzzling. I also want the student to consider at least one alternative rule -- for instance, a rule that keeps P(R) fixed and adjusts only within Blue -- and to say what that rule gives and whether it is more or less plausible than Infomin. The question should not be answerable by simply reciting the definition of conditionalization; it should force the student to see that conditionalization is undefined here because the evidence is not an event but a constraint on a conditional probability.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The model answer should set out the credence space, identify the constraint, explain why conditionalization does not apply, derive the Infomin posterior (you may give the answer in closed form or to three decimal places), explain the sense in which the result is puzzling, and assess the alternative. The marking scheme should list the must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. Assume the students are graduate students in philosophy or cognitive science who are comfortable with the probability calculus but have not necessarily seen this problem before.
