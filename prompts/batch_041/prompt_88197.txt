Every month or so I host a long supper for friends I've known since university, and the most recent one turned into an argument I was in no position to referee. Two of the guests, Rosa and Elias, are partners who have spent weeks trying to decide whether to move cities for Rosa's job, and the disagreement has drifted from the move itself into whether a claim such as "moving would be better for us overall" even has a truth value. I want to reproduce the exchange as fairly as I can, because they both said things I couldn't answer. (1) Rosa: to say it would be better for us overall is to compare her gain against his loss, but a person's utility is just a way of representing that person's preferences, and any rescaling that leaves those preferences in the same order describes them equally well; so there is no fact of the matter about whether her gain outweighs his loss, and when we say there is, we are dressing a value judgement up as arithmetic. (2) Elias: if that were right we could never say that a change which makes one person much better off and another only slightly worse off is an improvement, yet we can say it and we are right to; when Rosa says she would be miserable for years if they stayed and he would be mildly disappointed if they moved, that is a fact about their two psychologies, and intensities are comparable. (3) Rosa: that is two separate facts — how miserable she would be, how disappointed he would be — with a comparison slipped in that is contained in neither; and the "intensities" on offer are things like how much each of them says they would mind, what each would pay, how long each would hold out, none of which guarantees that her "mildly disappointed" sits at the same height as his. She is happy to grant the coarse claim that we can often say who is worse off; what she denies is that we can say by how much.

(4) Elias: the coarse claim is itself a comparison, since to say who is worse off you must place two people on a single line and see which is lower — so the principle is conceded and only the resolution is in dispute; and there are anchors, because everyone's scale runs from a life not worth living up to the best life they can imagine, and once the ends are pinned the middles line up. He adds that he can compare without ever leaving the first person: he can ask himself whether he would rather be Rosa in the staying scenario or Rosa in the moving scenario, and he can answer — a comparison of two states of one person, which her objection never touches. (5) Rosa: imagining being Rosa is still him imagining, with his own scale, and the manoeuvre works only if their scales already agree, which is the very thing in dispute; the anchors do not help either, since "worst" and "best" are vague and differ from person to person, and choosing where to pin them is exactly the arbitrary step she objects to. Moreover, when the two of them actually decide something they do not measure, they negotiate or fall back on a norm — "we take turns", "whoever would suffer more gets priority" — and a norm is not a measurement; deciding by how much each would pay to get their way would merely weight the outcome by who has more money. (6) Elias: then when the norms run out, her "better for us" is just whoever has the stronger preference in the room, and her own proposal presupposes the comparison she denies, since taking turns asserts that each person's turn is worth the same; she cannot avoid comparing, only do it silently. The case they kept circling back to was a shared pot of money and whether to spend it easing one friend's chronic pain or spreading small comforts across everyone, but they never got past whether "the relief is worth more than the sum of the small gains" is a claim with a truth value at all.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than telling me who won. Give me a markdown table with one row for each numbered move and exactly these columns: Move (the number, 1 to 6); Speaker (Rosa or Elias); Claim (that move restated in a single neutral sentence, in vocabulary that neither party would object to); Rating (exactly one of: Clearly mistaken, Mostly mistaken, Debatable, Mostly right, Clearly right); and Why (two or three sentences giving the strongest reason for the rating, saying what the move gets right and where, if anywhere, it overreaches). After the table, add a bulleted section headed "What both are missing" with three to five bullets, then a single closing paragraph headed "Overall" saying who is more nearly right and about what, and whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — without pretending that the disagreement dissolves. Write the whole answer in American English, and prefer precision to length.
