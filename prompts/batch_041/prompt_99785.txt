I am assembling materials on an old puzzle: why should knowledge be prized above a true belief that gets you to the same destination? The traveller who has merely been told which road to take, if the information is right, arrives exactly where the traveller who knows the way arrives; so what is the extra worth?

I keep meeting the same split inside real institutions. Take a professional certifying body — for arborists, surveyors, or the like. To be licensed, a candidate must be able to explain why the method works and defend it under questioning; a candidate with a flawless record of correct calls who can only shrug is refused. Yet the same body's disciplinary arm, when a licensee's work causes harm, asks only whether the outcome was bad, not whether the reasoning was sound. And its annual performance bonuses go to whoever had the most accurate calls, under a scoring rule that rewards accuracy and nothing else.

What does this institutional posture presuppose about why knowledge is better than true belief? Does it commit to a substantive answer — that knowledge is more reliably actionable, more stable under counterevidence, more credit-worthy, or intrinsically valuable — or do the licensing arm and the bonus arm presuppose different and possibly incompatible things? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of the value of knowledge would license the very same policy. Then say what would concretely have to change — in who is licensed, who is disciplined, and who is paid — if the presupposed answer were swapped for one of the rivals.

- "ruled_out": an array of objects, each with "view" (a rival position the practice excludes) and "why_excluded" (one or two sentences);
- "merely_compatible": an array of objects, each with "view" (a position the practice also fits) and "why_also_licenses_it" (one or two sentences);
