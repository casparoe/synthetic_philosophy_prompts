I am trying to work through Avicenna's flying man argument, and I keep getting stuck on one formulation of it. Here is the passage, in a standard English rendering:

> One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence.

What I want is not an introductory summary of the thought experiment but a close reading of this particular wording, and help with four points I cannot get straight.

First, the closing clause. Even if the man could imagine a hand, he "would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence." What is meant here by a condition of his existence, and why does the argument require that the imagined hand fail to be one? Is this clause doing real work, or is it a flourish? I suspect that everything turns on the difference between something being a part of the self and something being a precondition of the self's existing, but I cannot make the distinction precise or see which one Avicenna is denying.

Second, the list of things the man does not affirm runs "his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing." The brain is named alongside the bowels. Why include it, and what does its inclusion commit Avicenna to about what thinking is? If the man is unaware of his brain and yet aware of his self, has Avicenna already ruled out any account on which the self just is a process of the brain, or has he only ruled out the man's awareness of that process?

Third, the passage appears to deliver only an epistemic asymmetry — certainty about the self, the absence of any such certainty about the body — while the conclusion Avicenna wants is metaphysical, namely that the self is a substance that can exist apart from the body. Show me the step that carries the argument from the one to the other, and tell me whether it is a step Avicenna is entitled to or a gap he papers over. I would like the two readings distinguished clearly: one on which the thought experiment establishes the separability of the self, and one on which it establishes only that the self can be conceived without the body, and I want to know which the wording actually supports.

Fourth, and this is where I think the real difficulty lies, consider a variant. The man is said to affirm the existence of "this self of his" — his own self, not merely that some self or other exists. Now alter the case so that nothing in his awareness marks the awareness as his: he cannot tell whether the self he is aware of has existed for years or came into being a moment ago, nor whether it is the same self that will be aware a moment hence; the awareness is present, but it carries no first-personal tag. Can he still affirm this self, or does the affirmation collapse into the bare claim that selfhood exists somewhere? If the latter, does the argument survive only by smuggling in a first-personal character that the stipulated sensory deprivation should have stripped away? I want to know whether the first-personal, de se character of the affirmation is load-bearing for the conclusion or an accident of the phrasing.

I am also troubled by an objection from a different direction. A computationalist will say the man's ignorance of his brain proves nothing, since one is routinely ignorant of the vehicle of one's thoughts; on that view, the flying man's certainty no more shows the self to be immaterial than my ignorance of my own neurons shows my thoughts to be non-neural. How should Avicenna answer this, and is there a reply available to him that does not simply presuppose that thought cannot be a bodily process?

Reconstruct the argument step by step, tell me which steps are load-bearing and which are decoration, and identify the joints at which it is weakest. Where the wording is genuinely ambiguous, say so rather than resolving it by charity.
