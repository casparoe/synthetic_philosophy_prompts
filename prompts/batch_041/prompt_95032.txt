I hold a view about philosophical thought experiments and want to know what would count against it. Distinguish a case’s load-bearing features — the structural elements the argument actually needs to generate its conclusion — from its scaffolding: the vivid, emotionally salient, or narratively contingent details that could be varied without altering the argument’s logical skeleton. My position is that a thought experiment provides evidence for a philosophical claim only if the target intuition survives the removal of its scaffolding; when the intuition evaporates once the scene is stripped to its load-bearing features, the case is an intuition pump in the pejorative sense and should carry no probative weight. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including cases where scaffolding seems epistemically indispensable, arguments that the load-bearing/scaffolding distinction cannot be drawn independently of the intuition it is meant to test, and findings from experimental philosophy or cognitive science about the reliability of stripped-down intuitions. Which of these would a defender of my view have to concede as genuine costs, and which could they dismiss? Is the position at risk of insulating itself — if every recalcitrant intuition can be redescribed as scaffolding, is that fatal or an acceptable feature of a normative criterion? Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
