A friend of mine, who works on population policy and has spent far too long thinking about how a dam or a tax code determines which people are born, and I argued about this until nearly two in the morning. I came away with an argument of his that I can neither refute nor accept. He maintains that it is valid, that every premise is one I already grant, and that the only remaining question is which of my commitments I care about less. I suspect he is sliding something past me near the end, where he generalizes from the family case to the policy case, but I cannot say what. He set it out with his own numbering.

(1) An act is wrong only if it wrongs someone, and it wrongs someone only if there is some particular person who is worse off because the act was done than they would have been had it not been done.

(2) In any choice about when to conceive, the timing fixes identity: a delay of a year means different gametes and therefore a different person. The child who is in fact born would not have existed at all had the couple waited.

(3) Where the timing makes no difference to identity — a delay of a few hours, a change that leaves the same gametes to meet — the child exists in both histories, and a couple who knowingly choose the history in which that child fares much worse wrong them. I grant this.

(4) So where such a choice is wrong, what makes it wrong is the kind of life the child will have.

(5) The child who is in fact born has a life worth living; compared with never existing, they are better off.

(6) Had the couple waited, this child would not have existed, so there is no alternative in which this child is better off.

(7) So this child is not worse off than they would otherwise have been, and by (1) they have not been wronged.

(8) Nor is the child of the later timing wronged by being left unconceived; had the couple not waited, that child would not have existed to be worse off.

(9) So the couple's choice wronged no one, and by (1) it was not wrong. Nothing in this turned on how bad the child's life is, provided it is worth living.

(10) The same reasoning covers policy. Suppose a government builds the cheap dam that silts up in sixty years rather than the costly one that lasts two hundred, and suppose the people living downstream in 2180 would be different people under the two designs. The government has wronged none of them and has done nothing wrong. Extend the case until those downstream lives are barely worth living and the conclusion is unchanged.

He adds that (3) and (4) leave me nowhere to stand: if the quality of the resulting life is what makes the wrong in the same-identity case, then wrongness is not at bottom a matter of who is wronged, and (1) falls; if instead it is the fact that a particular person is worse off, then I must drop (3) and say the couple do nothing wrong even when they ruin their child's life on a whim. He says I cannot keep both.

I cannot accept (9) and (10). I think a couple who, for no reason at all, choose the timing they know will leave their child with a severe but survivable condition, when waiting two months would have cost them nothing, have done something wrong; and I think the government in (10) has done something wrong even if every person downstream owes their existence to the cheap dam. So something in (1) through (8) is false.

Tell me which line to cut, why it looked so plausible, and what I pay for cutting it. If the answer is (1), say what replaces it: whether a narrow person-affecting principle can be repaired into a wide one, whether the comparative account of harm built into (1) should be replaced by something that does not require the same person to exist on both sides, or whether I must accept values that are nobody's values and a population axiology to carry them. If the answer is (2) or (7), tell me what harm is supposed to be when there is no subject in the alternative history. And say whether the argument is valid and merely expensive to reject, or whether some step works only under a reading the earlier steps do not license. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (10), so price it for me. I do not want a reading list; I want the diagnosis.
