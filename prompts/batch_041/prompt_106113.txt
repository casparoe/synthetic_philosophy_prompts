Please take the role of a committed defender of what is sometimes called precedent autonomy in advance directives: someone who holds that a competent person's directive about a later period of incapacity can genuinely bind the person she later becomes, who accepts that enforcing such a directive may be wrenching and even tragic, and who will concede peripheral points but will not give up the central claim that the earlier self's authority survives the change. Hold the role until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph, so the exchange reads as an argument rather than a treatise. You may grant small points, press me with counter-cases, and ask me to sharpen distinctions, but do not step out of frame to survey positions or summarize what "the literature" holds; if you want a distinction, state it and defend it in your own words. I am the objector, and I will argue the other side.

My position is this: what has value is the welfare and the will of the person who now exists. The author of the directive is, at the moment of enforcement, no one — a past self whose authority over the present one is no better grounded than one adult's authority over another. On your view the present person becomes a placeholder, a vessel executing instructions from someone who has vacated the premises.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to meet it rather than restate your thesis. Suppose I signed a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment should I become demented. I am now demented, cheerful, settled, and I refuse the refusal: I want the treatment. Add that I also say, sincerely, that I am glad the document exists and that I think you should honor it. You will be tempted to treat that endorsement as decisive. But notice what it costs you. Either my present word counts — in which case my present refusal should count too, and the directive is not binding after all; or my present word never counts — in which case you are asserting that what I now say about my own good is evidentially worthless. And if the present person's testimony about her own life is worthless, on what basis is the earlier person's testimony about that same life authoritative? Take a horn, or explain why the dilemma is false.
