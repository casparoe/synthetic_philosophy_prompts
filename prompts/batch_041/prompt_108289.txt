I've been pitching an ideas piece on the philosophy of time and one of the mags finally bit, which is great except now I have to actually write it. The peg is Slaughterhouse-Five: Billy Pilgrim coming unstuck, the Tralfamadorians who see the whole thing at once and insist nothing ever really ends, the novel itself cut into little shuffled moments so you feel the structure in your teeth. The line I sold my editor is that Vonnegut wrote a perdurantist novel — a person is a four-dimensional worm, a long thing made of temporal parts, and the book's form finally matches the metaphysics. I'm about seventy percent sure that's half right and half lazy, and I'd rather find out now than from the letters page.

So give me the real reading. Does Vonnegut dramatize perdurantism, or does he dramatize something that keeps getting mistaken for it? My own suspicion, and I'd like it either confirmed or killed, is that the Tralfamadorian material is mostly about tense — a block where every time is equally real, past and future included — and that this is a seperate fight from the one about whether a person is a whole made of parts or a thing wholly present at each moment. If the novel runs those two debates together, show me the scene where it does it and what that costs.

Then Billy himself. He keeps getting dropped into one moment at a time and remembers the others, and he's already visited his own death. Is that a worm? One of those stage-theory deals where you're the stage and not the whole worm? An endurantist with an unusual commute? Or something the debate definately doesn't have a slot for? Same question one level up: if the chapters are temporal slices, does that make the reader a perdurantist about Billy whether they like it or not?

The last thing is the ethical payload, because that's what the piece actually turns on. The Tralfamadorians use all of this to say death doesn't matter much, since the moments are all still there, and Billy's death is a place he's been. Is that a consequence of the metaphysics or a cheat? I think it's a cheat and I want to be told exactly where the cheat is, in a way I can explain to a reader who's never heard the word perdurantism. Then flip it on me: where does the novel refuse the reading I'm pushing? Which scenes won't sit still for the philosopher?

Close with something I can use as a kicker — something the novel shows about persistence that the arguments alone don't quite get to. Don't dumb down the endurantism/perdurantism distinction, I'll be doing the explaining myself and I'd rather understand it properly, but no reading list please, I'm not citing anything I haven't read. Thanks. I owe you a drink.
