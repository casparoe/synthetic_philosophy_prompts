I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a long-ish monthly, mostly the messy places where philosophy and history bump into current fights — and I've just been given the go-ahead to apply for a nine-month fellowship at a university humanities center, the kind where you get an office, a library card and a stipend and are expected to produce something halfway between reporting and scholarship. My editor has already said she'd run the long piece that comes out of it, so the application has to sell a real research project, not just a series of articles. I've been circling a question for about two years now and I need you to turn my scribbles into an actual proposal. Here's the mess: I keep covering the fights over how we judge dead people — statues coming down, buildings renamed, museum labels rewritten, school curricula litigated — and both sides are working with a principle they've never spelled out. The historians I call say you can't judge the past by the standards of the present, that past societies are basically foreign countries and you don't get to arrest people for breaking laws that hadn't been written yet. But those same historians will happily condemn the Nazis, or the Atlantic slave trade, so never judge obviously isn't the rule they're actually using; they've just never said what the real rule is. And the other side — the people who want the statues down — say morality is morality and it doesn't expire. But that can't be the whole story either, because in ordinary life we let people off for things they genuinely couldn't have known, and if that excuse works for a person it seems like it should work for a whole society. So I think the real question is about notice and ignorance, and I want to steal the structure from criminal law: we don't punish people for conduct that wasn't criminal when they did it, and the usual reason given is fair notice. Does morality have anything like that? Is there a moral analogue of the rule against retroactivity, or is morality different in kind — no statute of limitations, no promulgation requirement, and nobody with the authority to promulgate it? I've been calling the general thing the fair notice problem and I want the proposal built around it.

Two half-worked-out things I'd like you to build in, because they're what keep this from being the same old presentism essay. First, I think presentism is two things glued together and the glue is why the arguing goes nowhere: there's a benign kind, where you pick your subject matter because it matters to you now (nobody thinks historians should only study whatever happened to be most important at the time), and a contested kind, where you pass moral judgment on the dead. Call them curatorial presentism and magisterial presentism. Everything worth fighting about is in the second, and a lot of the heat comes from people defending the first while being accused of the second. Second, and this is the bit I'm actually excited about: the ignorance excuse looks like it collapses for the cases people care about, because in those cases the ignorance was manufactured. Slaveholders in the antebellum South weren't simply ignorant that slavery was wrong; the institution was producing and maintaining that ignorance, punishing the people who said otherwise, and the people benefiting from the practice were the ones doing the maintaining. Same story for a lot of colonial administration. So the question becomes: when does a society's non-culpable ignorance turn culpable, and can you tell the difference from the inside, or only with hindsight? My rough test — tell me if it survives contact — is to ask whether there were available dissenters: people at the time, in the relevant place, who saw the practice as wrong, and whose seeing it that way wasn't just a matter of luck or genius. If there were, notice existed and the excuse fails. If there weren't — if literally nobody in a position to know thought otherwise — then maybe the excuse holds, and the interesting question is whether that ever really happens. Notice I'm not sure the test doesn't just smuggle hindsight back in: everyone who lost an argument looks like a marginal crank until they win. That worry is part of why the thing might be worth nine months.

You're a philosopher of history who also writes for a general reader — someone who could supervise a dissertation and still file 3,000 words on deadline — so: write me the proposal.
