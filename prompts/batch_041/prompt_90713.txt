I'm currently taking a seminar on probability and evidence, and we've just finished a unit on the reference class problem. I came into it assuming the problem was a technical embarrassment peculiar to frequentism: to compute a relative frequency you have to choose a class of trials, and nothing in the world tells you which class is the right one, so the frequentist has to appeal to something outside his interpretation. A classmate argued the reverse — that the problem is interpretation-neutral, that it reappears for propensities, for credences, and for Humean best-system accounts of chance, and that the fact that it wears a different costume in each case is itself evidence that none of them has really solved it rather than dissolved it. A second thing bothers me independently. The most natural repair, taking the narrowest admissible class — conditioning on everything relevant to the outcome — seems to drive every singular probability toward 0 or 1 once you are allowed to include the complete causal history of the token event in question (whether this particular storm system makes landfall, whether this particular coin toss comes up heads), which makes the "correct" reference class a strange object to want in the first place.

So the claim I would like you to price is this: for any well-formed singular probability claim about a token event, there is a fact of the matter about which reference class is correct — not that we could always discover it, but that the question has a determinate answer rather than being settled by convention, convenience, or the purposes of the inquirer. Please do not just hand me a number. Decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on, give each a rough probability with a line of justification, and show me how they combine: whether the problem really is interpretation-neutral or is parochial to some interpretations and avoidable by switching; whether the maximal-specificity fix yields non-trivial probabilities or collapses to 0 and 1 and so fails on its own terms; whether a best-system or chance-theoretic account genuinely fixes the class or merely relocates the choice into the theory's simplicity-strength tradeoff; whether some causal criterion — the class defined by the full set of causes of the outcome — can do the work without circularity; and whether the "fact of the matter" framing already presupposes a realist reading of probability that is itself up for grabs. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most. For calibration: my own credence is around 0.35, while a friend of mine who works on causal inference puts it at 0.8 — if you had to locate our disagreement in a single sub-question, which one would it be, and what would we have to settle first in order to find out? One last thing, and I recognize it may be unfair to ask: your credence is itself a singular claim about a token event, namely your answering this question, so tell me which reference class you took yourself to be in when you assigned it.
