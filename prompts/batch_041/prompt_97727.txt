Alright, so this has been nagging at me since I fell down the situationism rabbit hole again. Everyone trots out Milgram's obedience experiments as the great debunking of character: ordinary people will keep shocking a stranger because some guy in a lab coat says please continue, and 65% of them go all the way to 450 volts. Fine, sure. But the dispositions literature in metaphysics has a way sharper toolkit for this than the word "situationism," and I want you to actually run Milgram through it instead of giving me the usual essay.

The toolkit, since it's a bit in the weeds: the old conditional analysis said "x is disposed to M when S" just unpacks as "if S, then x would M." Then people noticed the conditional goes false in two very different ways. A fink is something that, if the stimulus S were about to show up, would stop it, or would strip the disposition itself before it could fire — the wire is live, but there's a gadget wired in that cuts the current the instant anything conductive touches it, so it never shocks you even though it's still live. A mask is different: the disposition stays fully intact, the stimulus is present, and something else just blocks the manifestation — the glass is still fragile, you've only wrapped it in foam, so striking it does nothing. The whole point of the distinction is that you can lose the counterfactual without losing the disposition.

So which one is Milgram's setup? Is the authority pressure a mask sitting on top of a real, intact disposition to refuse to hurt someone — the disposition is there, the situation just won't let it show — or is it a fink that would actually dismantle the disposition, so there was never a stable trait to begin with? Or is the situationist right that the trait-talk was always just a redescription of behavior and there's nothing there to be masked or finked at all? Take the actual design seriously: the 15-volt steps, the four escalating prods, the experimenter in the coat, the learner audible through the wall, and especially the variations where obedience falls apart — experimenter gone or reduced to a voice on the phone, learner moved into the same room, subject having to press the learner's hand down onto the plate. Each of the three readings predicts something different about those variations; tell me which one the pattern actually supports.

Then flip it on the framework. Does the fink/mask machinery even transfer from fragility and solubility to character, or is a character trait a different kind of thing that the conditional analysis was never meant to capture in the first place? And say where the conditional analysis just goes silent here instead of pretending it has an answer.

Skip the "situations are powerful" bit, I've read it. I'm definately not asking about the ethics fights either. I want the mapping done properly, and I want the places where it breaks.
