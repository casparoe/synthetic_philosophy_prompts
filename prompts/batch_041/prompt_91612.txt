A friend and I have been arguing, on and off for weeks, about whether a legal order can amend its own foundations — whether a rule that licenses changing every rule, itself included, is coherent or quietly eats itself. He thinks this is the deepest question in jurisprudence; I think it is a parlor trick dressed as one. To settle it we would like a piece of satire we can both read without flinching.

Write an Onion-style local-news report, roughly five hundred words, about a town that has adopted Nomic — the game in which changing the rules is itself a move, and the ruleset is the constitution — as its actual charter. The humor should come from getting the mechanics right rather than from generic jokes about bureaucrats and zoning boards. Let the reporting take for granted the things a player would: that rules come in a mutable and an immutable variety, that an immutable rule must first be transmuted before it can be touched, that there is even a rule instructing everyone to obey the rules, and that the original victory condition (a hundred points, accumulated by die roll) was always meant to be the deliberately boring part that players would soon amend away. Somewhere in the piece the town should run into the vertigo of self-reference: a rule governing how rules are changed, amended by the very rule it governs; an official whose office is to rule on whether a move was legal, appointed by a rule that the move in question may have repealed; entrenchment quietly deployed as a tactic by whoever is currently ahead. I want a reader who has played the game to recognize their own table, and a reader who has not to come away understanding why the question of who decides is the load-bearing one.

Keep it deadpan — no winking, no explaining the joke, no nudging the reader. Write it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and let the opening sentence do the work a headline would.

Since this is a parody and not a verdict, I do not expect you to adjudicate anything, but I would like a short closing note on where you took liberties with the actual mechanics of self-amendment or leaned on a contested reading of them; the paradox Suber named is not the same thing as the half-remembered legal paradoxes people usually mean, and I would rather know which one you used. And if I have set this up wrong — if a fully self-amending ruleset turns out not to be paradoxical at all, or my friend's side is the defensible one — say so plainly instead of writing around it.
