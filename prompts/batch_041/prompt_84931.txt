Ok so I've been rereading that Hofstadter book where he takes one tiny sixteenth-century French poem and translates it forty different ways, and the part that actually stuck with me isn't the poetry, its the shape of the problem. Every version is wrong somewhere and no two versions are wrong in the same place. I want a short story that treats that as a real constraint-satisfaction problem, not as a metaphor for how language is deep, man.

Use this setup. Invent the source poem yourself, don't reach for a real one. Twelve short lines, a refrain that comes back three times and is word-for-word identical each time — the repetition is the form, the poem stops being the poem if the refrain wavers. The twist: in the source language the refrain's key word is a homonym with two live senses. One is harmless — weather, or bells, or dawn. The other is a summons, the kind of word you'd use to call people into a square. The poem opens sounding like weather and closes sounding like a summons, and nothing in it changes except which sense you hear in the same fixed line. That drift is the entire trick of the poem, it's the only thing the poem is doing.

Now the translator. The target language has two separate words for the two senses and no overlap between them. She's doing this for an anthology under a culture ministry that is very touchy about the second sense. And the editor wants the same syllable count and the same rhyme scheme, and the available rhymes in the target language only line up if she commits to one of the two senses. So the form votes. The form always votes, and nobody ever mentions that when they say preserve the form.

What I want on the page: no magic perfect translation. If she finds a clever third option that keeps both senses, it has to cost something real — say it needs a construction so archaic that the audience the anthology is for can't read it, so faithful turns out to mean dead on arrival. Give me a rival translator who solves it by cheating (breaks the refrain, or leans on a footnote) and sincerely argues his version is the more faithful one, and let the two of them talk past each other for a while before anyone notices they're optimizing different things. And I want the moment she realizes that the thing she actually cares about, the drift, is not one of the three things she thought she was trading off against each other. Lossy compression is fine as a frame, but lossy compression has a distortion metric, and the whole fight here is about who picks the metric. No scalarizing it.

End with her choosing and having to live with the choice, not with the problem solved. Roughly 2000 words. And no translator's preface as a get-out-of-jail card — if a note rescues the poem, I want the story to notice that the note is a different poem.
