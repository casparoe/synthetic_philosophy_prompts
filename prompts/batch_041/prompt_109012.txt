I am a thirty-four-year-old archivist at a small county records office, and for the past eighteen months I have been failing to finish a catalogue of a collection that I myself proposed, secured funding for, and still believe is the most valuable thing I could be doing. The pattern is embarrassingly regular. When a deadline is a month or more away, I would sincerely choose to spend the next four weeks on the catalogue rather than take a smaller, immediate reward — say, the quiet admiration of clearing my inbox or the relief of leaving on time. As the deadline approaches, however, my preferences seem to flip: with a week to go I would trade almost anything to avoid sitting down with the collection, and I find myself doing trivial tasks that I would not have chosen from a distance. It is not simple laziness, because I do difficult work happily when it is for someone else, and it is not that I have changed my mind about the catalogue's value. A friend who works in finance says this is just a steep and inconsistent discount rate: my future self's preferences are not mine, and I should bind myself with deadlines, deposits, and a colleague who checks on me. Another friend, who teaches ethics, says that misses the point: I judge the catalogue to be best, I am able to do it, and I nonetheless do not; that is a failure of will, not a change in preference, and no contract can settle the question of what kind of person I am.

I have tried the obvious fixes. I have made a spreadsheet of my daily choices and found that the switch point is not gradual: at about eleven days out I am still resolute, and at ten days I am not. I have asked a colleague to hold a small sum of my money and return it only if I produce a section by Friday, and I have found ways to avoid asking her to enforce it. My flatmate, who designs games, suggests I think of myself as two players — Monday me, who can leave binding instructions, and Friday me, who cannot be trusted to follow them — and that the real question is what Monday me can do to make Friday me's defection costly. A counsellor suggested I write a letter from my future self; it was moving for an afternoon and useless by the evening. What I want from you is not a generic productivity plan. I want to know which diagnosis — the preference-reversal story or the failure-of-will story — better fits the details I have given, what each would predict about the cases where I do succeed, such as when the task is broken into thirty-minute blocks I comply but when it is described as finishing the catalogue I do not, and whether my calm month-out preference is my real preference or merely what I wish I were like. If the two diagnoses are not genuine rivals, explain how they relate: is one a description of the mechanism and the other an evaluation of the agent, or does one of them actually explain the other away? Finally, tell me what I should do on Monday morning, and be explicit about which parts of your advice depend on which diagnosis, and which parts would be good advice even if the diagnosis were wrong. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning applied to my case, and I would like your answer in British English.
