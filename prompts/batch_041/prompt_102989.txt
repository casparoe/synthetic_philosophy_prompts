Six weeks ago I changed my mind about something that matters to me a great deal, and I changed it in the strong sense: after years of hedging, I now hold a settled view with a confidence I did not think I was capable of, and it has quietly reorganized how I live. What unsettles me is a paradox I stumbled into shortly afterward, which seems to describe precisely what I have been doing ever since. As I understand it: if I genuinely know that P, then I also know that any evidence against P that ever surfaces must be misleading, since P is true; and so I appear entitled to treat every future scrap of counterevidence as misleading and set it aside without investigating it. The conclusion is repugnant, but I cannot locate the false step. It may be a reductio of a certain ideal of knowledge, a reductio of the demand that rational belief answer to all evidence, or merely a trick of self-reference that dissolves once stated with enough care. I need the disagreement laid out rather than summarized.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors, each as competent as the other, who disagree about this. Let the first be a dogmatist: someone who takes the paradox at face value, holds that it is sound, and argues that it is not a bug but a correct description of what rational commitment to a known proposition actually licenses — that the confidence I described is not a vice but the proper shape of knowledge. Let the second be a fallibilist: someone who insists the conclusion must be rejected, and who is willing to pay whatever theoretical price that rejection costs — including giving up the picture of knowledge on which the paradox rests. Neither may be a foil. Each must answer the other's actual arguments, concede what deserves conceding, and press where the other is weakest, rather than trading monologues.

Have them work through, at minimum, these four matters, and let each one genuinely move the discussion rather than being ticked off:

1. A case I keep returning to: the counterevidence in question is the record of my own past errors in this very domain. Does the dogmatist's license extend to dismissing evidence about my own reliability — and if it does, what is left of the knower who is doing the dismissing?
2. A case where the contrary evidence is the testimony of someone the knower has every antecedent reason to trust, who reports having examined the matter and reached the opposite conclusion. What does each side say the knower owes this person?
3. A distinction I suspect the paradox trades on: between dismissing evidence (refusing to look) and discounting it (looking and assigning it little weight). If the two are separated, does the paradox survive, collapse, or merely relocate?
4. The self-application. The dogmatist's principle, turned on the fallibilist's belief that the principle is false, seems to license the fallibilist to wave away the dogmatist's argument as well. Does this show the principle is empty, or does it break asymmetrically between the two? I want this one resolved, not gestured at.

Close with each interlocutor stating, in a few sentences, exactly what they still disagree about and what single consideration would change their mind. Do not let either one win by fiat, and do not end in vague agreement.
