I am a secondary-school student preparing an independent-study paper for a philosophy elective, and I have run into a question that the material I have been reading does not answer. The reading concerns Molinism, the position associated with the sixteenth-century Spanish Jesuit Luis de Molina, according to which God possesses middle knowledge: knowledge of conditional truths about what each possible free creature would choose in each set of circumstances, including circumstances that never actually obtain. As I understand it, these conditional truths are supposed to be contingent and true, yet settled neither by God's will nor by the creature's nature and situation, and they occupy a middle place between God's knowledge of necessary truths and his knowledge of the world he has freely decided to create. I have also been told that the familiar modern objection is that such truths seem to require something in reality to make them true, and that if they are grounded neither in God's decree nor in the creature's nature or causal history, it is unclear what could possibly do the grounding.

What I cannot find discussed anywhere, because it could not have arisen in Molina's time, is the following. Over the summer I took a data-science course in which I trained a fairly small model on a few thousand records of my classmates' choices, and it predicted, rather better than I expected, which elective each of them would pick in a situation none of them had ever been in; I invented the situation myself, so that no one's choice about it was ever actually made. Suppose the exercise were done properly, with an enormous training set and a model whose predictions about what a given person would freely choose in a circumstance that never occurs were almost always right. I would like you to argue, as carefully as you can, what Molina's position on such a thing would have been. Would he have treated the model's output as an instance of the middle knowledge he thought God grasps, or as something else entirely — a statistical generalization about a person's dispositions, grounded in facts about the actual world and therefore not middle knowledge at all? If pressed, would he conclude that reliable prediction of this sort shows the choices to be determined after all by nature and circumstance, or would he deny that predictability requires determination? And would he have held that a finite, non-divine intellect could possess knowledge of this kind at all, or would he have insisted that the relevant conditional truths are knowable only in a manner unavailable to any creature?

I would also like something like a residue report. Molina's vocabulary is one of natures, wills, decrees and conditional truths; mine is one of training data, features, distributions and generalization. Tell me which parts of the modern situation I have described carry over into his framework without loss, which carry over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in his vocabulary whatsoever; and then, in the other direction, which of his own questions about middle knowledge cannot even be posed in the vocabulary I have been using. Where you are extrapolating from his actual commitments rather than reporting something he plainly held, say so, and say how confident you are — I would much rather have an honest reconstruction with the seams showing than a confident verdict.
