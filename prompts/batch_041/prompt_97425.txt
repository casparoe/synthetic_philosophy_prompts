I am putting together a unit for a graduate seminar on decision theory and self-reference, and I want to pose an exercise that isolates a claim I have often heard asserted but rarely seen argued through: that the Solomonoff prior is malign for consequentialists who find themselves inside it. By the Solomonoff prior I mean the distribution over computable environments that assigns to each program a weight decreasing exponentially in its length; by a consequentialist inside it I mean an agent whose own source code is one of the programs over which the prior ranges, who is aware of this, and who chooses actions to maximize expected value under that prior. The thesis to be argued is not the familiar point that such an agent faces self-locating or simulation-style uncertainty, but the stronger claim that the simplicity weighting is malign in its practical upshot: because the agent’s action is itself an output of some program, the choice of action is evidence about which program the agent is, and a consequentialist who takes this seriously may find that the expected-value-maximizing move is to influence the prior’s distribution over worlds rather than to improve any world in the ordinary sense.

Please argue for that thesis. Address the most natural objection—that a well-constructed agent should simply conditionalize on its observations and act on the resulting posterior, so that any alleged malignity is just ordinary evidential reasoning—and explain why that objection either fails or is insufficient in the universal-prior setting. Assume no access to search or citation databases; I want a self-contained argument, not a literature review, and a small toy example or a few explicit steps would be welcome.
