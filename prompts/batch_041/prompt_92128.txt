I'm putting together a little humor zine for a grad reading group — this issue's theme is "formal epistemology you can live by" — and I need one more piece, so I want you to act as a satirical writer who actually understands algorithmic information theory and decision theory, because the whole thing only lands if the techincal content is right. The target I want you to go after is a family of arguments people call the malign-prior argument: if you really take the universal (Solomonoff) prior seriously — Turing machines weighted by 2 to the minus their description length, so shorter programs get more weight, and run-time doesn't matter — then a lot of the shortest programs that output your observations are ones in which some other agent, in some other universe, is simulating you, since "simulate a simpler universe, then exec our universe inside it" can be a shorter way to produce your data than just describing our universe head-on. Those simulated agents have preferences, and they have an incentive to steer the prior, so a consequentialist who actually uses this prior to make important decisions ends up, in a weird sense, taking orders from aliens who can nudge the measure. There's a sub-argument about an 'importance update' (the bits that get fed into priors used for important decisions get special weight) and counter-arguments that the whole thing is way less dramatic than it sounds — I want the piece to live inside that debate, not just name-drop it.

So here's the ask: write me a mock advice column, in the style of a syndicated agony aunt — "Dear So-and-so," maybe three letters and three replies, around 600–800 words all told. The letters should come (in the fiction) from ordinary people trying to live their lives while taking the universal prior seriously: someone who's noticed that the shortest program outputting their morning is one where they're being simulated and can't stop wondering what the simulator wants; someone asking whether their own deliberation is even well-defined inside the prior, given that programs simulating their deliberation are themselves just more programs in the set; and maybe someone who wants to know if they should try to "send a message" to the aliens by acting in a way that shows up in lots of short programs. The columnist has to take all of it dead seriously and give advice that's internally consistent with the framework — deadpan the whole way, no winking, no "lol isn't this silly." The humor should come from getting the actual structure right (why short programs dominate, why agents embedded in programs have real leverage over the prior, why all this is supposed to make the prior unusable for decisions), so that someone who's followed the debate laughs in recognition instead of wincing, while someone who's never heard of any of it can still follow the bit. Don't let the columnist escape by just declaring it all nonsense, and don't let it turn into a bibliography — no citations, no reading list, no "see the literature." Before you start writing, think for a minute and brainstorm which three questions would actually be the funniest if you answered them correctly, and pick the ones where the correct answer is genuinely unsettling rather than just cute. Keep the prose tight; the deadpan is doing all the work.
