After a long career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I keep coming back to transcendental arguments — the kind that purport to show that some belief must hold because it is a precondition of having any experience or thought at all. They put me in mind of the no-go results I spent my working life around: proofs that a given set of assumptions cannot be combined with a given outcome. I would like you to argue for a definite position on whether a transcendental argument is best reconstructed as a no-go result of that sort, and on what such a reconstruction would imply for its traditional ambition of refuting the skeptic. Make the case as forcefully as you can, then give the strongest objection to your conclusion and your reply to it, and say what would have to be true for the opposing verdict to be correct. No citations are needed; I care about the argument itself. Please write in American English and in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as one would lay out an argument in a letter.
