I'm a philosophy graduate student putting together the opening handout for a mixed reading group on science and pseudoscience — half the room is philosophy students, the other half working physicists and a science journalist — so the instrument has to be usable by someone who has never opened a book on the demarcation problem. Last week I got into an argument with a friend who flatly declared homeopathy "unscientific," and I found I couldn't justify the claim without already assuming the conclusion, which is what sent me here. Take the role of a philosopher of science who has taught this material for years and who is bored with the standard textbook menu of falsifiability, paradigms, research programmes, and "the problem is dead." I want a decision tree that sorts a reader from a few commitments about what the question is even asking into a named position on how — and whether — to separate science from pseudoscience. Work carefully, and before you build anything, spend a short paragraph mapping the logical space: tell me which axes you take it to have and why you chose those axes rather than the more obvious one of "which criterion do you favour?"

The branching should be driven by the structure of the question, not by a list of famous names. So, for instance: what is the demarcation meant to apply to — individual claims, whole theories, a sequence of theories, methods, the practices of a community, or the institutions that certify knowledge? Is the criterion supposed to be a definition, a single necessary and sufficient condition, or a cluster or family-resemblance judgment in which no one condition is necessary? Is it descriptive of what the word "science" has actually tracked, or normative, telling us what deserves the name? And when a practice fails the test, does the verdict carry epistemic force (the practice is not a route to knowledge at all) or only pragmatic and institutional force (it should not be funded, taught as science, or admitted as evidence)? Keep every branch node a question a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads — no node should be answerable only by someone who has already worked out which label they would receive.

Then place the leaves, and make sure at least these views appear somewhere: naive falsificationism; a view on which falsifiability is not a logical property of a theory at all but a methodological decision about which statements to hold immune from revision; the research-programme view on which the unit of appraisal is a series of theories judged progressive or degenerating over time; the view on which the unit is a community's problem-solving activity, so that "scientific" describes a practice in good working order rather than a theory's logical form; a multi-criterion or cluster view with a weighted list and no single necessary condition; a view on which "pseudoscience" is an evaluative, thick concept doing mainly rhetorical or political work, so the project should be reconceived or abandoned; a view on which the real distinction is not between theories but between inquirers, between those who are honest and responsive to evidence and those who are not; and a view on which there is no epistemically interesting distinction at all but the word still earns its keep as a rough social label. Use these cases as branch material, at least: astrology's habit of absorbing failed predictions by appeal to unspecified further influences; a speculative physics proposal that is currently unfalsifiable yet treated by its own practitioners as continuous with respectable science; homeopathy, whose advocates run controlled trials, a method shared with the sciences, while the theory behind it resists testing; and a case in which the label "pseudoscience" looks like it is doing social or institutional work rather than epistemic work. Each leaf should carry the name of the view (or "unnamed" if you think nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate and say why. Mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies and diagnose each one: is that branch empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered?

Give me seven to nine branch nodes and six to ten leaves, laid out as an indented text flowchart with each branch labelled by the question that splits it, followed by two or three sentences of commentary on which axis does the most work. Please leave out citations, reading lists, and dates — I have no interest in who said what, and neither will my readers; label the views by their content, and if you want to attach a standard label such as "sophisticated falsificationism" that is fine, but do not attribute a view to a person unless you are certain of the attribution, and never guess at a title or a year.
