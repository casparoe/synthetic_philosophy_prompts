ok, longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs (the sequences, the old OB stuff, all of it) and I want to actually stress-test a view I've held for years instead of just nodding along to it.

The view: when a big group of people converges on a wrong or arbitrary belief — a bad clinical practice, an investing fad, a whole research field going down a dead end, a country deciding some policy is obviously correct — the right explanation is almost always an informational cascade, and bias or motivated reasoning is the junior partner. The mechanism, as I hold it: people act in sequence, each sees what the ones before did but not why, and each reasons, correctly, that if that many people chose X then X is probably right, so they go along even when their own private evidence points the other way. After a handful of moves the accumulated actions outweigh any single person's signal and it locks in. Nobody in the cascade is being irrational; they're doing a valid update on data that has stopped containing information. The catch is that the early moves were arbitrary, so the content of the belief ends up basically uncorrelated with the merits, and two identical societies can land on opposite conclusions. And the flip side, which is the part I actually care about: cascades are fragile, because the whole thing rests on no individual's private signal being precise enough to outweigh the herd. A credible public announcement, or one or two people loudly stating what they privately know, should collapse it. So my practical conclusion is that most bad consensus is an information/coordination problem rather than a bias problem, and the fix is cheap.

What I want from you: everything that counts against this, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including the ones a defender of the cascade story would concede themselves, and an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Things I'm already chewing on and want you to sort out:

- the model seems to need a coarse action (buy/dont buy, adopt/not). Once actions are continuous and there's a price that moves, the price itself carries the info and I don't see why a cascade has to form. Does the pure story survive anywhere market-like, or is what we call herding there a different animal?
- fragility is the crux for me. If real herds turn out robust — you broadcast the correction and the belief reassembles itself — that looks like a direct hit. My worry is that a defender will just switch mechanisms: say it was reputational herding (people imitate because being wrong in a crowd is safer than being wrong alone) or investigative herding (everyone piles into the same questions because it's cheaper), both of which predict herding but aren't informational. If the defender can swap mechanisms whenever one mispredicts, has the view been immunized? And is that actually damning, or fine because the mechanisms are seperately testable?
- you can't observe anyone's private signal, so any herd is consistent with some signal distribution. Is there any observation that could actually separate the cascade story from plain conformist bias or a taste for following the crowd? What would the clean experiment or natural case look like?
- the story predicts the content of the belief is arbitrary. So a rival that predicts content — groups reliably converge on beliefs that flatter the group, or that are costly to challenge, or that serve whoever's in charge — is a problem, no? If you can predict which of two arbitrary options a group picks from features of the situation rather than the merits, the random-first-mover engine is in trouble.
- tension with the fact that aggregation does work a lot (prediction markets, averaging independent estimates). If cascades are this easy to trigger, why isn't everything a cascade?
- and how much of this survives if the agents are sophisticated — if they know the earlier people were also just following the herd and discount accordingly? I half-suspect the lab results lean on subjects who don't do that, in which case I'm back to a bias story with extra steps.

What would move me most personally: a real case where the early moves were demonstrably arbitrary and the group still landed on the truth anyway, or a case where you can call the eventual consensus from the merits before the cascade has run — if the merits predict it, the cascade was a detail, not the engine.
