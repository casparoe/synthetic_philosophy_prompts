I am a novelist at work on a book whose central episode turns on whether a man who has done something unforgivable still counts as a member of the community that raised him, and for the past year I have been reading in African philosophy — chiefly the literature on ubuntu and cognate Bantu ideas of personhood — in the hope of getting the question straight. What I did not expect is that the secondary literature would leave me less clear than my own characters. The dispute seems to have been running for decades, and it has the odd quality of an argument in which nothing is ever settled because nothing is ever quite joined.

As I understand it, one family of views holds that personhood is attained rather than simply possessed. A human being is born human, but becomes a person only gradually, through the recognition and the obligations of a community — coming of age, taking on responsibilities, marrying, raising children, contributing, being held to account. On this account personhood is relational all the way down: there is no person prior to or apart from the network of relations, and the network can in principle withhold or withdraw what it confers, which is why the wrongdoer or the exile can be described as having lost something real. A second family of views accepts the relational emphasis but denies that personhood is ever conditional. On this account a human being has full moral standing from the first moment and cannot lose it; community and relationship let that standing flower, but do not grant it. Read the other way, the first family's claims look to these writers like either an ethnography of how certain societies talk or a doctrine with ugly implications for infants, strangers, and the severely impaired.

Both sides work from the same stock of materials — proverbs, customary practice, ordinary usage, the contested translation of a few key words — and both reject atomistic individualism. Yet the dispute recurs.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about personhood at all, but about two different things sharing one word: one side answers a descriptive question about the concept a tradition actually operates with, the other a normative question about what standing we should recognize, so that the person of the first camp is an achievement concept, like adult or citizen, while the person of the second is a status concept, like human being. If that is right, most of the apparent contradiction dissolves.

Please test that hypothesis against what each side actually says. If it fails, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is — about facts, about which cases count as evidence, about what the question is, or about bedrock intuitions concerning whether worth may be conditional — and say what would count as progress: a distinction, a criterion, an argument that the middle position is unstable, or a change of question. And I would like to know whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. I am after a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey; references are unnecessary, as I have done the reading.
