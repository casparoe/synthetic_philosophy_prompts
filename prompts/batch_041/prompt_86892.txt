I co-lead a fortnightly reading group that has turned out to be about half philosophy graduate students and half economics students, with two people from the law school sitting in; none of them has had a formal game theory course beyond what we have read together, and the level of mathematical comfort in the room is uneven. The group spent two sessions on Nash's bargaining solution and the independence axiom, and the week before last on Rawls's difference principle, so everyone arrives carrying two habits I would like to break: they read any solution concept in this area as a proposal about fairness, and they picture a bargaining problem as a pie to be cut. Next month is my turn, the slot is ninety minutes, whiteboard only, about a dozen people, and I want to spend essentially all of it on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. What I need from you is not an exposition of the solution but a plan for how to teach it: the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why each depends on the ones before it, the one example that should carry the whole session together with what that example must have and must lack, the misconceptions the group will form and the precise moment in the sequence at which each should be headed off, the check for understanding I should run at the end, and a short list of what I should say outright that I am not going to explain at this level.

Two features of the mathematics should shape the plan, because they are where I think the session can go deeper than the standard treatment. First, the construction is anchored to the pair of best utilities, the payoffs each side could obtain if the other were maximally accommodating, and that anchor point is typically not feasible; my sense is that this is the crux of the whole thing, since it means the recommended division is benchmarked against a counterfactual about unilateral power rather than against anything the two parties could jointly have achieved, and I want the sequence to make that visible rather than to skate past it. Second, the solution is invariant when either player's utility scale is rescaled independently of the other's, so the equal-proportion-of-gain reading of it does not rest on comparing the two players' utilities; the room will assume that any fairness claim must involve interpersonal comparison, and I want them to see that this one does not, while also seeing what the axioms do demand of each player's utility scale. I also have a suspicion I have never checked: I believe the two-person characterization does not survive the move to three or more players. Tell me whether to say so in passing, and how to phrase it so that it closes the question rather than opening one we cannot finish in ninety minutes.

On the carrying example, I want a concrete recommendation with reasons, and I want to know what disqualifies the obvious candidates — the symmetric split of a dollar, the unit simplex, anything at all in which the two solutions coincide — since those hide exactly the contrast we are there to see. My own working summary, which I suspect is a trap, is that each side ends up with the same fraction of what it could have gotten on its own; tell me whether to keep that sentence at all, when in the sequence to risk it, and how to repair the reading of it once the group gets hold of it. My guesses at the misconceptions are that they will hear the solution as egalitarian in final payoffs, that they will treat the best-utilities point as an achievable or even a reasonable outcome, and that they will take any monotonicity axiom to mean that adding options can never hurt anyone. I want the ones I have not anticipated, in the order they will actually surface, each pinned to a specific moment in the session, plus one closing question that would separate a group that has understood the construction from a group that has merely followed it. Please plan the session itself: no reading list, no citations, and nothing that assumes anyone can look anything up during the hour.
