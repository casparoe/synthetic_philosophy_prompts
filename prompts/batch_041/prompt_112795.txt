I'm in a decision theory reading group this term, and before we meet I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position so the others can poke at it. Writing it down has made me suspect that the things I believe look innocent one at a time and dubious in a bundle, and I'd like a proper consistency audit — ordered by severity, with a price tag attached to each repair — rather than a tour of the literature or a list of things to read.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I'm a subjectivist about probability: I don't believe in unknown probabilities, only in facts I'm uncertain about and the degrees of belief I have about them, so there is nothing in the world that is "ambiguous" — it's just that my credence about it is less than fully determinate. (2) I take expected utility maximization to be the correct account of rational choice: what makes a choice rational is that it maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's credences and utilities, and nothing further is required. (3) I accept the sure-thing principle as a requirement of rationality: if two options have the same consequence in some state of the world, then whether that state obtains cannot be relevant to which of the two I prefer. (4) In the familiar Ellsberg case — one urn, 90 balls, 30 of them red and the remaining 60 black or yellow in a proportion I don't know — I would take the bet that pays $100 on red over the bet that pays $100 on black, and I would take the bet that pays on black-or-yellow over the bet that pays on red-or-yellow. (5) I don't regard that preference as a mistake or a psychological quirk; it strikes me as a perfectly sensible thing to do, and I wouldn't accept a pill that made me indifferent between the two colours. (6) Nor can I see why ambiguity aversion should be singled out for rational criticism when risk aversion isn't — the two look to me like the same kind of thing, attitudes about the shape of uncertainty rather than errors about the facts. (7) And I think the only genuine criticism one can make of an agent's attitudes is that they fail to hang together: the Dutch book and representation-theorem picture of rationality is broadly right, and there is no further substantive requirement that a rational agent's preferences must satisfy over and above coherence. (8) For what it's worth, I'd be indifferent if the 60 non-red balls had each been independently made black or yellow by the toss of a fair coin — even though in that case too I don't know the composition; the coin version feels to me exactly like the known urn, and I'd happily take it over the ambiguous one.

A friend tells me the trouble is between (1) and (4) and (5): that once I say there are no unknown probabilities, I've already conceded the ambiguous urn is no worse than the known one, and my preference is simply inconsistent with my subjectivism. That diagnosis has never convinced me, but I'm no longer sure where the real trouble is. What I want is this: tell me which of these commitments genuinely cannot be held together and which only appear to conflict; rank the genuine clashes by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, say precisely what would have to be given up and what is lost by giving it up; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most while costing the least, and whether that revision is even available to me given the rest of the package.
