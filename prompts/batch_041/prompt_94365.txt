A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether consequentialism demands too much of us — she thinks a morality that requires you to keep giving until the next dollar does more good in a stranger's hands than in yours is one nobody could live by, I think that is mostly an excuse — and we settled nothing. I would rather put the argument to work. I run donor advising at a small giving-advice outfit: people come to me wanting to know how much they are actually obligated to give, I get about twenty minutes with each of them, and neither they nor I have any training in moral philosophy. Turn the demandingness debate into a procedure I can actually run — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and tells the donor they have given enough, and where it must stop and hand the decision back because what remains is a question about the kind of life the donor wants rather than any matter of fact. Please preserve, rather than smooth over, the distinctions that do the work: between a demand being too hard and its being false; between a sacrifice that is marginal (a foregone holiday) and one that is absolute (health, a career, a relationship); the way the demands escalate as income rises and never reach a resting point; whether the donor's own projects are just one more good to be traded off against others or something the theory must make room for; and the reply that the same objection would sink ordinary morality, which nobody treats as refuted. Then run the procedure on two donors — a surgeon with a large salary and a chronic illness that makes her own comfort expensive, and a teacher with two children on a modest salary — including one case where it tells me something I do not want to hear. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and say what the procedure cannot do.
