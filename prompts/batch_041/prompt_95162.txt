Take the role of a philosopher who works on eighteenth-century aesthetics and knows Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" closely. I have been reading secondary commentary on it and want a careful audit of one paragraph, not a fresh exposition of the position.

The paragraph, from an essay in a general-interest philosophy magazine, runs like this: 'Hume's essay on taste is where modern aesthetics begins, and its upshot is that beauty is in the eye of the beholder. Hume agrees that we cannot argue about taste: a sentiment of beauty is just a feeling an object causes in us, with no counterpart in the object itself, so there is no fact of the matter for critics to be right or wrong about. He tries to soften this by allowing that some people have better taste than others — those with strong sense, delicacy, practice, comparison, and no prejudice, whom he calls the true judges — and he says the standard of taste is the joint verdict of such judges. But that is just an appeal to an elite: Hume gives no argument for why the verdicts of a trained few should bind the rest of us, and he concedes that the true judges are hard to identify. The essay therefore leaves us with subjectivism dressed up as a standard, and its real legacy is the relativism its own conclusion implies. Anyone defending objective beauty today is arguing against Hume, not with him.'

Before you judge anything, set out in your own words what Hume actually holds — the distinctions he draws and the machinery he uses — so that I can see the target. Then go clause by clause through the paragraph, sorting each claim into three piles: what a specialist would accept, what they would wince at as compression or conflation, and what Hume explicitly denies — stating the objection in each case. I want to know which of the paragraph's mistakes are harmless shorthand and which invert the position. Then say whether the closing claim, that Hume's view leaves us with relativism and that defenders of objective beauty are arguing against him, follows from anything true in the earlier sentences, and whether "subjectivism" is even the right label here, or whether something like a response-dependent or standard-grounded-in-human-nature reading fits better.

My own hunch is that the paragraph is too quick to make Hume a relativist, but I cannot locate the slippage. Assume I have no library and no internet: argue from the content of the positions themselves, do not send me to the secondary literature, and do not pad the reply with a reading list.
