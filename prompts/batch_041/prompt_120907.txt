A private foundation has set aside enough money to support roughly four years of postdoctoral work in the philosophy of biology, and it has decided to spend all of it on one narrow area: the intersection of Popper's falsificationism, his evolutionary epistemology, and his changing verdicts on Darwinism. The trustees are not philosophers; they want an agenda, not a survey, and they have asked you to set it. They are especially interested in questions that remain live after Popper's own partial retreat from his earlier claim that Darwinism is a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory — for instance, whether his later reformulation of natural selection as a claim about the relative frequencies of outcomes, given a specified range of available variation and a specified selection regime, actually restores testability or only redescribes the data; whether evolutionary theory contains laws or only a schema for historical explanation; whether the analogy between natural selection and scientific method is constitutive or merely heuristic; whether Popper's three-worlds ontology and his interactionist account of mind are needed to explain the emergence of the self, or whether selection plus language suffices; and whether his propensity interpretation of probability can ground a non-vacuous notion of fitness. You may add questions the trustees have not thought of, and you should drop any of these that you regard as dead ends. Rank the five lines of work that most deserve the money, from most to least urgent. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, and in each item cover, compactly: why the question is upstream of the others in this area — what becomes tractable or moot if it is settled; how far it can be pursued by conceptual argument rather than by new empirical biology; and what argument or finding over the next four years would move it up or down your ranking. No introduction or conclusion.
