I'm in an upper-year metaethics seminar and we've just spent three weeks on fitting-attitude accounts of value, the idea that for something to be good is for it to be the kind of thing there's reason to admire / desire / value. I missed the class on the wrong kind of reasons objection and my friend in the seminar gave me her version of it on the walk home. Essay's due Friday and I want to know how much of her version to trust before I build an argument on it.

Her version: the fitting-attitude analysis says a thing is good iff there are reasons to value it. The wrong kind of reasons objection is what kills the analysis. The point is you can have reasons to have the attitude that have nothing to do with the thing being good. Classic case: a demon will torture you unless you admire him, or an eccentric billionaire pays you to admire his terrible novel. You really do have a reason to admire, but the novel isn't good, so the analysis wrongly says it is. The fix, which she says everyone accepts now, is the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons: only object-given reasons, the ones about the features of the object, count for the analysis, and demon / billionaire reasons are state-given (they're about the state of you having the attitude), so they drop out and the analysis is fine again. She says the same structure shows up for belief: you can have reasons to believe something that don't make it true, like believing you'll pass the exam calms you down and actually makes you more likely to pass, which is why belief isn't something you can just decide to do.

Is that fair? Go sentence by sentence and seperate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what's actually wrong. In particular:

- Does the objection really kill the view, or is that an overstatement about a live debate where people are still proposing fixes?

- Does restricting to object-given reasons actually solve it, or is it a stipulation that just redefines which reasons count? Someone in class said the two distinctions come apart, e.g. a reason to intend to do something because intending makes you more likely to actually do it is about the state but still seems like a perfectly good reason for the attitude.

- Our seminar leader said the real criterion for right kind is whether the reason bears on the question the attitude is answerable to: admiration is answerable to whether the thing merits it, and the demon's threat doesn't speak to that question at all, so it's not a reason of the right kind, full stop. Is that a genuinely different fix from the object / state one, or the same thought in other words?
