I have been keeping, for some years now, a private list of cases about the value of information, and the list has stopped cohering. I would like you to find the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures my verdicts on the cases, and to tell me candidly which of those verdicts I have to give up.

I accept the theorem usually credited to I. J. Good: for a Bayesian decision maker, the expected value of information cannot be negative, since she is always free to ignore what she has learned and act as she would have acted. The argument looks to me like a proof, and yet the following is what I actually think, case by case.

1. A woman deciding whether to carry an umbrella tomorrow is offered a free and perfectly reliable forecast. She should take it. This one is not in doubt.
2. The same woman, the same forecast, now for yesterday. It is worth nothing to her — but it is not worth less than nothing.
3. A man is offered a test for a disease that is untreatable and certain to kill him, and nothing he does will change its course. He knows that he would spend the remaining years in dread, and he declines the test. I think he is right.
4. A woman is offered a test that predicts nothing, and she knows it predicts nothing, but she is a poor statistician and will treat the result as decisive, giving up her job if it comes out badly. She asks my advice. I want to tell her not to take it.
5. A man is told that he has been assigned to the group that usually does better, and the telling itself improves his performance. Here the information is a cause rather than an instruction; whether to give it to him strikes me as a question about what will happen to him, not about what he will do.
6. A researcher tells me she hopes her next experiment will destroy the hypothesis she has spent her career defending, and that she will run it regardless. She is not going to act differently whatever the result; she simply wants to know. I think her attitude is not merely admirable but correct.
7. I have already sold a house. I later learn what it went for. I would rather not have learned it, and the loss seems to me to lie in no action of mine at all.
8. I learn something about a friend that I did not want to know, because now I must decide whether to speak, and I will not manage to leave the decision unmade. The information has made my position worse by adding a decision to it.
9. A friend offers me free access to a complete archive bearing on a question I care about; using it would take a decade of reading. I would be a fool to accept, though I pay nothing for it.

The best principle I can state on my own, and I do not trust it: information is worth having exactly to the extent that it could change what I do. That covers 1 and 2, makes 3 harmless (he can ignore the result), makes 4 beneficial (she will act on it), and gives 6 no value whatever. Three of my nine verdicts fall to it, which is why I am asking.

What I would like from you: (a) the principle, or the smallest set of candidates, that does best by all nine verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a tenth case without asking you again; (b) your own verdicts, in the same concrete style, on three or four further cases that the principle is meant to decide; (c) if no single principle saves all nine, a plain statement of which verdicts you are overturning, and why those are the ones to sacrifice rather than others.

Please commit. Do not tell me that everything depends on the utility function and stop there: tell me what the utility function would have to contain, in each case, for my verdict to be the right one, and whether that is an honest description of the case or a way of rescuing the theorem by redescription. If you think my list is rigged — that 3 and 7 are one case twice over, or that 5 is not about information at all — say so, and say what follows for the rest.
