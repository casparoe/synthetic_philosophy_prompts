I am a doctoral candidate in social and political theory, and I have just spent an uncomfortable month in an archive. The small, self-consciously venerable college where I teach is preparing for its sesquicentennial, and I was asked to write the commemorative history. What I found is that nearly everything the place treats as immemorial was assembled within a single decade in the 1890s: the charter is a reconstruction, the ceremonial robes were designed by a committee, the motto was composed by a professor with a taste for heraldry, and the annual procession exists because somebody needed to fill a gap in the calendar. None of this is secret; it is all in the minutes, in the college's own hand. What unsettled me was less the fabrication than my own reaction to it, and the argument a friend and I have been unable to close since. Set against that case is my grandmother's village festival, which really is six hundred years old and which almost nobody under forty attends; and the college's manufactured procession, which reduces grown adults to tears. I would like you to take the role of an expert in social ontology, the philosophy of history, and the theory of collective identity — someone at home in the Hobsbawm–Anderson literature on invented tradition and imagined community but not inclined to treat it as settled doctrine. Work slowly and in stages: first restate the dialectic in your own words and identify what each step has to mean if the step after it is to follow; only then commit to a verdict. I do not need a reading list or citations — I know the literature well enough to be dangerous — I need the diagnosis.

Here is the argument, reconstructed as carefully as I can manage. (1) A community counts as imagined when its members' sense of belonging depends on representations that outrun anything any member could verify or experience at first hand. (2) Every community larger than a face-to-face village satisfies that condition — the nation, but equally the university, the profession, the church, the reading public. (3) So all such communities are imagined. (4) What is imagined in this sense is constituted by historically datable acts: print runs, school curricula, censuses, maps, committees, oaths. (5) Its boundaries, its membership rules, and its emotional charge are therefore contingent; they could have been otherwise, and often were otherwise. (6) What is contingent in this way is not natural. (7) What is not natural has no authority over me except the authority I choose to confer. (8) Therefore my college, my nation, my profession, and my language have no claim on me that I am not free to revoke at will. (9) By the same steps, the community of inquiry now assessing this argument, the practice of giving and asking for reasons, and the institution of promising are equally contingent and equally without authority. (10) So the argument's own demand that I accept its conclusion carries no authority over me either. Yet every step from (1) through (8) looked obvious when I wrote it down, and I would rather not surrender (2), which seems to me plainly true of the college.

Tell me which step I should give up, why it looked so plausible, and what giving it up costs — in general, and for the two cases above. Then, for each of the two or three most defensible exits, describe the position I am left with once the step is gone: what else it obliges me to accept and what it forbids me to say. One further thing I want your help with. I have been distinguishing traditions that are merely fabricated from traditions that are load-bearing, where the fabrication is not incidental but is precisely what enables the community to do what it does, so that exposure would not merely embarrass the members but dismantle the thing. My grandmother's festival looks ancient and decorative; the college's procession looks recent and load-bearing. If that distinction is doing real work, say where it enters the argument above and which premise it repairs; if it is a distinction without a difference, say why.
