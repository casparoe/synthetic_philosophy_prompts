I'm three chapters into a novel whose narrator is a translator of Latin poetry, recently handed a diagnosis he insists on calling "the verdict," and who has taken to consoling himself with Epicurus — a habit I gave him partly because it lets me put fragments of the De Rerum Natura into his mouth, and partly because I wanted a character whose calm is argued for rather than merely felt. I know the argument well enough to know I'm out of my depth: I can recite it, but I can't tell where a philosopher would say I have been reciting a slogan rather than a position. I recently came across a general-interest essay on the subject that I had been treating as my crib sheet, and before I let my narrator lean on it in a scene where he argues his brother out of grief, I'd like to know how much of it would survive contact with someone who actually works on the topic. I don't need a reading list or citations — I need to know which sentences to trust.

The essay runs roughly as follows: "Epicurus posed the oldest puzzle about death and answered it in a sentence. While we exist, death is not yet here; once death arrives, we are no longer around to be harmed by it. Nothing can be bad for you unless it is bad for you at some moment when you are present to suffer it, and death is never such a moment; so death is nothing to us. His Roman follower Lucretius supplied a companion thought — the famous symmetry — that we do not grieve over the eternity of time before we were born and have no better reason to grieve over the eternity after we are gone; but the two arguments are really one, and together they show that the fear of death is an error of the imagination. The one serious modern reply comes from Thomas Nagel, who argues that death is bad for us because it deprives us of the years and pleasures we would otherwise have had. That reply is decisive, but only if you abandon hedonism, since it asks us to count as a harm something the dead never experience; and because Epicurus was a hedonist, the whole dispute reduces to whether pleasure is the only good. The upshot is that dying is nothing to fear, that mourning the dead is a confusion, and that no one can be wronged by what happens after they are gone — their obituaries, their reputation, the misreading of their books."

Would you go through this claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on death would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is simply wrong? I am particularly unsure about four things. First, the essay gives the argument's key premise a name and a shape — nothing is bad for you unless it is bad for you at a moment when you are present to suffer it. Is that the premise the argument actually needs, or has the essay quietly smuggled in something stronger by making the premise about suffering rather than about existing? Second, is it true that the deprivation reply requires giving up hedonism, and that the dispute therefore turns on whether pleasure is the only good? Third, the closing sentence bundles several things together — that dying is nothing to fear, that mourning is a confusion, and that posthumous wrongs are impossible. Which of those follow from the argument as Epicurus gave it, which are later accretions, and which are simply false? And fourth, what would a defender of the Epicurean line say in response to the deprivation objection? I would rather my narrator lose an argument honestly than win one by a trick.
