I'm putting together a worksheet for the second half of a course I'm TA'ing on distributive justice, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than a set of reading questions, because the students keep memorizing labels ("I'm a luck egalitarian!") without being able to say what follows from that label on an actual case. The tree should start from something no one in the room would deny -- roughly, that the way a society distributes benefits and burdens can be just or unjust -- and then branch by questions, where every node is a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Concretely, I want the branches to turn on: whether equality is valued for its own sake or only as a means to something else (not being dominated, not having some people lording it over others); whether the thing to be equalized is the person's subjective life-state, their holdings, what they're actually able to do and be, or the relations they stand in to other people; whether the view has a threshold structure (everyone must get enough) or a comparative one (gaps matter as such, even above any threshold); whether the view cares about where the shortfall came from, i.e. whether it treats a shortfall the person chose as still a claim on the rest of us; and whether the view even wants a single index that lets us say person A is worse off than person B all things considered, or thinks that demand is a mistake.

I'd like the leaves to be the real positions: strict welfare egalitarianism, a resource-based view, luck egalitarianism as a separate leaf from plain resource egalitarianism (I want to see exactly which node seperates them, because I suspect on my tree it's only the source-of-inequality branch), the capabilities view, a sufficiency view, prioritarianism, relational egalitarianism (the view that the point isn't the gap in holdings but whether people stand in relations of equality with one another), and desert. Include at least three concrete case nodes rather than pure abstractions -- the person who's adapted their preferences downward and reports being perfectly satisfied, the expensive-taste case (someone who can only get by with a costly diet or a compulsion they didn't choose), and a case where two people reach the same level of functioning only if one of them gets more resources than the other (a ramp, an interpreter, whatever you like). Where a combination of answers has no defender, say so and say whether you think it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag the leaves your tree can't seperate -- I'd bet sufficiency and prioritarianism come out the same on most of my cases, and I'd rather you tell me that then quietly add a node to fix it. Keep it to about eight nodes and ten or fewer leaves, write each node as a single question with two or three answer options and no jargon in the node text itself, and please no citations or reading lists -- the students have those already, I just want the structure.
