ok so I need help settling an argument with a friend of mine. He keeps bringing up an old result in decision theory — Good's theorem, or the principle of total evidence, I can never remember which — that says a rational agent should never turn down free information. The expected value of a free look is never negative, because worst case you just ignore what you saw and act like you never looked. He thinks that's basically airtight and that anyone who says otherwise is confused. My gut disagrees in a bunch of cases, and I want to know what rule I'm actually running on.

Here's where my gut lands:

- Free test that tells me which of two antibiotics will knock out my infection, before I have to pick one: take it, obviously.
- Free test that tells me I carry a gene for a horrible disease with no treatment, or the exact date I'll die: I dont want to look.
- Free look at the last page of the novel I'm 40 pages into: no thanks.
- Someone spoils that same novel for me after I've finished it: delighted, tell me everything, doesn't bother me at all.
- Finding out, 20 years later, that my partner had a fling early on. We're happy, we're staying, the knowledge changes nothing I'd do: I'd rather not know.
- Finding out why my uncle died, even though he's gone and there's nothing to do with the answer, even if it's something ugly: I definately want to know.
- Told the result of a bet I already placed and can't change: I want to know, but I also feel like it genuinely doesn't matter either way.
- Free test that says whether I'd be happier as a nurse or an accountant, before I commit to either: give it to me.
- Same test, arriving a month after I've signed a five-year contract: worthless to me, and yet I'd still peek.
- A test that says whether my partner will someday leave me, where just knowing the answer changes how I act and therefore whether they leave: I want it, but I also think this one isn't really free — the looking is doing something to the world.
- A test that tells me where my partner is right now, which I'd use to check up on them when I'm anxious: I could act on it, and I'd rather not have it.
- Finding out my friend's spouse is cheating: don't want it, because the second I know I'm stuck deciding whether to say something.

"Can you act on it" covers the antibiotics and the contract case, and then the uncle case blows it up. "Does it hurt to carry it around" covers the uncle case, and then the antibiotic case blows that up, since learning the treatment is the bad one is also lousy news. I tried: information is worth having when you can act on it in time and you don't mind holding the belief. But the uncle case has neither and I still want it.

Is my friend right that I just have strange tastes about beliefs and the theorem is untouched? Or is one of my cases an actual counterexample? Give me the cleanest principle that captures most of my list — I want the instrumental part and the what-it's-like-to-carry-it part separated out and stated precisely — then tell me which one or two of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it predicts for a case I didn't list: a free test that says whether my kid will grow up happy, offered before I decide whether to have a second one. One more thing he said: the theorem is only about looks you're offered, not news that lands on you whether you ask or not — does that distinction do any work in my list? No reading list, just the argument, and American English please.
