Ok so I'm a software enginer, no real philosophy background (one ethics module in college, I mostly remember the memes), and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on Kavka's toxin puzzle. As I understand it: someone offers you a million bucks if by midnight tonight you *intend* to drink a vial tomorrow that will make you horribly sick for a day. You know that tomorrow, once the money is banked, you'll have no reason to actually drink it. So the question is whether you can rationally -- or even psychologically -- form that intention now.

Two reasons I care. One, at work I deal with protocols where a "commitment" is something you enforce by posting collateral: you stake money and get slashed if you defect. That's just changing your payoffs, and it's boring and mechanical. The toxin puzzle looks like the opposite -- the payoff for the intention and the payoff for the action are totally severed, so the commitment has to come from somewhere inside the agent rather than from the world. I want to know if that's a real distinction with legs or just a cute trick. Two, I keep noticing toxin-shaped situations in real institutions -- a central bank that wants to be believed, a legislature that binds itself to a rule, a guy who quits smoking by announcing it to everyone -- where what's being rewarded is a *state of the agent* rather than an action, and where the state only counts if its genuine rather than performed. Is that a real research area or am I just pattern-matching?

So: if you had to pick the three or four open questions around the toxin puzzle that most deserve someone's attention, what are they, and why in that order? I care about a specific kind of "deserves attention": upstreamness (how many neighboring questions get answered or dissolved if you settle this one), tractability (could a smart person with no lab and no funding make progress, or does it need empirical psychology?), and whether the answer would change anything downstream -- e.g. how we think about self-control, or about institutions that run on credibility.

Some candidate questions I'd want you to take seriously, but tell me if some are dead ends: (a) is the puzzle really about the *possibility* of the intention or about its *rationality* -- i.e. is the hard part psychology or norms? (b) is "intending to X while knowing you wont X" even coherent, or is intending partly constituted by settling on doing? (c) does the puzzle generalize to beliefs and other attitudes we can't adopt at will -- if you could get paid for sincerely believing something you know is false, is that the same puzzle or a different animal? (d) is the genuineness requirement doing all the work -- i.e. if a third party could verify your commitment instead of you having to bind yourself, does the puzzle evaporate, leaving only a residue about *self*-binding? (e) are the institutional cases the same puzzle or just metaphor?
