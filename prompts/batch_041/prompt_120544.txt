Last night a friend and I stayed up far too late arguing about the liar paradox, and we ended up on opposite sides of a distinction that I suspect matters more than it is usually allowed to. He describes himself as a paraconsistentist but not a dialetheist: he agrees that a satisfactory logic must not let a contradiction explode into triviality -- that is, must not license the classical inference from a contradiction to any sentence whatsoever -- but he denies that any contradiction is ever true. His move is to give up adjunction: from A, and from B, you may not infer the conjunction of A and B, so that the two halves of a paradox can each be asserted without the contradictory conjunction ever being assertable. I take the more orthodox dialetheist line: the paradox shows that some contradictions are true, and paraconsistency is a consequence of that fact, not a substitute for it.

Please compare these two positions. I would like you to press on three things. First, does his non-adjunctive maneuver genuinely avoid true contradictions, or does it merely relocate them -- for instance, by making the truth predicate or the conditional do work that the conjunction would otherwise have done? Second, what does giving up adjunction cost him: can he still state the T-schema (the principle that a sentence is true just in case what it says is the case), reason by cases, or use the conditional in the way a theory of truth needs? Third, and most importantly, which of the two positions gives the better diagnosis of why the classical principle of explosion has to go -- is explosion a symptom of a mistaken theory of truth, as the dialetheist says, or a symptom of an over-permissive rule of inference, as my friend says? I would be grateful for a comparison that takes both views seriously rather than treating one as a merely technical variant of the other.
