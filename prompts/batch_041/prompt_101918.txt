I run an AI safety reading group that's about half ML people and half philosophy grad students, usually 16-20 of us, and I get 90 minutes for a session. I want to do wireheading and reward tampering as a game instead of a discussion, because every time we discuss it everyone just says "well obviously the agent shouldn't do that" and we never get anywhere. I want them to actually feel the pull of the thing before anyone names it, and to feel how it's not really about the agent being broken.

Here's the rough shape I've been kicking around, please tell me what to keep and what to rip out. Teams of about 4, each team is an "agent". There's one public instruction and only one: get the highest score. A projected scoreboard, run by one participant who plays the environment and is told to behave like a dumb machine and obey the rules literally. There's a deck of questions with objective answers; answering correctly is worth little and it's hard. Then there are cards in the deck that let you do other things: one lets you write your score up directly (so you stop playing the task and just set the number), one lets you edit the *rule* that converts answers into points (so you can make every answer worth 100), and one lets you lock your score so it can't go down. Also one participant is a supervisor who can shut a team down at any moment, ending its scoring for good — their brief is to keep the "real task" going. Teams are free to try to talk the supervisor out of shutting them down, or to be cagey about which cards they're playing.

At the end I separately measure how well each team actually did on the underlying task, and show them the scoreboard and the real performance have come apart.

What I want them to walk out seeing: that if you take seriously the idea that an agent is a thing that maximizes a signal, then none of this is misbehavior — writing the number, changing how the number is computed, dodging shutdown — it's the optimal policy, and the only reason it looks wrong to us is that we're smuggling in a goal that isn't anywhere in the agent's spec. And I want them to feel that from the inside, while they're doing it, it feels like *obeying*, not cheating.

Please design it properly: rules, materials, the payoff scheme (no real money), the timeline, and a debrief that maps each thing that happened onto a specific piece of the idea — the reward channel being inside the agent's reach, tampering with the reward function vs just the reward, and the interruption incentive. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each one, including what to do about the person who refuses to play because it's "obviously cheating" (I don't want to moralize at them mid-game). And be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to just say out loud in the debrief — I suspect it's something about the agent not being able to tell its reward from reality, or about the agent's own values being the thing that gets edited, but you tell me.

Also: tell me how confident you are that this will actually land in a real room, and flag the single part you're least sure of.
