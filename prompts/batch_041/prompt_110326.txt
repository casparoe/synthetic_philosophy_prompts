I have been reading around the dissociations between the sensory and affective dimensions of pain, and I keep getting tangled in a question that seems more conceptual than empirical: what exactly is supposed to be *bad* about pain, and can that badness come apart from the sensation itself? My rough understanding is that in pain asymbolia — a condition usually associated with damage to the insula and its connections to limbic structures — patients report that a stimulus hurts, and can still say where it hurts and roughly how intense it is, while denying that it bothers them; some reportedly fail to withdraw and even seem amused. If that summary is mangled in any way that matters, say so, because I'm working from a rough gloss rather than the primary reports, and I don't want to build the question on a caricature of the syndrome.

To keep the discussion tractable I'd rather run it through a stipulation of my own than through the clinical cases, since I don't trust my grasp of those. Suppose a compound — call it K-17 — that leaves the sensory-discriminative apparatus of pain fully intact: the subject can localize the pain, rate its intensity, tell sharp from burning, track it over time. What it strips out is the alarm: the state no longer grips attention, no longer generates an urge to escape, no longer presents itself as something to be avoided. Now consider two layers of badness that I want to pull apart. Call the first *signal-badness*: a state is bad for you because of what it indicates about your body — tissue damage, ongoing harm, threat of worse to come. Call the second *grip-badness*: a state is bad for you because of the way it monopolizes attention and commands action. My tentative view is that K-17 removes grip-badness but leaves signal-badness untouched, so that a subject under K-17 would have a reason to pull her hand out of the flame while having nothing to complain about. I suspect that's too clean, and that the interesting work is in why.

Please explain the conceptual issue: whether pain's badness is intrinsic to its phenomenal character, or whether it's better analyzed in terms of what the state represents, what it motivates, or what the subject desires not to undergo. Say whether my signal/grip distinction tracks anything real or collapses under pressure, and where a defender of each of the main families of views — hedonism about welfare, desire-satisfaction accounts, and perceptual or representational accounts of pain — would come out on the K-17 case, and why they'd differ. I'd much rather you flag it plainly if the question as posed is confused — for instance, if "badness" is being asked to do too much work across prudential badness, reason-giving, and motivational force all at once — than answer smoothly around the problem. Be explicit about which moves are substantive claims and which are just artifacts of how I've stipulated the case, and calibrate: tell me what you take to be settled, what's genuinely contested, and what you'd need to know before committing.
