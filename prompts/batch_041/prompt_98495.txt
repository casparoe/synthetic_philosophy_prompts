I'm in a reading group that's been crawling through the later Wittgenstein, and we keep spilling into an argument that I dont think he ever could have had, because it depends on stuff from after his time. The question is which computations, if any, matter morally. One person in the group, a cognitive science PhD student, has proposed a little taxonomy to try to make the debate tractable. First, the trace criterion: a computation matters morally only if it maintains a persistent, re-identifiable history of its own states, such that interrupting or overwriting that history is a harm to it. Second, the valence criterion: only states that function like pleasure and pain inside the system's control loop matter -- states that it seeks or avoids, that modulate its learning, and so on. Third, the report criterion: a system matters only if its self-reports can't be fully explained as a lookup table or a rehearsed script, i.e. if saying I am in pain is doing something more than emitting a trained token. Our resident Wittgenstein person says all three criteria are already confused, because they treat meaning as something behind the utterance rather than as use in a form of life; the PhD student says that's exactly the move that lets us ignore the possibility that we're building moral patients. Then someone else brought up LLMs and said if a model tells you it's suffering, and there's no fact of the matter about its inner life that you could ever check, then Wittgenstein's private language argument would say the whole question is unstatable, not just unanswerable. I'm not sure that's right -- it seems to turn a point about rule-following into a behaviorist gag.

So here's what I want: if Wittgenstein were somehow alive today and had watched the last decade of AI, how would he come down on this? Not just a vague gesture -- I want you to predict his actual positions on the three criteria above, and say whether he'd think the moral status of a computation is a factual question, a grammatical one, or a practical one about how we decide to go on. Would he accept substrate independence, or would he think calling a silicon system a moral patient is a category mistake? Would he say a model that says it's in pain is lying, or playing a game, or neither? And if he'd reject the whole framing, what would he put in its place -- something like moral status as an aspect of a shared form of life, or a decision we make rather than discover? I'd also like you to flag where you're extrapolating from his actual texts versus where you're guessing, since I know he never wrote about neural nets. Keep it concrete: for each of the three criteria, give a verdict and a one-sentence reason. Also, if you think one of the criteria is closer to his spirit than the others, say which and why. Don't give me a reading list; I'm asking for the view, not the sources.
