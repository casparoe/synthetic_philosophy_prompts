I'm a third-year PhD student working on philosophy of mind and moral psychology, and I'm stuck on something that started out as a seminar joke and is now genuinely bothering me. In our reading group on irrationality we've been circling the self-deception question — whether it's even possible — for basically the whole semster, and nobody has moved an inch. The traditionalists say that for something to count as self-deception you need a deceiver and a deceived, and here they're the same person, so you either get a contradiction (believing p and believing not-p at once) or you have to split the mind into quasi-agents, which the other side thinks is just relabeling the problem. The deflationists say fine, drop the intention and the literal talk of deception, and self-deception turns out to be just motivated belief formation — biased search for evidence, selective attention, the usual — and the paradox evaporates. Then the traditionalists say you've changed the subject, and the deflationists say the subject was never coherent to begin with, and round we go. Throw in the evolutionary accounts where the function is to deceive other people and the self-deception is a byproduct of being convincing, and the whole thing splinters into three or four conversations that only look like one.

My hypothesis — and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis, not a verdict — is that the impasse isn't really about the psychology at all but about what kind of explanation people are after. One side wants an account that preserves the first-person texture, the sense that you are simultaneously the liar and the mark; the other side is perfectly happy with a third-person mechanism that either leaves that texture unexplained or explains it away as a feeling. If that's right, then part of the disagreement is about what level of description folk-psychological vocabulary is even supposed to operate at, and no amount of new work on biased reasoning is going to touch it. But I might be wrong — it could just be a verbal fight about whether calling it deception commits you to an actual deceiver and an actual intention, or a hidden empirical question about whether any real case involves anything worth calling intention.

Please act as someone who knows this literature cold — philosophy of mind plus the empirical work on motivated cognition — and think carefully before answering; start by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses, then commit to one. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually claims, plus an honest answer about what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question. And there's one thing I'd like you to take seriously as a candidate resolution rather than dismissing it: the analogy to fraud inside an organization. In a firm, the information usually exists somewhere in the system, but it's kept away from the part of the system that has to act — nobody has to hold two contradictory beleifs, and there's no little homunculus doing the lying. Does modeling self-deception that way actually dissolve the paradox, or does it just push the same problem down a level? And if it does work, tell me what it costs us, because my suspicion is that the debate has stalled precisely because everyone quietly agrees the bureaucratic picture is fine and nobody agrees that what it describes is still self-deception.
