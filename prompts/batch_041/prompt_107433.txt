I am in the sixth form and a member of my school's debating society, and we recently debated the motion that charitable giving ought to be guided by the greatest expected good per pound. The opposition's strongest speaker made his case almost entirely through a joke I had come across before in other contexts: a man is on his hands and knees beneath a street lamp, searching for his keys; a passer-by asks him where he dropped them, and he says somewhere over there in the dark, and when asked why he is looking here instead, he explains that this is where the light is. The speaker told us that this is precisely what the effective-altruism movement does. It directs money towards whatever can be counted — bed nets, cash transfers, deworming — not because those are where the good is, but because those are where the numbers are, and he concluded that we ought therefore to shift our giving towards the unmeasurable, structural work of reforming institutions and policies.

I found the joke memorable, and I suspect it is doing considerably more work than it is entitled to, but I cannot articulate why. Could you audit it for me? I should like a table setting out what corresponds to what — the lamp, the light, the keys, the dark, the searcher, the passer-by, the street itself — followed by an account of where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he drew from those that are merely decorative. Two features in particular puzzle me. The drunkard knows where he lost the keys and therefore has a definite alternative place to look, whereas the critic of effective altruism does not appear to know where the good is to be found; and the drunkard is searching alone, whereas the movement's defenders will say that different people can search different stretches of the street, and that some illumination is better than none. Tell me whether the strong conclusion — that the good lies in the dark, so that is where we should go — survives contact with these differences, or whether the joke can support only the weaker claim that the lit patch is not the whole street; and if a better analogy exists for the same complaint about measurability, what it would be.
