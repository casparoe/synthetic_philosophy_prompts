I've been reading alot about convention — the David Lewis stuff — and trying to line it up with things I actually deal with as an engineer plus ordinary life, and my gut verdicts won't settle into a pattern. Here's where I land:

- Driving on the right vs the left: clearly a convention, and obviously arbitrary, since half the world does it the other way.
- Tabs vs spaces: convention I guess, though in a repo with a linter it feels more like a rule someone imposed than a convention.
- Using port 443 for HTTPS: convention, or something close to it.
- Tipping ~20% in the US: I want to call it a convention, but it feels different from which side of the road to drive on.
- Not cutting in line: not a convention — that's just wrong, I have a reason to queue no matter what everyone else does.
- How you dress for a job interview: not a convention, more of a trend, even though I do it because everyone else does.
- The particular gesture a culture uses for "come here": convention, and arbitrary.
- A team's "we deploy on Tuesdays" habit: convention? rule? just inertia?

The one that keeps bugging me is queueing vs. port 443. If the whole office stopped queueing for coffee I think I'd still queue; but if nobody used 443 anymore I'd switch to whatever everyone else used without blinking. And yet I want to say both are conventions.

Is there one principle that gets most of these, and what does it say about the ones I can't place? Does it force me to drop calling tipping a convention, or to stop treating interview-dress as a non-convention? Also curious what it predicts for a case I didn't list: a regularity that started as pure coordination (say, everyone driving on the right) but that most people now follow because they think it's morally required — convention or not?

And since you're way more careful about this than I am: tell me how confident you are in whatever principle you land on, and flag the single point you're least sure about.
