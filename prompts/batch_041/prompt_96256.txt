I'm about sixty pages into a new novel and I've stalled out, and I'm fairly sure the reason is that I've been rereading Dennett's Kinds of Minds and now I can't stop seeing everything through his four-tier scheme — Darwinian creatures that only get to be clever because their ancestors died, Skinnerian ones that learn by doing and getting rewarded or punished for it, Popperian ones that can run a guess inside their own skull instead of risking their neck on it, and then the fourth kind, us, the ones who think with tools we didn't invent, words and notations and diagrams and other people's minds. What gets me is the tower image: each stage sits on top of the one below and never replaces it, so a tool-using creature like me is still a Skinnerian animal underneath, and still a Darwinian animal underneath that. And what I want for the novel is a scene where somebody goes down the tower instead of up — where the tools fail, and she has to think with her hands and her habits and her gut, and by the end it isn't clear whether she climbed back up or just found a more comfortable cage on a lower floor. So: write me a short story, 1500 to 2500 words, about that descent. Setting, since I've got some of it already: a small wildlife rehab place on a river that floods every few years, run by a woman in her fifties who took it over from a mentor who kept everything in a battered logbook she still follows like scripture, and there's a hand-raised crow in the place that she's never been able to decide about — whether it's reasoning or just performing tricks that used to pay off. Storm comes, water rises, she has maybe an hour to decide what to open and what to leave shut, and the logbook's rules and the crow's behavior start pointing in opposite directions. Don't let anyone lecture about the four tiers, don't name them, don't have a grad student show up to explain it — I want the ideas legible in what she does with her hands and what she stops being able to do. Then give me a short afterword, 250 words or so, where you commit: which tier you think actually does the decisive work in the story and why, whether you think the descent is a real descent or just a story about stress, and where you took liberties with Dennett or leaned on a reading of him that people argue about. I don't want a survey of interpretations, I want your position, stated plainly, with the parts you're least sure about flagged. One more thing — if you decide the crow is a Popperian creature, fine, but make it cost the woman something to believe that.
