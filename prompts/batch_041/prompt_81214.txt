I recently received a genetic result — a variant of uncertain significance — and I have been struck by how quickly I move from the statistical language of heritability to the fatalistic language of a script. I am also due to lead a discussion at a film club, and I want to read Gattaca through the lens of heritability: what it does and does not mean. Please give me a reading that takes both the film and the concept seriously. I want the technical meaning of heritability kept firmly in view: it is a population-level variance statistic, not a property of an individual; it can be high even when environments matter enormously; it does not license individual prediction; it does not entail immutability; and it is not the same as genetic causation. Please also bring in the environment-dependence of heritability estimates — change the range of environments and the estimate can change — and the distinction between heritable and inherited, along with the idea of a norm of reaction, the way a genotype can yield different outcomes across environments. Use those ideas to interrogate the film's central conceit, a society that sorts people by genetic profile and treats the result as destiny. Where does the film dramatise something real about the gap between a population statistic and a personal fate? Where does it trade on the very confusion it might be warning against — for example, by making genetic advantage look like a fixed script rather than a norm of reaction? Which scenes best illuminate the difference between heritability and determinism, and which scenes resist the reading? I am especially interested in whether the ending vindicates the thought that genes are not destiny, or whether it quietly replaces genetic determinism with a different kind of voluntarism, the thought that sheer will can override biology. I do not want the film reduced to an illustration of a textbook point, nor the concept bent to fit the film. Finally, say what the film adds to the debate that an argument about heritability alone does not — and where the honest critic has to admit that the film is not really about heritability at all, but about genetic discrimination and the illusion of merit.
