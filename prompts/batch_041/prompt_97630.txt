I've spent the past couple of years working through the free will and moral responsibility question on my own, mostly from popular books and long arguments with friends, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that I suspect can't all be true at once. I'd like you to audit them. I don't want a reading list or citations — I want to know which of these actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and what I'd have to give up to make the package consistent. Please rank the real tensions by how serious they are rather than treating them as equally bad, and tell me what the cheapest repair would be for each. If some commitment turns out to be doing no work at all, say so, so I can stop worrying about it.

Here is where I've landed.

(1) No one is ever ultimately responsible for anything they do. By ultimately responsible I mean responsible in a way that traces back to a source that is genuinely the person's own — a self that they are themselves responsible for having. But everything a person is traces back to factors they never chose: their genes, their upbringing, the accidents of their history. So there is no such source, and ultimate responsibility never gets off the ground. I take this argument to hold whether or not the universe is deterministic; sprinkling in randomness doesn't give the person any more ownership of who they are.

(2) None of that changes how I actually live. I still think resentment and gratitude are fitting responses, that it's right to praise good character and condemn cruelty, and that when someone does something cruel they deserve the condemnation they get. I also think we can run a criminal justice system that holds people answerable for what they do.

(3) I think what makes an action really someone's own — call it their authorship of it — is whether it flows from their settled character and considered values rather than from external pressure or a passing impulse. Someone who acts from who they really are is, in that respect, the author of the deed.

(4) I think authorship is what responsibility is for. If an action is truly yours in that sense, then you are responsible for it, full stop. It doesn't matter how you came to be the person you are; once the deed flows from you, the responsibility is yours.

(5) I think blame and punishment, if they are to be justified as something the person actually deserves rather than merely as a tool for shaping behavior, require more than authorship. They require that the person could have been a different person in the first place — that the self from which the action flowed was itself something they were answerable for.

(6) I don't think it matters whether a person could have done otherwise at the moment of action. What matters is whether the action expressed the person, not whether some alternative was open to them right then.

I can feel that something in here is incoherent, but I can't tell which pair is the real problem and which only looks like one. For each genuine tension, tell me precisely what would have to be given up and what I'd lose by giving it up, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your answer in American English.
