I've been hanging around this forecasting website for a few months now — it's one of those places where everyone posts a probability on a yes/no question ("will X happen before date Y") and the site shows a "community prediction" that's basically a weighted average of everybody's numbers. The thing that keeps bugging me is the culture. If you post 85% on something the community's sitting at 15%, people don't just shrug and go "well, priors differ." They treat it as either (a) you know something the rest of us don't, in which case you're obligated to say what, or (b) you're being irrational or a contrarian. There's this whole etiquette where if someone writes a long comment explaining thier reasoning, you're supposed to either update toward them or point to the specific piece of private info they're missing — just staying put is treated as a confession that you havent thought it through. And yet the same site constantly reminds you that you should report your honest probability and not just anchor on the crowd. I can't tell if those two norms are in tension or not.

A friend in my philosophy of science reading group says this is all downstream of Aumann's agreement theorem — that if two people share a prior, and it's common knowledge what each of them believes, then they can't rationally disagree, so any persistant disagreement has to come from someone having private information or from someone being irrational. But I'm not convinced the site's culture actually needs anything that strong, and I'm also not convinced it's even internally consistent.

So here's what I want from you. Figure out what this forecasting culture actually presupposes. Does it really presuppose a shared prior — and if so, what is that supposed to be, some objective prior that all rational agents would converge on, and is a website full of strangers entitled to assume one? Does it presuppose common knowledge of each other's beliefs, and of each other's rationality, which strikes me as a pretty heavy idealization to build a whole etiquette on? Which rival pictures of disagreement does the culture quietly rule out — say, the idea that two rational people with the same evidence can simply have different priors, or that some disagreements are about how to read the question rather than about the facts? Be careful to separate what the culture strictly requires from what it just happens to fit with, because my hunch is a lot of it could survive on much weaker assumptions. And if you think the practice is actually incoherent — demanding convergence under conditions where the theorem's preconditions clearly fail — say so and tell me what the site would have to change. If it turns out the culture is compatible with several different theories of disagreement, tell me which ones and what observation would actually tell them apart.
