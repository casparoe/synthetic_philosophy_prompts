I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size SaaS company and I keep getting into the same argument at planning time. Somebody wants to ship a thing, somebody else says we should test it first, or do a spike, or shouldn't security take a look, and then we burn two or three weeks on it. Half the time we ship exactly what we were going to ship anyway. The other half the result comes back muddy and we ship it anyway with a caveat. I don't think we're being rigourous in either direction, I think we're pattern-matching on what feels responsible.

A mate of mine who did a philosophy degree told me there's a theorem, I think the guy's name was Good, that says the expected value of getting information before you decide is never negative, because if it turns out useless you can just ignore it and act like you never looked. Which if you take it literally means we should never ship anything ever, we should just gather data until the sun burns out. He said there's conditions on it and I shouldn't take the pop version seriously, but then last orders got called.

So what I want is a checklist I can actually run in a planning meeting in about ten minutes, for deciding whether to go get more information (run the A/B test, do the load test, do the user interviews, book the pen test) or to just decide now with what we've got. It has to be built on what the theorem actually says, not the pub version. I don't need a proof. I need to know which of its conditions have to be true for the guarantee to apply at all, which ones we quietly break all the time without noticing, and what to do when they're broken — if the guarantee is off, does that make the test pointless, or does it just mean the theorem has nothing to say and I'm back to judgement?

Two things I specifically want it to catch, because I'm fairly sure we get these wrong:

- we announce we're "running an experiment to decide", but by then we've effectively committed to shipping, so no result is going to change anything. I suspect that quietly kills the guarantee, because the whole reason the theorem works seems to be that you're allowed to ignore what you find out.
- the thing we're going to learn isn't just going to shift the odds between options we already have on the table. It might reveal a whole category of option we didn't know we had, or tell us our model of the problem is wrong. I have a feeling that's a different kind of value and the theorem might not cover it, but I want to know if I'm right about that.

Then run the checklist on three things actually in our backlog:

1. A/B test on the checkout button copy. Two weeks, we can run it on half the traffic, and we'll ship whichever one wins.
2. A penetration test before we launch a new API that touches customer data. Six weeks and expensive, and depending on what they find we might have to redesign the whole thing.
3. A six-week benchmarking exercise to pick between two database vendors, when we've already got strong reasons to pick one and the migration deadline is in five weeks.

I want the checklist in an order I can literally read out loud to the room, and I want it marked up so it's clear which steps are the theorem actually doing work and which are just judgement wearing a philosophical hat. Where the checklist genuinely can't settle something, flag it as such rather than inventing a rule to cover it. And if one of my three cases gets an answer I'm not going to like, say so and don't soften it.
