I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading political philosophy on my own, and I am going to ask you for something of the kind I used to ask of my graduate students: a case built to order, with its controls.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on libertarian theories of justice and who knows Nozick's entitlement theory — justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and the rectification of injustice — together with the Wilt Chamberlain argument, in detail. Begin by thinking out loud for a few sentences about what a case would have to contain in order to do the job described below; then give me the case itself, followed by the analysis.

The job: I want a scenario in which the entitlement theory itself requires a coercive interference with holdings that were acquired through voluntary transfers — the very kind of interference the Chamberlain argument is designed to make us recoil from — so that the two lines of argument, which I have always seen presented as mutually supporting, deliver opposite verdicts about one specific act. I want to see exactly where they part company.

Constraints on the case:

- It must be realistic and present-day. No state of nature, no island, no hypothetical history, no device that exists only in a philosophy paper. A court could hear this case.
- Every transfer in the chain after the original acquisition must be voluntary, informed, and at fair value. No fraud, no duress, no mistake, no one ignorant of anything a reasonable buyer would want to know. The only injustice in the whole story is at the root.
- The original injustice must be one that can be rectified determinately: by the end of the story it must be clear who was wronged, what was taken, and roughly what would restore them. Do not build the case around the difficulty of computing rectification; that is a different point, and I want this one.
- Give me the case's twin as well: the same scenario with the root acquisition just and nothing else changed. The pair must isolate a single variable, and I want you to say which one.

Then, in separate sections:

1. Walk through what the entitlement theory says at each step, what the Chamberlain argument says at each step, and identify the single act of coercion about which they disagree.
2. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the disagreement when that feature is removed.
3. Tell me how a committed Nozickian would attack the case — in particular, whether the Chamberlain argument can be defused by saying that it was never meant to apply to holdings with unjust roots, or by appealing to the admitted underdevelopment of the rectification principle — and how much of the disagreement survives each reply.

What the case is for, so that you can see what would count as cheating: I am trying to determine whether the Chamberlain argument is a free-standing argument for market liberty or whether it silently presupposes that the starting distribution is just. A case that simply stipulates that the baseline is unjust, and therefore that rectification is owed, without showing the two arguments colliding over a specific act would not answer my question.

Finally, I want your honest estimate of your own reasoning: say where it is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind. Which fact about the case, if I have it wrong, or which principle, if I accepted it, would flip your verdict about the disagreement? I would rather have a case that survives your own best objection than one that survives only mine.
