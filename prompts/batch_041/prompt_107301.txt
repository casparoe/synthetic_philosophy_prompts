I'm maybe two thirds through a novel set in Cambridge in the 1880s and one of my main characters is basically Henry Sidgwick — the ethics man who helped start the Society for Psychical Research and then spent years sitting in dark rooms with mediums, half hoping, half testing. I've been going back and forth on one scene for weeks now and its driving me a bit mad, so I want to rehearse it out loud. The scene: a younger, harder colleague corners him after a sitting that produced nothing and needles him — you want the soul to survive death, he says, because only then do duty and self-interest stop tearing you in half, that's the whole ache of your book; but be honest, even if the mediums were real, even if you proved it tomorrow, all you'd have is more life, not a moral order, and a longer appetite is still an appetite, so the one thing you actually need is the one thing your method could never hand you. I want to play Sidgwick against that, so please play him for me and stay in character until I say cut.

Keep him historically himself — cautious, scrupulous, almost comically honest about his own motives, quick to grant a real point but never quite collapsing — and use the concepts he actually had: the dualism of practical reason, the insistence that belief wait on evidence, and the sharp line he drew between what he hoped and what he thought he'd shown. One tight paragraph per turn so it feels like two men arguing across a table; you can ask me questions as he would (what do you mean by a moral order, what would count as evidence, why assume the two pulls can't be reconciled some other way), and don't lecture in his voice, let him think it through. My opening move, in the colleague's words, is this: 'You keep telling everyone you've never seen a sitting you'd stake a shilling on, yet you keep going back, keep paying, keep defending it in print. Isn't the wish doing all the work here? And even if the wish came true, isn't it still just a longer appetite?' Your turn — be Sidgwick.
