I teach a 90-minute session on the paradox of voting to about twenty second- and third-year undergraduates in political philosophy; they know a little decision theory but no social choice. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the paradox rather than hear it described. The group must decide, under a small real stake, whether to turn out, where turning out costs each participant individually and the outcome is shared regardless of who paid. Arrange the rules so that abstaining is the individually rational move and the group still ends up worse off, so they run into the free-rider structure themselves; then add a second phase in which the same behaviour is redescribed as each voter having a negligible chance of being pivotal, and make them see that these are two different paradoxes sharing one name.

Give me materials and setup, the payoff scheme (no real money — points or a shared prize the class can allocate), a timeline, any roles, and a debrief mapping what happened onto (i) the pivotal-vote account and (ii) the public-good/free-rider account, and then onto four responses: the expressive account of voting as consumption; the minimax-regret argument, that one should vote because the regret of having abstained in the world where one was pivotal is catastrophic whatever its probability; the universalisability move, asking what would happen if everyone in your position did likewise; and the evidential thought that choosing to vote is evidence that others like you will vote too, so the act might matter where it cannot be causally decisive. For each, say whether it solves the paradox, dissolves it by changing the payoffs, or only relocates the problem. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply in the debrief.

I don't need citations. Keep settled matters apart from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present the expressive account as the consensus. End with a table with the columns 'Response', 'What it changes', 'Solves or dissolves', 'Standard objection'. British English throughout.
