I'm writing up my position statement for a reading group on consciousness and want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is where I have landed.

1. Illusionism. Nothing has a felt quality; what exists are states that represent themselves as having one. I call such a representation a reflexive seeming, and I hold that reflexive seemings are always false.

2. The stencil model. Attention does not illuminate a preexisting inner scene; it stamps the scene with the quality it then reports. On this picture the reflexive seeming is manufactured in the act of introspecting rather than read off a fact that was already there.

3. Evidential primacy. Introspective reports are the primary data for any science of consciousness, and a theory that has to treat them as noise rather than as evidence has already lost.

4. Causal efficacy. When I take my hand off a hot stove, it is the unpleasantness, not my belief about it, that does the work; I do not see how a mere representation could do that work.

5. A verdict. A system that represents its own states as its own — a thermostat does not, a large self-monitoring model might — is still not a subject of experience. Being a subject takes more than self-representation.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how severe is each genuine tension? For each, give the cheapest repair and what the repair costs, then say which single revision would resolve the most. I would rather have the two or three that matter, ranked, than an exhaustive list of every pair.
