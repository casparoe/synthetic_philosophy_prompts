Ok so I keep going down the rabbit hole on pain asymbolia -- the thing where people with certain brain injuries still feel pain, can tell you roughly where it is and how strong it is, but it just... doesn't bother them. They don't seem to mind it, and sometimes they'll laugh or crack jokes while it's happening. Now imagine someone actualy managed to bottle that: a drug that leaves the sensation completely untouched, same intensity, same location, all the usual reflexes, but strips out the part where the pain is awful, the suffering part. I'm trying to get clear on the most important implications of this for the philosophical question of why pain is bad in the first place. Is pain bad because of the sensation itself, or only because of how we relate to it and want it to stop? Does the asymbolia case actually decide anything, or does it just move the bump under the rug -- like, maybe those people are still in a state that's intrinsically bad even though they don't report minding it, in which case we've learned less than it looks like we have. And then the practical fallout, which is what I really care about: would it be wrong to hand someone the drug instead of a normal painkiller -- a soldier who needs to keep going, someone in palliative care, or a person with chronic pain who's just exhausted by it? Give me the big picture: which theories of the badness of pain the case helps, which it threatens, and what it does or doesn't imply about how we ought to treat pain. Please don't just tell me the condition exists, I already know that -- spell out the implications, and be honest about which ones are actually forced by the case and which are just suggestive.
