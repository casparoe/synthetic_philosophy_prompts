I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, which means I spend a lot of time at the edges of philosophy without ever having been trained in it, and lately I have been circling a puzzle I cannot get to sit still. It began as a reporting thread on prizes for being right — forecasting tournaments, prediction markets, cash awards for people who publicly change their minds — and turned into a question about whether a reward for having an attitude can ever be a reason for the attitude itself. In the toy case where someone offers me money to admire a mediocre novel, I can see that the money gives me a reason to want to admire it, and even a reason to do what I can to bring the admiration about. What I cannot see is whether it gives me any reason at all in favour of the novel, or whether it merely gives me a reason of another kind that has wandered into the wrong room. The distinction sounds crisp when I say it aloud and goes soft the moment I try to specify what makes a reason the right kind rather than the wrong one; I can produce three candidate criteria and then a counterexample to each, which I take to be roughly the state of the art and not merely my own incompetence.

I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session at a small interdisciplinary summer institute — about eighteen participants, a mix of philosophy graduate students, two working scientists, and a couple of journalists like me — and I want the session to be an activity rather than a talk, partly because I am the least qualified person in the room to lecture and partly because I think this puzzle is far easier to feel than to state. So design me a runnable game or structured deliberation that puts the group in the position of having a stake in their own attitudes. I want participants to be offered something they genuinely want — small but real, chocolate or a share of a pot or the right to leave early, nothing needing a budget or an ethics review — in exchange for coming to admire, approve of, or hold some specified pro-attitude toward an object I will supply, and I want the machinery of the offer to make certain questions first-personal rather than abstract: did the offer give you a reason, and a reason for what — to want the attitude, to cultivate it, or a reason bearing on the object? If you succeeded in admiring, did the object improve, and did you? I would like the rules to force the group to keep two ledgers, one for considerations that speak in favour of the object and one for considerations that speak only in favour of having the attitude, and then to have to place each new consideration case by case, with some cases deliberately built to resist placement. Build in at least one inverted case, where the prize is offered for disadmiring something plainly good; one case where the pressure is a threatened loss rather than a gain; and one control case where the attitude in question is a belief rather than a liking, so that the payoff is transparently not evidence. What I want the group to come away seeing is that a consideration can be perfectly good as a reason to want an attitude and yet no reason whatsoever in favour of the object, and that the criterion separating the two is much harder to state than to feel — ideally they should end the session having drafted such a criterion themselves and having found it leaking.

Give me the full kit: materials, roles, the exact sequence with timings, the payoff scheme and how it is enforced without real money, a set of facilitator lines or a light script so that my own framing does the minimum possible work, and a debrief that walks back through what happened and maps each moment onto the pieces of the problem — the picture on which value is being a fitting object of a pro-attitude, the two-ledger distinction, the difference between a reason for an attitude and a reason for the state of having it, and the point at which the whole construction leans on a restriction nobody has managed to explain. Assume the participants have read nothing and that I will not be citing anyone; if the debrief needs a term of art, put it on a one-page handout in plain English. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my own bets are a room full of people who simply lie for the chocolate, and a group that dismisses the prize as irrelevant far too quickly — and how to handle each failure without converting the session into a lecture. Finally, be straight with me about what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it cannot manufacture the real thing (a durable change in what someone actually admires), cannot settle the philosophical dispute, and cannot make artificial stakes bite as hard as a genuine threat; tell me what else is missing, and what I will have to supply myself.
