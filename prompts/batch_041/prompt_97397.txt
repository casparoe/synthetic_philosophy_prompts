Ok so my buddy in our decision theory reading group has been on one about Kavka's toxin puzzle and he's built this argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but every time I try to knock it down he has a comeback. Here's how he writes it out:

(1) A rational agent, at the moment of action, does whatever maximizes expected value given her beliefs and desires right then.
(2) By the time the choice to drink actually comes around, the million is already sitting in my account, so drinking only buys me a day of misery and gains me nothing.
(3) So at the moment of action, a rational agent does not drink.
(4) I know I'm rational, and I know (1)-(3), so I know I won't drink.
(5) You can't intend to do something you're certain you won't do -- intending to X requires thinking there's a real chance you'll X.
(6) So I can't intend to drink.
(7) The billionaire pays out for the intention alone.
(8) So I can never collect.
(9) And nothing here was special to toxins or billionaires: whenever the reward is paid up front for an intention to do something you'll have no reason to do later, a rational agent who knows her own mind can't cash in.
(10) That covers promises to repay favors, deterrent threats, basically any precommitment you'd want to make.
(11) But deterrent threats obviously work and people obviously keep promises, so (9) has to be false and something upstream has to give.

He says the upshot is that intention can't be a reason for action at all, which I think is nuts, but I can't find the crack. He's also got a variant where the payment is triggered by the billionaire correctly predicting your intention rather than by the intention itself, and he says the same argument goes through, which I don't buy either. I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. So which step do I cut, why did that step look so plausible in the first place, and what do I owe if I cut it -- does anything important fall with it? And if it's (5) that has to go, what does intending something actually require, if not that?
