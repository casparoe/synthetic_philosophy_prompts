I'm in a philosophy of language reading group and we're finishing a unit on definite descriptions, and I've been asked to bring a short poem to close the last session. I want a villanelle — 19 lines, two refrains — and I want the two refrains to be the two readings of the old example about the bald king of France and the negation. One refrain should be the wide-scope reading, where the not is outside and the whole thing comes out true because there's no unique king to be bald; the other should be the narrow-scope reading, where the not is inside and the whole thing comes out false because it says there is a unique king and he isn't bald. The poem should be spoken by the description itself, or by the phrase, or by the ghost of the king — I dont care — but it should feel the difference between being a thing that fails to refer and being a thing that refers to a failure. The turn should be the moment the speaker realizes the ambiguity isn't in the world, it's in where you put the not. And the last two lines should bring both refrains together, not to resolve them but to show that they were always the same sentence wearing different scopes.

One more thing: a friend in the group is a free logician and keeps insisting there's a third reading, where the description presupposes existence and the sentence is neither true nor false, just a misfire. I don't want the poem to adjudicate that, but I want it to leave a door open for it — maybe in a line that can be read either as a failed reference or as a refused one. Don't mention Russell by name, don't use the words quantifier or scope in the poem itself, and don't put any footnotes. Someone who knows the material should be able to tell which refrain is which and why they end up side by side; someone who doesn't should still get a poem about a king who isn't there. Also, no rhyming dictionary words like France/chance unless they're doing work. I'd rather it be a bit strange than tidy.
