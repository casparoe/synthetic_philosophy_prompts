I run a small macro research shop. Nine analysts publish probability estimates on discrete, resolvable questions — whether the central bank cuts at the next meeting, whether a merger clears, whether an incumbent holds a seat — and we size positions directly off those numbers. Every Thursday we hold a reconciliation meeting. Any question on which two analysts' numbers differ by more than ten points gets twenty minutes, and at the end of it I sign off on one house number.

A junior analyst has been pushing a rule he can state in two sentences: if two of us disagree and each of us knows the other's number, then under a common prior at least one of us must be wrong, so the fix is always to pull the two numbers toward each other — in practice, average them and move on. He is right often enough to be dangerous, and those two sentences are doing work the underlying result does not license.

Write me the procedure I should run in that meeting. I want it as a sequence of questions to ask about a given disagreement, in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a clear statement of where the sequence terminates in a single house number and how that number is computed, a clear statement of where it terminates without one, and a note on which steps are judgment calls that I should sign my name to rather than pretend are mechanical. I suspect the useful form of the thing is a diagnostic — a question whose answer tells me which precondition of the result has failed — rather than a pooling formula, but you should tell me whether that is the right shape. Whatever survives the simplification must preserve the distinctions the result actually needs: whether the two analysts are answering the same question over the same state space, whether each merely knows the other's number or the disagreement is common knowledge between them, whether their priors are common or merely similar, whether the stated number is the analyst's actual belief, and whether a persistent disagreement is evidence about the world or evidence about the analysts. Keep the meeting inside twenty minutes, and assume the analysts cannot show each other everything: some inputs are licensed and cannot be shared.

Then run the procedure on three disagreements and say what it outputs in each. First, two analysts working from the same model with different licensed data, at 0.70 and 0.45 on a rate cut, both numbers now on the table. Second, two analysts whose difference survives every test you impose and traces to a prior about how fast a regime reverts — a difference that no data we can buy will settle. Third, one analyst who proposes to settle it with a bet at even odds; I want to know whether that is a clean way to extract the other's information or a trap. For the second and third cases I expect the honest answer to be one I do not like, and I would rather have it than a number.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. In particular: can it protect me from the case where every analyst in the room shares the same wrong prior, and should agreement among my analysts ever be treated as evidence that we are right?
