I run a monthly reading group that grew out of a LessWrong-adjacent meetup — engineers, two doctors, one lawyer, no formal philosophy training, high tolerance for abstraction, and a house style that reaches for expected value within about four minutes of any ethical question. Next month's text is Kant's short 1797 essay against Benjamin Constant on lying to the murderer at the door; everyone will have read it, since it is only a few pages. I have the hour and a whiteboard, and I want the plan for the explanation, not the explanation. What I most need is the sequencing and the dependency structure: which component has to be in place before which, and why. My inclination is to keep the Groundwork machinery — maxims, universalizability, the formulas of the categorical imperative — almost entirely out of the room, on the theory that a group trained on game theory will treat universalizability as a formal toy, spend twenty minutes trying to break it, and never reach the actual argument. Tell me if that is a mistake, and if the essay does presuppose something from the wider system, what the minimum is and exactly where it has to enter the sequence. Relatedly, I do not think the murderer case itself can carry the session: the group has just read it, it may be the most meme-ified case in the history of ethics, and half the room will be performing a reaction to it rather than thinking. I would rather it be the last thing we test the apparatus against, not the vehicle. So give me the carrying case. It has to be constructible in two sentences with no props; the difference between saying something untrue and making a declaration someone is entitled to rely on has to be visible in the case itself rather than asserted by me; it must not involve a third party's death, because I do not want trolley reflexes in the room; and it must not be the kind of case where the group can escape by declaring the questioner to have forfeited all claims. Say what the carrying case must have and must lack, and why the obvious candidates fail.

Then the misconception map, in the order these will actually surface, with the specific point in the sequence where each gets headed off. I expect, roughly in this order: the absolutist-crank picture (Kant would hand the friend over, and this is why nobody should be a Kantian); the framing of the dispute with Constant as consequentialism versus deontology, which I suspect is the lazy frame and which will prevent anyone from noticing the move about who owns the consequences; the assumption that the wrongness of the lie is a wrong done to the person deceived, which is the one I think is genuinely hard and where I expect the group to stall; the escape hatch — that one can say something true but evasive, or simply decline to answer, and thereby be home free, which will tempt everyone into concluding the whole thing turns on bookkeeping about what counts as a declaration; and the rationalist-trained move of constructing a universalized maxim that permits lying to murderers and declaring the problem solved. I want the sequence to test a reading rather than assume it, so build the plan around this hypothesis of mine and tell me if it fails: that the engine of the essay is not rule-worship but imputation — that by lying you insert your own agency into a causal chain you do not control, and thereby become answerable for outcomes you never intended, whereas the murderer's subsequent acts remain his own. If that reading is wrong, I would rather learn it from the plan than from the room. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and how to say so out loud without the group feeling cheated: the derivation of the categorical imperative, the wider doctrine of duties, the question of whether Kant was personally admirable, and the state of the secondary literature are all candidates. I am not asking for sources or a reading list; the essay is the text, and I would rather spend the hour on structure than on proof-text hunting. Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that would distinguish a group that has understood the shape of the argument from a group that has merely memorized the punchline, and tell me what landing to aim for in the last ten minutes — whether "Kant is wrong but the objection is more interesting than we thought" is the right place to stop, or whether there is a sharper question I should leave them holding.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important sequencing decision, stated flatly — and then give me the plan and the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restating of my setup.
