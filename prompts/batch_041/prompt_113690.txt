I've been in a history-of-science reading group for a couple of years now — the kind where someone always brings a paper from the 1970s and someone else insists we go back to the primary source — and I keep noticing a pattern that I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether it already has a name or whether it needs one coined. Three instances. First: a survey of nineteenth-century physiology in which phrenology appears for exactly one paragraph, as an error that had to be cleared away, with no attempt to explain why serious people found it compelling, while a comparatively minor figure whose speculations happen to resemble a modern mechanism gets a whole chapter and a sympathetic reconstruction. Second: a book on the rise of the modern research university, written by someone who plainly dislikes what universities have become, and yet the narrative still runs as though the university were the destination — the medieval studium is a precursor, the nineteenth-century German reforms are a step, the twentieth-century critics are footnotes — even though the author's entire point is that we should not have ended up here. Third: a biography in which thirty years of a person's life are compressed into a single chapter because those years don't connect to the thing the person is now known for. In none of these cases is anyone explicitly saying the present is better, or judging past actors by present standards. The distortion isn't in the evaluation; it's in the selection and the proportioning. The present state of the field or the institution is doing the work of a filter: what gets a chapter is what can be told as leading to it, and what gets a paragraph is what can't, regardless of how much space it actually occupied at the time.

I know the obvious candidate is what people call Whig history, but every account of that I've read emphasizes a progressive or triumphalist moral — the past as a march toward a better present, with heroes and villains — and the thing I'm describing seems to survive the removal of all that. It shows up in pessimistic and critical histories, and in histories with no evaluative agenda at all, purely as an architecture of attention. I'm the sort of person who, once he notices a pattern, wants a name for it so he can stop re-deriving it every time, which is why I'm asking rather than just enjoying the observation.

So: does this have a name? If the nearest established concepts are Whig history, presentism, anachronism, and hindsight bias, I want to know exactly where each of them misses — presentism and anachronism seem to be about evaluation, judging the past by present standards, and hindsight bias about a psychological tendency to overestimate how predictable events were, neither of which is quite the same as a narrative whose proportions are set by its terminus. If nothing fits, coin something, and give me the definition plus the boundary cases I should test it against. Does a history of an institution that no longer exists escape it, or does the dead institution simply get replaced by whatever survived it as the new terminus? Is a decline narrative written as a warning still organized around the present, only negatively? And what would a genuinely non-terminus-centered history even look like — is it possible, or is the present always the only place from which one can write at all? I don't want a bare label; I want the carving.
