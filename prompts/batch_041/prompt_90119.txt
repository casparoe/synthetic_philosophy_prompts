I am in the lower sixth and I help run a small behavioural-economics discussion group at my school, largely because a close friend of mine, who is applying to read economics, is inordinately fond of the phrase "information cascade" and uses it to explain almost everything. Over the past term we have worked through a number of episodes together, and I have been quietly keeping a note of how he rules on each, because I suspect his usage is far less principled than he believes. On the café case — a queue forms outside a new place because the first few customers chose it for reasons of their own, and everyone afterwards joins the queue without any reason of their own, and the café turns out to be mediocre — he says, "textbook information cascade." On a school science fair where the judges voted in sequence, each of the later judges privately preferring a different project but deferring to those who had already voted, with the eventual winner turning out to be flawed, he again says "cascade." On a case where a crowd at a station all head for the same exit, but only because each of them has independently read the same sign, and they turn out to be right, he says, "no, that is not a cascade, that is just everyone having the same evidence." And on a passing fashion trend, where people copy one another for no reason beyond the copying itself and nothing is at stake, he says, "no, that is just fashion, not a cascade."

Next week the case is mine to choose, and before I commit to it I want to be able to predict his verdict rather than merely react to it, and to show him that he is running a rule he has never made explicit. My case is this: a group of doctors is diagnosing a patient in a teaching hospital, each of them forming, before any discussion, a private hunch that the illness is a rare condition; once the first two publicly commit to the more mundane diagnosis, each subsequent doctor suppresses their hunch and agrees with the mundane one; and as it happens the mundane diagnosis is correct, the rare condition not being present at all. I would like you to tell me, first, what he will say about whether this is an information cascade; second, which rules his earlier verdicts are actually consistent with — I have been toying with "a cascade is whenever people stop using their own information and follow the crowd", "a cascade requires the group to end up wrong", and "a cascade is any case in which later people are influenced by earlier people" — and which of those his earlier verdicts leave standing, which of them his new verdict would rule out, and whether the doctors in my case are even being irrational in the first place; third, which rule you think he is really running, and why you prefer that reading to the others; and fourth, what further case, as unlike mine as possible, would best tell the surviving rules apart. Please be candid about how confident you are in each of these claims, and single out the one point on which you are least sure. Write in British English.
