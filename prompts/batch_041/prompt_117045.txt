You are an expert both in AI safety and in the philosophy of action, and you are good at telling an equivocation apart from a real entailment. Think this through carefully before answering, and begin by restating, in your own words, what each step of the argument below is committed to, so that I can see the structure before you evaluate it.

I work on a team specifying a training signal for a decision-making agent: a penalty the agent incurs for changing the world in ways irrelevant to the objective it was given. The point of the signal is that we expect the objective to be misspecified, so we want a constraint that does not depend on our having gotten the objective right. I have argued in a memo that this is impossible.

(1) Any measure of side effects must compare what happens after the action with a baseline: the trajectory the world would have followed had the agent not acted, or had acted as little as possible.
(2) The baseline cannot be the trajectory that best satisfies the agent's objective. If it were, every departure from optimality would count as a side effect, and the notion would collapse into ordinary suboptimality.
(3) So the baseline must be characterized without reference to the objective.
(4) But characterizing a default trajectory requires deciding which features of the situation are the agent's doing and which are merely background it passed through: whether the agent's own continued operation counts as part of the baseline or as an effect, whether the resources it consumes and the options it forecloses for others are effects, whether changes it would have made even while idle are effects.
(5) Those decisions are not fixed by the causal facts. A causal graph is exactly as complete with a distinction between doing and allowing as without one.
(6) So they are fixed by what we care about, and every impact measure therefore presupposes a substantive specification of value.
(7) The reasoning generalizes. Every comparative action concept — harm, omission, means, foreseen but unintended, reversible, available alternative — needs a baseline, and every baseline is chosen rather than discovered.
(8) So there is no neutral description of an action at all, and the vocabulary in which anyone, including a consequentialist, says what an agent did is evaluative through and through. Notice that this cuts against the ordinary objective signal too: if describing an action requires values, so does stating an objective, and the argument does not single out impact measures.
(9) That cannot be right. I can describe a chess engine's move, a surgeon's incision, and the infection that followed the incision in one causal vocabulary, and I can say which of the two the surgeon produced without holding any view about whether the surgery was worth it.
(10) Therefore one of the steps above is wrong.

A colleague tells me the trouble is in (4): the baseline is simply the no-action counterfactual, and the counterfactual is read off the causal model rather than off our cares. I am not satisfied with that, because the model itself must be carved out of an indefinitely large set of variables, and I cannot see how that carving differs in kind from the carving any scientist does in modeling anything. I would sooner give up (9) than (1) through (3), which look definitional to me.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: if the argument fails only because its conclusion is far stronger than the one I need, what survives in the middle ground between the objective being all that matters and impact measures requiring a full theory of value, and what does that middle ground actually deliver to a team that cannot trust its own objective specification?
