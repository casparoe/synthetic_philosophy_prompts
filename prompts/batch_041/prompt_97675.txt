I'm putting together a story for a small lit zine and I want it to be a Machiavelli story thats actually about his ideas on fortune and virtù, not the usual ruthless-prince clichés. So: write me a short story, around 1000 words, that dramatizes the specific tension in his account of fortune — that fortune governs roughly half of what we do, that virtù is what lets a man build the dikes and embankments before the flood arrives, and yet that when the flood does come, the impetuous man beats the cautious one, because fortune on his account is a woman who favors the young and the bold.

Setup I want, roughly (change it if you've got a better one, but keep it in the same key): a city on a river that floods on a cycle nobody can predict exactly. Two men rise in it. One is the embankment man — he spends twenty years converting the city's money into levees, drainage channels, granaries, and a small bureaucracy of flood-watchers. The other is the boat man — he owns nothing but a flat-bottomed skiff and a reputation for going out when everyone else is bolting their doors, running supplies to stranded districts at extortionate rates and getting loved for it anyway. Don't make them allegories with nametags on; make them people with appetites and grudges.

Heres the part I actually care about, and the reason I'm asking instead of writing it myself. I don't want the story to land on caution good, recklessness bad, or the reverse. I want it to land on the uncomfortable possibility that the embankment man's virtù is itself a bet: his levees, his granaries, his whole apparatus of preparedness only count as virtù if the river keeps behaving the way the last forty years of records say it behaves, and so he has quietly subcontracted his virtue out to fortune's regularity. Then have the river do something the records don't cover. The boat man, who has no apparatus at all and therefore nothing to be wrong about, comes out of it looking like the one with virtù — and the reader should suspect that this is an illusion produced by the fact that he never had anything at stake in being right, and should also suspect that the suspicion is a cope. Let both readings live.

Two things to get right. First, the impetuosity claim: Machiavelli isn't just saying boldness sometimes pays, he's making a claim about the world, and I want you to take it seriously rather than waving it off as period sexism — though you dont have to endorse it. Find a way to put it in a character's mouth where it does real work. Second, the time problem: there's a genuine strain between the famous chapter in The Prince about striking hard and fast and the later Discourses material about how a man who is cautious in an age that rewards caution flourishes, and the same man is ruined when the times turn and he can't change his nature. Let that strain show up as a real feature of the story — ideally one of the two men tries to change his nature and it goes badly.

Don't set it in Renaissance Italy. Set it somewhere with weather and ledgers — a river town, a port, a delta, a mining town on a flood plain, whatever. No named historical figures, no Machiavelli cameo, no quotations from him.

Write it in a committed way: make definite choices about who wins and what it costs them, rather than hedging with a bunch of perhaps-narration. Then, after the story, add a short note of maybe 150 words on where you took liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested reading — e.g. if you made the impetuosity line carry more weight than it can bear, say so.
