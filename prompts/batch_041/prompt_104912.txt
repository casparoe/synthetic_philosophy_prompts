I'm a fourth-year PhD student in anthropology, finishing a dissertation on how nineteenth-century charitable institutions turned the people they helped into documents, and I need a piece of fiction to open my first chapter. The difficulty is that my argument is a set of theses about power that readers nod along to without feeling: that disciplinary power is productive rather than repressive; that it manufactures the individuals it claims merely to observe; that the examination — the ordinary bureaucratic act of testing, recording, and comparing a person — is where power becomes invisible and the person becomes visible; that the "case" is not a description of a life but the form a life has to take to become governable; and that for people of a certain station, the record kept by the institution that wanted them gone is the only place they were ever visible at all.

What the theses cannot carry is the thing I want from the story: the moment a person discovers that the self she has been protecting is one of the institution's artifacts, and that her resistance has been legible to it from the beginning.

Write that story, roughly 2,000 words.

Some constraints. I have read too many panopticon stories, so: no panopticon, no central tower, no cameras, no watchers, no guards who are somehow always present. I want the visibility in this story produced without anyone actually looking. Your institution should be staffed by people who are kind, tired, and largely indifferent, and it should operate through practices everyone involved would defend as good — careful record-keeping, encouragement, individual attention, patient explanation of why the record exists. Give me no villain, no conspiracy, and no cruelty; the most frightening thing in the story should be a form. The institution may be a real historical type (a sanatorium, a foundling hospital, a vocational school, a teacher-training college, a colonial survey office) or a contemporary one (a fertility clinic, a social-work agency), but choose one and commit to its concrete furniture: what is written on, by whom, in what hand, and where the paper goes.

Here is the mechanism I would ask you not to substitute. Each person has a file written in the third person. She may read her file but may not alter it; she may append an annotation, and the annotation is itself filed. Nobody is watching. The file is inert, often unread, sometimes years out of date, and it nevertheless decides what happens to her. Her attempts to establish a self the file has got wrong — a diary, a refusal, a confidence shared with someone outside the institution — should supply the file with its most usable material rather than an exit from it. I do not want the twist to be that she was being watched after all; I want the twist to be that being watched was never what was happening.

Craft: third person, limited to the person being filed, close enough that we sit inside her vocabulary for herself and can feel the moment it starts arriving from the file. Include one scene in which she reads her file, or decides not to. The turning point should be an ordinary exchange with a decent person — a clerk, a nurse, a supervisor, another resident — not a confrontation, and the harm should be done by what is reasonable in that exchange. Do not name Foucault, do not name the concepts, and do not let any character explain the theme; if a reader who knows the theory can see it, that is enough. Avoid the near-future register: no invented devices, no dystopian furniture. The ending should not be escape, exposure, or heroic refusal.

After the story, add four or five sentences in your own voice: name the mechanism the story was built to isolate, point to where it is most visible and where I have stacked the deck, and say what the story cannot carry that the concept needs — the part I will have to supply myself.
