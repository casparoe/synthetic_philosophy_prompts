I serve on a data safety monitoring board for multi-center clinical trials, and we meet quarterly to decide whether a trial should continue, stop early for efficacy, or stop for futility. Our decisions rest on interim analyses, and the hardest cases are those in which we can anticipate what the next interim analysis will do to our credence in the treatment effect: sometimes we can see that the next analysis will likely push us toward stopping, and we wonder whether we should already be acting on that anticipated shift, or whether waiting for the data is the more disciplined choice. I want a decision procedure, grounded in van Fraassen's reflection principle -- roughly, the principle that your present credence in a hypothesis, given that your future credence in it will be some specific value, should equal that value -- that I can actually run in a meeting. The procedure should give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a decision, and where it must hand the question to the board as a judgment call rather than a calculation. Then run it on two cases: one where the next interim analysis is expected to show a strong benefit, and one where the next analysis is expected to show futility but the current data are ambiguous. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do. Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
