I serve on a working group advising a national commission that is drafting a response to a historical wrong, and one dispute inside the group has been going in circles for more than a year. The question is what a state apology is actually worth. One faction holds that an apology without material repair is cheap talk: what the wronged need is a transfer of resources, and solemn words are at best a distraction and at worst a way of settling a claim on the cheap. The other faction holds that apology is a distinct act of recognition whose value cannot be reduced to any transfer, so that a state which pays but refuses to apologize has withheld something important rather than merely skipped a ceremony. Both sides accept the same historical record and the same evidence of continuing disadvantage; neither has budged.

The concrete flashpoint is a pair of draft resolutions. The first issues a formal apology and funds a monument but no payments. The second creates a payment fund whose text pointedly refuses to call the payments an apology or to attribute them to remorse, describing them instead as the settlement of a legal claim. Several members say the second is more just even though it is not an apology; others say it is an insult precisely because it is not one. The argument then recycles.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right. Is this a verbal dispute, in which the two sides attach different senses to the word apology and would converge once the senses were separated? Is it a substantive normative disagreement about what justice now requires? Is a hidden conceptual question doing the work — whether a state, being an artificial person, can have the remorse or regret that many take to be internal to apologizing at all? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one name: one asking what is owed to the wronged now, the other asking what it is to acknowledge a past wrong? I offer two observations as hypotheses to test rather than as settled points. First, the discussion keeps sliding between apologies to surviving victims and apologies to their descendants — people the state did not itself wrong and who are not the ones originally wronged. Second, it keeps sliding between apology as an act that looks backward, acknowledging what was done, and apology as an undertaking that looks forward, a commitment about future conduct. When someone proposes that these are two different things, the room briefly clarifies and then collapses back into the same argument.

Diagnose the disagreement using what each side in a dispute of this kind actually maintains, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a reformulated question, a conceptual or empirical result — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why. If you conclude the dispute is better described as two questions rather than one, say which question is worth answering and which should be dropped.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict: <label> -- <one-clause gloss>," where <label> is exactly one of: verbal, substantive-normative, conceptual, different-questions, or mixed.
