I am a software engineer on the clinical product team at a company that makes a mental-health self-assessment and triage app. A user answers a fixed questionnaire; our service assigns one of a small number of screening categories — for example, "likely depression," "likely anxiety," "elevated risk of self-harm" — and recommends a next step, ranging from self-guided material up to a human crisis line. The clinical lead signs off on the wording of each category and on the thresholds, but my team owns the questionnaire items, the scoring logic, the classifier, and the copy that explains a category to users and to the partner clinics that receive our referrals. I have no clinical or philosophical training, and neither does anyone else on the team.

Two experiences have convinced me that we need a standing rule rather than case-by-case judgment. First, three years ago we introduced a mild category intended to catch distress earlier. Neither the items nor the thresholds have changed since, but the share of users who meet that category has roughly doubled, and the clinical lead believes the category itself may have taught a subset of users a new vocabulary for describing ordinary unhappiness, which they then report back to us. Second, our relapse-risk model sends a supportive check-in message to flagged users. A retrospective look suggests that flagged users' self-reported symptoms rise in the weeks after the message, and we cannot tell whether the message detects a trajectory that was already there or contributes to it. In both cases, the thing we measured appears to have moved because we measured it.

I have read enough to recognize this as the family of problems Ian Hacking describes as looping effects: classifications of people, as opposed to classifications of minerals or electrons — "interactive" rather than "indifferent" kinds — can be taken up by the people classified, change how they understand and present themselves, change who shows up to be counted, and in some cases bring new ways of being into existence. The underlying view is sometimes called dynamic nominalism. I cannot pursue the literature myself and I do not want a reading list or a bibliography; I need operational content.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run. Requirements:

Trigger and budget. It runs whenever we propose adding, splitting, merging, retiring, or re-thresholding a category, or changing what a category's feedback says or does; and once per quarter as a standing review of categories already in production. It should cost one engineer about an hour plus a thirty-minute conversation with the clinical lead, who is the only person who can sign off, and it has to fit on two wiki pages.

Form. An ordered list of questions, and for each one: what counts as a sufficient answer, what data we need to answer it, what to do with each possible answer, where the procedure stops with a decision not to ship, and where it hands the question to the clinical lead because it is a question of values rather than facts — including what we must tell them so that they can decide well.

Conceptual content. Keep whatever in the looping-effects idea changes what an engineer should actually do, and drop whatever is of interest only to philosophers. In particular, at each step the procedure must distinguish among four possibilities and say how to tell them apart with data a small team can realistically obtain: (a) the category has changed the underlying condition; (b) it has changed only how people describe themselves to us, while the condition is unchanged; (c) it has changed who comes to us at all, so our user mix has shifted; (d) there is no loop, and we are seeing ordinary drift or an artifact of our own analysis. I expect these are not always separable in practice; where they are not, the procedure should say so rather than pretend otherwise.

Two complications it must handle. First, loops that run through institutions rather than self-conception: if a category now carries something concrete — a referral, an accommodation letter, insurance coverage, admission to a support community — people's reports may shift because the label buys them something, and the loop will look different from one driven by self-understanding. Second, semantic drift: a label we publish can change meaning as users adopt it into ordinary speech, so an item that was valid when we wrote it may decay without our touching it. The procedure should also make clear that a loop is not automatically a defect — some feedback is the point, if it helps people get help — so it must not collapse into "any feedback, do not ship."

Monitoring. Say what we must measure before release and after, over what window, and what we do if the loop appears: roll back, reword, keep but monitor, or something else, with an accountable person named for each branch. Assume we can run A/B tests for some changes but not for anything that withholds a crisis intervention, and that we have no ethics board and no ability to run long studies.

Then run the procedure on three proposals:

1. Adding "burnout" as its own category rather than routing those users into the depression screen.
2. Lowering the threshold on the anxiety screen by one response level, which would flag roughly fifteen percent more users. Growth wants this; the clinical lead is unsure.
3. Changing the relapse-risk check-in from a clinician-facing silent flag into a message shown directly to the user. My expectation is that this is the case where the procedure tells us something we will not want to hear, since the honest answer may be that we should either ship it only as a controlled experiment or not ship it at all, and both are expensive. If that is what the procedure implies, say so plainly and give me the least bad option.

For each case, give the verdict, the reasoning step by step, and the point where the procedure stops or defers. Close with a short list of what the procedure cannot do, and the questions it will answer with false confidence. Write the deliverable as a checklist with decision points, in the plain language of a software team, not as an essay.
