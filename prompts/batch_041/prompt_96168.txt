Hey, I'm in a course on the ethics of behavioural public policy and I've got a dialogue assignment due friday that I keep putting of. Could you write it for me? Two characters, both of whom are actually good at this: one designs nudges for a government behavioural insights team, the other thinks the whole enterprise is paternalism wearing a lab coat. Make them genuinely go back and forth — responding to each other's actual points, not one of them being the idiot who just says "but freedom" over and over.

Stuff from the course I want them to actually fight about, because otherwise I know it'll be 800 words of "manipulation!" vs "it's for your own good":

- Means vs. ends paternalism: you accept what someone is trying to do and just intervene in how they get there, versus deciding for them what they should be trying to do in the first place. My prof thinks a lot of nudges only look bad if you read them the second way. I want the critic to push on whether that line is really that clean, and whether "you still get to choose your ends" survives when the nudge is the reason you never form the end.

- The transparency and resistibility test: influence counts as a nudge rather than manipulation only if you could in principle notice it's being done to you and could cheaply opt out. The obvious counter is that a nudge that works is precisely one you don't notice, so the test might be doing no work at all. Let them go a few rounds on that instead of each just asserting it.

- Sludge — same toolkit, opposite direction: friction added to make cancelling hard, friction removed to make signing up easy. One of them should say the real question is never nudge or no nudge, it's who decides which way the friction points. And the critic should get to raise the minority-default problem: any default encodes somebody's preference, usually the majority's, and the person it doesn't suit is the one who pays.

- The bypass objection: even a nudge that leaves you better off might wrong you by working around your reasoning instead of through it. I'd like the nudger to have a decent answer to this, and the critic to concede the strong point on the other side — that some choice architecture is unavoidable, so "don't shape choices" isn't on the menu, only which shapes.

At the end, add a short note in your own voice (not the characters'): how confident are you in the positions you gave each side, and which single claim in the whole thing are you least sure about? Also flag it if any part leans on a contested reading of the ideas. I'd rather have that than a tidy verdict.
