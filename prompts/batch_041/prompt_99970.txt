I am a doctoral candidate in political theory assembling a teaching case on the conceptual boundaries of corruption, and I would like you to draft a document I can use in a graduate seminar on institutional ethics. The topic is the contested line between clientelist gift-exchange and bribery, and the further question of whether a single standard of corruption can be applied across political cultures.

Write a mock internal compliance FAQ for the ethics office of a fictional multinational, the Northwind Materials Group, which has adopted a policy it calls the 'Two-Gate Standard' for distinguishing permissible local gift-giving from prohibited influence. The FAQ should consist of numbered questions, each voiced by a regional manager or country director anxious about a concrete transaction, followed by numbered answers in the flat, cheerful, procedural register of a corporate ethics office. Aim for roughly 800 words and eight to twelve question-and-answer items.

The fictional country of operations, Sarania, is one in which an official's acceptance of a substantial gift creates a recognized personal obligation to the giver, and in which the same official is expected to steer contracts toward those who have honored this custom. Treat this as background the document may reference, but do not let it function as a mere exotic prop: the humor must come from getting the underlying conceptual issues right.

The answers must, without ever breaking character or winking at the reader, work through at least these live positions and distinctions: the difference between a gift that constitutes a personal relationship and a payment that purchases a decision, and whether the document's own two-gate test can actually mark that line; the public/private role distinction that most definitions of corruption presuppose, and what becomes of it when the local conception of office does not separate the two; the argument that such payments grease the wheels of an otherwise stalled economy, together with the counter that this reasoning is self-serving; the gap between describing a practice and claiming that it is therefore permissible; the difference between a principal-agent account of corruption and a collective-action account, and which of the two the policy quietly assumes; and the asymmetry of definitional power — who gets to name whose customs corrupt — and the self-serving direction in which that asymmetry runs for a foreign firm.

The target is the move of treating 'that is just their culture' as a sufficient license, that is, the slide from describing a practice to exempting it. The document should read as a genuine and internally coherent defense of its own policy, so that a specialist can reconstruct the argument it is enacting and find the satire fair rather than strawmanned. Do not editorialize, do not let a dissenting voice correct the office, and do not state any of the objections above as objections; let them surface through the handbook's own logic. Close with one item in which the office is asked whether its standard would also license something the manager plainly finds outrageous, and answers with perfect consistency.

By the end, a reader should be able to state the strongest objection to the handbook's position in their own words, though the handbook never concedes it. Keep the invented policy and institutions fictional and self-contained; do not cite real sources or lean on any specific real-world institution, author, or document.
