I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book set in a memory clinic, and I've got myself tangled up in something I assumed was just plot mechanics but is now eating my evenings. The setup: my protagonist, a woman in her early sixties, is diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's and signs one of those advance directives -- the kind where you lay out, while you're still yourself, what treatment you'll accept or refuse once you're further along. The clinic in my book runs what they call a continuity review: before they act on her directive, a small panel scores how much of her earlier narrative self is still present (they ask her to recount her life, they check whether her values and attachments have held, that sort of thing), and the directive only binds if she's judged to have fallen below a threshold. If she's still 'herself enough', they treat her as a competent person whose current wishes win. She sits right on the borderline, naturally, which is where my drama lives.

What I can't get straight is what the clinic's rule actually commits them to, philosophically. On one hand it looks like they believe the earlier self has authority over the later one -- that a person's settled, lucid preferences can bind a future self who'd repudiate them, which is more or less how we treat an ordinary living will. On the other, the continuity review only kicks in once she's changed, so they seem to think the later self is a different person, or a diminished one, whose wishes count for less. Those feel like two different pictures of what a person is across time and I'm not sure they sit together. It gets worse when I bring in the other things the same clinic does: they'll set the directive aside if she seems content and keeps asking, calmly and repeatedly, for a treatment she'd refused on paper -- yet they'd never dream of ignoring a competent adult's refusal of, say, an amputation. Then there's her son holding a proxy, and the awkward fact that in real life people can't sign away certain things no matter how clearly they consent.

Could you unpack what this practice presupposes about personal identity and the authority of the earlier self? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed -- what view of the self over time, of autonomy, and of precedent-setting it only makes sense under; which rival views it quietly rules out; whether the different bits of the rule presuppose the same thing or pull in opposite directions; and where the rule is genuinely underdetermined, in the sense that several theories of the self would license it just as well. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped the underlying view for the main alternative -- say, for one that treats the later self as fully authoritative, or one that says there's no fact of the matter about sameness at all. Please write it in British English, and don't bother with reading lists or citations -- I want the argument, not the bibliography. I should add that I've never studied philosophy formally, so if a technical term is unavoidable, just say plainly what work it's doing.
