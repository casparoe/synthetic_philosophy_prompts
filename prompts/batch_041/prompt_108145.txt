I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I have become preoccupied with the problem of choosing between rival models when the data are finite and the models are not nested. I have a half-formed distinction that I want you to take seriously, because it seems to me to capture something that the standard apparatus of Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor tends to miss. Call it the distinction between observational compressibility and interventional compressibility. A model has high observational compressibility when it shortens the description of the data. It has high interventional compressibility when it shortens the description of the set of interventions that would change the system, or of the experiments one would have to run to distinguish it from its rivals. My intuition, from decades in the laboratory, is that the second kind of compression is often what we actually care about, but the formal literature tends to reward the first. I do not need the formal definitions rehearsed; I need the translation into practice.

Please turn this distinction, together with the standard theory, into a decision procedure that a research group can actually run when it meets to choose between rival models. The group meets monthly, has ninety minutes, and must produce a recommendation for the head of the laboratory, who has the final say. The members are competent scientists but not specialists in algorithmic information theory. The procedure should be a checklist or a flowchart in words, with numbered steps, explicit stopping conditions, and should recognise which steps are objective calculations and which are judgements that cannot be eliminated. It should say how to approximate the ideal quantities with a practical proxy such as minimum description length, and where that approximation breaks. It should not pretend that the invariance theorem rescues us from the choice of reference machine: the additive constant can swamp the comparison when the data are finite. It should also say what to do when no one can agree on a reference language or compressor, and when the procedure should return a tie or a recommendation to gather more data rather than a winner.

Apply the procedure to two cases. First, a pendulum experiment: model A is a simple deterministic equation with one adjustable parameter; model B is a more complex equation with two adjustable parameters and a stochastic noise term, and it fits the data slightly better. Second, a biological network: model C is a simple network with one free parameter tuned to the data; model D is a complex network whose parameters are fixed by theory and which fits the data less well but predicts the effects of a novel intervention more accurately. Then analyse a third, shorter case where the procedure forces the group to prefer a model that they find ugly or unintuitive, and say how the procedure should handle their discomfort.

Finally, be honest about the limits. What can this procedure not do? Where does the distinction between observational and interventional compressibility break down? Where is it merely dressing up a prior preference for theories that make risky predictions? And what should the group do when the procedure's recommendation conflicts with the judgement of the head of the laboratory?
