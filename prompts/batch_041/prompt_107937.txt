I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on judgment and decision-making, and I want to use the Allied decision to launch the Normandy invasion on 6 June 1944 — the weather gamble, the single one-day postponement, the tide and moonlight window that made further delay nearly impossible — as a test case for satisficing and bounded rationality. I already have the narrative account; what I need is a disciplined mapping: what played the role of the aspiration level and how it moved as the window closed, what the search over alternatives actually consisted in (go on the 5th, go on the 6th, wait, or take the next usable window weeks later under worse tidal conditions), and whether the eventual choice is better described as the first option meeting a threshold or as maximization under a hard constraint. Then the reverse test, which is the part I care about. If the episode can be redescribed as optimizing subject to search costs, a deadline, and a coarse option set, does satisficing retain any distinctive content — and what would the ecological-rationality rejoinder, that a simple stopping rule can outperform optimization in the right environment, have to establish here to make the distinction more than verbal? I'm skeptical that the framework is doing real work, so don't assume it is vindicated: tell me plainly which features of the episode it illuminates, which it has to explain away, and whether the case is evidence for the theory or against it.
