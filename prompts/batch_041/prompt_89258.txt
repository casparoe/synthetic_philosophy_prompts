I serve on the board of a small housing cooperative — seven of us, elected by the membership — and for several months I have been trying to understand a problem that keeps surfacing in our meetings, one I suspect is not really about housing at all. I would like you to write an essay that helps me think it through and that actually lands somewhere, rather than cataloguing the options and shrugging.

The setup is this. A proposal comes to the board and can be justified only if two things hold: that the projected shortfall is real, and that our reserve fund can absorb it. To adopt the proposal is to accept both. When we voted, a majority of us judged the shortfall real; a majority judged the reserve sufficient; and yet a majority voted against adopting the proposal, because the two majorities were not made up of the same people. So the board, taken as a whole, seems to hold that both conditions are met and also that the proposal should not be adopted. I cannot make that add up.

I have found two ways of responding, and they give opposite answers. One is to count our votes on the two underlying questions and let the board's position on the proposal follow from them. The other is to count our votes on the proposal itself and let the underlying questions fall where they may. I cannot see how to choose between these without already assuming which one is right.

Two further wrinkles have made this harder rather than easier. First, each method seems to carry a different claim to authority: the first is defended as more likely to get the answer right, the second as more faithful to what each of us actually decided. Second, our board recently had to vote on whether one of our own members should be recused for a conflict of interest, and it struck me that the composition of the body doing the deciding can itself depend on which method we use — the question of whose vote counts gets settled by a vote that may or may not include the very person in question.

Please write an essay that weighs the arguments on each side, gives an honest account of the strongest case against whatever you conclude, and defends a definite verdict: which method the board should adopt, what it should do when the two diverge, and whether it even makes sense to speak of the board having a view of its own as opposed to seven views that happen to be counted up.
