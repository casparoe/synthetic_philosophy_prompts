I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am working up an essay about a discrepancy in how the law treats animals — one I keep failing to describe without smuggling in a philosophy I cannot defend. Here is the pattern. Animal-welfare statutes in most countries take vertebrates as their protected class, and a few legislatures have lately extended protection to octopuses, squid, and the larger crustaceans, doing so on the strength of evidence about pain: nociceptors, centralized processing, the capacity to trade off injury against reward, behavior that changes under analgesics. Farmed insects, now produced at industrial scale for protein and animal feed, remain outside all of it. There are rules about feed, disease, and hygiene, and almost nothing about how the animals are reared, crowded, transported, or killed. Ethics review for research stops at the same line. Yet the same states fund the protection of insect populations, and the pest-control industry kills insects in numbers that dwarf every other category of animal death on earth, and in neither case does anyone raise a welfare question. Meanwhile a small research community has started arguing that even if the probability that insects are sentient is low, the number of individuals is so large that the expected quantity of suffering is enormous, and the low probability stops being a reason for indifference.

I want you to treat this as a question about what the practice presupposes, not about what the science settles. Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral status that the regulatory line encodes — the theory that admits the octopus and excludes the cricket — and then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Four things interest me in particular.

First, the threshold. The practice seems to run on the thought that a creature has either crossed into moral standing or it has not, and that in the absence of convincing evidence we are entitled to act as though it has not. That is a rule about evidence and burden of proof, and it sits awkwardly with the arithmetic of the scale argument, where probability and number trade off against one another. Does the practice really need a threshold, or can the same outcomes be reconstructed in expected-value terms?

Second, the unit. The scale argument counts individuals; the regulation counts kilograms, hectares, and colonies. What does the practice presuppose about the unit of moral concern, and is that presupposition compatible with the individualist premise the scale argument depends on?

Third, the asymmetry between suffering and death. Welfare law is mostly about how animals are treated while they are alive, and killing them for a legitimate purpose is ordinarily permitted. What does that presuppose about whether death is itself a harm — and would an insect-welfare regime inherit that presupposition, or would it have to break with it?

Fourth, the taxonomy. The law sorts animals by biological classification rather than by capacity, which is an odd thing for a capacity-based theory to do; it looks like a coarse proxy standing in for a fine-grained property. Say where that proxy is defensible and where it is arbitrary.

Finish by telling me how much of the pattern is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories would license exactly the same statutes and habits — and then what would actually have to change if we took the scale argument seriously. I do not want a list of principles to admire. I want to know which rules, standards, and institutional habits would have to be rewritten, and which would survive untouched because the scale argument, whatever it implies about expected suffering, says nothing about how a regulator should act under uncertainty.
