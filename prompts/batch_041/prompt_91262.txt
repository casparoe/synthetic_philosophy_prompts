I'm a teaching assistant for a mid-level undergraduate course in metaphysics, and I'm currently grading the midterm. One of the questions asked students to work through the counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions, and I'd like your help with one answer in particular. The question was:

"Question 3 (25 points). The simple conditional analysis of dispositions says that an object is disposed to manifest M in stimulus conditions S just in case it would manifest M if it were in S. Explain how finkish cases and masking cases are supposed to threaten this analysis. In your answer, distinguish finks from masks, and assess whether the distinction can be sustained. Finally, briefly explain how one prominent defender of the conditional analysis responds to the finkish cases, and whether that response extends to masks."

The student's answer reads:

"The simple conditional analysis (SCA) holds that 'x is disposed to M when S' is equivalent to 'if x were in S, x would M.' The analysis faces two kinds of counterexample: finks and masks.

Finks were introduced by C.B. Martin. In the electro-fink case, a wire is live and so disposed to conduct electricity when touched by a conductor. But a machine is attached that detects the touch and immediately cuts the current. So if the wire were touched, it would not conduct. Yet intuitively the wire is still disposed to conduct; the machine is an external interference, not a loss of the disposition. This shows that the disposition can be present even when the corresponding conditional is false, so the SCA fails in the left-to-right direction.

Masks, discussed by Mark Johnston, are similar but involve a different mechanism. A fragile glass wrapped in packing material is still fragile, but if struck it would not break, because the packing absorbs the impact. Here the disposition is present but the manifestation is blocked. The mask does not remove the disposition; it merely prevents it from manifesting. So masks also refute the SCA's left-to-right direction.

However, I think the distinction between finks and masks is not as sharp as it is often made out to be. In both cases, something external to the object prevents the manifestation from occurring when the stimulus is applied. In the electro-fink case, the machine cuts the current; in the masking case, the packing absorbs the impact. In both cases, the object's intrinsic properties are altered by the interfering factor: the wire's current is cut, and the glass's surface is cushioned. So the two cases are really species of the same phenomenon, and the SCA fails for the same reason in both.

As for the defender of the SCA, David Lewis responds to the finkish cases by abandoning the simple conditional analysis. He argues that dispositions are categorical bases and cannot be analyzed in terms of conditionals at all. He therefore replaces the SCA with a view on which dispositions are identical to their physical bases, which avoids the finkish counterexamples. But this response does not help with masks, because masks show that even a categorical basis can fail to manifest when the stimulus is present."
