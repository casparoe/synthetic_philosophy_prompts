A friend of mine, Jonah, spent most of last winter trying to talk our mutual friend Marcus into taking a job in the city where Jonah lives. I was present for most of it. Afterward, two other people who had watched the whole thing — Priya and Dev — got into an argument about whether Jonah had manipulated Marcus, and I stayed out of it because I honestly couldn't referee it. I read a lot of rationality blogs and forums, so the reason-versus-cause distinction is the sort of thing I'm used to seeing invoked; I suspect it is doing quiet work in this argument without either of them naming it, and I'd like you to say whether that's the crux or a distraction.

What Jonah actually did, as best I can reconstruct it: every proposition he asserted was, so far as I can tell, true. He also (1) made the pitch late in the evening, twice, after Marcus had been drinking and had said he was lonely; (2) left out the two countervailing considerations Marcus had raised himself the week before, on the grounds that Marcus already knew them; (3) mirrored Marcus's own vocabulary back at him — Marcus says he wants to be someone who finishes things, and Jonah framed the move as finishing something; (4) tied the decision to Marcus's self-image, arguing that the person who plays it safe is not who Marcus has been trying to become; and (5) opened with a disclaimer that he was obviously biased, wanted Marcus in the city, and should be discounted accordingly. Marcus took the job. He told me later that he was glad, and that he would have talked himself out of it without the push.

Their exchange went roughly like this. Priya: this was manipulation. The tell isn't falsity but that Jonah chose the timing, the omission, the vocabulary, and the identity hook because they move Marcus, not because they bear on whether the job is a good idea; he could have run the mirror-image pitch for the opposite conclusion with the same fluency. Dev: by that standard almost nothing counts as persuasion — selection and framing are what every advocate does, the identity appeal is a reason if it's true of Marcus, and it is, and salience is an epistemic good rather than a hack. Priya: the omission is the clearest case; the two things left out were exactly the ones bearing on the merits. Dev: Marcus already had them, and nobody is obliged to argue the other side of a decision he has been asked about. Priya: transparency isn't a switch — knowing Jonah wants something doesn't undo anchoring or self-image hooks, and the disclaimer functions as a permission slip rather than a shield; also, Dev's implicit test would exonerate a fluent liar who happens to use true premises while convicting a clumsy friend who argues badly out of love. Dev: Priya's test would convict every teacher, editor, and good explainer, and it makes the outcome irrelevant — yet Marcus endorses the whole thing in retrospect, with full knowledge of what was done, which looks like consent. Priya: consent to what? He was never asked to consent to the method; he was asked to take a job.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the four rounds, and within them the specific claims, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; then say who is right about what, then what both of them are missing. I would rather you settle the case than hand me a general theory of manipulation in place of a verdict, though I do want to know which general distinctions actually survive contact with it. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each part of the verdict, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
