I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I've hit a snag that's turned out to be more philosophical than I expected. The book is set mostly inside a mid-sized medical school's admissions office — my protagonist is the associate dean who runs the committee — and I've got a dinner party scene where her sister-in-law, a biotech exec, explains why she's against any kind of quota or set-aside in admissions or hiring. Her line is that the real problem isn't the finish line, it's the pipeline: girls and minority kids get filtered out of the science track in middle school, again in college, again at the first job, so by the time anybody is admitting or hiring, the pool is already whatever it is. You don't fix a leaky pipe by dumping extra water in at the far end, she says, you patch the pipe. I've heared a version of this for twenty years and so has my dean, and I want the scene to earn its place instead of being two people trading bumper stickers. So: how far does the analogy actually hold?

Can you give me the correspondence table — what plays the pipe, the leaks, the fluid, the far end, the extra water — and then tell me where it breaks? I'm especially interested in what the picture smuggles in that isn't true: that people are fungible fluid, that the flow is the only thing setting the proportions at the end, that the end of the pipe isn't itself a place where people get lost or picked (my dean would say the last two feet of pipe are where all the interesting stuff happens). And there's a second metaphor she leans on in the same breath — the starting line one, level playing field, some runners start way back — which seems to put the injustice in a totally different place than the leaky pipe does. Are those two pictures even compatible with each other? Tell me what conclusion the pipeline picture can actually support on its own, if any, and whether there's a better metaphor for what she's really getting at, one that doesn't quietly decide the argument for her before it starts. And if you can, give me a version of this I can steal lines from.
