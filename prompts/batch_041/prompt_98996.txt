I retired last year after thirty-odd years in marine biology — benthic ecology, mostly, counting things in mud — and I've been using the free time to work through the sort of books I never had the head-space for when I was running a lab. Just now it's Nozick, and I keep catching on the same snag. Everyone talks about the Wilt Chamberlain case, and I can follow that well enough: start from a distribution you're happy to call just, let people freely pay to watch a man play basketball, and before long the pattern you liked has been shoved sideways by nothing but voluntary choices. Fine — liberty upsets patterns, I get it. But the move I actually want to put under the microscope is the other one, where he says that taxing the wages a person earns by working is really no different from making that person work for somebody else at gunpoint, that taking the fruit of my labour to fund whatever the state fancies is, morally speaking, the same kind of act as forcing me to toil for another's ends. He leans on that to get to the conclusion that redistributive taxation isn't a question of policy at all but a violation of rights. My friend, who teaches the stuff and has been very patient with me, says the analogy is carrying the whole argument, and that if it gives way the conclusion goes down with it.

So would you audit it for me? I'd like the correspondences spelled out plainly — what is meant to map onto what: the labour, the earnings, the tax, the gunman, the person who benefits — and then a straight account of where the mapping strains and where it actually breaks. What I really want to know is which of the dissanalogies bear on the conclusion he's using the thing to support and which are just noise, because I suspect the ones that bother me most (the gunman is a person with intentions, the taxpayer gets services back, the state is our own democratic state) are exactly the ones a clever defender will wave away as beside the point. And a colleague put a twist in my head that I can't shake: she said the comparison only bites when the tax money goes to ends you don't share, and that if it funds things you'd have bought anyway it's nearer a club subscription than a mugging — I can't tell whether that rescues him or wrecks him. So: give me your verdict on whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was the analogy doing the work all along. And if there's a better comparison that would do the same job, tell me what it is and what it would and wouldn't buy. No reading list needed, by the way — I've got the book, I just can't see my way through this bit.
