Last term a colleague taught Bell's theorem as a lecture and half the class left thinking it says that everything is connected; this term I want to teach the same material as something the students do with their hands, in one 90-minute session of a second-year undergraduate course on the foundations of quantum mechanics. There are about twenty students, the tables move, and I have the session plus whatever of the break people will surrender. What I want from you is a runnable activity — rules, materials, roles, and a scoring scheme that needs no apparatus, no money and no real quantum optics — built around teams of device builders. Each pair of devices is built from a sealed instruction card written before any settings are chosen: the card tells each wing which outcome, +1 or −1, to produce for each setting it might be asked about, and by construction no entry on one wing's card may be indexed by the setting chosen at the other wing. Teams should play their cards out with the settings drawn at random after sealing, so that the four correlations can be computed from the record, and then the whole class should be able to see, by enumerating the possible cards rather than by being told the inequality, that there is a ceiling no card can beat — I want the small shock of the ceiling being found from the inside, before it is named. Only then does the quantum box run, from my laptop or from printed random-digit tables, emitting pairs of outcomes at four settings spaced by 45 degrees and chosen so that it attains the maximum the cards cannot reach: I have in mind 0 and 90 degrees on one wing against 45 and 135 on the other, with the singlet correlations, i.e. minus the cosine of the angle between the two settings. You will need to fix the sign convention in the four-term CHSH sum that real Bell tests compute so that the cards top out at 2 and the box reaches 2√2 ≈ 2.83, and to state that convention explicitly for whoever runs the session. The box must also be built so that each wing on its own is an even coin whatever the other wing's setting is — for the implementation it is enough that the two outcomes are each ±1 equiprobably and agree with probability sin²(Δ/2) — and the class should be able to check this for themselves. Give me the scoring sheet, the round structure, the time budget and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the theorem: what the card encodes, what "local" was assumed when I forbade cross-indexing, why randomising a card cannot help (the mixture argument, which is the hole they will try to crawl through), and why the box's flat marginals mean it could not have been used to send a message even though it beats the ceiling.

Two things I want handled explicitly and without hedging. First, the debrief has to separate the two assumptions that get bundled together under "locality" — that the setting on one wing does not affect the outcome on the other, and that the outcome on one wing does not affect the outcome on the other — and say which of them the activity actually puts under pressure and which it quietly assumes. Second, I want an honest statement of what this activity cannot reproduce: that it establishes only that no pre-arranged cards match the box, not that nature violates the inequality, since the box is stipulated and the value 2√2 is handed to the students rather than derived; that it says nothing about the detection-efficiency and locality loopholes of real experiments; that a determined sceptic can escape by denying that the settings were independent of the hidden state, and that the activity has no answer to this; and that the interpretive question — nonlocality, no definite outcomes, retrocausality — is left wide open, because the box supplies statistics and nothing else. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I expect teams to smuggle communication between the wings into their cards without noticing, to spend the whole first half perfecting a single card and never reach the enumeration, and to protest that the box is cheating. Give me the enforcement mechanisms (sealed envelopes, a referee, hard time-boxing) and the way to turn each of those failures into a moment in the debrief; a plan for the group that finishes early; and a note on how many rounds of data are needed before the sampled correlations are stable enough to put on the board.
