I am a high school student, and I am writing a poem for the philosophy-and-science issue of my school's literary magazine. I would like you to help me draft it. Please take on the role of a poet who is also thoroughly at home in the foundations of quantum mechanics, and please think the problem through carefully before writing any verse: begin by setting out, in a few sentences, what separates the view that the wavefunction is a genuine physical thing in the world from the view that it is only a compact summary of what someone knows, then sketch the images you intend to use in each half of the poem and check that each image is equally available to both readings.

The poem should be in two halves of eight lines each, with the same rhyme scheme and, so far as the sense allows, the same rhyme words in the same order, so that the second half reads as the first half seen from the other side. In the first half, write as though the wavefunction were an object — something with a shape, something that can spread out and interfere with itself. In the second half, write as though it were only knowledge — a ledger, a summary, a way of keeping score — while making it clear that this is not the same as saying that nothing is real or that the world is made of minds: the second reading still leaves a world, just not a wavefunction-shaped one. Do not name either position in the verse itself, and do not use the words ontic, epistemic, realist, or instrumentalist; a reader who knows the debate should be able to tell which half is which from the images alone.

Both halves should be anchored to the same small concrete occasion — the pattern on a screen after many particles have passed through, a single detector click, the plain fact that anyone who looks gets a definite outcome — since that is the ground the two readings agree about, and the disagreement is entirely about what lies between the clicks. The poem should end without adjudicating: it should leave the two readings standing side by side, and should not imply that one has been refuted or that the whole question is merely verbal.

A few requirements of accuracy, since a physics teacher will read this. The poem must not suggest that the second reading denies the reality of the world, nor that the first commits one to a particle-like object sitting somewhere in particular; and it must respect that the two readings are readings of the same formalism with the same predictions, so that no experiment described in the poem could by itself settle which is right. In your outline — not in the poem — you may note that certain no-go results bear on how easily the second reading can be held consistently, but the verse itself should stay neutral. If any image you choose would smuggle in a commitment belonging to only one side, revise it before you write.

Please give me a short outline first, then the poem, and then two or three sentences explaining which choices in the poem are doing the work of keeping the two halves honest.
