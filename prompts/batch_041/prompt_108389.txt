I keep meeting a move in arguments that I cannot name, and I would like either to be told its name or to be convinced that it has none. I am a high-school student, so it is likely that I am missing something obvious, but the standard corrections I have read do not reach what bothers me.

The move has two parts. First, someone identifies a task that would have to be done if a practice were carried out properly — finding the uniquely best option, checking every implication of a rule, drafting a contract that anticipates every contingency — and observes that this task is one of those for which no efficient method is known, because the number of cases grows faster than any feasible amount of time can accommodate. (I have seen the label NP-hard attached to problems of this kind; I do not think the label is the point.) Second, they conclude something about the practice: that it is not a lapse or a shortcut but an adaptation, or, more strongly, that nobody can be held to the standard at all, since a standard that cannot be met cannot be a requirement.

Two instances I have actually met. An essay argued that the vagueness of legal rules is not a defect to be regretted but a response to the fact that a rule giving the right outcome for every possible situation could never be drafted or checked in advance, so a legal system that tried to be complete would be unusable. And in a debate about how a school district should divide its budget, someone said that because there is no feasible way to compute the allocation that would do the most good overall, the demand that the district act on that allocation is empty — nobody can be required to do what cannot be computed.

I already know the standard reply, and it does not reach my question. I understand that these results concern the worst case rather than the typical one, that a single instance may be easy even when the problem as a whole is hard, and that a good-enough answer may be computable even when the perfect one is not. What interests me is the shape of the reasoning that survives all of that. Even granting every qualification, the hardness claim is still doing two things at once: explaining why a practice has the form it has, and releasing us from any obligation to do better. Are these two separate moves that happen to share a premise, one move with two faces, or does the second actually follow from the first? It also worries me that the move is available for almost any practice — for almost any way of doing things, one can find a more demanding standard that would be infeasible to meet — and an argument that can be run for everything seems to establish nothing.

So: is there an established name for this? If there is, I want to know precisely what it covers and how it differs from the nearest things I can think of — the ordinary confusion of worst case with typical case, which I take to be narrower; bounded rationality; and the general warning against deriving an ought from an is. If there is no name, I would like you to supply one, define it, and mark its edges: whether a hardness claim can ever by itself justify a practice rather than merely explain it, and what else an argument of this kind needs before it can do the justifying.
