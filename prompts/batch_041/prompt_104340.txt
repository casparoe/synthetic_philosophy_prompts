I am a novelist, and this term I am co-teaching an advanced seminar on historical fiction with a colleague from the philosophy department. The seminar alternates between craft workshops and lectures on the philosophy of history; the final examination was written by my colleague, and because we divided the grading, the philosophy essays have come to me. I am not a philosopher, and my colleague will read my comments before the grades are released, so I need an assessment I can defend line by line rather than a general impression. I also have a professional stake in the question, since it bears on something I do for a living: whether arranging events into a story is itself a way of explaining them, or only a way of presenting an explanation that someone else has already supplied.

The examination question was this:

Hempel held that to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected, given general laws together with statements of particular conditions, and he granted that historians seldom state the laws their explanations depend on, so that most historical explanations are at best explanation sketches. His critics have replied along two quite different lines. Some, following Dray, have argued that explaining an action typically consists in showing that the action was reasonable or appropriate given the agent's beliefs and purposes, and that this rational explanation invokes no law. Others have argued that narrative itself explains: that a well-constructed account of how events unfolded is an explanation, and that its explanatory force depends on features — the ordering of events, the selection of what to include, the description of earlier events in terms that became available only later — which a covering-law reconstruction would discard.

Consider the following description, offered by a historian: that a riot in a provincial capital in the 1840s was the first outbreak of a revolution that would take a decade to run its course. The description is true, but no one present at the riot could have described it that way, since the revolution's duration was not yet settled.

Write an essay that (a) says whether this sort of retrospective description poses a problem for the covering-law model, for the narrative account of explanation, or for neither, and why; and (b) assesses whether rational explanation and narrative explanation are genuine alternatives to covering-law explanation or can be accommodated by it.

One student's answer is reproduced below. Please grade it: a letter grade, and then the specific comments that justify it, keyed to particular sentences or moves in the answer. Separate what the answer gets right from what it gets wrong, and separate errors of exposition from substantive ones. Where the answer goes wrong, state what the correct treatment would be, in terms I could defend to a philosopher colleague who will scrutinize every comment. Say also what would have to change for the answer to earn the next grade up. The prose is confident and fluent; please do not let fluency raise the grade, and do not soften the assessment out of politeness. If the answer is mixed in a way that a hasty reader would miss, I especially want that identified.

Student answer:

The covering-law model, as Hempel presents it, holds that an event is explained when it is shown to follow deductively from general laws together with statements of initial conditions. Hempel concedes that historians rarely state the laws their explanations rely on, and he calls such incomplete explanations explanation sketches — which seems to me an admission that, on his own account, most historical writing does not really explain anything.

The retrospective description is a serious problem for the covering-law model. If a historian explains the riot by deriving it from laws and antecedent conditions, then everything in the explanans must be available to anyone who is to follow the explanation. But the description of the riot as the first outbreak of a revolution that would take a decade to run its course could not have been formulated at the time, because the revolution's duration was not yet settled. The covering-law model therefore cannot accommodate this description, since what does the explanatory work is information that did not exist when the event occurred. This is really the same point that the symmetry thesis makes: Hempel holds that an explanation is a prediction that has been borne out, so if we could not have predicted the riot under that description, we cannot be said to have explained it under that description either.

For narrative explanation, the retrospective description is not a problem but the point. Narrative is necessarily written from the standpoint of the outcome, and a narrative that could have been written by a contemporary would not be a narrative at all. The historian's selection of which events to include, and the order in which they are placed, are what allow the reader to see how the outcome came about.

Dray's rational explanation is a genuine alternative to the covering-law model. On Dray's account, we explain an action by showing that, given the agent's beliefs and desires, the action was the only reasonable thing to do; this makes the action intelligible without subsuming it under any law. Dray's position is continuous with Collingwood's, since on both views the historian re-enacts the agent's thought. And because reasons are not causes in the covering-law sense, no amount of law-stating will turn a rational explanation into a covering-law explanation.

Narrative explanation is likewise a genuine alternative. A narrative explains by showing how a series of events made the outcome probable: the historian arranges the events so that the reader sees that, given what came before, the outcome was to be expected. This is not the same as deriving the outcome from a law, because the historian states no law and the connections traced are particular rather than general. The covering-law model, by contrast, requires causal laws, and it is precisely the absence of such laws from historical writing that shows the model to be the wrong ideal for history.
