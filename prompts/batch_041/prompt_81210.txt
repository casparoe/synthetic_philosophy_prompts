Next month I'm presenting at a small workshop on democratic theory — half political theorists, half people who do judgment aggregation with actual math — and I need a Q&A prep sheet, badly, because I've been working on this position alot in a bubble and I genuinely don't know where it's weakest. Here's the thesis I'm defending. The Condorcet jury theorem is usually put as: if every voter is independently more likely than not to get the answer right, then majority rule's probability of being right tends to one as the electorate grows. I want to argue that both of those standard assumptions cut at the wrong joints. The condition that actually does the work, I claim, is something I'm calling residual informativeness: each voter's vote has to carry some information about the truth that isn't already contained in the other votes — formally, it has to shift the conditional probability of the state given everyone else's votes. Once you state the theorem that way, I claim three things follow: (a) the independence assumption stops being a substantive requirement and becomes bookkeeping; (b) majority rule still converges under conditions much weaker than "everyone is uniformly better than a coin flip"; and (c) the usual panic about correlated errors from shared media and herding is mostly misplaced, since what matters isn't correlation as such but whether the marginal voter still carries any information. I'm fairly confident about (a) and (b), and I have a nagging feeling (c) is where I'll get killed.
