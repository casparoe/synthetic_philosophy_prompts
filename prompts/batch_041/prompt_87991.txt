Write a dialogue between two people arguing about what life actually is. One of them is a metabolism-first type — the view that being alive just means being a self-maintaining, self-reproducing chemical system that keeps itself going (the autopoiesis / chemoton kind of picture). The other is an evolution-first type — the view that being alive means being part of a population that undergoes natural selection and adapts, which is roughly the criterion you see floating around astrobiology. Neither one should be a strawman, and they have to actually answer each other instead of just restating their own definition on loop.

Have them grind through the usual borderline cases and see where each definition really lands: viruses, viroids (those naked little RNA circles that infect plants), prions, and the mitochondria inside your own cells, which started out as free-living bacteria and now can't make it on their own. I want them to hit the spots where their gut says one thing and their stated definition says another.

Then have them fight over two cases I'm making up: (1) a synthetic thing — a lipid bubble stuffed with a self-sustaining reaction network that grows and pinches off daughter bubbles, except it copies itself so faithfully that it never mutates, so it can't ever evolve; and (2) a population of digital organisms in a computer sim that compete, mutate, inherit, and visibly adapt over thousands of generations, but are obviously just software with no chemistry and no metabolism. Make each one commit to which case is alive and which isn't, and then live with the awkward consequence. Does the evolution person really have to call my perfect non-evolving bubble dead? Does the metabolism person have to call the adapting software dead? Let them push on each other — one of them might bring up the mule, since a sterile individual is still definately alive, so does the evolution criterion even apply to individuals or only to populations?

Toward the end, let them argue about whether life is even a natural kind — whether there's a fact of the matter we're discovering or whether we're just drawing a line for a purpose, and if it's for a purpose, whose (astrobiologists hunting for life on Europa, origin-of-life people trying to explain the transition, doctors deciding when someone has died). Let that disagreement be real too. Dont force a happy synthesis at the end.
