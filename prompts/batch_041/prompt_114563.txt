I've been lurking around adversarial-collaboration stuff since the old LessWrong days and I want to try running one on a fight that keeps breaking out in my book club: are performative utterances — "I promise", "I resign", "I christen this boat the Whatever" — a genuinely distinct kind of speech act, one whose identity is constituted by conventions and felicity conditions, or is that whole category an illusion produced by a special class of verbs? Call the two camps the institutionalists (force is constituted by conventional, often social-institutional, rules; the felicity conditions aren't a side constraint on the act, they *are* the act's identity, and where there's no shared practice there's no act) and the intentionalists (at bottom all that's happening is a speaker expressing a commitment and an audience recognising it; conventions are enabling shortcuts for recognition, not constituents, and the class of "hereby"-capable verbs is a linguistic artifact rather than a real cut in nature). What I want is the joint statement these two could actually sign after a real, good-faith adversarial collaboration — not a transcript, not a verdict on who wins, but the document: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can put their names to, including about the cases below and about what turns on which camp being right; (2) a disagreements table where the residual disagreements are stated twice, once in wording the institutionalist would accept as their own view and once in wording the intentionalist would, plus for each what would move that side and what its pre-registered prediction is; and (3) the cruxes underneath those disagreements, each with the case, argument, or mini-test both sides agree bears on it.

To stop it from collapsing into a textbook recitation, I've made up a little battery and I want it used — add to it if you like, but these have to show up and be scored by both sides. The cases: (a) a castaway, alone, promises his dead brother he'll stop drinking — promise, private resolve in promise-shaped clothing, or nothing at all? (b) someone types "I resign" into a draft they never send; (c) a bet struck with what turns out to be a hallucination, or with a chatbot that has nothing to lose; (d) an insincere promise, where the speaker never intends to comply; (e) two people alone on an island marrying each other with no community, no state, and nothing behind them; (f) a threat the hearer takes as a joke, and a joke the hearer takes as a threat. Two probes I half-invented and want stress-tested: the "hereby" probe — which verbs take "hereby" felicitously, and does that class line up with the institution-dependent acts or the intention-expressing ones? — and the "uptake ledger", i.e. whether uptake is constitutive (no uptake, no act) or merely the act's intended effect (the act happens, it just doesn't land), with every case above scored both ways. I also have a suspicion I want checked rather than assumed: that the real cut isn't performative/non-performative at all but institution-bound acts (christenings, verdicts, weddings) versus mind-bound ones (promising, apologising, asserting, ordering), where maybe one mind is enough — so the statement should say whether that cut is real, whether it just collapses into one of the camps, or whether it's the same dispute renamed. If a chunk of the fight turns out to be verbal, say which chunk and what disambiguation dissolves it; if some crux is one where both sides would privately admit nothing could move them, say that out loud instead of dressing it up as an open question; and if the cruxes point at a third position that neither camp occupies, flag it. No citations, no reading lists, no "as X famously argued" — write it as if both of you are in the room and have to sign the same sheet, describing examples rather than sourcing them. And please don't hand me the standard summary of what the positions supposedly are: the useful output is formulations that are neither so vague that both sides shrug nor so loaded that only one of them would ever sign.
