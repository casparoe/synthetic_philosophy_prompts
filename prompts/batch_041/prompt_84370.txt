I have become convinced of a claim about Bongard problems — the visual puzzles in which a set of figures is divided into two groups and the solver must find the rule that separates them. The claim is that their difficulty has nothing to do with the amount of data or compute and everything to do with the solver's description language: a problem is solvable exactly when the solver already possesses the right compositional primitives, and its difficulty is the distance between those primitives and the ones the solver starts with. On this view the problems are a clean demonstration that concept induction is hypothesis-space engineering rather than curve fitting. What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: cases where a solver appears to succeed without any single unifying relation; evidence that the relevant primitives are themselves learnable from the same few examples; problems that admit several equally good rules; and the possibility that human success is mostly trial-and-error search over candidate descriptions rather than possession of the right primitive. Tell me which of these the position's defenders would themselves concede, and what the view predicts that a similarity-based or purely statistical account does not. Finally, be honest about whether the claim is insulated by its own vagueness — whether any failure can be redescribed after the fact as the solver lacking the relevant primitive, and whether that unfalsifiability is a defect for a thesis of this kind or simply the price of any claim about hypothesis spaces.
