Quick context: I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy training, and I got roped into the little internal group at my company that writes the rules for when we're allowed to shut down or wipe an AI system. We ended up writing it as a ladder — tiers. Tier 0 is basically a thermostat, nobody's losing sleep. Then tier 1 is damage-signalling, tier 2 is integrated valenced states, tier 3 is a self-model, tier 4 is a narrative self, and the higher the tier the harder it is to justify pulling the plug. My teammate calls it the dimmer switch, because sentience isn't on/off, it's a dial, and the tiers are just notches we agreed to pretend are real. I basically buy this.

Then I reread Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (the Philip K. Dick book, not Blade Runner — I know the movie is basically a different story and I want the book) and now I'm not sure the dial is even the right picture.

What got me: in that book, moral standing seems to get handed out by things that aren't degrees of sentence at all. Owning a real animal instead of an electric one. Passing the empathy test. Being able to plug into a Mercer box. It's a line drawn by a gadget and a neighborhood, not a measurement of a mind. Two scenes I keep chewing on: the spider that Isidore finds, and the ending where Deckard finds a toad he thinks is real and it turns out to be electric (and, the way I remember it, he sort of keeps it anyway).

So: read the novel through the lens of sentience-as-a-matter-of-degree / graded moral status, and tell me honestly where my ladder illuminates the book and where the book breaks it. Stuff I actually want an answer to: does the novel have its own hidden capacity threshold (empathy), and if so is that just my ladder with one notch? Does the electric sheep / electric toad material imply moral status is about care and social meaning rather than minds — and if so, is that a reductio of my tier system or a repair of it? What does the androids mutilating the spider do to the reading where they're practically human? And if the degree-of-sentience frame is the wrong way in, say so — I'd rather hear that than have the lens forced.

Don't bother with citations or page numbers or exact quotes — I don't have the book with me and won't check them, just describe the scenes in your own words. And at the end, tell me how confident you actually are: which of your claims you'd defend, and the single point in the reading you're least sure about, even if it's the main one.
