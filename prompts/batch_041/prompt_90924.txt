I run a small maritime museum in a port city that got very rich in the 1700s, and next month we're opening a permanent gallery about the merchant families who paid for half the buildings in town. I have to give the opening remarks -- six or eight minutes -- to a room that's roughly a third civic donors and local business people, a third teachers and families who've volunteered with us, and maybe ten people from the university who work on the eighteenth century and will catch me if I fudge Smith.

Which is the thing: I want to build the whole speech around Smith's actual argument about colonies rather then the "nation of shopkeepers" line everyone half-remembers. I've got the passage where he says founding an empire to raise up a people of customers might look like a project fit for a nation of shopkeepers but is really a project unfit for one, and extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers; the one where he calls the colonies "a sort of splendid and showy equipage of the empire"; and the line about the rulers of Great Britain amusing the people with the imagination that they possessed a great empire on the west side of the Atlantic, an empire that "existed in imagination only." Please keep direct quotation to those lines, or to nothing at all -- I don't want to stand up there and discover I've been quoting something he never wrote.

What the speech has to do is hold a distinction Smith actually makes and that I think is the honest one for a place like this: the general gains from Atlantic commerce were real and spread out, but the particular gains to the colonizing country came through a monopoly that concentrated the benefit in a few hands and stuck the public with the bill -- his point that the expense of empire functioned as a bounty paid to support the monopoly, and that whatever inconveniencies came with colonies, the mother country engrossed those completely. So the speech needs to be able to say "the trade made this city" without sliding into "therefore it was fine," and to name the merchants as the ones who captured the benefit without turning the evening into a lecture or a scolding, because a good chunk of the room has those surnames and half the gallery labels are about their houses.

Two more things to keep in mind. Smith was not an anti-imperialist in any modern sense -- he thought the general commerce was a genuine gain, he was willing to have America contribute to the public debt on the grounds that the debt had been run up defending it, and he floated admitting the colonies to representation rather than simply cutting them loose. So don't use him as a stick to beat the past with; use his accounting. And the university people will know that the "imagination only" line is doing something specific -- he means the empire's value was largely a bookkeeping illusion in the minds of the people running it -- so whatever you do with it, don't flatten it into a generic point about nostalgia or self-flattery.

Write the remarks. I'd rather it end on something specific and unresolved then on a nice line about learning from history.
