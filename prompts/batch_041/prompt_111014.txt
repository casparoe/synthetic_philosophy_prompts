I'm a science and ideas journalist. Reporting on pandemic-era hospital staffing, I asked an ethicist whether off-duty clinicians had an obligation to take surge shifts; she waved the question off with a single line — that kind of work is "beyond the call of duty." Since then I've watched the same phrase do similar work elsewhere: to argue that wealthy states owe nothing beyond the aid they have already pledged, that a passerby who ignores someone drowning is uncharitable but not unjust, and — most troubling to me — from an administrator who implied that volunteers who had already gone beyond duty had no standing to complain about the conditions they worked under.

Before a follow-up interview I want the phrase precisified rather than explained. I know the technical name is supererogation; I don't want an essay on that literature. Give me a menu of the distinct claims the sentence could be making, ordered from the near-trivial (some morally good acts are not required) through the ones that carry real weight (there are reasons to act that never harden into obligations; omission is not blameworthy; no one may demand the act or resent its absence; roles and standing move where the line falls) to the reading that does cynical work, in which "beyond duty" marks the outer limit of what anyone can be asked at all. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim worth defending, or false, and say which one the speakers above most likely meant. Which reading, if any, actually licenses the conclusion that the shift, the aid, or the better conditions cannot be owed? And tell me what would change your verdict: which argument about the structure of practical reasons, or which feature of these cases, would move a reading from defensible to untenable — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
