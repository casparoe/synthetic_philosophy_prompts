I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about the way decision-theoretic arguments get compressed into catchphrases that then do more work than they can bear. The phrase I keep running into is the one that surfaces whenever Pascal's wager is discussed: you have nothing to lose and everything to gain. I heard it most recently on a panel about science and religion, where a philosopher used it to wave away the objection that the evidence for God's existence is thin — his thought being, as far as I could tell, that the lopsidedness of the payoffs makes the evidential question beside the point. I'm not a philosopher, and I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once depending on who is saying it. What I'd like from you is a disambiguation: set out, as a menu rather than a single favored reading, the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making. Some of them, I expect, are near-truisms; some are substantive and defensible; some may simply be false; and they will differ in what they require — about what counts as "losing," about whose losses are at stake, about whether belief can be adopted at will, about what decision rule is in play when a payoff is infinite or a probability is unknown. For each reading, say what it commits its holder to and whether it is trivial, substantive, or untenable. Then tell me which of these readings would actually license the move I saw on the panel — the dismissal of the evidence question — and which would not, since I take it that the precisification that matters is the one the argument needed.

One further request, since this piece will go to readers who will not check my work against the literature: separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the subject, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Where the verdict depends on which reading one adopts, say so rather than smoothing it over. I don't need citations or a reading list — just the structure of the thing, laid out plainly enough that I can see where the load-bearing joints are.
