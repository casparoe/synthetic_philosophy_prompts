For about a year now a small reading group I'm part of has been circling a question I assumed had been settled long ago, and by this point I suspect the deadlock is more interesting than either side's answer. The question is whether Bertrand's paradox refutes the principle of indifference, or merely shows that problems of this shape are incompletely stated.

The cases can each be put in a line. Choose a chord of a circle 'at random'. If its endpoints are independent and uniform on the circumference, the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle is 1/3; if the chord's midpoint is uniform over the disk, it is 1/4; if its distance from the centre is uniform along a radius, it is 1/2. A factory turns out cubes whose edge length is uniform over some interval, and indifference over edge length and indifference over volume disagree about the probability that the volume falls below a threshold. An unknown ratio is known only to lie between one half and two, and indifference over the ratio and over its reciprocal disagree. One member likes to add a physical variant: a device that spins a pointer to a uniform angle and then sets the chord at a uniform perpendicular distance, a procedure that looks every bit as arbitrary as the other three.

Three positions have hardened, and each has a stock reply to the others' central move. The first holds that the principle is simply inconsistent: it licenses incompatible answers to what is manifestly one question, so it cannot be a rule of rationality, and the honest conclusion is that probability requires a physical chance setup or a specified reference class. The second holds that nothing has been refuted, because 'at random' without a parameterization is not yet a question: each procedure is a different experiment over a different outcome space, each answer is correct for its own experiment, and the lesson concerns the incompleteness of the problem rather than the incoherence of the principle. The first camp replies that this renders the principle unusable, since it can only be applied once the answer is already known, and that it leaves unexplained why some parameterizations strike us as privileged and others as contrived. The third position reconstructs indifference as a demand for invariance under the transformations that leave the problem's structure intact, sometimes with a maximum-entropy step on top; where the symmetry group is rich enough this pins down a unique answer, and where it is not, the problem is genuinely underdetermined. The other two reply in unison that this works only where a suitable group exists, that the interesting cases have none, and that the method quietly imports the very parameterization it was meant to justify.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but at least three wearing a single name. There is a question about the world or the device: which random experiment, if any, is actually being performed. There is a question about credence: what an agent with no device and no frequency data is permitted or required to believe. And there is a formal question doing silent work underneath: whether the symmetries in play admit an invariant measure, and whether it is unique — which is why, I suspect, invariance pins things down so cleanly when the relevant group is compact and its Haar measure is a probability measure, and yields nothing at all when the group is non-amenable and no finitely additive invariant measure exists, the territory of Banach–Tarski. If that is right, the refuters and the ill-posed camp may be answering different questions and talking past each other rather than contradicting each other.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which answer to the chord problem is correct. Which parts of this dispute are verbal, which are substantive, which reduce to a hidden formal or empirical question, and which rest on bedrock intuitions about what rationality may demand — including the possibility that the correct response is not a single credence but a set of them, so that the demand for a determinate answer is itself the error. Say whether the three positions are genuinely incompatible or are compatible descriptions of different projects, and be explicit about which strand of my three-way split is actually doing the work. Then tell me what would count as progress here: a theorem, a distinction, a change of question, an experiment — or an argument that none should be expected, because the question was never well posed to begin with. And if the impasse is the correct resting state, say so plainly and explain why that is not a defect.
