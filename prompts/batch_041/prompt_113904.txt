I teach a seminar on Arendt and the moral psychology of perpetrators, and for the closing session I want to put the film The Zone of Interest (Glazer, 2023) beside Eichmann in Jerusalem. On its face the film is the most literal illustration of the banality of evil anyone has put on screen: Rudolf Höss, commandant of Auschwitz, and his wife Hedwig keep house and garden in a home whose far wall is the camp wall; the killing stays offscreen and largely auditory, a soundscape of shots, trains, furnaces and screaming that never becomes plot, while the camera attends to the lilacs, the river where the children swim, the inmate servants, and the small social anxieties of a family on the make. Glazer has said he wanted to demystify the perpetrators rather than let them keep the grandeur of myth, which is Arendt's ambition too. Yet I keep feeling that the film and the thesis are quietly at odds, and I want a reading that takes both seriously instead of using the film to illustrate the book.

Here is where I want you to dig. Arendt's claim, as she developed it from the trial report to her late writing on thinking, is not merely that Eichmann was a dull man but that evil on this scale is compatible with, and perhaps facilitated by, not thinking at all — by the absence of the inner dialogue she called the two-in-one, the self one has to be able to keep oneself company with. It is a strange successor to the radical evil of her totalitarianism book. The film, though, gives us almost no access to Höss's interiority; it observes him from outside, at his desk and in his garden, and locates the horror in the ambient sound and the wall rather than in the shallowness of a mind. So is the film Arendtian at all, or is it doing something else — compartmentalisation, the ordinary-men thesis, the aesthetics of domestic life under genocide — and can her thesis survive a treatment that refuses psychology? Note the two moments when the film does slip inside his head: the fairytale he reads to his daughters while a Polish girl hides food for the prisoners, and the phone call in which he reports having spent an SS party calculating the most efficient way to gas the guests. Do those moments confirm the banality reading or wreck it?

Second pressure point, the historical revision. The Eichmann reconstructed by Bettina Stangneth from the Argentina years is a boastful, committed antisemite rather than a thoughtless clerk, and the real Höss was no reluctant bureaucrat either. Does the film smuggle the old picture back in — the ambitious careerist who wants the promotion, the house, his mother-in-law's approval — and if so, does that falsify the philosophical claim or only the empirical one? My own hunch is that the interesting version of Arendt is about the structure of administrative life rather than about any particular man's head, but I want you to say which reading the film actually supports rather than leaving both doors open.

Third, the position of the audience. The film's central device is that we hear what the family has trained itself not to hear, which puts us where the perpetrators refused to stand; and its one near-miss with conscience, the grandmother who sees the crematoria burning at night and leaves a note, is a moral response consisting only of departure. Is that an achievement or a consolation, a way of assuring us that we would have heard the trains? And is the film vulnerable to the charge Arendt herself attracted, that the victims are present only as sound and smoke, never as persons with a point of view? Does that parallel between her critics and the film's form matter, or is it a coincidence?

I am not asking for a survey of the criticism, and you should not guess at page numbers or citations — I know the texts and I do not need a reading list. What I want is a committed reading: say what the film shows about Arendt's idea, where it betrays or outruns it, and where the honest critic has to concede that the film resists the lens. Take a position and defend it rather than laying out the options for me to choose from. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would write to a colleague teaching the same seminar.
