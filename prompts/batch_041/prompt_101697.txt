You are a philosopher specializing in normative ethics, with deep expertise in T. M. Scanlon's contractualism and in the long-running debate over whether that view can accommodate interpersonal aggregation. Work carefully: before you write anything, think through the full range of distinct claims the phrase could be making, then present only your polished final answer.

Background. I am a graduate student in an ethics seminar. We were discussing a proposed municipal rule that would impose a real but limited hardship on a small, identifiable group of residents (the households that would be displaced by a new facility) while delivering a large aggregate benefit to everyone else in the city. A fellow student kept invoking the contractualist formula "no one could reasonably reject" as if it settled the matter. His argument was that, since the total benefit is so large, the rule is one that no one could reasonably reject, and therefore it is justified. He even floated a specific principle: maximize total benefit, but keep everyone above a basic minimum. I am suspicious that the phrase is doing far more work than it can bear, and that the readings which would license his conclusion are readings on which the phrase means something quite different from what contractualists intend. I do not want a summary of contractualism. I want a menu of the precise theses the phrase could be expressing, a verdict on each, and an assessment of which of them would actually support his aggregative conclusion.

Please produce the following sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths.

1. Bottom line up front. Two or three sentences: which reading the phrase must be taking for his argument to go through, and whether that reading is one the phrase can plausibly carry. About 100 words.

2. Candidate readings. A numbered menu of at least six distinct precise claims the phrase could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical. For each one, state the claim in a single sentence and then unpack it in two or three sentences, being explicit about (a) whose standpoint the reasonableness of rejection is assessed from, (b) whether complaints arising in different people's lives may be added together, and (c) whether "reasonable" is doing independent normative work beyond the fact that people actually object. About 700 to 900 words in total.

3. Verdicts. For each numbered reading, give a verdict of platitude, substantive and defensible, or false, with a one- or two-sentence justification, plus a column stating whether the reading licenses my fellow student's aggregative conclusion (yes / no / only with added premises). A table is fine if it is clearer. About 300 words.

4. The separateness-of-persons crux. Explain in your own terms why the choice in section 2 about whether complaints may be summed is the pivot of the whole dispute, and say which reading best fits the contractualist's stated aim of justifying oneself to each person individually. About 250 words.

5. What the slogan was probably doing in my seminar. Your best guess at which reading my fellow student intended, whether he is entitled to it, whether his added minimum-floor proviso changes the answer, and the single sentence I could say to press him on it. About 150 words.

Write in American English. Do not cite sources or literature; I want the analysis, not a reading list.
