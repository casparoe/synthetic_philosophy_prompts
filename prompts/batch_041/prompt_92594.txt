Two colleagues of mine — both of whom work in forecasting and both of whom say they accept the Aumann agreement theorem — spent a lunch hour disagreeing about it, and I couldn't referee. They were both estimating the chance that a startup we all follow gets acquired within a year; she put it at about 30%, he at about 10%, and each knew the other's number. She argued: we share a prior and we're both Bayesians, so by the theorem a state in which we *know* we disagree is impossible unless one of us has mishandled the information; since we plainly do disagree, one of us must be in error, and the rational move is for me to shade my credence toward his. He replied that the theorem is a claim about an idealized configuration — common prior, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of the posteriors — and that it tells you such a configuration cannot obtain, not what to do once you find yourself outside it; you can't treat the mere fact of disagreement as a signal without a model of why the disagreeing happened. She came back that the disagreement *is* informative precisely because it forces a diagnosis: either our priors aren't really shared, or one of us is being irrational, or the disagreement isn't common knowledge — and in the first case we aren't both being led by the evidence, so she should still move toward him. He said that last step is exactly what the theorem refuses to license: if the priors genuinely differ, the result is silent and there is no shared prior to update toward, so averaging our numbers would be an arbitrary courtesy rather than a rational requirement. She then asked what the theorem is even *for* if not to counsel deference, and he said its content is that disagreement between common-prior agents must trace to asymmetric information and that pooling the information must dissolve it — which is a claim about markets and aggregation, not a rule for two people at lunch. They asked me who was right and I genuinely couldn't say.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the claims they make, say whether it is a correct statement of what the theorem does and does not imply, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what each of them is missing, and who has the better overall grip on the issue.
