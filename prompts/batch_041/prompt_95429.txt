Okay, so I write novels — nothing technical — but I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading about this scheme for keeping AI honest by having two of them argue in front of a human judge. The pitch is that it's easier to spot a flaw in somebody else's argument than to come up with a correct argument yourself, so the honest one should win. There's a worry about this that I think people call the obfuscated arguments problem, and I want to write a scene where it bites. But I don't want to invent the failure myself and get it subtly wrong, so please build me the case to order.

Here's the machine I'm assuming. Two systems, call them A and B, get the same question. Each gives an answer, then they take turns for a fixed number of rounds. A human judge — competent in the subject, honest, no favorites, and with enough time to actually read every line of the transcript — picks the winner. Nothing in my scene may work because the judge is lazy or biased, or because B is vague, or because B just drowns the judge in pages. Every step B writes has to be a definate claim the judge could in principle check. And A is not sandbagging: she knows exactly where B's argument goes wrong and says so out loud.

The case I want: B wins with the wrong answer. A is right, knows it, and still can't move the judge, and the reason has to be a real cost asymmetry — some step in B's argument that is cheap to write, plausible to read, and expensive to actually check, so the judge's only honest option is to take it on faith. I don't want hand-waving about exponential blowup; give me costs a person could feel, like "checking this one claim eats an hour and there are forty of them."

Then walk me through it. State the question, both answers, the exact flaw, and why the judge goes with B anyway. Tell me which single feature is doing the work, and what happens when I take it away: give the judge ten times as long; let A just say "step seven is wrong, look there" without proving it; let the judge abstain and default to the safer answer; add a third debater. Then give me the best repair to the protocal you can — I don't want "a smarter judge" or "more time" — and tell me honestly whether a craftier B wriggles around it. And tell me what A's best available move actually is in the case as built, because she has to say something and I need her dialogue.

Last thing: give me the strongest speech a true believer in this whole scheme would make to argue my scene is rigged or unrealistic. I need a character who believes in it and isn't a strawman.

Keep it under a page, and keep the subject of the question something I could put on the page — two people in a dispute over routes, schedules, a budget, that kind of thing. No exotic mathematics, I have to write dialogue about this.
