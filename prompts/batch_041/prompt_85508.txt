Good's theorem is the one where free information can never lower an expected-utility maximizer's expected utility, and the proof is basically a one-liner: if you'd rather not know, just do what you would have done if you hadn't looked. I want a case where that one-liner actually breaks.

Specs: keep it realistic — a clinic, an office, a family — no demons, no brain scanners. The information is free and perfectly reliable and it arrives before you choose, so this isn't a cost-of-information or noisy-signal story. It's about the world, not about what you're going to decide, so no self-prediction. Nobody else can tell whether you got it — no tells, no one who knows you were informed — so no signaling and no adversary reading you. And the loss can't just be that knowing feels bad; the information has to change how things actually turn out.

Then do the work. Give the probabilities or at least the shape of them, show expected utility is strictly lower with the information than without, and pin down exactly which step of the one-liner fails: what the informed agent would have to be able to hold fixed for "just act like you didn't look" to work, and why she can't. Then take a side — is this a counterexample to the theorem, or does it only expose a premise the theorem was quietly assuming all along? — and give the best reply available to someone defending the theorem. Commit to a verdict, don't just lay out the options.
