I write a science-and-ideas column, and for a while now I've been circling a piece about why people who agree on all the facts still can't agree with each other. The usual stuff about polarization and epistemic bubbles doesn't quite do it for me, so I've been reading up on Aumann's agreement theorem, the 1976 one where two Bayesians with a shared prior who both know each other's credences can't agree to disagree — if their posteriors are common knowledge and they're both rational, the posteriors have to be equal. I want to use Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957, from Reginald Rose's teleplay) as my test case, because its a movie everyone has seen and it's basically a machine for manufacturing a disagreement and then dissolving it one juror at a time. So read the jury room with the theorem in hand. Which of the assumptions actually hold in that room and which ones fail — is there a shared prior at all, or does the outspoken bigot just come in with a different prior and thereby falsify the setup from the start? Do the jurors have common knowledge of each other's rationality, and does the last holdout's breakdown at the end count as a violation of the theorem or as the theorem working exactly as advertised? And what about Fonda's holdout at the beginning, the guy who votes not guilty while explicitly admitting he doesn't have a theory of the case, he just wants to talk it over? Naively that's stubbornness or a hunch; I'd like to know whether there's a reading on which he's the only juror whose behavior the theorem can actually license, because he's the only one who hasn't collapsed a private signal into a public verdict yet.

The other thing that nags me is that a vote is not a credence. The theorem is about agents announcing probabilities and then updating on those announcements, and a jury vote is a coarse, strategic, thresholded action — the men are being asked to decide beyond a reasonable doubt, not to report a number. So does the film's arc of agreement even bear on the theorem, or is the theorem simply blind to what a jury does, in which case the interesting question becomes what the film shows that the formal result can't see? The knife he produces, the old man's limp, the woman's glasses, the noise of the el — those are private information becoming public one at a time, and I want the mechanism of convergence made explicit, not just gestured at, along with a straight answer about where the film cheats. Related: I keep running into the complaint that the common-prior assumption is so strong that it rules out basically all real disagreement, and that the no-trade results falling out of the same machinery are a reductio of one of the premises rather than a discovery about belief. How seriously should I take that in a popular piece? Don't send me a reading list or citations, I have library access and that's not the bottleneck — what I want is the argument, a reading that doesn't reduce the movie to an illustration of the theorem or bend the theorem to fit the movie, and an honest paragraph at the end on what the film adds to the philosophical discussion that the math alone doesn't. If you think a different film or novel would serve me better, tell me why, but commit to 12 Angry Men if you can.
