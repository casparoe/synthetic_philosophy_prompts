I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and this year I serve as the graduate student representative on my university's Committee on Student Accommodations. A case has come to us that I expect to be contentious, and the chair has asked me to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

The petitioner is a second-year undergraduate with a documented memory impairment resulting from a childhood injury. She is asking to use a wearable device during closed-book, in-class examinations in its normal mode, which includes both retrieval and generative completion. The device is not a simple recorder. It continuously captures lectures and readings, stores them in a searchable personal database, and, using a model trained on her own notes and prior writing, generates suggested phrasings and completions as she composes answers. She has used it daily for three years, says she now thinks through it, and reports that when it is unavailable—when the network is down or the battery dies—her ability to organize her thoughts collapses. She also concedes that she sometimes cannot tell whether a sentence she has written originated with her or with the device, and that she occasionally overrides its suggestions. The device is cloud-based and therefore occasionally unavailable, and she keeps a paper notebook as a backup. Her first examination is in three weeks.

The university's examination policy permits accommodations that do not fundamentally alter the skill being assessed. The committee has no settled view on whether a device of this kind is a memory prosthetic or a study aid that any student could in principle use. Two positions have emerged. One member argues that if the device is genuinely part of her cognitive system—meeting the criteria that defenders of the extended mind thesis usually invoke, such as reliable availability, easy accessibility, and automatic endorsement—then denying it is like denying a student her glasses. Another member argues that the device is a tool, not a mind; that reliable coupling is not the same as constitution; and that granting the request would commit us to allowing calculators, spell-checkers, and search engines in every closed-book exam, which would make the examination format incoherent.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. I need a concrete recommendation—grant, deny, or grant with specific conditions—stated in a way the committee can vote on, along with the reasoning. The recommendation should say what we should tell the student and the registrar for this term and what review date, if any, we should set. The reasoning should engage seriously with the strongest version of the position I end up rejecting, and it should say what turns on the device's generative features as opposed to its storage-and-retrieval features. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need an argument that my colleagues, most of whom are not philosophers, can follow. Please write in American English.
