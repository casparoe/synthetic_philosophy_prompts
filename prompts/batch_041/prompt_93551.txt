I am preparing a graduate seminar on teleosemantics and the broader project of naturalizing intentional content, and before we begin assessing the existing proposals I want the class to construct the specification first. Please give me the desiderata that any theory attempting to explain intentional content in non-intentional, naturalistic terms must satisfy in order to count as adequate. State each requirement as a requirement, and for each one give the case or consideration that motivates it: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse (for instance, between a state's merely co-varying with something and its representing that thing, between content and reference, between a state's having content at all and its having a determinate content, and between representing and being disposed to behave as if one represents), and the results from biology or the cognitive sciences it must remain consistent with. For each requirement, say what work it does — which otherwise attractive theory it rules out — and whether it should be treated as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp.

To keep the list from becoming a set of platitudes, I want it to be able to handle the following cases, which I intend to press on during the seminar. First, the ordinary possibility of error: a state can misrepresent its object, and the theory must say what makes this a mistake rather than a different, equally correct content. Second, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate that comes into existence by accident, equipped with a full complement of internal states but with no selection history whatsoever. Third, a lineage that evolves a single internal state co-varying with both a genuine predator and a harmless shadow, where the organism has never encountered the shadow on its own. Fourth, a state that was selected for one task and later, within the same lineage, is recruited to a different task, so that its evolutionary history and its current functional role point toward different contents. Fifth, a system whose internal states were shaped by some process other than natural selection — an artifact whose states were tuned by gradient descent on a task, or a creature that acquired the relevant state through learning within its own lifetime. I care most about whether a theory grounding content in evolutionary function can satisfy the list at all once cases three through five are on the table, and I would like you to say plainly if it cannot.

Finally, flag any pair or subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect the interesting result of this exercise to be a tension rather than a clean checklist, and I would rather see the tension named than smoothed over. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and whether the list should be revised if we give up on the etiological element and try to ground content in something other than a system's history.
