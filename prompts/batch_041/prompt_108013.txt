A friend in my reading group has a reading of Kant's Perpetual Peace that I cannot shake, and I would like you to audit it rather than adjudicate who is the better Kant scholar. His claim is that the essay rests on one analogy and that nearly everything else is ornament. The analogy is the domestic one: states stand to one another as individuals do in a state of nature—each is judge in its own cause, none is subject to a common superior, and the condition is therefore a condition of war. Individuals escaped that condition by submitting to a common sovereign and a coercive law; so, on his reading, states must do the same, and perpetual peace therefore requires a world republic with enforceable authority. He concludes that Kant's preference for a mere voluntary federation of free states, together with Kant's own fear that a single world sovereign would be prone to tyranny, is a failure of nerve: the analogy, followed honestly, delivers the world state, and the essay's second definitive article quietly contradicts its own premise.

Give me the correspondence table first. Set out what in the individual case is meant to map onto what in the international case, including the elements the analogy needs but does not announce—the contract, the sovereign, the right of coercion, the exit option, and the contrast between the lawless freedom of persons and the freedom of a state that already possesses a lawful constitution internally. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion and those that are merely picturesque. I am particularly interested in the strains the analogy generates inside the essay: the worry that a single world sovereign would have nothing outside it to check it; the fact that Kant settles for a federation rather than the civil union the analogy seems to demand, and treats it as something less than the real thing; the fact that the analogy needs each state to have a single will, whereas the first definitive article makes a state's will depend on its constitution being republican; and the third article's reappearance of individuals as parties in their own right, which an analogy that treats states as the only persons in the picture seems to suppress.

Then the two questions that matter to me. First: if the disanalogies hold, does the world-state conclusion survive in a weakened form—an enforceable international law, a standing court, a federation with teeth—or does it collapse altogether, and if it survives, what is doing the work when it does? Second: Kant appears to offer a second route to peace that does not use the analogy at all, namely the claim in the supplements that nature, working through commerce and mutual self-interest, will push states toward peace regardless of their virtue. If that route is sound, how much does the analogy actually matter to the essay? And if the analogy is carrying the argument, is the world state a requirement of right or merely a prediction about what will come to pass? Finish by saying what a better analogy for the international case would be, and what it would and would not buy.
