Write a dialogue between an epistemicist and a supervaluationist about vagueness. Both are experts, so they may skip exposition of the sorites, admissible precisifications, supertruth, and bivalence, and go straight to the points where the theories come apart. Have each press the objection that is hardest for the other to answer: the supervaluationist should ask what precise fact about our use of a term fixes the sharp boundary the epistemicist posits, and why a boundary so fixed should be unknowable even in principle; the epistemicist should press the consequences of treating truth as truth at every admissible precisification, especially whether the view can accept a true existential claim locating the boundary somewhere when no claim locating it at a particular place is true, and whether the maneuver relocates vagueness into the notion of admissibility instead of removing it. Ground the exchange in a single concrete predicate applied to a single concrete case: a rule that triggers a heavier penalty when the quantity a defendant possessed was small, with a stated number of pills in evidence. Let one character use that case to demand a yes-or-no answer, and have the other say exactly what such an answer does and does not settle. Neither character may be a strawman, and neither may simply restate their opening: each reply should engage the specific move just made. End with each character naming the one commitment they would have to abandon if the other's objection succeeded.
