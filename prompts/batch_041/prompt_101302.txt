I have been reading about pain asymbolia for a seminar on the philosophy of mind, and I keep finding that my reactions to the cases do not line up with any of the standard slogans about pain. In the ordinary case of a stubbed toe or a tension headache, I think the experience is bad for the person, and bad in itself, not merely because it signals tissue damage or makes them less productive. In the asymbolic case, where a patient reports that a pinprick is painful but says it does not bother them and does not withdraw, I find myself thinking that there is still something bad there, though much less bad than ordinary pain; I am inclined to say the sensation is bad but the suffering is absent. By contrast, when someone feels a pain that is intensely unpleasant but has been told it is harmless and will last only a second, I still think the unpleasantness is bad, even if the episode is too brief to matter overall. If a person is under deep analgesia and feels nothing at all while their body is being damaged, I do not think there is anything bad in the experience itself. When a masochist seeks out a pain and takes pleasure in it, I do not think the pain is bad for them, even though the same sensation in another person would be bad. But when a torture survivor reports that they can feel the pain without being bothered by it, I am pulled toward saying the pain is still bad, because of what it means about what has been done to them, even though the felt quality sounds like the asymbolic case. And when I imagine an animal or an infant with the same asymbolic condition, I am not sure whether to say the pain is bad for them or not, since they cannot tell me whether it bothers them.

I am tempted by the thought that pain is bad just in case it is unpleasant, but the torture survivor case makes me doubt it, and I want to know whether that doubt is well founded. Please help me find the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts. State the principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I have not listed: for example, a pain that is felt as painful and is mildly unpleasant but that the person would not remove if offered, a pain that is felt but is completely forgotten immediately, a pain that is not felt but causes long-term damage, and a pain that is felt as unpleasant in a person who has no capacity to mind it. I am especially interested in whether the principle should treat the sensory component of pain and the affective component as separable, and whether badness attaches to the sensation, to the unpleasantness, to the person's attitude toward it, or to something relational like the violation of bodily integrity. If one of my verdicts has to go, tell me which one and why; if the only principle that saves all of them is baroque, say so and tell me which simpler principle you would accept instead. I am not looking for a reading list or a survey of positions, just the principle and its consequences.
