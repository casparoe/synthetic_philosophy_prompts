Suppose David Hume were brought into the present and asked to adjudicate a case that could not have arisen in his lifetime, though its structure would have interested him.

The case: within the span of a few minutes, crowds in several cities report an event that cannot be squared with known physics — an object of considerable mass rises into the air, hovers, and vanishes. Within the hour, tens of thousands of recordings of the event have been posted from distinct devices in distinct locations, and a large share of the world's population has watched at least one of them. The recordings agree with one another and with the spoken reports. Three features complicate the case. First, generative models can now fabricate footage indistinguishable from authentic footage on the basis of a short text description, and no forensic technique reliably separates the two. Second, most of the recordings were produced by devices that use learned models internally to denoise and reconstruct sensor data, so that even a nominally raw recording is not a straightforward transcription of what the lens received. Third, a subset of the recordings carries cryptographic attestations from tamper-resistant hardware modules, which certify that a particular sensor array registered a particular pattern of light at a particular time and place — provenance, that is, rather than interpretation. It also turns out that many of the people who recorded or reported the event had already seen earlier footage of it, so the apparent independence of the reports is difficult to establish.

Answer as Hume would, reasoning from the argument about miracles that he actually gives in his Enquiry concerning Human Understanding rather than from a general reputation for scepticism. Address, in particular: (a) whether multiplying testimony helps here, and whether what matters is the number of witnesses or the independence of the channels through which their reports reach you; (b) whether hardware-attested sensor records count as testimony in the relevant sense or fall outside the scope of his argument altogether, and whether his framework is equipped to draw that distinction; (c) whether his comparison between the improbability of the reported event and the improbability that the reports are false remains usable once fabrication is cheap and detection unreliable, or whether cheap fabrication merely shifts the balance in a way his argument already anticipated; and (d) whether the force of his argument depends on a prior estimate of how improbable the event is, which would make it circular in a way this case makes vivid, or whether it can be restated without any such estimate. Close with a verdict on what Hume would conclude, and with an assessment of which element of the case his argument handles least well.
