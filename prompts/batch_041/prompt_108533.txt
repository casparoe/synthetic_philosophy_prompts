I'm a journalist — I write a fortnightly column on technology and ideas for a small European magazine — and I've somehow talked myself into giving the opening provocation at a two-day data-protection conference in Berlin next month. The audience is going to be the problem. Roughly half are German data-protection lawyers and regulators of the old school, the sort who can recite the census judgment the way other people recite poetry and who treat informational self-determination as a load-bearing constitutional concept rather than a slogan; maybe a third are American tech-policy people and civil-liberties types who think the whole German edifice is bureaucratic theatre and that the real enemy is private surveillance rather than the state; and there's a small delegation from China — two academics and, I think, someone from a provincial credit bureau — whom I'd rather not insult and definitely don't want to flatter. My thesis, which I'll be defending for forty minutes and expect to be taken apart in the Q&A, is deliberately awkward: the standard ranking of these three regimes (Germany protects, America exposes, China controls) is either backwards or useless. What actually seperates them isn't how much the state watches but whether the watching is reciprocal — whether the watched can watch back. Germany hands the citizen a right to inspect and correct the file the state keeps on her, then leaves her naked in front of her employer, her insurer and the data brokers; America barely restrains collection at all, but has a real if rickety culture of publicity — freedom-of-information requests, leaks, an actual press — that lets people turn the lens around; and China, which everyone treats as the purest surveillance state, has quietly wired a crude reciprocity into its credit systems, so that ordinary people are nudged into reporting on one another and, here and there, on officials. I want to call this the visibility ledger and argue that on it Germany looks a good deal less free than it claims and China a good deal less alien than we're comfortable admitting.

Before I write a word of the thing, though, I need a proper Q&A prep sheet, and that's what I'm asking you for. Take on the role of a political philosopher who is also a seasoned conference-hand — someone who has watched a hundred keynotes die in the question period — and think carefully about this specific room rather than about privacy in the abstract. Give me the objections I'll actually get, not the generic ones about privacy being dead, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my argument, each phrased the way the particular people in the room would phrase it (a German regulator's attack on my reciprocity point should sound nothing like an American civil-libertarian's). For each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Start by brainstorming the three or four strongest lines of attack before you commit to anything, and don't pad the list — I'd rather have six real objections than fifteen thin ones. And finish with the one question I'm dreading, the one I'm hoping nobody in that room has thought to ask.
