I'm in a reading group on distributive justice and we keep going in circles on luck egalitarianism — the view that inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck but fine when they trace back to people's own choices. Everyone nods along to the distinction in the abstract and then it falls apart the second you try to apply it, which is honestly where we got stuck. But someone floated a proposal last week that I've been thinking about alot, and I'd like you to stage the fight over it. Call it risk pre-registration: before you make a big risky choice — take the dangerous job, move onto the flood plain, sink your savings into the shop — you'd file a binding public declaration that you're doing this on your own dime, and in exchange you'd waive any later claim on the rest of us if it goes wrong. The pitch is that this makes option luck visible and actually voluntary instead of something we reconstruct after the fact, and it lets everyone stop guessing which losses were really chosen. The person who floated it thinks it vindicates luck egalitarianism by making the brute/option line administrable. My friend thinks it's the clearest possible proof the whole view is rotten.

So write me a dialogue between those two — call them Devi, the luck egalitarian defending pre-registration, and Ola, a relational egalitarian who thinks the very project of sorting people by what they chose is the disease and not the cure. Neither should be a pushover and neither should be a caricature; I want them actually answering each other rather than trading monologues. Have Ola press the abandonment angle (what does the person who signs and then gets catastrophically hurt actually get?) and the exploitation angle (who ends up signing, and is a signature made under bad options worth anything), and have Devi press back — that the alternative is the prudent forever subsidizing the imprudent, that relational equality still owes an account of what people are owed and by whom, and that refusing to distinguish choice from luck just means the distinction gets made silently by whoever holds the purse strings.

Push them into the regress too: if every choice is shaped by unchosen factors, does the line collapse, and does pre-registration save it or just relocate the problem? Somewhere in there let them disagree about whether the trouble with luck egalitarianism is that it draws the line in the wrong place or that it needs a line at all — and let them come out sharper about exactly what they're disagreeing over, even if neither converts.
