I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm finishing a feature on a strange idealization that runs through recent work on machine reasoning: an idea its proponents call HCH, in which a person confronting a hard question is free to hand sub-questions to copies of herself, and those copies to further copies, recursively, until every remaining question is small enough for someone to answer outright. Alongside it sits the companion claim, usually called factored cognition, that a task becomes tractable once it has been broken into sub-tasks independent enough that each can be settled on its own by a reasoner of modest capacity, with the partial answers recombined afterward. What interests me is less the engineering than the picture of deliberation underneath it — thinking as a tree of delegated errands — and I'd like to run a poem with the piece. I'm commissioning that poem from you, and I want its form to carry the argument rather than illustrate it.

Three requirements. First, the poem should be a closed loop: its last line should be the same line as its first, so that the reader lands back on the question she started with and cannot tell whether she has reached the bottom of the recursion or merely come around again. That is what it means for a system of this kind to be defined in terms of itself, and the poem should make the reader feel the queasiness of it rather than being told about it.

Second, the body of the poem — somewhere between fourteen and twenty lines — should enact the descent and the return: questions giving way to smaller questions, each one narrow enough that the next is obviously manageable, until the poem reaches a question small enough to be answered at a glance, and then the answers coming back up in reverse order, so that the shape on the page is a tree laid on its side. The turn belongs at that bottom, where the structure finally touches something a person can just do.

Third, the poem should take seriously what the idealization quietly assumes and what it quietly leaves out. It assumes that sub-questions come apart cleanly, that the person answering at the leaves is doing something reliable, and that a question loses nothing essential in being handed off. It does not say who pays for coordinating the pieces, or why the question at the top was worth asking in the first place. I'd like the poem to let a reader feel that the same mind is present at every level of the tree, that delegation is free only if you decline to count the cost, and that nothing in the arrangement accounts for the urgency of the original question. Leave that unease standing; don't resolve it into either optimism or dread.

A reader who knows this literature should recognize the idealization in the poem and find the treatment fair. A reader who has never heard of it should simply find a good poem. So please keep the vocabulary of the field out of the verse, and skip the stock furniture of artificial-intelligence imagery — no servers, no blinking lights, no machines announcing themselves. The recursion should be audible in the syntax and visible in the line breaks, not named.
