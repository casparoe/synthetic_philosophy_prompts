I teach a second-year undergrad course that's half formal logic, half phil of mind, and I've got one 75-minute session with about 18 students to get them to actually feel what it is to jump out of a formal system -- the moment you stop pushing symbols around by the rules and start reasoning about the rules from outside. I don't want to use the MU puzzle itself; half of them have heard of it and someone will just recite the answer from somewhere. So I've made two of my own toy string-rewriting systems and I want you to build the whole session around them. Materials should be cheap: rules printed on cards, a target string on the board, no money.

System A: symbols a and b. Start from the single string a. Rule 1, you may add a b to the right end of any string. Rule 2, you may double any string (write it twice in a row). Rule 3, you may swap any adjacent ab into ba. Target: reach aaa. (They can't. The number of a's is always a power of two -- start 1, doubling doubles it, the other rules leave it alone -- and 3 isn't a power of two. Don't tell them this, obviously.)

System B, used later: symbols a and b. Start from a. Rule 1, wrap any string: add an a to the front and a b to the back. Rule 2, double any string. Rule 3, wrap the other way: add a b to the front and an a to the back. Target: reach ab. (Also unreachable, but by a different invariant -- the difference between the count of a's and the count of b's, taken mod 3, is 1 or 2 at the start and stays nonzero. Note the obvious trick from System A does not carry over.)

Here's the shape I want. Phase one, small groups race to hit target A, and it needs to actually take a while so the failure stings -- let them flail, don't hint. Phase two, when they're stuck, ask whether they can show it's impossible, and give one nudge card that just says look for something that never changes. Let them find the power-of-two thing themselves. Phase three, hand out System B with the same ask, so they reach for the invariant move and it doesn't immediately pay off; the lesson is that each jump is bespoke, there's no master trick you can just reapply. Then a last beat: let them add the proven claim (aaa unreachable, say) as a brand new rule to System A and ask what that changes and how they'd now reason about the enlarged system -- to feel that every jump gets re-absorbed and you need a fresh jump to see the new system's limits.

Give me the runnable plan: group sizes and roles, the timeline across 75 minutes, the exact wording on the cards, how to stage the three phases so the ideas show up in what happens rather than in what I say, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the idea of jumping out. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- my guess is one group finds the invariant in four minutes and gets bored while everyone else spirals, so how do I handle that. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: it can't give them the incompleteness flavor where the meta-system itself has limits you can't see from inside, and it can't touch the meaning side of it, that we're treating strings as pure syntax the whole time -- I'll have to supply both, so tell me where to slot them in.
