I have been trying to work out when numbers count in moral argument. The contractualist formula — an act is wrong when it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject — strikes me as either right for the wrong reason or else silent once more than two people are involved, and I cannot tell which without first knowing what I think about the cases. What I want from you is not a survey of positions but the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures the verdicts I actually have. I am not asking who has argued for what; I want the substance, and I would rather end with a principle that is honest about the verdict it loses than with one complicated enough to keep them all.

Here are my verdicts. In every case the parties are strangers to me, no one's consent is available, and no compensation is possible.

1. Two people are drowning on my left and one on my right; I can reach only one side. I save the two.

2. Five are drowning on my left and one on my right, under the same constraint. I save the five.

3. I can save one person who will otherwise die, or save two hundred people who will otherwise each lose a hand. I save the one.

4. A million people will each suffer an hour of headache this afternoon, and the only way to prevent this is to break a stranger's finger. I do not break the finger.

5. The same million hours of headache will instead be suffered by one person over the course of her life, and the only way to prevent this is to break a stranger's finger. Here I do break the finger, and I do not hesitate much.

6. Five patients will die today unless a healthy person is killed and her organs distributed among them. I do not kill her, though the arithmetic is the same as in case 2.

7. A runaway vehicle will kill five people unless I divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one. I divert it.

8. I can spare a million people an hour of headache by destroying a manuscript on which one person has worked for a year; she will never learn what happened. I do not destroy it. My verdict does not change when I stipulate that she will never find out, so I appear to think the loss need not be felt in order to count.

9. Ten million people will die of a plague unless a single immune carrier is killed and her blood used to make the antitoxin. I do not kill her, though I hold this verdict with less confidence than the one in case 6.

The principle I have been working with, which I will call the like-for-like principle, runs as follows. A person's complaint is the whole of what she stands to suffer, and complaints are not added across persons. Numbers count only where each of the many has a complaint individually as serious as the complaint of the one who loses out; in that case nothing needs to be summed, since no one's complaint is being outweighed by anyone else's, and the many can simply be preferred. Where the many's complaints are individually lighter than the one's, numbers do not count, because counting them would require summing losses borne by different people, and that treats the one person's life as a place to deposit the many's smaller losses.

This principle delivers all of my verdicts from 1 through 5 and 8. It delivers none of 6, 7, or 9, which appear to need a separate clause about killing and about using a person as a means. I cannot tell whether that clause leaves the aggregation principle doing any work or whether it does all the work itself.

The place where I am least settled is the following family. Hold the one person's broken finger fixed and vary the many: their individual suffering rising from a headache to an agony, their number rising from a million to a trillion, in every combination. I am confident of my verdict when the many's individual complaints are far lighter than the one's, and confident again when they are equal; somewhere just below equality I have no verdict at all, and that is precisely where I need the principle to decide for me.

Please do the following. State the principle, or the two or three candidates worth taking seriously, as precisely as you can, and for each clause say which of the nine cases forces it. Then say what the best candidate implies for cases I have not listed, with the boundary family and an astronomically large number of individually small losses at the top of my list. Finally, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and which I should give up; if the principle needs a clause that exists only to save one of my verdicts, say so, and tell me to drop the verdict.
