The idea concerns superrationality. As it is normally stated, a superrational player assumes that every other superrational player who faces the same problem will arrive at the same answer, and so chooses the strategy that maximizes the common payoff; this is what is supposed to make cooperating in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma defensible. What bothers me is that nearly every clean illustration assumes a population of players who are either all superrational or all ordinary expected-utility maximizers. I care about the mixed case: a superrationalist who knows that only some fraction of the others reason as she does. The toy game I want to use is a lottery of the Luring-Lottery kind, where a single fixed prize is divided by the total number of entries, each player may submit any positive whole number of entries, and one entry is drawn at random to take the (already divided) prize. Among identical reasoners this game has a tidy superrational answer, but once the superrationalist assigns a probability to facing non-superrational opponents, I cannot tell whether her recommended number of entries is even well-defined, and I suspect the whole notion either reduces to a heuristic that just restates an assumption about how others will behave or collapses into ordinary expected-utility reasoning with an oddly chosen prior.
