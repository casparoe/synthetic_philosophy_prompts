Write an essay on whether the self-indication assumption can be defended against the presumptuous-philosopher objection, or whether that objection should push us toward the self-sampling assumption or to some restriction on one or both. (SIA: a hypothesis that implies more observers is, for that very reason, more likely to be true, since a larger pool of observers gives your own existence more support. SSA: you should reason as if you were a random observer drawn from all the observers that exist according to the hypothesis under consideration. The presumptuous philosopher is the figure who uses SIA to conclude from the armchair that the universe is as large as it could be.) Reach and defend a verdict; do not merely survey the terrain.

To keep the argument off the beaten track, build it on the following two cases rather than the standard ones. Case A: two hypotheses agree on all physics and on the total number of conscious experiences in the world, but disagree about how those experiences are bundled — one says a few long-lived subjects each contain many experiences, the other says many short-lived subjects each contain few. Case B: two hypotheses differ sharply in total observer count, but the many-observer hypothesis is antecedently much less probable and gives each of its extra observers a far worse life. Set out what SIA and SSA each imply in A and in B, whether the two principles come apart, and whether the presumptuous-philosopher objection was really tracking that divergence. Then decide which principle — perhaps in a restricted form — is the better one for reasoning about self-location, and what the objection is best understood as an objection to: armchair cosmology, the choice of reference class, or the counting of observers as such.

Refer to arguments by name if you wish, but do not cite or list any sources; I will supply the references myself. State how confident you are in your verdict and identify the single step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
