I'm teaching an undergrad seminar on the ethics of the long future and I want to open the unit with The Time Machine -- everyone's read it, it's short, and it beats starting with the economics. The lens I want is pure time preference. Sidgwick thought it was arbitrary to care more about your own century than a later one; Ramsey said discounting future enjoyments just because they're later is ethically indefensible and comes from a weakness of imagination; Parfit argues that our instinctive preference for the near over the distant in time is a bias that falls to the same arguments that kill other biases; and then there's the Stern/Nordhaus fight over whether to discount the far future at near zero or a few percent a year, which is really a fight about whether distant people's interests count the same. Rawls is lurking in the background too, since he didn't think the difference principle reaches across generations and had to bolt on a separate savings principle.

Heres my hunch, and I want it tested rather than agreed with. The Time Traveller is the only person who ever actually visits the future, and he still treats it as scenery. He fights the Morlocks, starts a fire to cover his escape, loses Weena in it, and goes home for dinner. If direct acquaintance with the future doesn't make a person care about it, then the problem isn't ignorance, and the whole discount-rate debate might be beside the point. Where does the novel back me up, where does it push back, and which scenes should I actually put in front of the students -- the flowers he brings back, the White Sphinx, the last chapter with the dying sun? Tell me honestly where the reading distorts the book, too; Wells is up to something about class and degeneration rather than about our duties to posterity, and I dont want to flatten a good novel into an illustration. No quotes or page numbers, I can't check them anyway. And finish by saying what the book has that Parfit and the economists dont.
