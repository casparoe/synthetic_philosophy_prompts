I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I have spent the last two years reading my way into the Nyāya school of classical Indian epistemology on my own, and next month I have a slot at a departmental epistemology reading group to present a short paper. I need a question-period preparation sheet, not help writing the paper itself.

The room will be about a dozen people. Most work on the epistemology of testimony and social epistemology, and they know the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate in that literature cold. Two are formal epistemologists who will try to restate everything as a probabilistic model and will ask what my claims predict. One is a historian of philosophy who knows Indian philosophy far better than I do and whose questions I am most afraid of. The rest are faculty and graduate students sympathetic to naturalized epistemology, mildly allergic to anything that sounds like exotica, and quick to suspect that a non-philosopher is romanticizing a tradition.

My thesis is that the Nyāya treatment of verbal testimony (śabda) as an irreducible source of knowledge — a pramāṇa in its own right rather than a species of inference — is closer to right than the reductive accounts of testimony that dominate the contemporary literature, and that Nyāya's insistence that its other sources (perception, inference, and comparison or analogy, upamāna) are likewise not inter-derivable gives a better taxonomy of how we come to know things than the standard menu. I will defend three sub-claims: that śabda is irreducible; that upamāna is a genuinely distinct source and not a disguised combination of perception and inference; and, as a closing provocation, that knowledge I get from an instrument or a model whose workings I do not understand is closer to testimony than to perception. My argument for the first claim runs through an analogy from my own field: requiring every claim to be derived from one's own perception and inference is like requiring every component in a system to recompute its dependencies from first principles, and the Nyāya position looks like the recognition that some channels are trusted as inputs rather than derived. I am aware that this analogy may be doing more work than it can bear.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way these people would phrase it and in their vocabulary, with a short reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Separate the objections that are really about Nyāya from those that are about my competence or my framing, since I expect a good many of the latter. Tell me what to concede early to buy credibility and what I must not concede. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep it to substance; I do not need advice about slides or delivery.

At the end, tell me what would change your mind about this forecast: which objection you would demote if the room turned out more sympathetic than I have described, and where your reading of the room is most likely to be wrong. Also flag anywhere my own thumbnail of Nyāya is likely to be off, since the historian will find it before I do.
