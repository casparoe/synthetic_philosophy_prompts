I'm workshopping a paper next week and I want to know what's coming for me. The thesis: the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, in the Rawls/Nozick form, is really two claims stapled together -- a metaphysical one (persons are numerically distinct, so you can't sum welfare across them the way you sum across time-slices within one life) and a moral one (it's wrong to sacrifice one person for a bigger total). I argue the metaphysical half does no work. The utilitarian already treats a single life as an aggregate over stages; if intrapersonal aggregation is fine, then distinctness by itself can't block interpersonal aggregation. So the objection has to stand on the moral premise, which needs defending on its own and usually just gets asserted.
