I was appointed in March to the independent values and alignment review board of a publicly funded foundation that builds AI tools for public benefit, and the first substantive decision to land on my desk is also the one we have deferred three times. Our flagship deployment — a decision-support assistant that residents use to navigate the provincial disability-benefits system — goes live in eleven weeks, and we must settle, in a written opinion I am to circulate before Thursday's meeting, whose values the assistant should serve. I did not come to this cold: I spent four years as a benefits caseworker before I was appointed, and I have sat on the other side of this desk.

Some background, since you will need it. The assistant does not merely answer questions. It helps users work through consequential choices: whether to appeal a denial, whether to attempt a return to part-time work, how to divide a fixed benefit between housing and care. Any such tool ranks some goods above others, and our engineers have already built a working prototype around an adjustable value vector — a set of weighted dimensions (self-reliance, security, family obligation, personal autonomy, and others) that can be tuned. The question before us is how those weights should be set, and by whom.

The board has split four ways. One group holds that a public instrument must be neutral among citizens' conceptions of the good life, and that any weighting it adopts is a covert endorsement of one. A second group replies that neutrality is a fiction: a model that presents options without ranking them has already ranked them in what it treats as salient, and a benefits tool has an affirmative obligation to advance some substantive good — harm reduction, or capability, or something else. A third group proposes radical personalization: let each user set the weights, so that no one's values are imposed on anyone. A fourth proposes aggregation: derive the weights from a representative survey of the population and let majority priorities govern, subject to minority protections. Our counsel has warned that whatever we choose will be cited in the litigation that is already threatened.

Three facts bear on the choice, and I would like you to take them seriously rather than around. First, the prototype ships with default weights set by the engineering team, which privilege autonomy and self-reliance, and in our usability trials the large majority of users never adjust a default. Second, a substantial share of the intended users — those with cognitive disabilities, and minors — cannot set weights for themselves, and a surrogate must do it, whose interests are not always the user's. Third, the board itself is not neutral: we were appointed by the funder, and two of our members are drawn from the disability community while one represents the funder's financial interest.

Draft the opinion. Recommend a concrete course of action — a stance on the weights, a mechanism for setting and revising them, and a time frame keyed to the eleven-week launch — and give the reasoning that would let a dissenting member see exactly what they are being asked to accept. Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, rather than as a concession in passing. And say plainly what we should count, a year from now, as evidence that we chose wrongly.
