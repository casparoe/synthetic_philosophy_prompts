I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I have to run a forty-five minute lunch session for a group of machine-learning engineers and data scientists. The topic I have chosen is accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory. My rough grasp of the view is this: the sole fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, a credence function is graded by how close it comes to the truth at each possible world, and the surprising result is supposed to be that this one commitment, run through expected-utility machinery, forces things like probabilism and conditionalization. I do not want you to explain the subject to me; I want you to plan how I should explain it.

The audience is the hard part. Everyone in the room computes log loss and Brier scores as part of their job, everyone knows the phrase "strictly proper scoring rule" and can tell you that the log score is one, and most of them will assume that this is already the whole of the subject. My worry is that the session collapses into scoring rules, which they already use, and that they leave thinking the philosophical literature is a slow restatement of their own tooling.

What I need from you:

1. The order of ideas, with the dependency structure made explicit: what has to be in place before what, and why. Tell me in particular where the difference between calibration and coherence has to enter, and where the distinction between epistemic and pragmatic utility has to be drawn, given an audience that will reach for decision-theoretic habits immediately.

2. The example that carries the whole session, and why it is the right one. My candidate is a colleague's model that emits three numbers for rain, no rain, and rain-or-snow, which do not satisfy additivity, followed by the repaired version of the same three numbers. Say what that example must have and must lack if it is to do the work, and if something else should carry the session instead, give me that and say why.

3. The misconceptions the room will form, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. My guesses: that accuracy means being right, so a 0.7 credence in a true proposition is simply inaccurate; that accuracy is a property of a model measured against a test set rather than a property of a single credence function at a single world; that coherence is justified because it pays off, or because of Dutch books, so the accuracy-first version is that same argument in a costume; that if accuracy is the only good then the view has no room for understanding or explanation, which someone will offer as a reductio; and that the upshot is that one should be certain of everything true, which is where they need to see that accuracy is graded and certainty is expensive. Tell me which of these are the real obstacles, which are not, and what I have missed.

4. One hinge I care about and do not trust myself on. I have been told that the argument that incoherent credence functions are accuracy-dominated goes through for any strictly proper scoring rule, so the practical question of which loss function to use is beside the point for the philosophical argument even though it is central to our daily work. Either give me a way to make that land in front of this audience or tell me I have it wrong and what I should say instead.

5. The check for understanding at the end: one question I can put to the room that separates the people who have understood the view from the people who have learned its vocabulary, and what each kind of wrong answer would reveal.

6. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so without making the room distrust the rest of the session.

I do not need a reading list; I need the plan.
