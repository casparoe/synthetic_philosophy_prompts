Here's the position I've landed on and I want to know what would count against it.

I think the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are the same paradox in different clothes, and that both are symptoms of one mistake: treating "belief" as a single, all-or-nothing attitude that's subject to the same closure logic as the propositions it's about. My view -- call it the two-ledger view -- is that a rational agent keeps two seperate ledgers. The credal ledger holds graded attitudes and is where rationality lives; it has to be probabilistically coherent, non-negotiable. The acceptance ledger holds flat, all-or-nothing commitments, and it is *not* governed by deductive closure across the board -- only within the scope of a single inquiry or a single practical decision. So the lottery ticket holder can accept, for the purpose of "which ticket do I tear up", that ticket 1 won't win and ticket 2 won't win and so on, and also accept that some ticket will win, with no contradiction to repair, because those acceptances never have to sit in the same ledger entry. Same story for the preface author: each claim gets accepted in its own local context, "some claim in this book is false" gets accepted in the meta-context, and the grand conjunction is never something she has to accept. No revision to logic, no giving up consistency for credences, no mysterious degree-of-belief threshold. The paradoxes are just artifacts of running one ledger's closure rules over the other ledger's contents.

Now tell me what counts against this. Real defeaters, not "some philosophers disagree". Specifically I want:

- Where the two-ledger move is just relabeling the problem instead of solving it, and how I could actually tell the difference.
- Whether "closure only within an inquiry" quietly makes acceptance vacuous. If acceptance is scoped to an inquiry, in what sense is it belief at all? Does the view collapse into the threshold view, or into a decision-theoretic account of acceptance where accepting is just preferring to act as if?
- The internal strain: if the credal ledger has to be coherent, coherent credences *of what*? If I'm 0.999 that ticket 1 loses, and likewise for each ticket, probabilistic coherence caps my credence that no ticket wins -- so I can't be near-certain of each and stay coherent. So what's actually sitting on the credal ledger when I "accept" the lottery claims? Do I need some notion of credence conditional on ignoring the partition, and is that even a coherent thing to have?
- Anything formal or empirical that sinks it: results suggesting belief is binary rather than graded, or cases (the preface author who's *certain* of each individual claim, Moore-paradoxical cases, self-fulfilling beliefs) where the two ledgers can't be kept apart.
- And an honest verdict: does the view have any teeth, or is it engineered to be unfalsifiable? I'd genuinely rather hear "this is well insulated and here's why that's a problem" than get a padded list of challenges.

Rank the defeaters from "this kills it" to "annoying but survivable", and for each one say whether a defender of the two-ledger view would concede that it counts against them or just shrug it off.
