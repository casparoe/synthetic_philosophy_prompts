I hold a doctorate in statistical mechanics and have spent my career with the tools of that field: microstates and macrostates, coarse-graining, ensembles, the distinction between deterministic laws governing individual trajectories and probabilistic laws governing ensembles, effective theories, and the way irreversibility enters only at the level of description. Take Einstein's mature position on religion and on free will — his Spinozist identification of God with the lawful order of nature, his rejection of a personal or interventionist deity, the attitude of awe he took to be the root of genuine religious feeling, and his denial that human beings possess free will in any sense that would satisfy an incompatibilist — and restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. I am fluent in this vocabulary; do not explain ensembles or the arrow of time to me, and do not smuggle in the target framework's assumptions and call the result a translation.

For each element of the position, say what translates cleanly, what can be carried over only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in statistical-mechanical terms. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. I want to know whether the elements that fail to translate point to something the vocabulary is genuinely missing — something about agency, normativity, or the first-person standpoint that no arrangement of ensembles and coarse-grainings can capture — or whether they reveal that Einstein's position was less substantive than it looks, a set of attitudes dressed in the language of physics. In particular, tell me whether my vocabulary can even state the difference between the claim that the will is determined and the claim that the will is free but merely appears determined, or whether that distinction simply has no counterpart in a framework where probability is a feature of our description rather than of the world.
