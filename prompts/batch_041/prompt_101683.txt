What frustrates me is that the discussion keeps collapsing into the same two moves — either "obviously you aggregate, the numbers win" or "obviously you don't, a speck is nothing" — and nobody in the room can say what would actually settle the matter, or even what the disagreement is really about. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on aggregation, population axiology, and the moral status of very small harms, and to think carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming a wide list of the genuinely live questions in this area, then give me a defended agenda rather than a survey. Concretely: I have one semester of independent study and a term paper to write, and I want to spend it on whichever question is most upstream of the others — the one whose answer would most change how the rest of the debate goes — instead of on something that merely sounds profound. Please also tell me which candidates you would deprioritize, and why. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the reasoning.

For each candidate question, make the criteria explicit: whether it is a question about the structure of the value function (say, whether the badness of harms is continuous and Archimedean, or whether there is a lexical threshold past which no quantity of small harms competes with a large one); a question about whether numbers as such have moral weight, or only the interests of the individuals involved; or a question about what makes a harm "count" at all, and whether a harm can be real yet too small to register in the ledger.
