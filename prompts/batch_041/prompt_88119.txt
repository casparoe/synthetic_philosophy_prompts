I belong to a biweekly reading group on behaviour genetics and quantitative methods — half of us are psychologists who fit twin and family models, half are philosophers of science who don't — and I keep running into a slogan that has started to function as a conversation-stopper. Whenever a heritability estimate comes up, someone will say, with the air of having settled the matter, that heritability is a property of populations and not of individuals, and then draw a conclusion from it: that the estimate therefore says nothing about any particular person; or that it cannot bear on whether an intervention would work; or, oddly, that a high heritability for a trait shows the differences we care about are inborn and fixed. The same sentence gets used to make a very weak point — merely that the number is defined relative to a particular population at a particular time and would change if that population changed — and also to make something much stronger, as if any heritability estimate were automatically irrelevant to individuals, to causation, or to change. I can't tell which uses are legitimate and which are sleight of hand, and I want a proper disambiguation rather than a general lecture on the topic.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, say what it would have to mean, whether it is true, false, or merely contentious and why, and — importantly — whether that reading would actually license the conclusion the speaker was drawing from it in the case at hand. Along the way, address the specific puzzles that keep surfacing in the group: how a trait can be strongly genetically determined and yet have a heritability of essentially zero (the standing example is having two legs); what exactly a heritability coefficient is a fraction of; what happens to it when the range of environments or of genotypes present in the population is restricted; whether heritable ever licenses a claim about one individual's traits or about the cause of a difference between two people; and what follows, if anything, for the prospects of an intervention. Finish by telling me which reading you think people who repeat the slogan usually have in mind, and which reading the argument in the room actually needed.

I'd like you to commit: give me your own verdict on each reading rather than presenting the options neutrally, tell me which uses you think are simply mistaken, and if the slogan is true in some sense but does no work in the argument where it gets deployed, say so plainly. Where the matter is genuinely contested, pick the position you think is best supported and defend it, flagging what you take to be the strongest objection to your own view.
