I am a novelist, and I am writing a book whose crisis is an advance directive. Before I draft the final act I need to know whether the position the book is built on is one I can actually hold, or whether it is two positions wearing one coat. Here is what I believe, stated as plainly as I can manage.

1. What matters in the survival of a person is not sameness but connection — memory, character, the carrying-forward of what one cares about — and connection comes in degrees. There is no further fact of identity underneath that does the real work.
2. A life is not merely a sequence of moments but something that can be authored, and its good is partly a matter of its having the shape its author wanted. Some of what we care about — how we die, what we refuse, what we are willing to be remembered as — is not about how any particular hour feels.
3. An advance directive is an exercise of that authorship, and it should be honored even when the person now in the bed protests. It speaks for the person across the whole of a life, not for the hour.
4. Harm, though, is felt. If the person in the bed is frightened and struggling, she is being harmed, and that counts, whatever her connection to the woman who wrote the document.
5. Nothing in any of this requires a soul. I am not smuggling in a ghost; the whole account has to run on naturalistic fuel.

The crux of the novel is not the standard case, in which the patient is either fully herself or wholly gone. It is the middle stage: my protagonist still recognizes her daughter, still says she wants to live, and cannot grasp her own prognosis or weigh a reason. The book turns on whether a partial self's preferences have partial authority, or whether a person who can no longer value in the way the directive presupposed has no authority at all, so that only her distress matters.

I am not asking you to agree with me. I am asking for an audit. Which of these five pull against one another, which only appear to, and how severe is each real conflict? Rank them rather than treating them as equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost — I want to know what I would be surrendering, not merely what to change. I do not know which of the five I would abandon, which is precisely why I am asking. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most while leaving the book's tragedy intact. I want an ending in which both parties are right, and I need to know whether my commitments permit that or whether the book's shape requires me to make someone wrong.

Do not send me to the literature; I want the argument itself, worked out from these commitments. And where something I have said is not so much false as underdescribed, say what it would have to be turned into.
