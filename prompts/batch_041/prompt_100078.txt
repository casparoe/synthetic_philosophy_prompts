I want to read The Great Gatsby as a test case for the philosophy of fakes and forgeries, with the terms of that debate transposed from works of art to persons, and I want an honest account of how far the transposition survives.

In the philosophy of art the governing question is whether a perfect copy can have the value of an original, and the answers cluster into a few positions. One holds that only perceptible qualities matter aesthetically, so that a forgery indistinguishable from the original is, as art, on a par with it. A second holds that the original's value lies in its provenance, in the causal history that ties it to a particular maker's act, so that the forgery is defective in a way no amount of looking can reveal. A third, less discussed, holds that the original matters evidentially, as a witness to a past that the copy cannot testify to — valuable for what it is rather than for how it appears, the way a relic is valued for contact with an event rather than for beauty. And running alongside all three is a moral dimension, the deception and the misattributed credit that forgery involves, which is separable from the metaphysical question of identity.

Gatsby is an unusual test because its central counterfeit is a person. James Gatz invents Jay Gatsby at seventeen and keeps faith with the invention to the end; the name, the inherited fortune, the Oxford years he cannot make good on when Tom presses him, the war medal, the manner of speech — all of it is fabricated provenance attached to an object that is otherwise indistinguishable from the thing it imitates, and arguably finer. Read the novel against those positions and tell me which one it presupposes. Does it treat the forged self as aesthetically equal to the original, or does it insist that something is missing precisely because of where the self came from? Which scenes carry the weight — the drunken guest in the library who is astonished that the books are real and then deflated to notice that their pages have never been cut; the pile of shirts Daisy weeps over; Gatsby's rehearsed past coming apart under Tom's questioning; the green light across the water? And where does the novel resist the analogy altogether?

I am especially interested in whether Gatsby inverts the original/copy hierarchy. The original he is faithful to, the Daisy he remembers, may be as fabricated as he is, while the forgery is the more coherent thing and, on the narrator's final verdict, worth more than the whole lot of them. If that is right, the novel may be pressing on a question the aesthetics literature tends to keep separate: whether the value of an original was ever really about works of art, or about the kind of thing that can bear a history at all.

Give me a reading disciplined in both directions. Do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a distinction from aesthetics, and do not loosen the distinction until it fits. End by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that argument alone does not.
