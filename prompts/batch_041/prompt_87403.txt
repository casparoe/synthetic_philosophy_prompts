I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I would like you to take the role of an expert on personal identity, multiple realizability, and the metaphysics of distributed cognition — someone at home both in the analytic literature on diachronic identity and in the more speculative claims about minds spread across substrates. Please reason carefully rather than quickly. Begin with a scratch space in which you enumerate the candidate correspondences, then the disanalogies, and then sort the disanalogies by whether they bear on the conclusion at issue; only after that give me the audit proper.

Here is the analogy I keep meeting. In my reading group, several members lean on a picture of the self as a tune. On this picture a self is a pattern rather than a substance: it can be realized in many materials — a piano, a voice, an orchestra, a recording, another person's memory of it — it can be sounding in several places at once, and no single realization is privileged as the real one, since the score is no more the tune than the performance is. (It is a cousin of the older mind-as-software picture, but the tune is the version they actually use.) From this they draw a substantial conclusion: if a self is a pattern, then there is no deep obstacle to a single I being distributed across several brains; the pattern need only be tokened in each of them, the way the same tune can be sounding in two concert halls. They lean on this to argue that personal identity need not be tied to one organism, and even that something of me could persist in you after my death, because you can carry the pattern. I take this to be the line of thought in the strange-loop literature, though the group states it more bluntly than the literature does.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more than it can bear, but I want a fair audit rather than a debunking. Please give me:

1. A correspondence table — what plays the role of the tune, the score, the instrument, the performer, the performance, the listener's memory, and the acoustics of the hall — and a note on which of these mappings the picture forces and which are free.
2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that are merely picturesque and those that genuinely bear on the conclusion about a distributed I.
3. An account of what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. My own list, which I would like you to test rather than simply endorse: tunes are individuated by public conventions of sameness; a tune has no point of view; it does not remember being played; and two performances of one tune do not add up to one experience. If any of that holds, does the conclusion survive, or does it survive only in a weakened form — that several distinct selves may share a pattern, which is not the same as one self spread across several heads?
4. If the tune is the wrong vehicle, a better analogy, and what the group would have to add to get from a shared pattern to one subject.

Be explicit about the type/token equivocation you think is doing the work here, if there is one, and end by saying plainly which parts of the group's conclusion you would keep and which you would drop.
