I am a philosopher of mind, and for the past two years I have co-run a standing workshop with a colleague in cognitive science. Our subject is conceptual slippage: the way a concept bends, stretches, or drifts in the course of solving an analogy problem. The workshop has hardened into two camps. The first, which I will call the structure-first camp, holds that the space of concepts is fixed in advance by a vocabulary of descriptors, that the analogical relation is fundamentally an alignment of relational structure, and that slippage is a byproduct of search over that fixed space. The second, the concept-first camp, holds that the concepts themselves are what slippage happens to, that there is no fixed space to search, and that slippage is the constitutive mechanism of analogy rather than a failure mode of it. We have been talking past each other for two years, and we have agreed to produce a joint statement before we publish separately. I want you to draft it.

A few constraints. Neither of us will sign a document that leans on authority, so do not cite any literature and do not appeal to what a named researcher has established; where you invoke a framework, state its content in your own words and say why it applies to the case at hand. Everything must be argued on the page. And because the standard illustrative examples have been worn smooth by overuse, the statement must work in a fresh microdomain, specified as follows.

The microdomain is strings of small positive integers acted on by a small inventory of primitive operations: increment every element, double every element, square the last element, reverse the order, delete the first element, delete the last element, append a copy of the last element, insert the successor of the last element, and group the string into consecutive runs or into halves. Any analogy problem in this domain consists of an initial string, a transformed string, and a target string; the task is to say what the target becomes. Slippage is what happens when the reading of the initial transformation must shift in order for the target to be answered.

Produce the joint statement with exactly the following sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths.

1. Preamble and shared microdomain (150 to 250 words). State the two positions as fairly as each side would state its own, and fix the microdomain precisely enough that both sides agree what counts as a problem in it.

2. Agreed claims (eight to twelve numbered claims, each one to three sentences). These must be substantive enough that a reader could disagree with them; avoid truisms. Include, at minimum, claims about what the primitive operations can and cannot express, about whether the initial transformation in a problem ever determines a unique reading, about what the two sides agree counts as evidence of slippage, and about which features of the domain are artifacts of the notation rather than facts about concepts.

3. Residual disagreements (four to six, each stated twice: first in the wording the structure-first camp would accept for its own view, then in the wording the concept-first camp would accept for its own). Neither formulation may be a strawman, and neither may be so hedged that it becomes compatible with the other.

4. Cruxes (a table with four columns: the crux; the case, argument, or experiment both sides agree would bear on it; the structure-first prediction; the concept-first prediction). I expect one crux to be whether the inventory of concepts is fixed or generated on the fly, and another to be whether a stochastic or temperature-like parameter is an essential feature of the mechanism or an eliminable convenience of the implementation. Each crux must be stated so that settling it would move at least one side, and each prediction must be specific enough to be checked against a worked example.

5. Worked example (300 to 500 words). Construct one analogy problem in the microdomain — an initial string, a transformed string, and a target string — such that the two camps' preferred readings of the initial transformation yield different answers for the target. Show both readings, show the divergent answers, and use this example to test at least two of the cruxes from section 4.

6. Verbal disputes (short paragraph). Identify any part of the two-year disagreement that you judge to be merely terminological, and say which terms are doing the damage.

7. Signatures (brief). One sentence per camp stating what would change that camp's mind, phrased so the camp itself would accept it as a genuine concession condition.

Total length around 1,500 to 1,900 words. Write it as a document both signatories could put their names to, not as a summary of a debate or a verdict on who is right.
