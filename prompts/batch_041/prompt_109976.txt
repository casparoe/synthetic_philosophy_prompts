I am a historian of early modern science, and next month I am to give the toast at the retirement dinner of my doctoral advisor, who spent forty years assembling a monumental and deliberately exhaustive catalogue of every surviving letter, instrument, and marginal note connected to a minor but fascinating figure of the Scientific Revolution. The dinner will be mixed: a third of the room are historians who know the catalogue and will catch any factual slip, a third are philosophers of science who will enjoy the epistemological joke, and the rest are family and friends who simply want to hear something warm and true about her. I have been asked to speak for about seven minutes. Here is my difficulty, and I would like it to be the organising principle of the speech rather than a joke at the beginning: I believe every individual thing I want to say about her -- that she never missed a deadline, that she read paleography the way other people read novels, that her footnotes have rescued three generations of graduate students, that she was kind to me when I was useless -- but I do not believe the conjunction of all of them, because no human life is that consistent, and I suspect at least one of my warm claims is false. That is the preface paradox, and I have been living with it because I am also writing the preface to my own first book, where I have to say that despite my best efforts some of what follows is wrong. I would also like the speech to make quiet use of the lottery paradox: just as I can believe of each ticket that it will lose while disbelieving that every ticket will lose, I can believe each of my claims about her while not believing the conjunction, and the audience should feel the parallel without being told it.

What I want from you is the text of the toast itself. Please make the paradox do structural work: let each sentence stand on its own as something I can sincerely assert, and let the whole speech never quite assert the conjunction. The ending should feel like a preface rather than a peroration -- an acknowledgment that the person being praised, like a long book, is not the sum of any list of true sentences, and that this is not a failure of love but a condition of it. Do not explain the lottery paradox or the preface paradox to the audience as a lecture, and do not use the phrase preface paradox or the phrase lottery paradox unless they arise naturally in a sentence I might actually say. The speech should be about her, not about me or my book; my own preface is only the private occasion for asking. Keep the tone formal but affectionate, and write it so that the non-historians in the room can follow every sentence. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter.
