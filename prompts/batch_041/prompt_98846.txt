A reading group I'm in has been working through the literature on the "actual sequence," and a friend has built an argument that I think proves too much but that I can't refute. Here it is, in her steps. (1) In a standard Frankfurt-style case the counterfactual intervener — the neurosurgeon on standby, the device wired to fire — never actually does anything; the agent deliberates, forms a desire, and acts. (2) The standby device makes no difference to the actual sequence of events: every event that actually occurs would have occurred exactly as it did, in the same order, had the device never been installed. (3) An agent is morally responsible for an action when the action issues from her own deliberative agency in the ordinary way — responsibility is fixed by the actual sequence, not by what might have been. (4) So in the standard case she is responsible even though she could not have done otherwise; that is the intended lesson, and I accept it. (5) Now the variant: the device does its work decades before the agent is born. It arranges which desires she will have and, at the moment of decision, which of her own standing desires will win; it never has to fire, because the desire it has rigged to win is in fact the one she acts on. Her deliberation on the day runs exactly as in (1), down to the last hesitation. (6) By construction the actual sequence from deliberation to action is identical in (1) and (5); the only difference lies in the causal history of her desires, not in the sequence that runs from them. (7) Given (3), responsibility depends only on that sequence; so she is responsible in (5) as well. (8) The pre-natal device is just a vivid stand-in for whatever in fact shaped her desires — genes, upbringing, culture, a thousand accidents. Every agent's desires have some such history. By (7), then, an agent is responsible for whatever flows from her desires, however those desires were produced. (9) So responsibility survives even total manipulation of a person's character, provided the manipulation is complete enough to leave no seam at the moment of action. (10) But an agent whose every motive was installed by a designer precisely so that she would do this is not responsible for doing it; that isn't a delicate intuition, it is the whole point of calling it manipulation.

I accept (1)–(4), and I accept (10), and I cannot have both. My friend blames (3), and concludes that the original Frankfurt verdict in (4) is a mistake too. I would rather keep (4) and give up (10), but I can't see how to distinguish (5) from (1) without smuggling back the very alternative-possibility condition Frankfurt was supposed to kill. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked fine before you looked, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: does rejecting (3) or (7) leave me any principled way to say the standard Frankfurt agent is responsible while the pre-natal one is not, or does any such distinction either (a) reintroduce a "could have done otherwise" condition under a new name, or (b) rest on nothing more than the fact that one history is natural and the other engineered?

And if instead I keep (3) and give up (10), what does that commit me to for the intermediate cases — the agent whose operative desire was installed last week rather than before birth, the agent whose desire was installed but who would have had it anyway, and the ordinary agent whose character was formed by causes she never chose at all? I don't want a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the bill.
