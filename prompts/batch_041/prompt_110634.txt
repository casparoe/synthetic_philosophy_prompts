I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history and memory, and I keep getting stuck on a question the readings circle without ever settling. The setup: a community's shared memory of its past and the professional historical record sometimes conflict in substance, not just in emphasis. A town commemorates a wartime reprisal as the massacre of three hundred civilians; the parish registers and the occupation-era administrative files that survive point to a figure closer to ninety, and suggest that a share of the dead were armed irregulars. A diaspora narrates its expulsion as one catastrophic rupture; the record shows a decade-long, uneven, half-bureaucratic process. A nation's founding story treats a frontier as empty; the record shows it was not. In each case the memory does real work — it underwrites identity, mourning, obligation, solidarity, claims for reparation — and in each case historians or documents push back. Our seminar's version of the question is not "which one is true" but the narrower question of authority: when memory and record conflict, which should give way, and over what?

Take on the role of a philosopher of history and memory — someone who argues from first principles and is willing to reach an unflattering conclusion rather than a diplomatic one. I'd like you to assess three positions I've been trying to state precisely, and to treat them as proposals to be refined or rejected rather than as settled vocabulary. The first I'll call the asymmetry thesis: where memory and record conflict, the record has authority over what happened, while the community retains authority over what it means. The second, the unity thesis: there is no clean separation, because what a community takes an event to mean is itself a factual claim about the event, so any concession to memory on meaning quietly concedes ground on the facts. The third, the sunset thesis: communities may hold interpretive authority over their own past, but only for a time — after some interval, or once the last generation with direct experience has died, the record's authority becomes complete and the memory should be reclassified as heritage rather than as knowledge.

Write an essay on this controversy that weighs the three positions and reaches a verdict; something like "the asymmetry thesis holds for meaning-claims but not for identity-constituting factual claims" is a perfectly acceptable conclusion, but do commit. Two hard cases must be addressed explicitly rather than waved at, because I think they do the most damage to the neat versions of these views: (a) a case in which the meaning the community assigns is causally downstream of a factual error, so that conceding the meaning concedes the error — say, a community that mourns as victims partly because it has forgotten that its own side did the killing; and (b) a case in which "the record" is itself an instrument of power — the occupier's files, the colonial archive, the census taken for the purpose of expropriation — so that deference to the record is not deference to a neutral tribunal. Say what your verdict implies for each, and consider the objection that any general principle of the kind I'm asking for is a philosopher's imposition on practices that are properly political, to be settled by the communities involved and not by argument. Reason carefully before you write, and argue from your own analysis; I don't want a reading list or citations, and I'd rather have one sharp objection to your own position handled honestly than a survey of the field. Begin your response with the essay itself: the first sentence should state your verdict, with no preface, no restating of my question, and no announcement of what you intend to do.
