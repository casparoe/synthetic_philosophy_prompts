Here's the position I'd like you to stress-test, because it's more or less mine. I'm a Hayekian about this, and I mean the strong version: rational economic calculation under socialism isn't just hard or inefficient, it's impossible in principle, because the knowledge it would need doesn't exist in any centralizable form. The relevant knowledge is dispersed, much of it tacit, much of it tied to particular circumstances of time and place, and the price system doesn't so much gather it up as let people act on their local scraps of it — it's a discovery procedure more than a computation. Mises had the original version (no market prices for capital goods means no way to compare alternative uses of them), and Lange answered with the competitive solution: keep genuine markets for consumer goods and labour, have a Central Planning Board adjust prices by trial and error, give managers a couple of simple rules (expand until marginal cost equals price, combine factors until their marginal value products line up), and the thing allegedly converges on the allocation a competitive market would have found anyway. I think Hayek came out ahead in the exchange, but I don't fully trust my own confidence here.

- Someone once put it to me that the Austrian case compares an idealized market against a flesh-and-blood planner, and that if you compare like with like it becomes a question of information *costs* and institutional tradeoffs, not impossibility. Is that a real defeater or a cheap shot?
