I am putting together a small anthology of philosophical parody, and I would like you to write a piece for it. The piece should be a parody of an official style manual or user guide, and its subject should be Otto Neurath's ambition for a picture language: a system of pictograms intended to serve as a universal, transparent medium for science and everyday life, one that would avoid the ambiguities and metaphysical entanglements of ordinary language. Imagine that such a language has been adopted by a fictional international bureau -- call it the Central Institute for Unified Pictorial Science -- and that the Institute has issued a deadpan, bureaucratic document titled Guidelines for the Construction and Use of Picture Statements, now in its seventh edition. The manual should take itself entirely seriously. It should contain numbered sections on such matters as how to depict negation, disjunction, and generality in a purely pictorial notation; how to distinguish a picture of a fact from a picture of a proposition; what to do when a pictogram is ambiguous between two readings; how to represent abstract economic or sociological concepts without smuggling in verbal definitions; and how to handle a citizen who insists on expressing a metaphysical claim that the language was designed to make ungrammatical.

The humor should come from the actual philosophical difficulties rather than from slapstick: the tension between pictorial immediacy and logical articulation, the need for conventions that are not themselves pictured, the problem of translating 'is' and 'ought,' and the recurring suspicion that the manual's own prose is doing work the pictures cannot. The target is the dream of a purely pictorial, theory-neutral language -- not Neurath the person, and not any living figure. Please write about 700-900 words, in the voice of a patient but slightly defensive Institute functionary, and let the satire remain legible to a reader who knows no philosophy while still being fair to someone who has thought about Neurath, picture languages, and the unity of science. You may invent any pictograms, bureaucratic categories, or historical footnotes the manual needs, but do not cite real works or supply a bibliography.
