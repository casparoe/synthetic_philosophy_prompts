I teach a second-year philosophy of science unit and I've got a student who's done something clever and annoying in equal measure. He's reworked Goodman's grue business into an arguement that no predicate is projectible, and half the seminar now belives him. I'd like your read on it before Thursday.

His arguement, as near as I can reconstruct it from the whiteboard:

(1) A predicate P is projectible just if the fact that all so-far-observed Ps have been Q gives us reason to expect the next P to be Q.
(2) 'Grue' (green if first examined before some fixed date, blue if not) is not projectible. All observed emeralds have been grue, but that gives us no reason to expect the next emerald to be grue, since we already know the next one will be examined before the date and is therefore green.
(3) But 'green' is interdefinable with 'grue' and 'bleen' in exactly the mirror-image way, so the pair stand on all fours; neither is somehow the more primitive of the two.
(4) If two predicates are interdefinable and stand on all fours, they're projectible or unprojectible together.
(5) So green is unprojectible too.
(6) So no predicate is projectible, and induction is just a description of a habit we happen to have.

Two things I'd like from you. First, where does this go wrong — which step, and why did that step look so innocent? If it's actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what surrendering it costs me; I'd very much like to keep (1), and I'd rather not land at (6). Second, a side worry: does the arguement need the temporal index at all? If he'd defined his odd predicate using a spatial parameter instead — green on one side of some fixed line, blue on the other — would the same reasoning still run, and does the answer tell us anything about where the real asymmetry lies?

Don't just tell me to invoke natural kinds and leave it there; I want the options priced. Say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and flag the point you're least sure of. British English, please.
