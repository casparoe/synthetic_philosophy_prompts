So I've ended up in a proper row with my mate Dan about rent control and the minimum wage, and he's given me an argument I can't get out of, and I've started to suspect it proves far too much.

Here it is the way he lays it out:

(1) Whether a transaction may go ahead turns only on whether anyone is wronged by its going ahead.
(2) The only ways a transaction can wrong someone are by harming them or by not being something they genuinely agreed to.
(3) A minimum wage stops an employer and a worker from agreeing, freely, to a wage below the floor.
(4) Nobody is made worse off by that particular agreement going ahead: the worker would rather have the job than be unemployed, the employer would rather make the hire than not, and no third party is touched by it either way.
(5) So nobody is harmed and both parties genuinely agree.
(6) So by (1) and (2) the agreement wrongs nobody -- and by (3) the minimum wage blocks it regardless.
(7) Anything that blocks a transaction which wrongs nobody is itself a wrong.
(8) So the minimum wage is wrong. Same story for rent control, energy price caps, anti-gouging rules after a flood -- wrong, all of them.

And look, part of me is fine with (8) for the minimum wage. But (1) through (8) also seem to show there's nothing at all to object to when a loan shark lends to someone who's skint, or a landlord lets a damp flat to a family with nowhere else to go, or a clinic buys a kidney off a bloke who needs the money for his daughter's operation -- so long as everyone "genuinely agrees" and nobody ends up worse off than their next-best option. That I don't buy for a second, and I don't think Dan does either. So something in there has to give.

My suspicion is (2), the bit about harm, because the others look almost definitional to me. But I can't make the objection land: every time I say "but he's exploiting her," Dan just says "compared to what? she's better off than her alternative, that's all 'harm' can mean here." Where does the slide actually happen -- is it the jump from (5) to (6), or (2), or the move from a single agreement to a rule that blocks a whole class of them? And whichever it is, what do I have to give up to reject it? I'd like to keep the rent control, if I'm honest, so I'd rather the fix didn't cost me that.
