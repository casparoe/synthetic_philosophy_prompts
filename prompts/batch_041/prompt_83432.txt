A colleague in my department, a computer scientist with a good nose for conceptual questions and no philosophical training, circulated a short explainer on the no-free-lunch theorems ahead of our reading group, and I would like a careful audit of it before I open my mouth in the room. Here is the substance of what he wrote, near enough to his words:

"The no-free-lunch theorems, published by Wolpert and Macready in 1997, are usually stated too weakly. What they really show is that, averaged over all possible problems, every learning algorithm and every optimization algorithm performs exactly as well as every other one, and not merely as well as its rivals but as well as random guessing. As the authors themselves concede, whatever an algorithm gains on one family of problems it gives back on the others; the moral is a conservation law. Nor does the result hold only in the aggregate: since the averaging is over all problems, no algorithm can beat random guessing on any particular problem either. This is Hume's problem of induction stated in mathematics. Hume asked what justifies our expectation that the future will resemble the past, and the no-free-lunch theorems answer: nothing. The only way induction could work is if the world happened to be structured so as to reward our particular biases, which is precisely the assumption Hume showed we are not entitled to make. The practical upshot is that the choice of an inductive bias is arbitrary, a matter of taste, convention, or luck rather than reason, and that machine learning has no objectively best method, only methods that happen to suit the world we find ourselves in."

I suspect the trouble is concentrated in the last two sentences, but I do not trust my own diagnosis and would not be surprised to learn that it starts earlier. What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on, not a survey of the literature and certainly not a reading list or citations, which I would have no way to check. Take the passage claim by claim, including what it attributes to the authors themselves, and tell me which parts a specialist in learning theory would endorse as stated, which are the sort of simplification a careful popularizer may reasonably permit, and which are genuine errors, and why. Where the passage is wrong, give me the correct statement; where it is right for a reason it obscures, supply the reason. Then tell me whether the moral drawn in the final two sentences survives the corrections or is a non sequitur that the real theorems do not license. I want you to commit to a definite verdict on each point and give your reasons, rather than rehearsing the range of opinion and leaving me to choose; if one of my suspicions turns out to be misplaced, say so plainly. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no bold.
