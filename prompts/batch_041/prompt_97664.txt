Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on Dutch book arguments again (like the fourth time, I never learn) and I want a straight ranking out of you instead of another 5000-word comment thread. Here's my list of the main flavors I can think of — tell me if I've merged two things that should be seperate, or split one thing that's really one:

1. The classic synchronic one: your credences violate the probability axioms, so there's a set of bets each of which looks favorable to you individually but which together guarantee you lose no matter how the world turns out.
2. The diachronic one aimed at people who don't conditionalize on their evidence — the book is arranged so that even if you see it coming, you're already committed, because you accepted the first bet before you knew which way the evidence would break.
3. The version that tries to dodge the objection that maybe you just don't care about money, or you're risk-averse, or your utility in dollars isn't linear, by either picking stakes you can't help but value or by dropping the actual betting and talking about purely hypothetical willingness to bet.
4. The one that goes after countable additivity: violate it and a bookie can pick you off with a countable infinity of bets.
5. The one that goes after someone who violates reflection, i.e. who thinks their future self will be systematically miscalibrated — book built across time and across possible future evidence.
6. The one that's supposed to underwrite decision theory itself rather than just credences: if your preferences violate the expected utility axioms, there's a book against you too.
7. The imprecise-probability case: someone whose credal state is a set of probability functions instead of a single one. My vague sense is the standard book doesn't obviously go through here and people fight about whether some weaker version does.
8. The scoring-rule / accuracy version, where instead of money you use something like a proper scoring rule and the incoherence shows up as your credences being accuracy-dominated rather than money-pumped.
9. The one people sometimes claim exists against someone who's perfectly coherent but just never updates on anything. I don't actually think that one works, but rank it anyway and tell me why I'm right or wrong.

What I want is a rating by how much each of these should actually move me — like, if I'm trying to become less of a mess epistemically, how much should item 3's repair job really convince me compared to item 1? I care way more about whether the thing survives the standard deflationary objections (bookie needs to know your credences, you have to be a compulsive bettor, money isn't utility, hypothetical bets are cheap talk) than about which one is historically the famous one.
