A colleague in my reading group keeps pressing a comparison on us, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. The comparison treats consciousness as the brain's user interface: the desktop, the icons, the folder you drag to the trash. The phrase is usually credited to Alan Kay, who used it for the graphical desktop at Xerox PARC — the interface presents a simplified, manipulable picture of a machine whose real operations look nothing like it. Tor Nørretranders turned the idea on the mind in his book The User Illusion, and something close to it now sits inside Daniel Dennett's deflationary program, along with his denial that there is any single place in the brain where experience is assembled.

Her argument has two steps. First, if consciousness is the brain's interface, then it is a summary and not a window: it exists to let the organism act, not to disclose the machinery that produces it. Second, the interface is transparent — we look through it rather than at it — which is why the hard problem feels intractable. On this view we are asking what the disk is made of when we should be asking why the folder looks yellow. Her conclusion is that the hard problem is a pseudo-problem.

A second colleague accepts the analogy and draws the opposite moral. If it is an interface, he says, then it is an interface to something; a desktop is not a fiction but a compressed picture of a real file system, and by the same token experience is a real, if heavily lossy, representation of real neural activity. On his reading the analogy preserves the hard problem rather than dissolving it: we still have to explain how a physical system comes to represent anything at all.

I would like an audit of the comparison, not a ruling on which of them is more congenial. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table. What plays the role of the hardware, the operating system, the interface elements, the user, the designer, and the act of clicking? Flag any slot in the source that has no obvious occupant in the target.
2. Say where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that are merely decorative from the ones doing real work for one of the two conclusions.
3. Say what the analogy quietly imports. The source has a designer with intentions, a user who is already an agent, a machine that can be run headless, an interface that can be redesigned or swapped out, a public and intersubjective surface, and a vantage point — the programmer's — from which the illusion can be seen through. For each of these, tell me whether the target really has it and whether either conclusion leans on it; add any candidate I have missed.
4. Tell me whether the analogy is underdetermining. Two people are using the same comparison to reach incompatible conclusions. Which features is each relying on, and does the analogy have the resources to decide between them, or is it doing no work at all?
5. Then take the two conclusions one at a time and say which survives the disanalogies, and what it has to be weakened to if it does not.
6. If there is a better comparison — one that keeps what is right about the interface picture while dropping what does not transfer — say what it is and what adopting it costs.

I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the analysis.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
