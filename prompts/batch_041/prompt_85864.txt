I am preparing an application for a one-semester internal research grant, with a small workshop attached, and I would like you to draft the proposal itself — roughly 1,500 words — from a half-formed idea of mine. Write the proposal, not a defence of the idea; I will revise the substance myself. Do not supply a bibliography or reading list, and do not attribute positions to named authors, since I will add the citations myself and I would rather not have plausible-looking attributions I then have to check. Treat me as someone who knows the primary text well and the secondary literature only in outline.

The domain is the cluster of questions about framework certainties running through Wittgenstein's last notes, the ones collected as On Certainty. The standing disagreement there is familiar: whether the propositions that stand fast for us are exempt from doubt by virtue of a logical or grammatical role, or are instead ordinary empirical commitments held with unusual tenacity, and correspondingly whether doubting or revising them is something for which one can be answerable at all. I do not want to relitigate that. I want to introduce a notion that, as far as I know, nobody has made anything of, and see what work it does. Call it the hinge tariff: the price a speaker pays, in the currency of the practice she is in, for refusing to grant some proposition the status of a hinge — the silence, the raised eyebrow, the redescription of her as unserious or as not really playing the game, the loss of standing to ask certain questions, the sheer inferential labour of rebuilding her position around the refusal.

The project is to develop that notion and put it to work on a question the existing debate leaves open: when the tariff on refusing a hinge is very high, is that a reason to think the hinge is true, or only a reason to think it is load-bearing? And can those two things be told apart by any method available to someone working inside the practice — by comparing cases where the tariff is high for different reasons, or by attending to what happens in communities where the same proposition functions as a hinge for some members and as a live hypothesis for others?

Please give me: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the positions already on offer leave it open; the method I would actually pursue across a semester and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives; what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure; and a candid assessment of scope — what can be done in a semester, what has to be cut, and at what point the project would need empirical work that this grant cannot buy. The proposal will be read by a general philosophy panel, so it should be intelligible to someone who works on none of this.
