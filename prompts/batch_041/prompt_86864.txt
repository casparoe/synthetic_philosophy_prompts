I am a novelist — four books, the last two about people who build things meant to outlast them — and for the past year I have sat as the lay member on the selection committee of the Threshold Prize, a foundation award for what its charter calls "the first synthetic living system." This year the committee is deadlocked over a single submission, and the chair has told me that my vote will settle it, which is precisely why I want the reasoning on paper before the meeting rather than reconstructed after it. The candidate is a stirred flow reactor containing short peptides, thioesters, and mineral grains. The mixture catalyses its own regeneration: strip out any one component and the network rebuilds it, so long as fresh reactants keep flowing through. Vary the flow rate or the temperature and the composition of the network drifts, and the drift is heritable — split the contents between two vessels and both lineages keep their compositional signatures for hundreds of "generations" in the lab's accounting. But the network has no genome, no membrane of its own, no ribosomes, and no metabolism in the textbook sense; it lives off the reactor's energy gradient; switch the pump off for an hour and it is simply gone; and the lab concedes it has never existed anywhere except inside their apparatus, and is descended from nothing. Two of my colleagues say that by the committee's long-standing working gloss — a chemical system that maintains itself far from equilibrium and is subject to Darwinian selection — the thing plainly qualifies and the prize is owed. Two others say we are looking at a glorified flame or a crystallisation front: an externally driven process whose "inheritance" is no more real than the shape a river delta passes on, and that awarding the prize would debase it. They keep invoking viruses and prions, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that analogy sharpens the case or muddies it.

Please draft the short written recommendation I will circulate before the vote. I need an actionable bottom line — award, withhold, or award under a reworded citation — with the reasoning laid out, and with the strongest version of the losing side stated honestly rather than waved away, since the two scientists I would be outvoting are people I respect and will have to sit beside for years to come. Because the foundation will quote the decision in its annual report and future committees will treat it as precedent, I also need you to say what criteria the committee should adopt going forward, and to be candid about whether "life" is the sort of question a committee can settle by definition at all, or whether we are really choosing a policy under a word that has no settled edges. Write it as a memo, not an essay: I am the only member who reads philosophy for pleasure, and everyone else will want to know within a page what they are being asked to vote for, and why.
