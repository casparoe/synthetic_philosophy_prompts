Things I've already got on my list, so you can see the granularity I'm after. Feel free to tell me some of them are dead ends:

- The September Programme: Riezler's memo of 9 September 1914, which Fischer treated as the smoking gun of premeditated annexationism and which a lot of later historians treat as a discussion document the government never actually adopted. How much can legitimately turn on one document?
- The December 1912 war council: Fischer and Röhl read it as a decision to prepare for war, others (Strachan, for one) read it as the Kaiser sounding off in front of his generals. Is that a live dispute or a settled one?
- Article 231, the clause everyone calls the war-guilt clause, which -- if you actually read it -- never uses the word guilt. Here it is: "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." So legal responsibility and moral accusation come apart at the seam. Does that distinction do real work for a general reader, or is it a gotcha?
- The collective-responsibility puzzle: can a nation be the bearer of blame at all, or only leaders, elites, structures, voters? The old problem of a decision that comes out of a room -- everyones hands on it, nobodys prints on it.
- The meta-question: is there any conceivable evidence that would settle this, or is the persistence of the argument itself the story?
