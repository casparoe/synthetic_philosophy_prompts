Exam question. The revealed preference approach in consumer theory takes an agent's choices as the basis for attributing preferences to her. (a) Explain what the approach claims about the relation between choice and preference, and what work the notion of "utility" does within it. (b) A critic argues that the approach must either treat preference as a claim about the agent's mind, in which case choice is only fallible evidence for it, or treat preference as nothing more than a summary of choice, in which case the theory says nothing about the agent and cannot be tested. Assess this dilemma. (c) Does the approach carry any commitment about whether agents are self-interested? Explain.

Student's answer. Revealed preference theory was introduced to rid consumer theory of the hedonistic psychology it had inherited from earlier utilitarianism. On the older view a choice was a fallible report on a quantity of felt satisfaction, so the theory needed a psychological premise about how much satisfaction each option yielded. On the newer view the choice itself is the datum: to say that an agent prefers A to B is to say that she chooses A when both are available, and the preference ordering is recovered from her choices. Utility therefore names a representation rather than a quantity of feeling. Any consistent ranking can be assigned numbers that increase with the ranking, and since those numbers are arbitrary up to any increasing transformation, the resulting utility is ordinal only. This is why the approach cannot underwrite the interpersonal comparisons that the older welfare economics wanted.

The critic's dilemma is real, but I think only the second horn is available. If preference is inferred from choice, the theory is best read as a definition of what it is to have a preference rather than as a hypothesis about a mental cause of behaviour; a disposition to choose in certain ways is what a preference is. It follows that the theory is a framework for organizing choice data rather than an empirical hypothesis, and that no pattern of choice could count as evidence against it, since any behaviour at all can be represented by some preference ordering. The one constraint the framework imposes is a consistency requirement: the weak axiom of revealed preference rules out an agent who chooses A over B, B over C, and C over A.

On (c), the approach does carry a commitment to self-interest. Since preferences are recovered from choices and the agent is the one doing the choosing, the preferences recovered are necessarily the agent's own, so the theory presupposes that agents maximize their own utility. Apparent altruism is reinterpreted rather than accommodated: the altruist chooses to help because helping satisfies her, so her choice still maximizes her own utility. Talk of social preferences in the empirical literature does not change this, since a preference for fairness is still the agent's own preference and satisfying it is still utility maximization for her.
