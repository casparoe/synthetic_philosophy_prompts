I have come to hold what I take to be Augustine's position on lying, and I want an honest map of what would count against it. The position, stated in his terms: the liar is the one who "has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind"; what makes the utterance a lie is not the falsity of what is said but the speaker's will, the fault being "the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind"; hence one can say what is false and not lie, if one believes it, and say what is true and lie, if one utters it in order to deceive; and this fault is never permissible, so that the lie told to save an innocent from a murderer is not an exception to the prohibition but a case in which the prohibition simply binds.

What I want is not a survey of the debate but an assessment of this position's exposure. Identify the observations, cases, arguments, or results that would count against it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward; say which of them a committed Augustinian would concede as genuine costs and which he would be entitled to dismiss, and on what grounds; and say plainly whether the position is falsifiable at all, or whether it survives by redescription.

I am especially interested in the following, and in whatever I have missed.

1. Cases in which the double heart and deception come apart. A falsehood entered in a private diary for no reader; a falsehood spoken to someone unconscious, or in a language the hearer will never understand; a false statement addressed to a machine. The speaker has the double heart but no one to deceive. Does the position classify these as lies? And in the other direction, does it really mean to count a true statement uttered in order to mislead as a lie, or has it quietly substituted a broader wrong of attempted deception for the wrong of lying?

2. The boundary at signification. Augustine extends lying to non-verbal signs, which still leaves outside the class of lies everything that deceives without signifying: misleading silence, selective disclosure, a staged scene, a feint, a decoy, a magician's misdirection, camouflage. If the wrong lies in the misuse of signs, then the deceiver who never signifies anything is less culpable than the bungling liar, which looks arbitrary; if the real wrong is deception as such, the view widens into a prohibition far more demanding than its adherents admit. I want to know whether the position collapses into a rival or can defend the special status of signification.

3. The falsehoods it cannot condemn. The propagandist who believes what he says is not lying on this account; nor is the ritual formula or diplomatic euphemism that everyone understands to be false and that no one intends anyone to be deceived by; nor, it seems, the joke. Does this mean the position fails to reach the most dangerous class of false speech, the sincerely believed one? If so, is that a defect of the position or evidence that it was never meant to do that work?

4. The classical counterexamples — the Hebrew midwives, Rahab, the lie to the murderer at the door — and the standard replies (that these are not lies at all, being figurative, or that the hearer has no right to the truth, or cannot use it). Are the replies principled, or is each case redescribed until it is no longer a lie? Is there any way of telling in advance which cases will be absorbed?

5. What the position predicts or requires that its rivals do not, and whether the resulting combination — forbidding what the consequentialist and the rights theorist permit, while excusing the sincere falsehood — is its signature or its embarrassment.

For what it is worth, I find (2) the most threatening and (5) the least; tell me if I have the weighting backwards, and name the single consideration that, if it held, should make me give the position up. I do not need citations or a literature review; I will supply those myself.
