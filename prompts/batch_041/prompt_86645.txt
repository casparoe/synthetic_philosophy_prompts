I have been trying to understand how Germany elects its parliament, and the underlying logic will not add up for me. Voters cast two votes, one for a local candidate and one for a party; seats are then apportioned so that each party ends up with roughly the share of seats its share of the party vote deserves, except that a party falling below five per cent of the national vote is excluded altogether, unless it wins three local districts outright, in which case it is admitted and seated in proportion to its vote after all. In 2021 that exception let a party with 4.9 per cent of the vote take 39 seats in a 735-seat chamber, a larger share of the seats than of the votes, whereas a party in a comparable position that won only two districts had just its two winners seated and nothing more. At one point the rules also allowed a party to lose a seat when its vote total rose, and the constitutional court struck that down as a violation of electoral equality. Then in 2024 the same court held that the five per cent threshold without the district exception was itself unconstitutional and ordered the old exception restored until parliament legislates anew.

What view of representation does this arrangement presuppose? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed: what a vote is taken to be, what a representative is taken to represent, whether the seat is owed to the party, the district, or the electorate as a whole, and why the same law can treat a small party's votes as insufficient in one case and a handful of districts as decisive in another. Which rival views does the arrangement rule out, which of its parts are strictly required by the theory and which merely happen to fit, and where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same rules? Finally, what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a strictly numerical conception of electoral equality, or by a strictly territorial one?
