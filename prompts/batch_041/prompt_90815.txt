I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book set in a Kent seaside town, and I've hit a wall that isn't a plotting problem. The book revolves around a man loosely based on my late uncle — mid sixties, ex-merchant navy, framed print of the little ships over the stairs, voted for Brexit, thinks the country has gone soft. His one move, at every family meal, is Dunkirk. 1940, we were on our own, nobody was coming, and a flotilla of ordinary blokes in their own boats just went and fetched the army off the beaches, and we got through it. So we'll get through this. He runs the same argument about the fishing quotas, about the council, about the fact that his daughter's job exists because of a grant from Brussels. Nobody's coming to save us. We did it before, we can do it again.

I don't want to write him as a fool. I want the strongest possible version of the argument — steelman it hard, the book definately dies if the reader smells me sneering — and then I want to know where it actually gives out, so his daughter can take it apart in chapter nineteen without me cheating on her behalf.

So: you're a historian of twentieth-century Britain who also works on argument by analogy, someone who's spent a career watching people pile more weight onto a historical comparison than it can bear. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the candidate correspondences before you commit to anything.

What I want:

- A correspondence table. The nation, the army, the sea, the boats, the enemy, "alone," the aftermath — what maps onto what in the thing he's actually claiming.
- Where the mapping breaks, then a clear separation between the breaks that matter for the work he's making the analogy do and the ones that are just decoration.
- What the analogy quietly imports that he never argued for.
- A verdict on the conclusion. Can this analogy carry "we can do it on our own," or is that conclusion riding on the analogy rather than on any of the history?

Two things I've turned up that I don't know what to do with, and you should tell me if either is just me being pedantic. First, the boats weren't quite the spontaneous volunteer flotilla he thinks — I gather alot of them were requisitioned, called into service. Second, a good many of the men lifted off those beaches weren't British at all. Does any of that touch the argument, or is it trivia?

And one more thing I can't make sit still: in the speech everyone knows — 4 June 1940, the House of Commons — Churchill called the whole business a colossal military disaster and told the House not to assign to the deliverance the attributes of a victory, since "wars are not won by evacuations." My uncle loves that speech. Is that caveat a rhetorical flourish, or does it actually break the analogy he's running?

If the analogy can't carry the weight, say so flatly and name the single disanalogy that does the most damage, because that's the line I'm giving the daughter. Don't hand me five breaks and leave me to choose, and don't hedge with "it depends what you mean by alone" unless you tell me which meaning saves it and which one kills it. If you're leaning on a historical detail you're not sure of, flag it in one line and move on — don't let that turn into hedging the verdict. And if there's a better analogy that would carry something like what he means, tell me what it is. No reading list please, I'm not going to get through a bibliography, I need the argument itself.
