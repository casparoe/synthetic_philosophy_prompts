Ok, longtime reader of the rationalist blogs here (I was lurking back when every other post was about decision theory), and I've got a puzzle plus a writeup of it that I want to sanity-check. I know this stuff well enough to smell when something's off, but not well enough to say exactly where.

The puzzle, as I have it: you're driving home and there are two intersections on the route, and at each one you can either turn off or keep going. Turning off at the first one gets you 0. If you keep going you reach the second, where turning off gets you 4, while continuing past it leaves you stuck in a bad neighborhood for 1. The gimmick is that you're "absent-minded": when you arrive at an intersection you can't tell whether it's the first or the second, they're indistinguishable to you, so whatever you do at one you'd do at the other.

A blogger I read summarized it like this, and I want to know how much of it to trust:

"This is the absent-minded driver problem. The surprising answer is that the driver should randomize: he continues with probability 2/3 and turns off with probability 1/3, which earns him an expected 4/3, better than always continuing (1) or always turning off (0). Since he turns off with probability 1/3, he makes it to the good outcome about a third of the time. The puzzle is meant to be deep, but really it's just about a guy who's forgotten where he is — a real driver would leave himself a note or check his phone and the whole thing dissolves. What's actually interesting is the inconsistency. If you reason act by act, at the first intersection you should obviously continue (turning off gets you 0) and at the second you should obviously turn off (4 beats 1), and if you did that you'd earn 4, better than any plan you can actually execute. So expected utility maximization gives contradictory advice here, which is why it's a classic argument that you have to stop evaluating acts and start choosing a policy, and why people say this is really just Newcomb's problem in disguise and that what you need is something like UDT. He'd do even better if he could commit to a plan in advance, which is the general lesson: don't ask what to do, ask what kind of agent to be."

So, claim by claim: what would a decision theorist sign off on, what would they wince at, and what's just wrong? And does the "expected utility maximization gives contradictory advice" conclusion actually follow from the parts of the summary that are true? I'm definately suspicious of the note thing and even more suspicious of the jump to UDT, but I can't articulate why, so I'd rather you show me where the actual joints are.
