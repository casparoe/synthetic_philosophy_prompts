I am a novelist, and I keep meeting a problem for which I suspect philosophers have a name, though I have not been able to find it. Two of my characters witness the same quarrel and give me incompatible accounts of it. Neither is lying and neither is simply misremembering; each report is faithful to what that person had reason to notice, and if I let either stand alone the scene is false in a way I can feel but cannot point to. The truth of the matter is not a compromise between the two accounts, nor a third account that supersedes them. It is the two held together — each true in its own respect, each false if taken as the whole.

I have heard this called the Rashomon effect, but that seems to me to say only that accounts conflict and no one can be trusted, whereas my instinct runs the other way: that there is a truth, that it is many-sided, and that a claim can be true from one standpoint and false from another without either standpoint being merely arbitrary. Nor is it the relativist claim that all accounts are equally valid, or the Hegelian claim that the contradiction is dissolved into a higher unity that cancels both.

Is there an established name for this, and more importantly, is there an account of how the standpoints are to be organised — what makes one standpoint legitimate rather than merely someone's opinion, and how the partial truths are supposed to combine into the whole? I do not want the bare label; I want the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Please answer in British English, and end with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the name you judge best, or 'none' if there is no established term.
