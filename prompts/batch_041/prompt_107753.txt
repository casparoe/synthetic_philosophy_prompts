I chair the panel that decides parole in my state, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's vote on whether to release one particular guy. I don't just want a recommendation from you, I want the reasoning I can actually defend to the other members and to the victim's family, who get to read whatever I write.

Here's the mess. We score every case with the state's risk instrument, and it's handed back a high-risk number for him, which under our rules is a presumption against release. But I got hold of the validation manual for the instrument, and the sample it was built on doesn't include anyone with his profile at all, so its number for him is extrapolation at best, maybe just noise. Meanwhile the things I actually weigh: eleven years clean inside, a psych evaluation that reads as genuinely positive rather than boilerplate, a job and a sister lined up on the outside, and in the interview he was... I don't know how to put it, he was present. My honest first-order read is that he's low risk and should go home.

Now the part that's eating me. We did an internal audit last year, and it turns out that when this board overrides the instrument toward release, those people recidivate at a rate noticeably higher than the instrument's own high-risk band would predict. So the evidence says that when I and people like me trust our gut in exactly this kind of case, our gut is worse than the tool. And I can feel myself about to do exactly that. So I've got a belief (he's safe) and I've also got good reason to think that belief is the output of a process that's unreliable in this very situation. Someone on the board said there's a name for holding both at once and then we moved on to lunch.

But it's not even clean, because I don't fully trust the audit either: it's small, it lumps every override together regardless of why we overrode, and if the instrument was never validated on his group then maybe the audit's the-tool-is-better conclusion doesn't apply to him in the first place. So now I have evidence about the reliability of my evidence about my reliability, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a real regress or me looking for a way to do what I already wanted to do.

What I need: the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should land on one concrete course of action, grant, deny, grant with conditions (say which conditions), or defer, with a time frame, and it should be something the other members can argue with rather than a sermon. Engage the strongest case for the losing side honestly. If you're telling me to deny, take seriously that we'd be keeping a probably-low-risk man locked up another three years on the strength of a number that may not even apply to him; if you're telling me to grant, take seriously that I might be doing the exact thing the audit warns about and dressing it up in philosophy. And say something about what this board should do going forward when its own judgment and its instrument disagree, because this is going to happen again next month whether or not we get this one right.
