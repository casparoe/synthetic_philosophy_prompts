It's meant to be a provocative piece rather than a scholarly one, so don't worry about situating it in the literature — argue with it directly. Please focus on three things: (1) whether the physics and the entropy accounting are actually correct, especially where I claim the standard resolution of Maxwell's demon is stated in the wrong currency; (2) whether my concluding thesis is a substantive claim or just a redescription of the usual one; and (3) which single paragraph is weakest and what you would do with it. Here is the draft.

The Demon's Overdraft

Maxwell's demon sorts fast molecules from slow ones and so builds a temperature difference without doing work. The received resolution is that the demon must eventually erase its memory, and that erasure costs at least kT ln 2 per bit, which cancels the profit. I want to argue that this resolution is stated in the wrong currency. What the demon spends is not erasure but recording. A memory that holds a bit is a physical system with a definite state, and to load it with a fact about the gas is to put it into one of two states correlated with the gas; what changes is the entropy of the demon-plus-memory system, not some abstract ledger. Erasure is merely the reversal of that operation, and it is expensive for the same reason that recording was profitable.

So the interesting question is not how the demon pays, but when. Call a demon that extracts its work first and defers erasure an overdraft demon. Its books look alarming: it takes kT ln 2 out of the gas on each cycle and pays nothing until the end. I claim this is not merely a bookkeeping artifact. If the demon's memory is finite — say one bit, the smallest useful demon — then it can win exactly once: after a single sorting decision the memory is saturated and no further measurement can be recorded. A one-bit demon is therefore not a perpetual motion machine but a battery with capacity kT ln 2, and this, rather than Landauer's bound, is what does the real work in the argument. If the memory is unbounded, the demon can run for as long as its memory lasts, and the debt falls due only when the memory is full.

This makes the second law look like a statement about the demon's memory rather than about the gas: a constraint on how much a bounded observer can extract before it must stop, not on what the world can do. I call this thermodynamic externalism, and I take it to be the genuinely interesting lesson of the demon. The gas is not what enforces the law; the observer's finitude is.

Be blunt about where this goes wrong, and if the argument can be repaired rather than abandoned, tell me which repair you'd make.
