I am a novelist, and the disagreement I want adjudicated is not mine: it broke out between two friends who had both read the manuscript of my current book, and I stood by while they went at it. I have no stake in which of them is right — I can write the scene either way — and I have tried to report both of them as fairly as I can.

The scene: two accomplices in a robbery, who have never met before the job and cannot communicate, are put in separate rooms at the end of it. On a signal, each must press one of two buttons. If both press HOLD, each leaves with $200,000. If both press BOLT, each leaves with $40,000. If one holds while the other bolts, the one who bolted leaves with $350,000 and the one who held leaves with nothing. The two men rank the four outcomes identically — bolting while the other holds is best, mutual holding next, mutual bolting after that, being left with nothing worst — but the money is worth very different amounts to them, since one is drowning in debt and the other is comfortable, so the temptation to bolt presses far harder on one than on the other.

(1) Nadia, who teaches game theory, said the scene writes itself. Bolting strictly dominates: whatever the other man does, each does better by bolting. So both bolt, both leave with $40,000, and the point of the scene is exactly that two rational men cannot do better.

(2) Peter, whose background is in Kant, said the dominance argument assumes the two choices are independent, and they are not. The two men are in the same situation under the same description, and if the description is complete enough to fix what one of them should do, it fixes what both should do. So one man's choice is evidence about the other's: conditional on his holding, the other holds; conditional on his bolting, the other bolts. The real choice is therefore between the two diagonal outcomes, and $200,000 beats $40,000. This is the position usually called superrationality, he said, and it is the same reasoning that tells you to take one box in Newcomb's problem, where a reliable predictor has already filled the boxes.

(3) Nadia answered that he has assumed the correlation he needs. The argument goes through only if the two men reason identically, and here they plainly do not: their stakes differ, so the thing each is calculating — his own interest — is a different thing in each case. And even if we made them twins, the argument would not single out holding, because mutual bolting is as much a fixed point of identical reasoning as mutual holding is. Peter's principle does not derive cooperation from rationality; it presupposes a rule of reasoning that already contains it.

(4) Peter replied that the fixed point objection is empty: of course mutual bolting is a fixed point — that is the bad outcome — and the question is which of the two patterns a man should choose to be part of. As for the asymmetry, it is beside the point. Superrationality requires that the two share a ranking of the outcomes, not that they share stakes; unequal stakes govern how strongly each is tempted, not what the shared rule should be.

(5) Nadia said that if a shared ranking is all it takes, the principle has no stopping point. Whether two people count as being in the same situation is either settled by the fact that they would reason alike, in which case the argument is circular, or it is an empirical question about how their deliberations are in fact correlated, in which case Peter owes an account of the correlation — and in this scene he has none, because the correlation is my stipulation as the author, not a fact about the two men. Let a little slippage in, and the correlation decays until dominance reasserts itself.

(6) Peter answered that a correlation does not need a mechanism to be real. If both men arrive at their decisions by the same deliberation from the same description, they are correlated as tightly as two things can be — more tightly than any predictor's track record. The disagreement, he said, is not about logic at all; it is about whether such people exist.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, including the moves that are half right. I do not want a general essay on the prisoner's dilemma or on decision theory; I want a verdict on the arguments as they were actually made. Give it to me as structured data rather than prose: return a single JSON object with exactly the following fields, and no others.

- verdict_summary: string, no more than 150 words, giving your overall verdict before the details.
- move_ratings: array with one object per numbered move, in order. Each object has:
  - move: the move number, as an integer.
  - speaker: "Nadia" or "Peter".
  - claim: one sentence restating the move in your own words.
  - rating: exactly one of "clearly mistaken", "partly mistaken", "defensible but incomplete", "clearly right".
  - reason: two to four sentences justifying the rating, saying both what the move gets right and what it overlooks.
- crux: string, one or two sentences naming the single question the dispute actually turns on.
- nadia_misses: string, one or two sentences on what Nadia fails to see.
- peter_misses: string, one or two sentences on what Peter fails to see.
- what_would_settle_it: string, one or two sentences on what fact or argument would decide the crux.
- overall: string, two or three sentences naming who is more right overall and why.
