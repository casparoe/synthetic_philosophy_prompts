I am a novelist at work on a book set in an imaginary archipelago of merchant city-states, the League of Free Ports, which has for two generations outsourced its defense to hired fleets and treated war as a regrettable but purely instrumental interruption of trade. In drafting a pivotal chapter, I want the intellectual pressure of the chapter to come from Hegel's account of war in the Philosophy of Right—specifically his insistence that war is not simply an external calamity or a moral failure but can have an ethical and constitutive role in the life of a state, exposing the vanity of private concerns and binding citizens to a larger whole. I am not looking for a summary of Hegel's position; I already have a rough grasp of it. What I need is your best reconstruction of the most important implications of that position for a polity like my League, which has tried to purchase security without accepting the deeper demands Hegel associates with war. Suppose the League is forced into a genuine existential conflict, and its leaders must decide whether to conscript citizens, suspend commerce, and subordinate private property to the collective effort. What follows from Hegel's view about the likely moral and political consequences of that choice—for the League's internal cohesion, for the authority of its governing council, for the meaning of citizenship among people who had thought of themselves chiefly as traders, and for the possibility that a successful war might either corrupt or regenerate the League? Please write no more than 350 words, in one continuous paragraph, and lead with the single most important implication before turning to the others.
