Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about a set of choices he had me make, and I would like an outside ruling on what principle, if any, my answers express. The stakes were in dollars, small enough to be hypothetical and large enough that I answered as myself rather than as a game theorist. Offered a sure $5,000 or an 80 percent chance at $7,000, I take the sure $5,000. Offered a 25 percent chance at $5,000 or a 20 percent chance at $7,000, I take the 20 percent chance at $7,000, and without hesitation. Offered what he insists is the same pair again, but in two stages — first a 75 percent chance that the whole thing is void and I get nothing, then, only if I get through, a choice between $5,000 for sure and an 80 percent chance at $7,000 — I take the sure $5,000 at the second stage, and I do not think of that as the same choice as the one above at all. Offered a sure $5,000 against a ticket carrying an 89 percent chance of $5,000, a 10 percent chance of $7,000, and a 1 percent chance of nothing, I take the $5,000; but when that 89 percent chance of $5,000 is replaced by an 89 percent chance of nothing, leaving a 1 percent chance of $5,000 and a 10 percent chance of $7,000 against a flat 11 percent chance of $5,000, I take the ticket with the $7,000 in it. I will also pay $300 to insure against a 1 percent chance of losing $10,000, and I will pay $50 of real money to have one of these gambles resolved by a spin I do not watch rather than one I do, even though the payout is identical either way. My friend says my first two answers cannot both stand under the independence axiom, that my fourth and fifth cannot either, that the two-stage case shows I am not even describing a single choice consistently, and that the whole pattern is nothing but risk aversion plus a taste for long shots. The best principle I can offer on my own behalf is that I buy certainty when it is available and buy long shots when it is not, which sounds to me like two rules wearing one coat. What I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that actually generates these verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause, what it predicts about a case I have not listed, and an honest statement of which verdict I have to give up.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; state the principle itself in the first item and the verdict you cannot save in the last. American English, please.
