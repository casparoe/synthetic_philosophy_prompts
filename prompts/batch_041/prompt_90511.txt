I am teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and I want to build the term around Arthur Miller's The Crucible as a case study in presentism. The play is almost always taught as an allegory for McCarthyism, and I want to get past that reading rather than repeat it. My real interest is historiographical: Miller reconstructs Salem in 1692 as a parable for his own moment, and to do it he alters the record — he invents the affair between Proctor and Abigail, adjusts the ages and motives of the accusers, and turns what were in large part property disputes into a drama of individual conscience.

Please read the play as an act of historiography rather than as political allegory. Begin by separating two things the word presentism is asked to carry: the descriptive claim that we cannot help understanding the past through present-day categories, and the evaluative claim that we ought to judge the past by present-day moral standards. Say which, if either, the play is committed to, and whether the two can be pried apart. Notice that the play is presentist in both directions at once — it judges 1692 by the standards of its own time, and it invites us to judge its own time by the moral clarity of the play — and tell me whether that doubling vindicates presentism or exposes what it costs. Point me to the scenes that carry the most presentist weight, and say where the play resists the reading. Finally, since a historian is not free to invent: is there anything the play shows about the past that a scrupulous contextualist would have to suppress?

Take a definite position — is the play's presentism a virtue or a vice, and what do we give up by conceding the point — and defend it. Do not survey the debate; commit to a reading.
