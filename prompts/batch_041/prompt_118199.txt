The thesis I'm defending: HCH gets sold as an idealization of deliberation -- "here's what a perfectly patient reasoner would conclude, if it could decompose any question into subquestions small enough to answer and then aggregate" -- and I think that's the wrong kind of idealization. In the HCH scheme the decomposition of every question is handed to you for free at each node: you, the human, just get to *see* how to break the question up, and you never have to notice that the question was badly posed in the first place. So HCH is really an idealization of checking, or of an already-solved computation, not of thinking -- because thinking is mostly the search for the decomposition. A proof-checker is a great idealization of *verifying* a proof and a useless one of *finding* one. And when people point at HCH as the target for "what would a safe superintelligence actually do," they inherit that gap.
