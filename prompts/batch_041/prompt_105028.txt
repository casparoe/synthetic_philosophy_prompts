I'm putting together a seminar on whether the badness of pain is baked into the feeling itself or comes from our attitude toward it, and I want a case built to order instead of the usual tired examples. Raw material: there are neurological patients — I'll describe the syndrome rather than name it — who, after damage to a particular bit of cortex, will tell you a pinprick or a burn hurts, they can localize it and rate how intense it is, but it doesn't bother them at all; they dont pull away, sometimes they even laugh it off. So we seem to have the hurt without the minding.

Build me one thought experiment in which these three verdicts fall out of the theories rather than out of my stipulation: (1) a hedonist who says a pain is bad just in virtue of how it feels has to call the state bad; (2) a desire/attitude theorist who says it's bad because the subject minds it has to call that same state not bad for that subject; (3) a representationalist who says it's bad because it represents bodily damage lands on a third verdict — tell me which and why. One paragraph, physically possible, and no cheating by making the patient misreport or their introspection unreliable. Then show your work: each verdict and the one feature of the case doing the driving. Finish with the hedonist's best comeback — can she redescribe the patient as keeping the unpleasantness and losing only the motivation, and if so, is the case still worth anything?
