I teach human factors in a simulation lab, and I keep running into a puzzle about how we treat checklists. In aviation, the sterile cockpit rule forbids nonessential conversation during taxi, takeoff, and landing, and pilots are required to call out certain speeds and altitudes; in surgery, teams now run a verbal safety checklist before incision, and nurses are expected to speak up if a step is skipped. When a checklist is well designed and the team follows it, we treat it as part of the operator’s competence: we say the pilot or the surgeon was thinking with the checklist, and we train people to rely on it rather than on memory. But when something goes wrong, the explanation often shifts. If the checklist was ambiguous or badly placed, investigators blame the design or the institution; if the operator skipped a step, they blame the individual. The flight recorder, by contrast, is never treated as part of anyone’s mind; it is just evidence. I want to know what philosophical commitments this whole practice presupposes.

Does it presuppose the extended mind thesis, or only a weaker claim about distributed cognition or cognitive offloading? Does it presuppose that the boundary of the cognitive system is fixed by biology, by function, or by social attribution? And does it commit us to a view about agency and responsibility: that we are responsible for the parts of our cognitive system we have voluntarily incorporated, but not for the ones that fail us? Which rival views of mind and agency does the practice quietly rule out, and which is it merely compatible with? I am especially interested in the apparent inconsistency: we seem to enlarge the cognitive boundary when things go well and shrink it when things go badly. Is that a coherent way to assign responsibility, or does it reveal that the practice is philosophically underdetermined? Finally, suppose a hospital or airline adopted a strict brain-bound view of cognition, treating the checklist as a mere external aid and the operator’s memory as the only genuine locus of cognition. What would have to change in training, in incident investigation, and in liability? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice could be rebuilt on a different foundation without much friction.
