Two colleagues of mine in a philosophy of mind reading group spent most of a session arguing about Moore's paradox. The disagreement was not about whether the relevant sentences are absurd, but about what the absurdity consists in. I was taking notes and have reproduced the exchange as fairly as I can.

A began: The oddity is purely pragmatic. The conjunction of the proposition that it is raining with the proposition that I do not believe it is raining can be true—people can fail to believe a truth—and it is not contradictory. What is absurd is the act of asserting it. When you assert the first conjunct you represent yourself as believing it; the second conjunct then denies that very commitment. So the paradox is a matter of conversational implicature, not semantics.

B replied: That cannot be the whole story. The absurdity survives when the conjunction is not asserted but merely considered as a belief. Try to believe that it is raining and that you do not believe it is raining. That does not seem like a merely awkward assertion; it seems like something you cannot coherently believe. And the commissive version—asserting that it is raining while avowing the belief that it is not raining—has the same air, even though it is not just a failure to believe but a positive conflicting belief. So the problem is not just about assertion; it is about the relation between first-person belief and the content believed.

A responded: The belief case is not decisive. We can believe all sorts of things that we cannot assert. What you are calling the impossibility of belief may just be the impossibility of simultaneously occupying the first-person perspective and the third-person perspective on the same belief. The first conjunct is expressed from within the belief; the second conjunct reports on the belief from outside. That is a perspective clash, not a logical contradiction. I would add a two-slot view: the first conjunct occupies the world-directed slot, the second the self-directed slot, and the paradox is that the same utterance cannot fill both slots with the same proposition and remain coherent. But that is still a pragmatic phenomenon.

B came back: The two-slot view is illuminating, but it does not explain why the clash is unavoidable. And embedding shows the problem is not semantic: if it is raining but I do not believe it, then I am in for a surprise—that conditional is perfectly intelligible. The absurdity is tied to the unembedded first-person present-tense assertion, which suggests a norm of assertion—perhaps the norm that you must believe what you assert—rather than a semantic contradiction. But then the norm itself needs a rationale. Is it constitutive of assertion, or merely a conversational regularity?

A: If you make it constitutive, you owe an account of why it is constitutive. I can imagine a community that asserts things they do not believe, as a game or a ritual. The Moorean sentence would be odd for us, but not because it violates the essence of assertion. And the embedded case shows that there is no semantic defect. So I still say: semantics is clean, pragmatics does the work.

B: And I still say: pragmatics cannot do all the work, because the commissive version—asserting that it is raining while avowing the belief that it is not raining—is not just a failure to believe but a positive conflicting belief. That looks closer to a contradiction in the speaker's commitments, not just a conversational misfire. And there are cases where a Moorean sentence is true and yet we would not say the speaker has made a mistake about the weather; we would say they are in a peculiar epistemic position. So the paradox is about the norms of belief, not just assertion.

That is roughly where they left it. I would like a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the main moves—A's pragmatic diagnosis, B's belief-case objection, A's perspective-clash and two-slot reply, B's embedding argument and norm-of-assertion proposal, A's constitutive-norm challenge, and B's commissive-version point—say who has the better of it and why. Then say what both are missing: is there a formulation of the paradox that makes the semantic, pragmatic, and epistemic dimensions fit together, or is one of them fundamental? I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want an assessment of the arguments as they stand. Be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and if the dispute is framed in a way that makes a clean verdict impossible, or if one of the premises is confused, say so plainly rather than forcing a winner. Where the argument would need to go next is also of interest.
