I have just taken a seat on the allocation committee of the worker cooperative I joined this year, and the experience has pushed me back into the socialist calculation debate in a personal way: I need to know how much of the case against planning I actually believe, since it bears on whether we should introduce internal transfer prices between our teams or keep allocating by committee judgment. To test myself I built the argument below, and it now looks to me as though it proves too much.

(1) Rational use of scarce resources requires comparing the opportunity costs of alternative uses. (2) Opportunity costs are comparable only where there are exchange ratios, that is, prices, that track relative scarcities. (3) Prices tracking relative scarcities emerge only from decentralized exchange among parties who own what they trade. (4) Therefore any allocation not governed by such prices is arbitrary: whatever it yields, we cannot say it is better than the uses it forgoes. (5) A firm, however, allocates internally by direction rather than exchange — a manager reassigns a worker or a machine with no purchase or sale — and every committee I have sat on does the same. (6) By (1)–(4), such allocation must be arbitrary, hence inferior to running everything through market contracts. (7) Yet firms persist and frequently beat a pure nexus of contracts on cost, and our teams do function; on (1)–(4) that would be a standing miracle. (8) So one of (1)–(4) is false — and yet I still take the calculation argument to be the strongest case against a fully planned economy, and I am reluctant to give it up.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (3) than (7). Tell me what would change your mind about where the error lies, and where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong.
