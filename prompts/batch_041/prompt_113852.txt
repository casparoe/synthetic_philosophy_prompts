I teach a formal epistemology seminar, and I'm also one of those philosophers who did a CS degree first and never fully let go of it, so every time I teach the agreement theorem I hear it in the vocabulary of distributed systems and then spend the rest of the week wondering how much of it actually survives that translation. So here's what I'd like from you. Take the result that two agents with a shared prior cannot end up with different posterior credences (or, if it really is a generalization and not just a relabeling, different posterior expectations of a random variable) when those posteriors are common knowledge between them, and restate it as faithfully as you can in the terms I actually think in: nodes with local states and histories, information as what's in a node's view, common knowledge as the fixed point where everyone knows that everyone knows and so on, full-information broadcast and gossip, and the two-generals / coordinated-attack business where common knowledge over a lossy channel is unreachable no matter how many rounds you run. Assume I'm solid on protocols and on CRDT-style convergence, I remember what a partition is, and I'm rusty on the measure theory — so if the meeting-of-partitions machinery has no real counterpart in a node's local state, say so instead of fudging it, and if the theorem's notion of common knowledge is doing something the protocol literature's version doesn't, tell me which one.

Then the part I actually care about, which is the residue report. Which of the theorem's claims come through cleanly as statements about what nodes must converge to; which survive only as distortions; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. My suspicion — and I want you to tell me if it's wrong — is that the whole load is carried by the common prior, and that once you drop it you're no longer talking about disagreement between agents but about how the agents were built, which is not a claim about rationality at all. Related thing I keep going back and forth on and my notes on it are a mess: the protocol literature hands me impossibility results (you cannot get common knowledge over a lossy channel) whereas the theorem hands me a consistency constraint on beliefs that were never communicated in the first place — is that a deep difference, or am I just conflating cannot-know with cannot-rationally-disagree? And there's a friend in the systems group who insists the whole theorem is just consensus with extra steps; if he's basically right, say so plainly and tell me what's left over for us, and if he's not, tell me what my framework is blind to and whether that blindness is a defect of the framework or a sign that the theorem is doing more than it looks like it's doing. Don't smuggle the philosophical assumptions into the translation and call that a translation — I'd rather have an honest list of what gets lost.
