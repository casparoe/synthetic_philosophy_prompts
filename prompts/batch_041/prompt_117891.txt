You are a philosopher of mind with a second speciality in the philosophy of biology, and for this task you are acting as a hostile-audience consultant. A colleague has asked you to prepare her for the discussion period after a conference talk, and the value of your work depends entirely on how accurately you model the people in the room — not on how thoroughly you summarise the literature. Work in two stages: first reason through who these people are, what they already believe, and which moves will strike them as a category mistake; only then write the dossier. Their vocabulary is the constraint on how every objection must be phrased. Do not pad the dossier with general advice about public speaking, and do not give her a summary of her own view.

**The talk.** She defends what she calls the loop-first account of representational content. She is content to be described as a teleosemanticist in the Millikan-style etiological tradition, she accepts Dretske's thought that content has something to do with what a state carries information about, and she rejects the asymmetric-dependence strategy familiar from Fodor as unnecessary. Her claims:

1. The etiological tradition assigns content by asking what a producer state was selected for. That is the wrong unit of selection. Selection does not act on producer states; it acts on whole sensorimotor loops. There is no fact of the matter about what a state was selected for, only about what a loop was selected to do.
2. Content is therefore assigned to a state relative to a loop, and is fixed by the task-variable the loop is selected to track: the variable whose value, if the consumer's response were adjusted to it, would keep the response adaptive across the range of conditions in which the loop was fixed.
3. Content is fixed by the error space rather than the success space. What determines content is the class of situations the loop was selected to keep the organism out of, not the class it was selected to bring about. A state means whatever has to be true of the situation for the response it triggers to be non-maladaptive.
4. Misrepresentation is cheap on this account: a state misrepresents when it occurs in a situation outside the error space, so that the response it triggers is maladaptive. No extra machinery is needed.
5. Two stress cases she will present, both her own inventions and neither drawn from any published study. The two-channel case: an imaginary burrowing crustacean detects a predatory flatworm through two independent channels, a chemical trace and a substrate vibration, either of which triggers the same burrow-deepening response; a producer-side account seems to give the two states different contents, hers gives them one. The dual-role case: a single hunger state in an imaginary social insect figures in two different loops with different error spaces, a foraging loop and a brood-tending loop, so her account seems to give one state two contents.
6. She also claims the account covers learned content without invoking selection within an individual's lifetime, by treating reinforcement and error-driven learning as within-lifetime analogues of selection acting on the loop. She knows this is the weakest part of the package.

**The room.** A joint meeting: the weekly seminar of a department of ecology and evolutionary biology, most of whose members work on animal signalling, alarm calls, and the evolution of communication, plus two philosophers of mind invited by the local philosophy group — one a committed representationalist of the Fodorian sort who thinks naturalistic theories of content are a dead end, and one who works on predictive processing and enactivism and thinks representation talk should be abandoned. The biologists know selected-effects accounts of function intimately, use them daily, and are sceptical about importing them into psychology. Nobody in the room works on teleosemantics; she is the only person present who has read that literature. The audience is allergic to jargon and quick to ask what could be measured.

**What to produce.** A prep dossier with these parts.

1. Your read of the room in three to five sentences, before any objections: what these people will hear when she says content.
2. What she should concede in her first two minutes, and what she should state up front to pre-empt.
3. Eight to ten objections she will actually get, ordered by expected damage (likelihood multiplied by cost), grouped by who raises them and phrased the way that person would phrase it. For each: the objection in the questioner's voice in one to three sentences; a damage rating with a one-clause justification; a two-minute reply in her voice; and one line on where the reply is soft — what the questioner will say next. At least two objections must come from the signalling biologists, at least two from the Fodorian, at least one from the enactivist, and at least one from someone who will not speak in the room but will corner her in the corridor afterwards.
4. The three objections that look fatal but are not, and what makes them survivable.
5. The one question she is hoping nobody asks, why it is the dangerous one, the best answer available, and what that answer costs her.
6. Two sentences she must not say — the ones that will hand the room an easy win.

Constraints. No citations, no reading lists, no names of living researchers, no quotations attributed to anyone, no appeal to a specific study, date, or figure — she will be standing at a podium with nothing in front of her, so anything she cannot reconstruct from memory is useless. Attribute objections to roles in the room, not to individuals. Mark clearly which objections are really about biological function and which are really about indeterminacy, since the concessions differ. Where a reply would need an empirical fact she could not be expected to have to hand, say so rather than assert the fact. Use the two stress cases in at least two of the objections, and say plainly which way each case cuts.
