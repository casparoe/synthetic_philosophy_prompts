I'm putting together materials for a reading group on animal ethics — we've done factory farming to death, so i want to try something further out. Could you write a dialogue between two people arguing about whether we have any obligation to reduce suffering in the wild? Take the role of someone who knows both animal welfare science and ecology/conservation biology well enough to argue either side from the inside, and think carefully before you start writing — maybe sketch the strongest version of each position in a few bullet points first, then write the dialogue itself.

Character A is a wild animal welfare researcher: she spends her days running nociception assays on insects and writing grant proposals for "humane" interventions. Her case runs roughly like this — most animals born in the wild don't make it, and the typical death is being eaten alive or starving over days; natural selection only cares about getting genes into the next generation, so there's no reason to think it tuned hedonic set points to be positive by default, since being chronically hungry and jumpy is a perfectly good way to make an organism reproduce; and since r-strategists like fish, insects and rodents vastly outnumber the mammals we sentimentalize, the total quantity of suffering is enormous and almost entirely invisible. So she wants contraceptives and vaccines for wildlife, food supplementation to get juveniles past the starvation bottleneck, maybe even gene drives that dial down pain perception, and she thinks "leave nature alone" is a bit like leaving a burning building alone because the fire got there first. Character B is a conservation biologist who drifted into philosophy — not a sentimental nature-lover, she'll happily grant that nature is carnage. Her objections: the welfare comparison is uncalibrated, since we have no idea whether a fish's hunger is anything like ours, and if cross-species comparison fails then you can't call the total "enormous," only unknown; A is smuggling in a hedonist theory of the good when for wild animals the relevant good may be species-typical flourishing, in which case a salmon that dies swimming upstream has had a good life rather than a bad one; and every intervention creates an indefinite management obligation — once you start feeding the juveniles you own that population forever, and our record with well-meaning introductions (cane toads, rabbits) doesn't inspire confidence.

Let them actually engage rather than trade monologues: have B press A hard on the calibration problem and let A give a real answer, have A press B on whether "flourishing" is just a way of dignifying suffering you'd rather not count, and let them find at least one place where they discover they were talking past each other. Neither should win by cheap rhetoric or by being dumb. I want to see where the disagreement bottoms out, and wether they turn out to disagree about the facts, about the theory of welfare, or about what we owe under deep uncertainty — and don't have them converge at the end just to be polite.
