I am a graduate student in philosophy, and I am taking a seminar on aesthetics that has turned into a running argument outside class. At our reading group, we recently discussed a contemporary novel—call it The Inventory—that consists almost entirely of a warehouse inventory list, repeated with small variations, punctuated by a handful of fragmented memories. The author has said in interviews that she wrote it while grieving her father, and that the repetition was the only way she could get the feeling down. Most of us found the book cold and tedious; two people in the group found it devastating. This split produced an argument between Mira and Jonah that neither could resolve, and I was asked to referee but honestly could not. Mira argued that the novel is art because it is a sincere expression of the author's grief; the author's own account of writing it, she said, shows that the feeling is really there, and the fact that most readers miss it only shows that they are not the right readers. Jonah replied that sincerity is not enough: art is not merely expression but communication, and if the feeling does not actually pass from the work to an audience, then the work has failed as art. Mira responded that this makes art depend on the audience's capacities, which seems backwards—surely a song can be art even if no one ever hears it. Jonah said that a song no one hears is a score, not yet art, and that a private code that only the author understands is not art either. Jonah added that genuine art must be accessible to ordinary people, not just a coterie of critics; otherwise it is counterfeit, a mere imitation of art. Mira said that if accessibility were required, most of the canon would be non-art, and that this consequence is absurd. A third member, Priya, then suggested that the novel is art but bad art: it is sincere and formally daring, but it fails to infect most readers, and for that reason it is a flawed artwork rather than a non-artwork. Jonah insisted that on his view a failed infection is not bad art but failed art, while Mira said that if bad art is still art, then art cannot be defined by successful communication. At that point they began arguing about whether the moral quality of the feeling matters: Jonah said that art which transmits cruelty is not true art at all, while Mira said that moral evaluation is separate from the question of what art is.

I would like you to work through this exchange step by step and give a verdict on each of the main claims. Please act as a philosopher specializing in aesthetics and the theory of art, and begin by brainstorming the different definitions of art that might be in play—expression, communication, infection, formal achievement, institutional recognition, and so on—so that your analysis is explicit about its assumptions. Then assess each move, saying how defensible it is and why, and identify any point at which one of them is tacitly shifting the question. Finally, say who has the stronger overall position, and what both Mira and Jonah are missing. I am especially interested in whether the same object can be art in one sense and not art in another, and how the answer changes if we take seriously the idea that art is essentially a means of transmitting feeling rather than a kind of object or a kind of intention.
