I spent thirty-five years as a soil ecologist and retired a couple years back, and since then I've gotten weirdly deep into philosophy — animal ethics mostly, then the little corner of it that asks what we owe insects, which is where you come in. My old colleague Dana is retiring next month after a long career in agricultural entomology, most of it spent building integrated pest management programs for stone fruit growers — in plain terms, working out exactly how little insecticide you can spray and still bring in a crop. I've been asked to give the toast at her dinner, five minutes or so. The room is about half entomologists and IPM people who know every acronym and have opinions about each one, and half her family — her sister, two grandkids, some cousins from out of state who think she 'worked with bugs.' I want to honor her honestly, and thats the whole problem. I've been reading about whether insects can suffer, whether a bug is the kind of thing that can have a good of its own, and if the answer is anything like yes, then her life's work — which I really do admire, and which cut pesticide use way down, which was good for the farmworkers and the birds and the bees — still added up to an enormous amount of killing, and I can't figure out how to hold both of those in one short speech without either lying by omission or turning her retirement dinner into a seminar on invertebrate nociception. So: can you write the toast for me?

Please take the role of a philosopher who actually works on the moral status of animals and has thought seriously about invertebrates — not someone who'll wave it off with 'insects probably don't feel anything.' Start by brainstorming the real options I have as the speaker — ignore the moral question entirely, allude to it, name it head-on, reframe it as a tribute to restraint, whatever else you see — and say what each one costs and which you'd choose and why. Then write the toast itself, in American English, at a natural spoken length of about five minutes. It has to land for both halves of the room: the IPM crowd should hear it as respect for the craft and for the real good the work did, and the family should hear a warm tribute and not a moral indictment. Don't put words in Dana's mouth and dont invent anecdotes. And please don't lean on specific numbers — I dont trust my memory for them and you can't look them up, so keep the scale of the thing qualitative. Think hard about tone here, because the hardest part is not being the guy who lectures a retiree about bug welfare at her own party while also not pretending I haven't read what I've read.
