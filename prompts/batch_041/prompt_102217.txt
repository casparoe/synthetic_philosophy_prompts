I'm in a reading group on the modern Stoic revival — we're going through Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius alongside some of the pop-Stoicism stuff, and I keep getting stuck on the dichotomy of control. A friend in the group laid out this argument and I can't find the exit. (1) The only thing that is really "up to me" is my own judgment — what I assent to, what I decide is worth going after. (2) Anything that is not up to me is entirely outside my agency, so my happiness can't depend on it. (3) If my happiness can't depend on something, then that thing is neither good nor bad for me — it's indifferent. (4) My health, my reputation, whether my kids outlive me, whether the plane lands — none of that is up to me. (5) So none of it is good or bad for me. (6) Therefore the consistent Stoic should be as unmoved by his child's death as by rain on a picnic, and since only what's up to him is worth pursuing, should not even get up to try to save her. (7) But that's monstrous, and every Stoic I've read clearly thinks you should try to save your child, and treats a parent's grief as at least intelligible. So something above is wrong, and I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs me.

I have a vague feeling the trouble is somewhere in how the argument is using "up to me," but I can't make it precise — is it "mine to determine" or just "mine to answer for," and does "not up to me" mean "not caused by me at all" or "not solely caused by me"? A friend wants to patch it by saying control comes in three grades (things I fully control, things I merely influence, things I can't touch) and that the middle grade is where all of life actually happens, but another friend says that just abandons Stoicism and turns into Aristotle with extra steps. What I really want to know: is there a reading on which (2) and (3) can both stand without dragging in the monstrous conclusion, or is the whole selling point of Stoicism — that you can be untroubled because nothing outside your judgment really touches you — exactly the thing that has to go? And if instead I keep (5) and (6) and deny (7) — bite the bullet that the sage really would be indifferent to his own child — is that a coherent position anyone would actually want to hold, or does it collapse into something that isnt Stoicism at all but just a very calm psychopath? Don't send me to the library for sources, I just want the diagnosis and the price of each option.
