I run the analytics team at a ~400-person company and I've somehow ended up as the unwilling owner of our quarterly metric review. Teams pitch new OKRs and dashboard numbers, I'm supposed to sign off before they go live, and right now my whole process is vibes: I ask "does this seem gameable?", everyone says no, and six months later we find out the sales team was doing something gross to hit the number. I've read enough about Goodhart's law and specification gaming to know that "gameable" isn't a yes/no property and that the interesting failures are structural, but I have no idea how to turn any of that into something my team can actually run in a 45-minute meeting. Nobody on the team is a statistician. Whatever we adopt I have to defend to the exec team, so the process needs to leave a paper trail of why a metric passed.

Two things I keep tripping on and I'd like the procedure to actually handle. First, I think there's two different failures and everyone lumps them together: in one, the number was a decent measure to begin with but people start optimizing it directly and it drifts off the thing we cared about; in the other, the number never measured the thing at all and we all just quietly agreed to call it the thing. The fixes seem completely different and I can never tell the two apart in a meeting. Second, someone once told me there are like four flavors of this and I can never remember them — something about the number being fine on average but falling apart at the extremes, something about the causal link between the number and the goal breaking once you push on it, something about deliberate sabotage, and something about the very act of selecting on the number changing what it predicts. I'd like the procedure to force us to name which one we're worried about, because "it might be gamed" is useless as a finding.

Can you write me the procedure? Format it like this:

1. "What's actually going wrong" — one short paragraph, plain language, so I can paste it at the top of the doc.

2. "The procedure" — the bulk of it. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where it stops and rejects a metric outright, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's really a values or incentives call and not a measurement call. Keep it runnable by non-statisticians in under an hour, and flag any step that's secretly a judgment call dressed up as a test.

3. "Three reviews" — run the procedure on (a) a proposed per-agent "customer happiness" score for our support team, (b) a "code quality" score we want to put on the engineering dashboards, and (c) an "incidents per quarter" safety number. Roughly a third of a page each. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like — the procedure clears a metric I personally distrust, or kills one I like, or says the metric is fine and the real problem is our threshold or the incentive attached to it, which I'd then have to take back to the exec team.

4. "Where I still have to guess" — 5 to 8 bullets, the places the procedure can't decide for us.

5. "What this procedure can't do" — short and honest, half a page.

Don't bother citing papers, I won't read them — just tell me what's actually known and usable.
