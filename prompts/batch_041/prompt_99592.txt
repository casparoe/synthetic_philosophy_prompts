Consider the following decision problem, a variant of the one known as the absent-minded driver. A driver must pass through two intersections on the way home. At the first, the best available action is to continue; at the second, to turn off. Turning off at the first yields a payoff of 0; turning off at the second yields 4; continuing past both yields 1. The driver cannot tell the two intersections apart: the information available at the first is exactly the information available at the second, and nothing about the driver's history is retained. It follows that any deterministic policy must prescribe the same action at both intersections, so such policies yield either 0 or 1, and the best of them yields 1; whereas a policy that independently continues with probability two-thirds at each intersection yields four-thirds. The puzzle is that this optimal policy requires the driver, at each intersection, to act in a way that looks like a mistake from where they stand, and it never tells them which of their two choices is the one that matters.

Now suppose that absent-mindedness is not a fate but a choice. At each intersection, before acting, the driver may pay a fixed cost c to consult a display that reliably reveals which intersection they are at. If the driver declines, they act on exactly the information described above; and whatever the display shows is not retained after the driver acts on it.

Please describe the most important implications of this modified problem, and, where they carry over, of the original, for each of the following three questions. First, what it means for a policy that randomizes to be the rational policy for a single agent making a single trip, and how such randomization is to be understood when the agent cannot carry out a plan whose later stages depend on its own history. Second, how the value of information is to be understood for an agent with limited memory, including the cost at which buying information ceases to be worth it. Third, the relationship between the ex ante ranking of policies and the ranking available to the agent at the moment of choice, and whether the modified problem makes that gap look like a paradox or like an unremarkable consequence of costly memory. Where the implications pull against one another, say so, and rank them by importance.
