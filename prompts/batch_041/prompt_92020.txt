My friend Maya and I got into a long, stupid argument last night after a seminar on welfare economics and I've been going back and fourth on it ever since, so I want to see the thing actually played out. Here's the shape of it: Maya is a full-blooded revealed-preference person. Her line is that the great 20th-century move in economics was to stop pretending we can get at some inner glow called "utility" and just define a person's preferences as whatever her choices are disposed to do — if it never shows up in a choice, it isn't a preference, and if two different choice situations make her look like she has two different orderings, then she has two different orderings. End of story, no ghost in the machine. I'm the one who kept insisting that can't be the whole story, that she's confused the measurement with the thing measured.

The thing that set us off was a hypothetical I made up on the spot: imagine a clinic that rewires your tastes. You walk in hating opera and hating the gym, you pay them a pile of money, and you walk out genuinely loving both. Nobody's deceived, you signed up while of sound mind, and by every behavioral test the new you picks opera over silence and the gym over the couch, happily, forever. Maya says great, the surgery made you better off — your preferences changed and now they're getting satisfied, and the fact that the new ones happen to be cheap to satisfy is just luck, not a problem. I said that's a reductio: by her logic the clinic could make anyone better off by installing a taste for staring at a blank wall, and there's got to be a difference between a preference being satisfied and a person actually faring well, which her story seems to have thrown out with the bathwater. Then she pulled what I thought was a sneaky move — she said the surgery actually satisfies a preference you already had, namely a preference about the kind of person you'd like to become, so it's not that your preferences got replaced, it's that one of them got fulfilled. I said that's cute but it makes the old you's preference satisfied only by its own obliteration, which is not what anyone means by "satisfying a preference."

So can you write me the dialogue? Two characters, names your call, one a genuinely competent defender of the pure choice-based / revealed-preference line, the other a competent critic who thinks utility has to bottom out in something other than choice — felt satisfaction, or the person's own endorsement of her life, or whatever they can actually defend — arguing first over my clinic case, including Maya's second-order-preference move, and then over what's really at stake underneath. No strawmen: the choice theorist should get her strongest version on the table (including that the critic's "real welfare" sounds exactly like the unmeasurable mental stuff the whole program was built to avoid, and that the clinic case is a feature rather than a bug), and the critic has to respond to that instead of just repeating herself. Don't let either of them hide behind "well, it depends on your theory of welfare" — I want them to commit. No citations or paper names needed, I just want to see whether the argument actually goes somewhere, and ideally whether they can at least agree on what they're really disagreeing about by the end.
