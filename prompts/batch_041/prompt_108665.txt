Next month I am convening a two-day workshop that brings archaeologists and philosophers together around a single question: what can material remains tell us about the beliefs and institutions of people who left no writing? I am to give the opening remarks, and I want them to set the agenda rather than merely welcome people. The organizing thought I want them to carry — and to make vivid enough to provoke argument over the following two days — is that belief and institution are not recovered from objects in the same way, and that the difference is not one of evidential strength but of kind: a belief can be held once, by one person, and leave no trace, whereas an institution exists only in repetition, and repetition is precisely what deposits and assemblages preserve. If that is right, the material record may be far better evidence for institutions than for beliefs, and the familiar slide from a recurring pattern to a claim about what people believed is not an overreach but a category mistake. Write the remarks — spoken, not read, about ten minutes — for a room that is half field archaeologists who distrust abstraction and half philosophers of mind and of the social sciences. Make the case sharply enough to be worth objecting to, concede the strongest objection you can see coming, and close with two or three questions the workshop could genuinely take up. Do not include citations; I will supply those myself.
