I convene a final-year seminar on thought experiments and philosophical method, and the take-home component of the final paper asks students to analyse a specimen intuition pump they have not seen before. I write a fresh specimen each year precisely so that no one can reproduce a seminar discussion from memory, and this year's specimen is my own composition — no published commentary on it exists. Please produce three deliverables: the student-facing exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks.

The examination is closed-book and timed at 90 minutes, and students have neither library nor internet access, so the question must be answerable from the specimen, from the working vocabulary set out below, and from general analytic competence alone. The model answer must not invoke, name, or attribute any published position on knowledge, information, or consciousness; it should do its work on the specimen in front of it. The difficulty is to come from the analysis rather than from recall, and an answer that names a doctrine and stops should not pass.

Specimen. Two rooms. In the first, a surveyor sits at a bare table. She has charted an island's coast for thirty years and carries no map: asked to draw the coastline she draws it, inlet for inlet; shown an official chart with a misplaced headland she catches it; set down in fog in a small boat she brings it to the one safe harbour. In the second room there is no surveyor and no furniture. Walls, floor, and ceiling carry the same coastline at the same scale and in the same detail; every feature the surveyor can reproduce is inscribed somewhere in the room, and nothing is inscribed that she cannot reproduce. The two rooms therefore contain the same information about the coast. Yet the second room knows nothing. The ink has no idea where the harbour is, and would have no idea if every wall were repainted in the dark. So the information cannot be what the surveyor has and the room lacks. Whatever she has — call it understanding — is not information; and no arrangement of information, however complete, will add up to it.

Working vocabulary. The following four terms are the unit's own coinages, not established terminology; students should use them, and neither the question nor the model answer should attribute them to anyone. Engine: the concrete feature of a case that generates its intuitive pull. Payload: the conclusion the case is advertised as supporting. Description asymmetry: a case's habit of describing one arm of a comparison in the vocabulary of mechanism and the other in the vocabulary of experience, so that the reader is invited to compare something described as inert with something described as alive. Residue: whatever intuitive pull survives once the case has been rewritten in a single vocabulary throughout.

For the marking scheme I want five must-hit points at four marks each, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates lose marks there. Include an explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a competent second: I want that boundary to turn on whether the student performs a specific piece of analysis, not on how much they write. The scheme should require that the candidate actually carries out the translation between vocabularies rather than merely announcing that a description asymmetry is present, and that the candidate reaches a verdict on the residue — saying whether anything remains and what it shows — rather than merely listing candidate residues. The model answer should also construct a counter-pump of its own and say what the existence of that counter-pump does to the original's payload.
