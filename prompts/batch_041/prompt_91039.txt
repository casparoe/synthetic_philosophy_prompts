I'm preparing a session for a graduate seminar on historical analogy and the "lessons of history," and I want to build the discussion around a passage I have never been able to explain satisfactorily to students. Here it is, in Richard Crawley's translation of Thucydides, Book 1, chapter 22:

The absence of romance in my history will, I fear, detract somewhat from its interest; but if it be judged useful by those inquirers who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the interpretation of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it, I shall be content. In fine, I have written my work, not as an essay which is to win the applause of the moment, but as a possession for all time.

A colleague wants to use this as the ancient charter for analogical reasoning in statecraft. On his reading, the author promises that because human nature is constant the future will repeat the past, so statesmen may read present crises off past ones; he takes the "Thucydides trap" diagnosis from the same book's account of why the war broke out — "The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war inevitable" — to be exactly the kind of recurrence the passage licenses.

I'd like help understanding the passage well enough to run the session. What does the conditional "must resemble if it does not reflect it" actually commit the author to — what would it be for the future to reflect rather than merely resemble the past, and why choose the weaker word? What is offered as "an aid to the interpretation of the future," given that interpretation is not prediction? And does the diagnosis in the other passage, a shifting balance of power and the fear it produces, support the analogy, or is it a different kind of explanation from the one the appeal to "the course of human things" seems to promise? I would also like a clean way to mark the difference between arguing that a present case will unfold like a past case and arguing only that the same mechanism is at work, so that students can see which of the two the passage, and my colleague, are entitled to. Please work from the text as given; I don't need a reading list.
