I have no training in history or economics, and famine is something I have read about on my own for years. I have reached the point where my judgments about particular famines no longer answer to any single rule, and I would like to know what rule, or what small set of rules, they actually add up to.

I accept the argument, which I believe is Amartya Sen's, that famines are seldom a matter of a country having no food; what fails is people's ability to get at the food that exists, through wages, prices, and the question of whom the government has decided to feed first. I also accept the related claim that mass starvation does not occur where the press is free and the government can be voted out. My verdicts, case by case, sit oddly against both.

Ireland in the 1840s: a natural disaster. The blight destroyed the crop; I know perfectly well that grain and butter kept leaving the country while people starved, and that relief was administered on a doctrine that made it deliberately ungenerous, and I still call it weather. Bengal in 1943: a crime. Rice went out, boats and stocks were taken from the coastal districts for military reasons, the war economy bid food away from the poor, the provincial government never declared a famine, and London would not spare the shipping. The Chinese famine of the late 1950s and early 1960s: the clearest crime of the three, even though I doubt anyone set out to starve anyone; what killed was a set of policies defended after the evidence was in. The Dutch hunger winter of 1944-45: a crime, because the German authorities cut off food transport to the western cities as a reprisal for a railway strike, so the hunger was aimed at people. The Sahel in the late 1960s and early 1970s: a natural disaster, though I grant that relief was possible in principle and simply did not arrive in time. Ethiopia in the mid-1980s: a crime, because the government was fighting its own regions, obstructed relief, and concealed what was happening. The Russian famine of 1921-22 is the one I keep going back and forth on: the drought was real, but the state had requisitioned the countryside into ruin and refused help for a time.

The pair I cannot square is Ireland and Bengal. Both were ruled from elsewhere, both exported food while their own people starved, and in both cases officials had been told what was happening. Why is one of them weather and the other murder? The only difference I can name is that nobody in London wanted the Irish dead, whereas in Bengal the war effort was meant to come first — but that speaks only to intention, and by that test Ireland is still a case of people being informed and doing very little. I have the same trouble with the Sahel and Ethiopia: two droughts among poor farmers, one nature and one crime. If the real difference is that by the 1980s the world had the aircraft and the agencies to stop a famine and in 1970 it did not, then the natural disaster is a shrinking category, and it may only ever have meant a famine that happened to people with no one to answer to.

My own attempt at a rule is this: a famine is a crime when those with the power to stop it knew people were dying, could have stopped it, and chose not to; it is a natural disaster when no one had both the knowledge and the power. That makes Ireland a crime, which I resist. The only clause I can invent that would save Ireland is that the dying were not the people the government took itself to be responsible for, and I do not want to accept that clause, since it seems to license almost anything done to a population that does not count.

So: is there a principle that keeps my verdicts, or close to it? If not, which of them should I give up, and what does the surviving principle say about cases I have not put to it — a government that prevents a famine at real cost to itself, a city starved out by a besieging army, a drought in a place with no state worth speaking of, and the chronic hunger that never becomes a famine because nobody dies in the street but a third of the children are stunted for life? And is the distinction between natural and political here a distinction between two kinds of events at all, or only between two things I am calling by the one name — the crop that fails and the people who die?
