I am a novelist, and next month I am giving a faculty workshop talk at the law school where I am currently writer-in-residence. The audience is roughly half statutory-interpretation scholars — two committed textualists, two purposivists, and one who works on legislative intent — and half law-and-humanities people who are sympathetic to literary approaches but nervous about them. The textualists care above all about notice, predictability, and constraining judicial discretion; the purposivists care about legislative supremacy and about courts not producing absurd results; the humanists will like my vocabulary and then worry that it licenses anything.

My thesis is that the textualism–purposivism dispute is really a dispute about which reader a statute is written for, and that narratology already has the terms for this. The textualist reads the statute as addressed to its 'implied reader' — the competent reader the text itself constructs, who knows the language, the register, and the conventions of the genre. The purposivist cannot be reconstructing the intentions of an actual author, since a legislature is a composite body that does not have intentions in the ordinary sense; so the purposivist is reconstructing an 'implied author', the agent whose purposes would make the enacted text coherent. On this view, appeals to plain meaning, to absurdity, and to what the drafters would have wanted are not competing theories of meaning but moves within a single interpretive practice, and the residual disagreement is about which reader a court should be trained to be.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this specific audience will actually raise, not the ones I would get from a room of literary theorists: order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each the way that objector would phrase it, and for each give me a two-minute reply plus a note on where the reply is soft. Sections, in this order: 'The objections, in the order I will get them' (the bulk of the response, roughly 600 words); 'What I should concede before anyone asks' (about 150 words); 'The softest point in my own position' (about 150 words); and 'The question I am hoping nobody asks' (about 100 words).
