The problem: teleosemantic accounts ground a state's content in the biological function of the mechanism that produces it — roughly, in what that mechanism was selected to do. The difficulty I want explained is that the selection history appears to license indefinitely many true descriptions of what such a state represents. A mechanism selected to fire in the presence of prey can equally be described as representing the prey, a proximal vibration pattern, a disjunction of causes, or a relational property that happens to correlate with all of them. Please explain (a) precisely why the appeal to selection history by itself underdetermines content here, and why this is not merely the familiar "disjunction problem" in a new dress; (b) how the standard move of splitting the mechanism into a producer and a consumer — fixing content by what the consumer is set up to do with the state rather than by what triggers it — is supposed to resolve the underdetermination, stated exactly enough that I can see what it buys and what it presupposes; and (c) one case in which that resolution still seems to fail, or to deliver a verdict that is intuitively wrong.
