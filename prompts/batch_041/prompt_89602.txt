I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and through a slightly ridiculous chain of events I've ended up as the discussant for a departmental session on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia next month. The ridiculous part: I only got into this literature a few weeks ago, after a genuinely stupid fight with my flatmate about whether our landlord was lying to us about the deposit. Halfway through I noticed I was pretty sure he was lying and also pretty sure that my being sure was mostly down to the first email I'd read that morning, and I couldn't tell whether that was a perfectly rational place to sit or a kind of self-undermining mess that I should be embarrassed by. Since then I've been collecting cases like it, and I want to use the seminar to test a hunch: that most people's sense of which of these states are "irrational" is driven by stuff that has nothing much to do with the structure of the case.

So here's what I want from you. Take the eight cases below and give each a number from 0 to 1 for how irrational the agent's overall doxastic state is at the end of the story — 0 meaning totally fine, no fault anywhere, 1 meaning something has clearly gone wrong and the agent is making a mistake. Then rank all eight from most to least irrational, and give me two or three sentences per case on what's actually doing the work in your rating. Please commit to the numbers instead of hedging into "it depends" — I know it depends, that's the whole point of the exercise. The cases: (1) Ana takes a pill she knows makes people bad at arithmetic, works through a long sum on paper, gets 431, and goes on believing 431 while also believing her arithmetic right now is unreliable. (2) Ben and a colleague whose track record he regards as roughly equal look at the same biopsy and disagree; Ben keeps his original confidence and doesn't move at all. (3) Cara reads a study reporting that people who read that exact study overestimate its effect size, and keeps her newly boosted confidence in the effect. (4) Dev is told by a colleague that his reasoning about a case is unreliable, but Dev has watched this colleague say that to literally everyone in the department, including people who were plainly right. (5) Elena is confident a friend did something cruel, then reads that people's moral judgments about in-group members are heavily distorted by loyalty, and she's firmly in-group here; she keeps the judgment at full strength. (6) Farid is paid by a company to assess whether its product is safe, concludes it's safe, and knows that paid assessors tend to reach whatever conclusion their payers want, yet doesn't discount his own conclusion at all. (7) Grace is confident her department's hiring decision was wrong, then learns she was severely sleep-deprived and mildly feverish during the meeting and that people in that state weigh evidence worse, and keeps her confidence. (8) Kai is told his reasoning is compromised, and concludes that this very testimony is probably unreliable, on the grounds that his reasoning about the testimony is compromised.

Two more things. If you think rating these on a single scale is the wrong move, tell me at the end and say why, but give me the numbers anyway — and flag any case you think is malformed or sneaks in a question-begging assumption. And I'd like to know where your own reasoning is most likely to fail here: name the one case, or the one consideration, that if you weighed it differently would flip your top three, and say what would have to be true for that to happen. Also, if any two cases strike you as ones most people would rate far apart but where you think the gap is an illusion, seperate them out for me.
