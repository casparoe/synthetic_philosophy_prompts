A litigator friend of mine who also reads a good deal of historical-Jesus scholarship has been arguing that the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, and the rest — are really just the rules of evidence applied to ancient texts: the historian is the trier of fact, the Gospels are witnesses, an embarrassing saying is a statement against interest, and a saying that flatters the early church's agenda is the self-serving claim you discount. On his view, that is why the criteria can in principle settle what Jesus actually said and did. Audit the analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of witness, party, trier of fact, admissibility, weight, and burden of proof — and then identify where the mapping breaks: which features of an actual trial (live cross-examination, an independent finder of fact, authenticated documents, a clear separation between admissibility and weight, and the fact that interested testimony is normally weighed rather than excluded) have no counterpart in the historian's situation, and which of the criteria behave unlike any evidentiary rule. Then consider whether the criteria function as a likelihood ratio — telling you how much more probable a saying is if authentic than if invented — or whether they are being read backwards, as if a low probability of the saying given invention settled the probability of invention given the saying; if the latter, say what that does to his confidence. Finally, state whether the conclusion he draws, that the criteria can settle who Jesus was, survives the disanalogies or whether the courtroom picture was carrying it all along, and name a better analogy if one exists, with what it would and would not license.
