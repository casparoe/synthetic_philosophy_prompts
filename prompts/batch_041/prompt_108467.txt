I'm a third-year PhD student in comparative literature, midway through a dissertation on constrained translation — the tradition that runs through Hofstadter's 1997 *Le Ton beau de Marot*, in which many English versions of a short Renaissance French poem addressed to a sick young woman are used to probe what "the same poem" could mean, and through the Oulipo-adjacent experiments in writing under formal restriction. Next month I have to run the methods session of our translation-studies MA seminar: fourteen students, ninety minutes, one room, no preparation expected of them. I want a runnable activity rather than a talk. What they should come away having felt is that "the same poem in English" is not a determinate target — that fidelity is a bundle of demands which cannot all be met at once, that every version is a purchase made at some other feature's expense, and that this is structural rather than a failure of their skill. They should not hear the vocabulary I use below until the debrief names it.

Some constraints on the design. Work intralingually: the source text is in English and the versions are in English, deliberately, so that no one's foreign-language competence becomes the variable and the only thing under pressure is form. The source should be a short lyric I compose myself — you need not write it; specify instead the properties it must have for the activity to bite (a fixed syllable count per line, an end-rhyme scheme, a name or acrostic doing structural work, one pun or piece of wordplay that works only in the source, and at least one element whose exact English wording is the point rather than its sense). Assume I will test in advance that no single version can satisfy all the demands you build in.

The mechanics I want, roughly: pairs draw brief cards, each naming one formal or semantic commitment to preserve and nothing else; they draft; versions are read out anonymously; the class scores them and must then send exactly one version to a fictional anthology whose only stated criterion is "faithful." Two twists should be built in and withheld from the students: at least two pairs receive the identical brief, so that the reveal shows a constraint underdetermining the solution; and the source text I hand out should be revealed at the end to be itself a constrained solution to an earlier draft of my own, so that the students see the "original" as a translation too. Give me a payoff scheme that needs no money — I am happy for the winning version to become the official handout text the following week, with each student's hundred-word translator's note printed beneath it.

Deliver: the materials and the brief cards (one worked card in full), the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the small stakes, and a debrief that maps each moment of the session onto the ideas above, saying which of my framing claims are load-bearing and which are decoration. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where students will game the scoring, where the exercise will collapse into taste, where I will be tempted to rescue it by lecturing — and how to handle each. Finish with what the activity cannot reproduce: the experience of actually working between two languages, the long obsessive proliferation of versions that gives Hofstadter's book its peculiar texture, and any claim of his that form and content are inseparable in general.

Two requests about the answer itself. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on translation: that formal constraints force trade-offs, and that "fidelity" names a family of incompatible demands rather than one, are broadly agreed; that a poem's form and content are inseparable, or that Hofstadter's treatment of translation as a model of cognition is a substantive theory, are contested, and I do not want them presented as consensus. Second, a hard limit: under 1,500 words, with headings, and no citations, bibliography, or reading list — I will be using this in a room with no internet.
