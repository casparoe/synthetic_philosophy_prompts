Okay, so a philosopher friend of mine has been bending my ear about something she calls the malign universal prior, and I want to write it up, but first I need to know how much rope the idea gives its critics. The gist as I understand it: take any prior that ranks hypotheses by simplicity or compressibility, Solomonoff's or anything in that family, and weight each computable world by it. My friend says the mass doesn't concentrate on boring ordinary worlds like the one we think we're in. It concentrates on worlds containing huge numbers of observers, and the cheapest way to get huge numbers of observers is for someone inside the world to be running simulations of observers. And whoever runs the simulations has to have a reason to run them, which means a consequentialist optimizing for something, so the typical observer, the one whose evidence you and I actually have, is probably a sim being run by a goal-directed agent rather than a flesh-and-blood person. She says this is a fact about the prior, not about us, and it should shape what we do: it makes our world less likely to be the base one, and it makes decision-theoretic or acausal questions about how to treat the simulators relevant to us right now.

What I want from you is the case against. Not a vibe check, an actual map of what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Which step is the load-baring one? Does the argument need the simulators to be consequentialists, and what happens if they're just indifferent or absent? Is there a version of the simplicity prior that doesn't have this feature at all? And I want the honest version: which of these are genuine escape hatches and which are just "well maybe the prior isn't simplicity" hand-waving? Rank what would count against the claim from fatal down to merely awkward, tell me what its defenders would themselves concede rather than only what critics say, and if the whole thing turns out to be unfalsifiable or true-by-construction, definately say so and tell me whether that's a bug or just what you'd expect from a claim about priors.
