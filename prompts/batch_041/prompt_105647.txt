I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of science, and I am preparing a teaching module on the problem of verisimilitude. For that module I want a piece of sustained irony that will be legible to students who have encountered Popper's idea that science aims at increasing truthlikeness but who have not yet worked through the technical literature. Write a mock grant-panel summary, in the deadpan register of an official funding decision, for a fictional research programme called the Institute for Comparative Truthlikeness. The Institute proposes to rank a set of scientific theories — from Newtonian mechanics to phlogiston chemistry to contemporary climate models — on a single 'Verisimilitude Index' computed from a weighted sum of explanatory content and empirical accuracy. The satire should arise from the real difficulties: that any such index must choose a language or a partition of possibilities, that greater content can lower probability, that comparisons of truthlikeness across theories can trivialize when their truth and falsity sets are related in the wrong way, and that empirical success is not the same as closeness to truth. The panel should be enthusiastic in exactly the way a committee would be if it had understood none of these difficulties, and the summary should never state the objections outright; instead let the objections show through the panel's praise, its requested revisions, and the budget lines. About 600–700 words, formatted as a panel summary with a decision, a score, and a list of conditions. Do not mention living people; the Institute and its applicants can be invented. Do not include citations or reading lists; the piece should work for a reader who cannot look anything up.
