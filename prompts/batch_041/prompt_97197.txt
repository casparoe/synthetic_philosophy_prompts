Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two readings of Buddhist ethics that have been talking past each other in my reading group. The first position holds that Buddhist ethics is best understood as a form of consequentialism: the criterion of right action is the promotion of welfare and the reduction of suffering, and the precepts are defeasible rules of thumb whose authority derives from their consequences. The second holds that Buddhist ethics is a virtue ethics: what is fundamental is the cultivation of a transformed character, the precepts express the conduct of one who is already skilled rather than instruments for producing outcomes, and consequences serve at most as a diagnostic sign rather than as the standard of rightness. Neither party is a specialist in the other's framing, and neither will accept a settlement that quietly treats its own reading as the merely edifying version of the other.

Produce the document they could both put their names to. It should contain, in order:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept, covering what the early discourses and the later commentarial and Mahāyāna materials are actually committed to, what the labels consequentialist and virtue-ethicist are doing in this dispute, and which questions the texts leave underdetermined. These claims must be substantive; an agreement that survives only because it says nothing is worthless.

2. A precise statement of each residual disagreement, each written in wording that the side it belongs to would itself endorse, so that neither position is refuted merely by the phrasing of its own statement.

3. The cruxes, that is, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. At a minimum, address whether the good to be promoted is welfare or liberation, whether karma functions as a consequentialist feedback mechanism or as a virtue-theoretic one, whether the precepts are absolute or defeasible, and whether a consequentialist reading can be coherent at all given that Buddhism denies a self to whom aggregated welfare could belong.

4. For each crux, a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. They should agree that the story of the ship's captain, familiar from the Mahāyāna literature on skilful means, who kills a would-be murderer in order to save his passengers, bears on the defeasibility crux; and they should construct at least one further case in which an act maximises welfare but issues from a corrupt motive.

5. A short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and why, including whether the apparent disagreement over the role of intention is one of substance or of terminology.

Where the vocabulary of the debate is itself contested, whether that is the term consequentialism, virtue, intention, welfare or skilfulness, say so and propose a neutral formulation. Note in particular whether Buddhist consequentialism, if it is true at all, could be maximising rather than satisficing, given that the soteriological goal looks like a threshold to be crossed rather than a quantity to be increased.
