I am a graduate student in a comparative-history seminar, and I have been asked to open a session on whether macrohistory can be made quantitative and predictive. The assigned material has been the demographic-structural theory of secular cycles associated with Peter Turchin: the claim that agrarian states tend to pass through long integrative and disintegrative phases, with the disintegrative phase driven by the interaction of population growth, elite overproduction, fiscal exhaustion, and intensifying intra-elite competition, and with the integrative phase restoring order through some combination of demographic collapse, state consolidation, and new institutional arrangements. A classmate has proposed what she calls a coupled-oscillator alternative. On her picture, each polity has an internal cycle of legitimacy, extraction, and elite competition, but polities are also coupled to their neighbors through trade, migration, war, dynastic marriage, and the circulation of elites; the central object of study is therefore not the single society's turning point but the synchronization regime, meaning whether neighboring cycles phase-lock into regional waves, drift apart, or settle into anti-phase, and whether exogenous shocks such as pandemics, climate anomalies, or military revolutions reset the phase or merely perturb it. She says this makes Turchin's framework look like a special case, while I suspect it changes the explanatory target and the kind of prediction one can responsibly make.

I would like you to compare the two frameworks rather than merely summarize them. In particular: what does each framework treat as the fundamental unit and the fundamental clock? What causal commitments does each make that the other can avoid or must accept? Is the coupled-oscillator model genuinely a rival to secular-cycle theory, or is it better understood as a refinement, a generalization, or a change of subject? What would each predict about a case in which two neighboring agrarian empires both exhibit elite overproduction and fiscal strain, would Turchin's theory expect their breakdowns to be roughly synchronous, and what would the coupled model expect under weak versus strong coupling? How would each handle a shock that kills a large fraction of the elite but leaves the productive base intact? Finally, which framework gives a more defensible answer to the question whether history can be quantified and predicted, and what would each have to assume about measurement, ergodicity, and the stability of causal parameters to make good on its predictive ambitions?

Please structure your response under the following headings, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths: The two frameworks in brief, about 200 words; Turchin's secular-cycle commitments, about 250 words; The coupled-oscillator commitments, about 250 words; Rivalry, refinement, or change of subject, about 250 words; Three test cases, about 350 words; and Bottom line, about 150 words. In the Three test cases section, address the two-neighbor case, the elite-killing shock, and the problem of quantitative prediction. Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I want the comparison itself.
