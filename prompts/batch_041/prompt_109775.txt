I'm giving a talk in two weeks to our departmental consciousness reading group and I want to know what I'm actually going to get hit with. The room is roughly half vision scientists and half people who do contemplative/meditation research, plus one philosopher who is a genuine qualia believer of the acquaintance sort, plus a couple of people who broadly agree with me but think I always overreach. The vision people care about color appearance, adaptation and individual differences; the contemplative people run first-person phenomenology interviews and treat the felt character of experience as the primary datum you're not allowed to explain away; the philosopher thinks acquaintance is the one relation that can't be functionalized.

The talk is a stronger version of the quining line than the usual one. Everyone there has read the usual stuff, so the usual moves won't score. I'm not going to argue that qualia are explanatorily idle, I'm going to argue the concept is incoherent, i.e. that you can't specify what a quale is supposed to be without the specification eating itself.

Here's my argument sketch so you know what to attack. I split qualia claims into three grades. Grade one: there's something it is like to be in the state. I grant this, it's trivially true and does no work. Grade two: the experience has a determinate intrinsic property that the subject can re-identify on another occasion, so this taste now is the same property as that taste then. Grade three: that property is known by direct acquaintance and can't be captured in any relational or functional description. My claim is that two and three can't be held seperate. The only way to make the re-identification non-arbitrary is to let something relational in, memory or report or discrimination capacity or functional role, and the moment you do that you've lost three. If you refuse to let anything relational in, the re-identification is arbitrary, because the subject has no way to tell same from different that isn't just the fact that they said so. So the concept either collapses into a functional property or into an empty one, and either way it isn't doing what qualia were posited to do.

I also want to run something I've been calling the twice-over test and I need you to tell me if it's a real test or a cheap trick. Take a subject mid-experience. Ask them to judge, without using anything they can say or do or remember, whether the character of this experience is the same as the character of the one they had a moment ago. If they can do it, name the faculty. If they can't, then the alleged intrinsic property isn't something anyone, including the subject, is in a position to track, and if the subject can't track it, in what sense is it a property of their experience at all?

There's also a bit from my own lab I want to use carefully and I'm not sure I should. We ran people through a color adaptation thing where they will swear the patch looks different before and after, and then can't say one thing about how it differs beyond that it does. I think that supports me. My grad student thinks it just shows reports are thin. Definately not sure I want it in the talk.

What are the objections I'll actually get from this specific room, from the vision people, from the meditators, from the philosopher, and from the ones who basically agree with me but think I've gone too far? Order them by how much damage they do rather than how likely they are, and phrase each one the way that person would actually phrase it, because I need to recognize it when it comes. For each, give me a two-minute reply and then tell me where the reply is soft, meaning the follow-up that would break it. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
