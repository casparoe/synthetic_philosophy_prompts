In my decision theory reading group we spent basically the whole term circling the money pump and I still can't tell what we were actually fighting about. Here's the setup as we rehearsed it a dozen times: someone has intransitive preferences, say she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, or more realistically she'll pay a penny to trade her current bundle for a slightly better one, and again for the next, and again for the one after that, and the sequence loops back to where she started with her three pennies gone. One side of the room takes this to show that intransitive preferences are irrational, full stop, there's a requirement and the pump is the proof of it. Another side says the pump only shows that a certain sequence of choices leaves you worse off, that this is a fact about what happens when you keep trading, and that nothing normative follows unless you already accept a requirement of diachronic consistency, which is exactly the thing in dispute. And a third cluster mutters that the whole thing is a Dutch book argument in a different suit and inherits all the objections to those, so it can't do the work people want it to. We never got past restating those three positions at each other.

So I want a diagnosis, not another round. Is this a substantive disagreement about whether rationality is a property of time-slices or of temporally extended agents, i.e. is the real fight about what the unit of assessment is? Or is it a verbal dispute where one side means the agent was irrational and the other means the agent was exploitable, and nobody notices those aren't in contradiction? Or is there a hidden formal question doing the work, namely whether any chooser who gets pumped can be redescribed as a single expected-utility maximiser over some larger state space, so the pump is really evidence about representability rather than about rationality at all?

Here's a wrinkle that might sharpen it, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than swat it away. Suppose we build the pump so it needs no change in the agent's preferences over final outcomes at all. Her ranking of outcomes is fixed and perfectly transitive the whole time; what's unstable is only how she ranks options within whatever menu she's facing, so she takes X over Y when those two are the only things on offer and Y over X when a third option is sitting next to them. Now run the pump across menus rather than across time. If you can still extract the money, then either the pump was never really about diachronic rationality and the transitivity requirement is really a requirement about context-independence, or diachronic rationality is doing no work in the original argument and the standard framing is a red herring. I genuinely don't know which, and I suspect the answer tells us something about what kind of disagreement the original one is. Tell me whether this wrinkle is a real problem for either side or just a curiosity.
