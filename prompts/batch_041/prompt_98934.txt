I write a column on science and ideas and I'm working on a piece about the self — specifically the Dennett-ish line that the self is a "center of narrative gravity," a convenient fiction the brain spins rather than a thing it contains. I keep getting into arguments with readers who treat this as either obviously true or obviously nihilistic, and I want to hand them something better than a label: a decision tree they can walk through themselves that lands them on a position about whether there's a self and what kind of thing it is. Each node should be a question they can answer without already knowing the position it leads to — concrete stuff, not "are you a realist?". Cases I have in mind: you meet someone with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes you, is the same self still there? a split-brain patient gives two different answers to the same question, is that one self or two? you're writing a memoir, is the "I" in it the same "I" doing the writing? a chatbot holds a consistent persona across a thousand conversations, does it have the begining of a self? a person in deep meditation reports the narrative just stops — did the self stop too, or only the telling? And so on.

Two things I want the tree to actually take seriously, because I think that's where the real action is and most popular treatments skip straight past them. First, the narratology distinction between the one who tells the story and the one the story is about — the narrating I vs. the narrated I — and whether the self is one of those, the other, or just the seam between them. Second, the possibility that a narrative center of gravity could be real the way a phase of matter is real: it only exists at a certain scale, it isn't reducible to the parts, but it also isn't a thing over and above them — so there might be a coherent position that's neither "the self is an illusion" nor "the self is a substance." I want leaves for both of those, plus the usual suspects (self as a real pattern, as a useful fiction, as nothing at all, the no-self view, and the deflationary "self is just a family-resemblance term and the question is malformed").

Aim for maybe ten to twelve leaves; name each one and give a single line on its most awkward commitment. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate. And tell me which combinations of answers the tree allows that nobody seems to occupy — with a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
