For a reading group on scalable oversight, please build a decision tree that carries a reader from a shared starting commitment to a named position on how debate-style oversight of stronger systems could work. The starting commitment: I accept that we will have to oversee systems whose answers I cannot check myself, that the overseer will be weaker than the system, and that the protocol must be robust to a system optimising against it rather than merely being helpful. Branch only on assumptions on which the positions genuinely come apart, each phrased as a question answerable without knowing which position it leads to: whether the overseer's weakness is only a shortfall of compute or also an inability to recognise a good answer when shown one; whether the honest advocate can be required to win with a short, checkable argument or may need an arbitrarily long one; whether the protocol lets the judge interrogate the advocates — press for the weakest link, ask follow-ups — or gives each side one simultaneous answer; whether the judge is the human overseer or a weaker model trained to imitate one; and whether the verdict rule makes honesty the equilibrium when the judge is only approximately calibrated. Place the positions at the leaves — standard adversarial debate; a variant in which the honest side must supply a short certificate; decomposition-and-amplification rather than debate; consultancy baselines in which the overseer only asks and does not adjudicate; weak-model judges; and a leaf for the view that the protocol is not the bottleneck, the judge is — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark the combinations no one occupies and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Use concrete tasks as the cases the branches turn on: a mathematical proof, a question about a long document, a question whose truth is contested or ill-defined (whether a poem is any good), and a case in which one advocate is more persuasive than truthful. Six to eight leaves, no more than five branch levels.
