I have been reading about a way of idealizing deliberation from the AI-safety literature, and I would like a poem that takes one half of it literally and lets the reader feel what the other half idealizes away. The idealization is defined recursively rather than procedurally: an agent's considered judgment is whatever answer a human would give if she had unlimited time and, whenever a subquestion arose, could hand that subquestion to another copy of herself whose considered judgment was, again, the very same thing — a fixed point, not a recipe. The companion picture replaces the unlimited human with a bounded one and approximates that fixed point by a finite tree of subquestions, each answered by a fresh copy of the same voice on a strict clock, with answers flowing upward to the root.

Write the poem as that second picture. Use a tree of one-line fragments, indented to show nesting, three levels deep and no more than eighteen lines in total, with a single root line at the top. Each fragment must read as a response to the fragment immediately above it; the whole tree must be spoken in one continuous voice; and no fragment may presuppose anything established in a branch other than its own. Lines should shorten as the tree deepens, so that the leaves are only a few words. Every leaf must be metrically identical to the root and rhyme with it, so that the recursion bottoms out by arriving back where it began, and the final leaf must be able to stand as the first line of the poem without the poem ceasing to be the same poem.

The subject should be something ordinary and concrete — a decision, a morning, a difficult conversation — and the poem must never name the theory, deliberation, computation, or any of the machinery above. Two constraints are the point of the exercise. First, reading one branch before its sibling must change what the root line appears to mean, and the poem must not adjudicate between the readings. Second, the poem should register the cost of the decomposition without complaining about it: what a thought loses when it is split among copies who cannot hear each other, and what it would mean for the leaves to be as good as the root. Someone who knows this literature should recognize the fixed point and the loss inside it; someone who does not should find a poem about how a mind divides itself in order to finish a thought.
