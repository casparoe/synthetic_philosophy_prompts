I sit on the value-assessment subcommittee of a regional public health insurer — I'm an analyst in the health economics unit by day, but since my father's application for a treatment was turned down last spring on cost-effectiveness grounds I've become the member who reads the modeling reports line by line and asks the awkward questions — and the chair has told us to stop issuing recommendations as a black box. She wants a written procedure, grounded in the actual theory of how individual preferences are supposed to aggregate into a social ordering, that any one of us can run in an afternoon on a new technology, that leaves a paper trail defensible to a patient group, and that stops pretending the number is neutral. We know the relevant theory is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result that if individual and social preferences each satisfy the expected-utility axioms and the social ordering respects unanimous individual preference, then the social ordering is a weighted sum of individual utilities — and I want the procedure to be an honest operationalization of exactly that, not a generic cost-per-QALY checklist with the theory's name pasted on top of it.

Please write it as an ordered procedure: the steps to run, what evidence or elicitation each step requires, what counts as passing, and — this is the part the chair will care about — the points where the theorem simply does not determine an answer and a value judgment has to be recorded as such rather than buried inside a spreadsheet cell. In particular I need the procedure to force an explicit decision on at least three things the theorem leaves open: where the weights come from and who is entitled to set them; whether we aggregate each person's expected utility before the uncertainty resolves or aggregate outcomes and then assign utilities to them (our current model quietly does one of these, and the distributive consequences of the two differ, which nobody on the committee realizes); and whether we take patients' reported preferences as they are — adaptation, relative-position concerns and all — or substitute some corrected version that we think they ought to have. It should also state plainly which distributive principles our procedure cannot accommodate without breaking its own axioms; I have a suspicion that giving priority to the worse off is one of them, and if that is right the committee needs to read it in the document rather than discover it when a patient group challenges a decision.

Then run the procedure end to end on two proposals: a new therapy that adds a handful of months of life for a small group of very ill patients, at high cost and with burdensome side effects; and a low-cost intervention producing a modest improvement for a very large number of people with a chronic but non-life-threatening condition. I also want a third application, deliberately chosen, in which the procedure returns a recommendation I personally find repugnant — I would rather watch it misfire on the page now than have a patient's family watch it misfire later — and for that one, tell me what the procedure cannot do and what the committee's only remaining moves are. Throughout, keep the vocabulary usable by the clinician and the lay member who will not read any of the theory, and mark every step that is a judgment call wearing the costume of a calculation.
