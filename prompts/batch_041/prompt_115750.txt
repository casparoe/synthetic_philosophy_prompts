I run a ninety-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of action, fourteen students, one room with movable tables, no technology needed. They have had some action theory — intention, reasons for action, the difference between doing something intentionally and foreseeing it — but nothing on shared or collective agency, and I want to keep it that way until the end: the session should make them run into the problem rather than hear about it. Please design a runnable activity for me, with rules, materials, timings, a debrief, and an honest account of what will go wrong the first time. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, no tables. If a scoring scheme is essential, describe it in sentences.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though you should change any detail that does not work. I split the room into crews of four. In the first phase each crew must perform one small physical act that no member can perform alone: all four members put an index card face down on the table inside the same one-second window, judged by an observer from another crew, with no talking during an attempt and sixty seconds of talk between attempts. A success is worth three points to each member of the crew. They will solve this quickly, and that is the point — I want them to feel how natural it is to say "we" about what they are doing, and to notice that the plan they built (someone counts, everyone moves on three) is made of four separate intentions that happen to mesh.

In the second phase the task is identical, but I hand out private cards describing what each member scores, and I say nothing about rules. Three cards in each crew read that the holder scores three if the crew succeeds and zero if it fails. One card reads that the holder scores four if their card is the first of the four to land, whatever the crew's result, and two if the crew succeeds without them being first. So the holder of that card is privately better off breaking the window, and no rule forbids it. I want the crew to fail at least once this way and then have to say what, if anything, the early mover did wrong. My prediction is that they will not be able to name a rule that was broken, and that they will still want to say something was owed — that is the moment I am buying, and I would like the activity built so that this moment is not accidental.

The third phase is where I most need your help. Before the phase begins, each crew must say aloud, in front of the others, one sentence of the form "We will land four cards inside the window in the next round," with a definite verb rather than "we will try." Immediately after, I announce a release: from now on any individual may declare "I am out," which keeps their own points, freezes the crew's total, and ends that crew's participation. I want to see whether crews treat the declaration as binding on individuals — whether a member can release only themselves or needs the others' consent — and I want the disagreement to be genuine rather than staged, because I think the inability to answer the question without inventing a normative structure is the phenomenon.

The debrief, twenty-five minutes, should map particular moments onto the positions I want them to be able to distinguish afterwards: that a shared plan is just several intentions with meshing subplans plus common knowledge, each revisable with notice; that saying "we will" creates an obligation that neither party can unilaterally cancel and gives the others standing to demand, not merely a prediction to rely on; and that the "we" might not be built out of individual intentions at all. Name the standard positions only if you are confident of the attributions; nothing in the activity or the debrief should depend on a name or a title. They should not hear any of these terms before the debrief.

Please also tell me, specifically and without softening it, what will predictably go wrong: where crews will solve the task so smoothly that the second phase feels like a puzzle rather than a betrayal, what to do when nobody defects because the room is polite, how to handle the student who announces that the rational move is obvious and turns the whole thing into a lecture on free riding, and how to stop the crews from arguing about what the special card said rather than about what they owe each other. Finally, say which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
