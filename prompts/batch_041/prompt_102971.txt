I sit on the heritage committee of a small town — a volunteer body with no professional historian on it, a budget for roughly one new plaque a year, and a permanent exhibit we can only afford to revise every few years. Over the past while we have had a run of disputes of the same shape: something the community remembers, and repeats at dedications and in the local paper, does not match what the documentary record shows, or the record is silent where memory is loud. The record says the land was bought; the town remembers a gift. The record shows a restrictive covenant; older residents remember their street as always having been mixed. The written sources give a fever as the cause of a death that a family has always understood as something else. We are not a court and we are not writing a dissertation; we have to decide, by vote, in a meeting, whether to leave a plaque alone, add a line to it, replace it, or say publicly that the record and the memory disagree.

I would like a decision procedure we could actually run on a case like this: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it should hand the matter to the committee as a question of values rather than of fact. I have no training in this, but I have been reading around it on my own, and two distinctions of my own have been useful to me, so please build them in or argue them out of the procedure if they don't hold. First, that a memory can be wrong about what happened and still be right about what the community has made of it, so that "the record wins" and "the memory wins" are not the only two options. Second, that a record can be accurate in every particular and still be a distortion, because of what it selected, who kept it, and what it was kept for — which means "the record says otherwise" is not by itself a reason to correct a community.

I would like the procedure to be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules, and to say plainly what it cannot settle. Then run it on three cases: the bought land remembered as a gift; the restrictive covenant against the memory of a street that felt mixed; and one where the record's silence is itself contested — a death the family understands one way and the sources another. At least one of the three should come out against the way the committee, and I, would like it to come out. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the procedure — which case or consideration would lead you to design it differently — and where you think its reasoning is most likely to fail.
