I just moved into a data science role and I keep hitting the same wall: I genuinely have no information about some parameter, so I slap a uniform prior on whatever scale I happen to be working in, and then a coworker writes the exact same model in terms of a different parameter and we get different answers. Last week it was a toy thing a friend sent me -- a machine makes rectangles of perimeter 2, whats the chance the area comes out under 0.1? Uniform on the width gives me one number, uniform on the area gives me another, and the aspect-ratio version apparently disagrees with both. Same deal as the old random-chord puzzle giving you three answers depending on how you build the chord.

Before I just pick a convention and stop thinking about it, I want the spec. What does any account of priors-from-ignorance -- indifference and symmetry, invariance arguments, max entropy, imprecise or interval-valued probabilities, whatever else is in play -- have to deliver to count as usable by someone who has to actually commit to a prior and defend it? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right, the invariances it can't violate, the distinctions it can't collapse (no information vs symmetric information; a prior being undefined vs vague vs improper), and what it has to be consistent with -- coherence, conditioning, countable additivity, if you think those are requirements. For each requirement, the case or consideration that forces it, and a note on which ones are non-negotiable and which are just house preferences of whichever camp invented them.

The bit I most want: which of these can't be had together. If "invariant under any reparameterization" and "always returns a definite number" are jointly unsatisfiable, spell that out as the result rather than glossing it. And tell me which single requirement rules out the most otherwise attractive option -- I'm betting it's the reparameterization one killing the plain uniform prior, but tell me if I'm wrong. Skip names and citations, I don't need the literature, just the criteria and where they conflict.
