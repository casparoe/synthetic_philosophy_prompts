I want to argue with someone about the Condorcet jury theorem, and I would rather you take the other side than help me take mine. Please play a committed defender of the theorem as a justification for majority rule: someone who knows the mathematics and its weak points, who will concede a genuine point when one is owed, but who will not surrender the claim that majority rule tends to track the truth under conditions near enough to real ones to matter. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and do not step out of the role to summarise what has already been written on the subject or to suggest reading.

Here is the objection I have worked out on my own, and I may well have it wrong. The theorem treats each voter as an independent reading of a fact that is already settled before anyone votes — whether the accused did it, whether the well is poisoned — and I do not dispute the arithmetic. What I doubt is that much of what we put to a vote has that shape. Consider a village voting on whether the river is safe to drink when every villager's private evidence is a neighbour's rumour: the more a person expects the others to agree with her, the more confident she becomes, so her chance of being right is a function of the majority she is helping to form. Or take the question whether an economic policy will work, when whether it works depends on how many people, having voted for it, then behave as though it will. In cases of this kind the fact is not read off by the voters but made by them. My question, then, is whether the theorem becomes not merely inapplicable but circular whenever it is applied to a question whose answer is constituted by the collective decision rather than discovered by it — the group is right because it agrees, and agrees because it expects to be right. And if your reply is that the theorem was only ever meant for questions with an answer independent of the vote, I want to know whether any democratic decision that matters actually has that shape, or whether the defence preserves the theorem by confining it to questions for which nobody needs it.
