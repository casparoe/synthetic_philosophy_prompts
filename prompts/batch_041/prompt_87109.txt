I chair the editorial standards committee at a mid-sized scholarly publisher. There are four of us, two without any linguistics background, we meet monthly for ninety minutes, and the docket routinely runs to eight or ten disputed usages. Our decisions bind every imprint until the next revision of the house style guide, three years out, and the editor-in-chief signs off on whatever we send up. Until this past spring I would have called this the least interesting job in the building. Then a manuscript on early modern political thought came in using "man" and "he" for the generic human throughout the argument, not just inside quoted sources, and when the copyeditor queried it the author replied that the usage was traditional, that "man" has always included women, and that changing it would import a political agenda into a work of scholarship. Two of my committee members agreed with him. The third said the usage made the book unreadable. I had no way to decide, and I have eight more items like it on the docket.

What finally gave me a handle on the problem was Douglas Hofstadter's satire "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," written under the pseudonym William Satire, in which a defender of the status quo argues that "white" is the natural inclusive term for all people and that the reformers who want to substitute "person" everywhere are politicizing the language. The narrator's central defense — that "white is a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow, including black," and that used inclusively the word has no connotations whatsoever of race — is precisely the move I keep hearing from authors about "man." The piece also mocks the etymological defense, in which the "-white" of "chairwhite" is said to be a different word from the noun "white" because it is pronounced differently, and it mocks the supposed shrillness of the people asking for the change. My reading is that the satire's real target is a term that looks neutral to the people it already includes while marking everyone else, and that the way to expose this is to swap in a category that is not the default and watch the sentence turn strange.

Please write the procedure my committee can actually run. I do not need a reading list, and I do not need a survey of positions on language and gender; I need something four people can execute in a single meeting, on a single sentence, without me in the room. Specifically:

- Give me an ordered set of no more than eight questions, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, what to do when the answer is contested or unavailable, and where the procedure terminates.
- Include a substitution test the committee can perform on a sentence in the meeting, with instructions for choosing the substitute term, for handling terms that have no clean marked counterpart, and for the case where the substitution produces something merely odd rather than revealing.
- Mark clearly the three kinds of output the procedure can produce: change the usage, keep the usage, and this is a values question rather than a usage question, in which case the committee decides and records the dissent. Do not let the third category swallow the first two.
- Say which distinctions from the satire have to survive the translation into a committee procedure — I take it the default/marked asymmetry, the difference between a term used generically and a term merely claimed to be generic, and the difference between an author's own voice and a quoted source are candidates — and which can be dropped, including the race analogy itself.
- Run the procedure on three items from my docket. First, the early modern manuscript. Second, a history monograph whose quoted eighteenth-century sources use "men" for persons, where the copyeditor wants to modernize inside the quotation marks. Third, a proposal to replace "manhole" in our engineering list with a neutral compound, where the term is a term of art and the replacement would confuse readers. At least one of the three should come out against the reform-minded instinct, and I want to see the reasoning that gets there.
- Tell me what the procedure cannot do, including the case where the house itself wants to use ironic or parodic framing in the style guide's rationale, and the risk that a reader takes the parody straight.
- Identify where judgment cannot be eliminated and the committee has to record a minority position rather than manufacture a rule.

Be committed. Give me the procedure you would actually adopt, with definite recommendations on each of the three items and definite answers about where the procedure stops. If you think one of my instincts is wrong — that quoted sources should be left alone, that terms of art are exempt, that "man" is a special case because of its etymology — say so and say why. State your confidence in the procedure as a whole, and identify the single step most likely to misfire on my docket.
