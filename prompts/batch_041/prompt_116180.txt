I am trying to get clear about the argument that the special theory of relativity leaves no room for a privileged present, and I keep meeting a comparison that I would like you to audit rather than merely illustrate. The comparison is between the present and the here. It runs roughly as follows. I occupy one place among many, and no place is privileged; that does not make the other places unreal, since they are all equally real and my being here rather than there is a fact about my standpoint rather than about the world. And just as my here is fixed by where I stand, my now is fixed by how I move: an observer in a different state of motion has a different now, and nothing privileges mine. By parity, then, other times are no less real than the present, and the passage of the present is a fact about my standpoint rather than about the world. My friend, who leans on the comparison, makes it vivid with a second image: the world is a landscape through which a train travels, with the headlight marking the present, so that the country ahead of the beam is as real as the country behind it and only our forward motion makes it seem to come into existence. From this he draws a further conclusion, namely that the future is as settled as the past, that the outcome is already there like the town the train has not yet reached, and that deliberation and dread about what is to come are therefore misplaced.

Please audit the comparison on its own terms. Set out the correspondences explicitly, in a table if that helps: what is meant to play the role of the observer, the here, the other places, spatial distance, and travel, and what is meant to play the corresponding roles for time. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that are merely picturesque from those that are load-bearing for what my friend wants it to do. I am especially interested in what the spatial side quietly imports that the temporal side may not possess — whether notions such as already being there, or our moving through, can be understood without covertly placing the describer outside the series being described, and whether the comparison is entitled to the contrast between what is settled and what is still open. I have also heard it suggested that the comparison can be repaired by distinguishing what is real for an observer from what is simultaneous with that observer; say whether that distinction repairs the analogy or quietly abandons it. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion about the future, and about the pointlessness of deliberation, follows from the block picture itself or is being carried by the analogy, and, if the comparison is worth keeping at all, what a better one would be.
