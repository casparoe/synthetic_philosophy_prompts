I'm writing an essay on the encryption wars of the 1990s — the Clipper chip and the key-escrow standard that followed it, the export-control fights, the Bernstein litigation — and I want to run the episode through computational complexity rather than the familiar privacy-versus-security frame. The relevant apparatus, as I understand it, is this: cryptography's security rests on unproven assumptions that certain problems are hard on average and not merely in the worst case; the barrier results (relativization, natural proofs, algebrization) suggest our present techniques cannot settle such questions; and the taxonomy of possible average-case worlds leaves open that we might inhabit one in which hardness is only worst-case, so that no one-way function exists at all. What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than an analogy. Which players were implicitly betting on which world, and did the government's escrow proposal presuppose that hardness can be relocated out of the mathematics and into the key-management institution — and is that presupposition even coherent? Where does the theory illuminate the episode, and where is it simply silent: does it bear at all on whether escrowed access is legitimate, or only on whether it would hold? And in the other direction, what does the episode show about the theory, given that the scheme's first significant failure was a protocol flaw in the escrow field rather than any attack on the underlying cipher — what does that gap between formal hardness and deployed systems tell us about what complexity results can and cannot underwrite in policy? Run it both ways, and say plainly where the mapping thins out.
