Right — for the seminar I'm running on decision theory under deep uncertainty, I've got two students who've been circling each other for three weeks over the dark forest argument from Liu Cixin's novels (the one with the two axioms about survival being a civilization's primary need and civilizations expanding while the universe's total matter stays fixed, plus the chain of suspicion and the technological explosion), and the reason they keep circling is that neither of them has ever layed the argument out end to end. Write me the dialogue they should be having.

The pro side: someone who thinks the conclusion really does follow — you can't verify another civilization's intentions and never will, because any message can be a lie and any benign regime can be replaced; any civilization might be one breakthrough away from leapfrogging you, so the one you leave alone today can be unanswerable tomorrow; and with fixed resources the whole thing is zero-sum. On those premises, staying silent while keeping a first-strike option isn't paranoia, it's the only strategy that survives contact.

The con side: someone who thinks the argument doesn't get its conclusion from its premises but from something smuggled in, namely that a first strike is free and anonymous. I want them to actually fight over that. The critic's point, made sharply: if an attack even sometimes reveals who launched it, the predicted equilibrium doesn't just weaken, it can invert. Stay hidden and shoot first can become stay hidden and never shoot, and from the outside those look identical — silence — while implying completely different things about what the silence we observe means for us. The defender has to answer that rather than restate the premises. Let the critic also raise a signal that's genuinely expensive to fake, something like a proof of benign intent a hostile civilization couldn't cheaply produce, and let the defender say why that still wouldn't dissolve the chain of suspicion, if he thinks it wouldn't.

Then give them a case to chew on. A civilization that has obviously been around for a very long time, has obviously noticed us, and does nothing at all — no message, no probe, no strike. The defender will say fine, that's the theory working and we should be terrified; the critic will say it's equally what you'd expect from someone who simply doesn't expand, or who already lives under a norm against shooting first. Let them argue over which reading the silence actually supports, and make them say what observation would separate the two.

Keep both of them competent and honest — no strawmen — and don't let either win with a slogan. Unresolved is fine, as long as the reader can see exactly where the crux is.
