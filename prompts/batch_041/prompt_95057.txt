I am a retired geochemist — thirty-five years on a research faculty, now ten years out — who has spent the last several of those years reading philosophy with more appetite than method. For the past two years I have sat on the grants committee of a small private foundation that funds public-history work, and the committee has asked me to prepare the written opinion we will vote from at our next meeting. I would like you to draft it. I have no background in the subject matter, so I need an argument, not a reading list; please do not give me a bibliography, since I will not use it.

Two things are before us.

The first is a policy question. Our draft guidelines state that the foundation's default model for projects concerning historical atrocity is the German one — permanent, unavoidable, officially endorsed commemoration in which the perpetrating state names what it did — and that this model should be applied to applicants regardless of country. The draft then offers three tests for deciding when to deviate from the default. The first is continuity: is the state or institution that would now be doing the commemorating the same entity that committed the act, and has it ever admitted the act? The second is presence: do descendants of the victims now live as co-citizens in the polity where the memorial will stand? The third is a distinction between two kinds of contest: a contest over fact, in which what happened is denied, and a contest over meaning, in which what happened is admitted while its significance and its ownership remain disputed. Our board keeps invoking four cases as though they settled matters — Germany's reckoning with the Nazi period, Japan's handling of its wartime conduct and the official visits to a war shrine in Tokyo, Turkey's position on the events of 1915, and Confederate commemoration in the American South. It has occurred to me that these four cases pull the three tests in four different directions, and that we have been using them as rhetorical furniture rather than as a comparison.

The second item is a live application. A mid-sized city has asked us to co-fund a memorial on a site connected to a nineteenth-century massacre of an indigenous community; the descendants of the victims are now a small minority in the city and a majority in a neighboring jurisdiction. Two designs are shortlisted. The first is didactic and permanent: a wall of names, dates, and archival documents, an explicit statement of who did what, and a standing commitment to school visits. The second refuses the didactic register altogether: an unmarked structure with rotating temporary commissions by artists from both the descendant and the settler communities, no permanent interpretive text, and a provision that the structure be dismantled after thirty years unless the city's residents vote to renew it. Three further applications of the same kind are queued behind this one, so whatever we decide here will operate as policy whether we intend it to or not. Our charter states that the foundation exists to repair relations among living communities.

Please give me a memo of roughly a thousand words, in continuous prose, that does four things.

First, it should recommend a concrete course of action on the pending application — fund the first design, the second, both, or neither, and if funding with conditions, exactly what conditions and who verifies them — with a timeframe for the decision and for any conditions attached.

Second, it should say whether the German default should stand as written, and if it should not, supply replacement policy language the board can vote on, including how that language would dispose of the three queued applications. Where the three tests are doing real work, say so; where one is a proxy for something else, or where two of them conflict in a way that leaves the policy silent, say that too, and say what should happen when continuity is high and presence is low, or when denial and interpretive dispute are entangled rather than cleanly separable.

Third, it should tell me whether the four comparison cases actually support any general model, or whether each is too historically specific to bear the weight we put on it. I am particularly unsure about the fact-versus-meaning distinction: it looks useful, but I cannot tell whether it marks a real difference in what a memorial can accomplish or whether it is a distinction we invented to avoid admitting that some of these disputes are simply unwinnable.

Fourth, it should address a worry about our charter. If the foundation's purpose is the repair of relations among living communities, what is the purpose of a project whose principal wrong was done to people who are dead, and whose descendants may be absent from the polity entirely? Does that purpose make the presence test central, or does it make the presence test a distraction from something the charter does not name?

I should tell you where I lean, so that you can argue with it. I favor the second design, the one with no permanent text. But I do not trust my reason for favoring it. I spent a career on questions where a claim that cannot be checked against something outside the claimant is worth nothing, and I suspect I am smuggling a laboratory standard of evidence into a domain where it does not belong — mistaking the absence of a settled verdict for intellectual honesty when it may only be the absence of courage. Test that. If the didactic design is the better answer, say so plainly, and give the strongest case for the design I am inclined against its full due before you reject it.
