For thirty years I did experimental work in molecular biology, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have now run into a case from my old field that I cannot settle with the experimentalist's habits I used to trust. A former student of mine runs a laboratory and has spent four years gathering measurements that fit two rival accounts equally well. On one account the effect is simply the known mechanism behaving as it should under conditions no one has yet measured precisely; on the other it requires an additional, so-far unobserved entity. The two accounts make the same prediction for every experiment anyone has performed, yet they point toward different five-year research programs, and she must commit to one in order to renew her funding. She has asked me which to back. My reflex is to tell her to get more data, but I no longer trust that reflex: I have come to see that any single experiment rests on a whole web of auxiliary assumptions, so a clean measurement seldom isolates one hypothesis, and I have a vague sense that some of these standoffs between theory and evidence are temporary, in that a cleverer experiment would break them, while others may be permanent. Could you tell me how to judge which kind she is facing, whether it is rational for her to believe either account on the present evidence, and what she should actually do given that she cannot wait for the question to resolve itself? I would prefer an answer that treats this as a general problem rather than a pep talk about perseverance.
