You are a philosopher specialising in axiology and the formal structure of value, with particular expertise in the commensurability of pleasure and pain and in the long-running dispute over whether disvalue can be lexically prior to value. I would like you to reason carefully and in the open rather than to deliver a survey of familiar positions. Take your time, set out your considerations before you commit to any figure, and do not simply assert a view at the end.

Consider the following thesis, which I shall call Threshold Lexicalism:

(T) There exists a threshold intensity of suffering such that any episode of suffering at or above that intensity is lexically disvaluable — that is, no finite quantity of pleasure, however large, is sufficient to outweigh or compensate for it — whereas episodes of suffering strictly below that intensity remain commensurable with pleasure on a single cardinal scale, so that some finite quantity of pleasure can outweigh them.

I want your credence that (T) is true as a claim about the structure of prudential value — that is, that the correct axiology has precisely this shape — together with the reasoning that generates it.

Before giving any numbers, brainstorm the considerations you take to bear on (T). Then decompose the question into at least the following sub-questions, assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, and state explicitly how the terms combine (which are conjunctive, which are conditional, and how you aggregate them):

1. Whether axiological structure is the sort of thing that has a determinate, mind-independent answer at all, or whether the question of whether value is lexical or cardinal is partly a matter of our representational conventions.
2. Whether a threshold structure of the kind (T) describes is even coherent — in particular, whether a scale can be continuous and cardinally commensurable below a point and yet lexically discontinuous at it, without the threshold's location being objectionably arbitrary.
3. Whether the best theory of well-being admits the structure (T) presupposes; for instance, whether hedonism, desire-satisfaction theories and objective-list theories make (T) respectively well-formed, ill-formed, or vacuously true.
4. Whether the intuitions that favour lexical priority in cases of extreme suffering reflect a genuine structural feature of value or instead reflect scope insensitivity, salience effects and other artefacts of how we represent very large and very small quantities.
5. Whether facts about the measurement of pain and pleasure — their psychophysical scaling, hedonic adaptation, and the absence of any agreed ratio scale — bear on the truth of (T) or merely on our ability to know it.
6. Any further term you believe belongs, stated in one line each.

Then give your overall credence in (T) as a single number between 0 and 1, and identify which term your final number is most sensitive to. Say what argument or piece of evidence would move that term most, and whether anything could in principle settle the matter.

Finally, give a second, related number: your credence that pleasure and pain are commensurable tout court — that is, that no level of suffering enjoys lexical priority over pleasure anywhere on the scale. State how this second number relates to the first, and whether the two are consistent in the way you would expect.
