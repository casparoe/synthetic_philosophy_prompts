I've been bouncing between labs — a year measuring what various bits of the gut actually do in a physiology lab, and now an evolution lab where everyone talks about function in a completly different register — and I've realized the word is quietly doing two jobs and nobody will admit it. So I finally started reading the philosophical literature on biological function, figuring it would sort me out, and it's just... stuck. There's the camp that says a trait's function is whatever it was selected for — the historical thing that makes it true that a heart that can't pump is malfunctioning and not just doing something else — and the camp that says a function is simply the causal contribution a part makes to some capacity of a system, no history required, which is what lets you talk about the function of a trait that's never been under selection for it, or of a part in a mechanism someone just built. Both have been going at this for decades, every objection is on the table, and when I read them each side seems obviously right about its own cases and unable to say why the other side's cases aren't really functions. A friend in the department says it's a merely verbal dispute: one side is asking what the word 'function' means in biology, the other is asking what makes something a function out in nature, and there's no fact of the matter about which of those jobs the word is *really* for, so the whole thing should just dissolve. That sounds too quick to me but I can't quite say why.

Can you actually diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, rather than giving me a survey? I want to know: are they answering different questions under one word — and if so is that fatal or perfectly fine — or are they disagreeing about which cases count as the data (a heart that can't pump, a brand-new trait no selection has touched, a byproduct, a trait in a species about to go extinct)? Or is it bedrock intuition all the way down, where one side simply won't let go of the thought that function has to be normative and the other won't let go of the thought that it has to be mechanistic? And is there a hidden conceptual question doing the real work here — something like whether selection is the only thing that could underwrite the normativity both camps seem to want, or whether the historical side can even make sense of a function that's never once been performed?

Then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress. A distinction that splits the word cleanly, a formal result, a mutual stipulation that they were always talking about different things, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why no progress should be expected, with reasons and not just a shrug.
