I have no philosophy training and no library within an hour of me, so I am not after reading — I have been chewing on this on my own for the better part of a year, ever since an argument with my brother about something our mother did, and I want to know whether there is a principle underneath my reactions or whether I am just making it up case by case. For context, I spent six years on a sales floor before I started my own small landscaping crew, so I have done my share of steering people and had my share of being steered, and I stopped believing some time ago that the line falls between lying and not lying. My best attempt at a rule so far is this: it is persuasion if the person, seeing the whole thing afterward — what I did, what I wanted, and why — would still shake my hand, and manipulation if they would feel like a mark. But that only tells me to predict how someone would feel about a picture they never saw, so I do not trust it, and I suspect the real work is being done by something else I cannot name.

Here is where my gut actually lands. Telling a customer the cheaper mower is the one I would buy for my own mother, when it is, is fine. Running the let-me-check-with-my-manager routine when the manager is a rubber stamp and the discount was always on the table is not fine, and I was good at it, which is part of why I left. Recommending the pricier mower because I know from the returns bin that the cheap one comes back with a cracked deck is fine, even though I do not hand over the returns data. The cafeteria putting the fruit where your eye lands first is fine; the countdown timer on a website that quietly resets when it hits zero is not fine, and that one needs no agonizing. A doctor who gives me the survival numbers instead of the mortality numbers, because he thinks I will hear them better, is fine; a surgeon who leaves out the alternative because he wants to do the operation he is good at is not. My old shift manager's speech before inventory, the kind that has you running through a wall for him, is fine, even though it works on your gut and not on your reasons. My mother's line about everything she has done for me, to get me home for Thanksgiving, is not fine, even though every word of it is true and I would probably have come anyway. A friend hiding my keys when I have had too much, and swearing he does not know where they went, is fine, and I would have thanked him the next morning; the same friend hiding them to keep me from a party he was not invited to, and saying he lost them, is not fine. Telling a client another crew is bidding on the same week when none is feels fine to me, the way haggling over a truck feels fine; telling my wife I had to work late when I did not does not, and I cannot say the difference lies in what was said. And the one I keep going back and forth on: a friend who can see I am about to take the job, and who tells me a true story about someone else in that exact situation, chosen because it will push me one way, without ever admitting that is what he is doing. Nothing he says is false. I cannot decide whether that is just a friend with an opinion, or whether the hidden reason is the whole offense.

What I want from you is the principle, or the two or three competing ones, stated plainly enough that I could apply them at the counter: which of my verdicts each one captures, which verdict it forces me to give up, and what it says about a case I have not listed — offer me one I have not thought of and tell me where your principle puts it. If the honest answer is that I am tracking three different things at once, deception and who benefits and how close we are, then say so, tell me which of the three is doing the most work across my list, and tell me whether the Thanksgiving case and the key-hiding case can survive inside a single theory. I would rather have a small principle that makes me drop one of my verdicts than a large one that saves them all by growing a clause for each.
