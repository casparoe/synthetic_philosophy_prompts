Last night a friend of mine spent the better part of two hours arguing that belief, desire, hope, intention and the rest of our mental vocabulary amount to a bad empirical theory — a set of posits we invented to explain and predict behaviour, on a par with phlogiston and caloric, and due for the same fate once cognitive science grows up. What unsettled me was not the argument but my own performance against it. Every time I tried to explain why someone did something, I cited what they believed and wanted; when he forbade the words, I reached for synonyms; when he forbade those too, I mostly fell silent. He read the silence as a concession. I am not sure it was, and I would like my students put in the same position, under better conditions than a kitchen at midnight.

The setting is an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: fifteen students, one ninety-minute session. They need no prior reading on eliminativism, though they should already know that the mind-body problem is a live dispute. What I want them to come away with is a felt sense of two things at once — that folk psychology is extraordinarily hard to do without, and that its being hard to do without is not yet evidence that it is true. The name of the view should not be spoken, and no card or rule should contain it, until the debrief.

The activity is a prediction game with a banned list. Materials: twenty-four case cards, each a two- or three-sentence vignette of a person caught mid-situation with three candidate next actions, one of them the action any ordinary predictor would expect (someone has just found the milk sour and the kettle boiling; does she pour tea without milk, drink the milk, or leave the kitchen). Write them so the right answer is obvious and the interest lies entirely in the justification. Also needed: a scoresheet, a printed banned-word card per team, and a badge for the vocabulary inspector.

Round one, twenty minutes. Four teams of three or four. All teams get the same six cards. Two points for a correct prediction, and up to three more for a one-sentence justification, scored on a rubric you publish in advance: three points if the justification names a specific belief or desire that actually does the predictive work, one if it merely describes the situation, zero if it names a state with no bearing on the action. Predictions will run near ceiling and justifications will be saturated with beliefs and desires. That is the intended result, not a failure of design.

Round two, twenty-five minutes, new cards, double points. Now the banned list applies to everything a team says aloud as well as to what it writes: no belief, desire, want, hope, fear, intention, plan, expectation, memory, preference, decision, thought, feeling, understanding, or knowing-that, and no synonym coined on the spot. What is allowed is prior observable behaviour, features of the environment, habits and dispositions described behaviourally, and physiology. A vocabulary inspector drawn from a rival team polices each team; each upheld strike is worth two points to the inspector's team, and appeals go to you for thirty seconds with your ruling final. Score the round three points for a justification that is genuinely non-mentalistic and predicts correctly, one for one that predicts correctly but only because it is tailored to this case, and nothing for a strike.

Round three, fifteen minutes, no points. Each team gets eight minutes to draft a founding postulate for a psychology without beliefs and desires: a handful of primitive terms, defined by what they license you to predict, plus one worked prediction. Then six minutes of cross-examination, in which another team must name one prediction that folk psychology makes and the new framework cannot. The team that produces the most embarrassing gap has, in effect, won.

The debrief is twenty-five minutes and carries the weight. Begin with the scoreboard and the obvious question: why was round one so easy? The answers will be that we know what people believe and want, and that this is simply what people are like. Press on how they know, then point at round three: the framework they built in eight minutes is markedly worse than the one they inherited for free at birth. Two readings are available and you should leave both standing — folk psychology is true, or folk psychology is so deeply entrenched that we mistake its entrenchment for obviousness. Then take up the silence in round two, since the students will have produced the self-refutation objection themselves: if you cannot even state the eliminativist thesis without the vocabulary you are trying to eliminate, is it statable at all? Draw the distinction between the pragmatic bind and a logical contradiction — an astronomer who has given up crystalline spheres may still have to say, in a language built for them, that there are none. Next, run the phlogiston parallel with care: phlogiston was a research programme with a respectable track record, and its defenders had precisely the round-two experience. Ask what would count as the analogue for belief, and let them notice how thin folk psychology is in exactly the places where we most want a theory — mental illness, memory, perception, dreaming, creativity. Finally, separate three claims they will have run together: that belief-talk is useful, that its posits refer, and that a mature science will keep them. Eliminativism denies the second and third, not the first; instrumentalism grants the first and denies only the third. The activity bears only on usefulness, and the step from usefulness to truth is exactly the step the eliminativist is asking them to stop taking for granted. You may name the view and its principal living exponents at this point, and you should flag that the argument has a moderate version — our mental categories will be revised — and a radical one: there are no beliefs at all.

What will predictably go wrong. Teams will litigate the banned list rather than play, and you should budget for it, because the litigating is the round. Rule in advance and consistently on the hard cases: a phrase is a strike if it can only be applied by someone who already understands belief-talk, or if it is doing explanatory rather than descriptive work, so that she wanted to is a strike and she reached for it is not. One or two students will refuse to play on the grounds that the game is rigged — that a vocabulary built for predicting motion cannot be expected to predict action, and that folk psychology is not a theory but a constitutive framework of interpretation, the line from Davidson through Dennett's intentional stance. Do not let this derail the session; write it on the board as a thesis to be assessed and give it five minutes at the end. A team may win round two by writing a description so fine-grained it is effectively a lookup table; let it win, then ask in the debrief what that theory would cost to state in full, and make the point that a framework needing a separate clause per case has bought prediction at the price of being a theory. Watch for the quieter trap in which disposition-talk quietly acquires the inferential role of belief-talk, so that the team believes it has eliminated the posits when it has only renamed them; that moment is worth surfacing explicitly. Rotate the inspector role halfway through round two so it does not curdle into a grudge.

Be frank with the students about the limit. The game can make vivid the constraint of the present moment; it cannot make vivid the future of a science, and eliminativism rests on an empirical bet about what neuroscience will eventually deliver and on a case that beliefs are more like caloric than like atoms. Nothing here supports or undermines that bet. The activity also cannot represent the difference between eliminating the posits and reducing them, and its scoring rewards usefulness while remaining silent on reference — those distinctions you must supply from the front of the room.
