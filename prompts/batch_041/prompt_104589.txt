I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on legislation and statutory interpretation, and I am grading the third question of a take-home examination. The question and one student's answer are reproduced below. I need a grade and the specific comments that justify it.

The question, worth 20 points, read as follows:

"The Bayshore Reserve Fisheries Act is a hypothetical statute. It provides:

Section 1. Purpose. The purpose of this Act is to protect the fish stocks of the Bayshore Reserve from depletion by large-scale commercial harvesting.

Section 3. Definitions. As used in this Act, 'fishing vessel' means any watercraft that is used, in whole or in part, to take, hold, or transport fish for commercial purposes.

Section 7. Permit requirement. No person shall operate a fishing vessel within the Reserve without a permit issued under this Act.

Section 9. Eligibility. A permit under Section 7 shall be issued only to an applicant who demonstrates a lawful commercial fishing history in the Reserve.

Delphine Kayak Tours LLC sells guided kayak excursions in the Reserve. On each excursion a guide carries a hand net, nets a few baitfish for the clients to inspect, and releases them. The company has never engaged in commercial fishing. The permitting agency has notified the company that its kayaks are 'fishing vessels' within the meaning of Section 3, and that the company may not operate them in the Reserve without a permit, which, having no commercial fishing history, it cannot obtain.

Analyze whether the kayaks are 'fishing vessels' under the Act. Address separately (i) the analysis a textualist would give, (ii) the analysis a purposivist would give, and (iii) whether the two approaches converge on the same result. For each, identify the specific feature of the statute on which the analysis turns, and note any respect in which the analysis is underdetermined by the materials given. Answers are expected to state each approach accurately, identify the statutory features on which each turns, address convergence, and flag anything the materials leave open."

The student's answer read as follows:

"The ordinary meaning of 'fishing vessel' is a watercraft used for fishing. Textualism requires a court to enforce the ordinary meaning of the statutory words and, where that meaning is plain, to look no further. The kayaks here are used to take fish, so they fall within the ordinary meaning, and Section 7 applies to them. It might be objected that a kayak is not really a fishing boat, but textualism rejects resort to statutory purpose or legislative intent, so the objection fails. The phrase 'for commercial purposes' in Section 3 does not change the result: the excursions are sold to clients, so the taking of the baitfish is commercial. The textualist therefore concludes that the kayaks are fishing vessels. Purposivism asks instead what problem the legislature was trying to solve. Section 1 states that the aim is to protect the Reserve's fish stocks from depletion by large-scale commercial harvesting, and Section 9 limits permits to applicants with a commercial fishing history; both indicate that the Act is aimed at industrial fishing rather than at sightseeing excursions. The purposivist therefore concludes that the kayaks are not fishing vessels. And because the purpose is clear, the purposivist is entitled to depart from the literal words in order to serve it. The two approaches accordingly do not converge."

Please assess the answer. Your response should have two parts.

First, a point-by-point assessment. Go through the answer's claims in order. For each, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or correct but imprecise, and give the reason. Where a claim is wrong, identify the precise defect: a conflation of two distinct ideas, an inference that does not follow, an overstatement, or the omission of something the question required the student to address. Where a claim is right, say what makes it right, so that the comments can also serve as positive feedback. Note separately any place where the student reaches a defensible conclusion by an unsound route, and any place where a defensible point is stated so broadly that it would be false as stated.

Second, a grade out of 20, followed by a short paragraph explaining how the point total was arrived at: what the answer earned credit for, what it lost credit for, and whether the errors are of the kind that a full-credit answer had to avoid.

Two cautions. The student's prose is fluent and confident; do not let that substitute for correctness. And do not grade the answer by whether you would have reached the same conclusions; grade it by whether the analysis is sound, whether it engages the features of the statute that the question asked about, and whether it distinguishes what the materials determine from what they leave open.
