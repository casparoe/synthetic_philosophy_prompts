The two jobs of belief-talk

I run an LLM agent that fixes failing tests. In standups I say things like "it thinks the failure is in the tokenizer" or "it believes the cache is stale." What am I actually doing when I say that?

The standard answer is Dennett's idea of the intentional stance: you predict a system's behavior by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, and the stance is "earned" if it lets you predict well. You don't need the beliefs to be real in some deep sense; you need the prediction to work. That's the gist as I understand it from summaries, so correct me if I'm mangling it.

Here's my wrinkle. Belief-talk seems to do two different jobs, and we slide between them without noticing.

Job A is prediction / engineering leverage. "It thinks X, so it will probably do Y." This is the Dennett job. It's a modeling convenience. If the attribution helps me anticipate the next action or debug faster, great, it's earned.

Job B is accountability. "It believed the file was safe, so it's on the hook for the bad edit." Here the belief is being used to assign something like responsibility -- to justify trusting it, distrusting it, blaming it, or praising it. This is a different kind of move.

These two come apart. An attribution can be excellent for A and worthless for B. "It thinks the tokenizer is broken" might be a great predictive handle while giving me zero grounds for holding it responsible.

The part I find interesting: the usual defense of the stance doesn't even try to cover B, and for an LLM the A-case gets weird. Dennett's practical argument, roughly, is that you can't feasibly run the low-level mechanistic model, so the intentional stance earns its keep by being cheaper than the alternative. But with an LLM I can run the low-level model -- I've got weights, activations, logits, an interpretability dashboard. The mechanistic account is often right there, and it's the thing that actually tells me why the tokenizer attribution was a hallucination. So the predictive case for the stance seems weaker than in the original settings, while the accountability case is completely untouched by any of that. The interesting question moves.

So I want to propose a test. Call a belief attribution load-bearing if dropping it costs you predictive or explanatory power that a lower-level (mechanistic or purely behavioral) account can't recover. My claim: almost all useful belief-talk about AI systems is accountability-talk wearing prediction-talk's clothes. Engineers slide from "it thinks X" (fine, harmless shorthand) to "it knew X and did Y anyway" (a moral-ish claim) without noticing the slide, and the stance literature mostly has tools for the first and not the second.

Things I'm worried about: (1) maybe this is just the is/ought gap in a hoodie, (2) maybe it's just the anthropomorphism complaint with extra steps, (3) maybe the load-bearing test is circular because "explanatory power" is doing all the work and I never define it, (4) I might be attributing a cleaner argument to Dennett than he actually makes.

What I want from you: tell me which of those worries are fatal, whether the A/B split is genuinely useful or just relabeling something standard, whether the load-bearing test is salvageable or should be cut, and how to make the whole thing tighter -- it feels waffly and I keep repeating myself.
