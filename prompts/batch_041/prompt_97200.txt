My friend and I spent an evening arguing about the handicap principle and ended up exactly where we started, so what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Here is the standoff. I take the principle seriously as an explanation: a signal is honest because it is costly, and the cost is what keeps cheaters out, so wherever a trait looks like it exists to be seen, the principle earns its keep. He thinks it is a just-so story. Any signal that survives is, by definition, not too costly, so on his view nothing could count as a counterexample; and where the trait is causally tied to the quality it advertises — an index, in the usual contrast with a signal — no strategic cost is needed at all. When I point to formal treatments showing that honest signaling can be stable only when the cost is real, he replies that this shows only that some cost is compatible with equilibrium, not that the cost is what carries the information. Neither of us moved an inch. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually — a factual one about how much of the biological world works this way, a disagreement about what counts as a test of an adaptationist hypothesis, or a verbal one about what "cost" and "signal" are being made to mean? My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that it is mostly the second: that we are applying different standards of evidence under a single heading. Take a definite position on this and defend it. Tell me what the disagreement really is, using what each side actually claims, and then say what, if anything, would move it — a decisive case, a formal result, a distinction — or argue that no progress should be expected here and why.
