I run a fortnightly reading group on coordination failures and I keep hitting the same wall: the moment someone says 'multipolar trap' or 'Moloch' the conversation dies, because everyone nods and nobody has to say what the label is doing that 'tragedy of the commons' or a bog-standard prisoner's dilemma wasn't already doing. So I want you to take one case and squeeze it properly: the collapse of the northern cod off Newfoundland and Labrador. Foreign factory trawlers gutting the Grand Banks through the 60s, Canada pushing the 200-mile limit in the late 70s, the foreign fleets out and Canadian ones taking their place, quotas set too high, inshore fishermen saying for years that the fish were gone while the assessments kept coming back fine, and then the moratorium in '92 with the stock down to a sliver of what it had been.

Heres the criterion I've been working with, and I'd like you to tell me if it's the right one. A multipolar trap needs (i) each actor's best response to what the others are doing making things worse for everyone, including themselves; (ii) no unilateral exit, not because you'd be punished but because not playing means being selected out; (iii) an outcome that is nobody's goal and may not even be in anyone's preference ordering; (iv) no shared payoff matrix, just a lot of actors following the same gradient. Run the cod case through that. Who are the players? Is the trawler operator choosing to overfish, or is he just the one who survives? Could any single actor have stopped it alone -- a firm, Ottawa, the DFO scientists?

Then flip it, which is the part I actually care about. What does the case do to the theory? Specifically: how much of this was a trap at all rather than a straight epistemic failure -- the assessments were wrong, and if a trap requires the actors to know the structure they're in, a crowd that's simply mistaken about the stock size isn't obviously in one. Second, is Ottawa's refusal to cut quotas itself a nested trap (every government faces the same jobs-now gradient, so the regulator is just another actor on the same slope), or is that too generous a reading? Third, the moratorium was imposed on the industry, not agreed to by it. Does that mean the trap was never really 'solved', only interrupted from outside, and if so does that gut the no-exit condition?

Dont hand me 'tragedy of the commons with more players'. Say which piece of machinery does the real work here and where each piece misdescribes the case. And tell me how confident you are in the main claims, and flag the single point you're least sure about.
