I'm a backend engineer (Go, Postgres, some half-broken NLP pipelines) with basically zero formal philosophy training, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole about definate descriptions — the whole 'the current king of France' / 'the guy who wrote Waverley' thing. As I understand it there are two big stories. Russell's version says a sentence like 'the F is G' is secretly an existential claim — there's an F, there's exactly one F, and it's G — so when nothing is F the sentence just comes out flatly false. Then there's the other tradition (Frege, Strawson, I think?) where 'the F' is doing something more like presupposing that an F exists, so if it doesn't, the sentence isn't false so much as it misfires and has no truth value at all.

Here's why it's bugging me. In our codebase we're super careful to seperate three things: a field that was never set (undefined), a field explicitly set to nothing (null), and an exception that gets thrown when you dereference a pointer that points nowhere. I've got this half baked feeling that Russell treats 'the F' like a null — you can still run predicates against it and you just get back false — whereas the presupposition crowd treats it like dereferencing a dangling pointer, where the whole evaluation blows up instead of politely returning false. My own even more half-baked third idea is to treat a description like a promise/future: the sentence is well-formed, but its truth value is 'pending' until the reference resolves, and if it never resolves you get a distinct outcome that's neither true nor false nor an error. I honestly can't tell if that's a real third position or just me renaming the presupposition view in JS.

So could you compare the Russellian view and the presupposition view for me, and tell me straight which one my null-vs-dangling-pointer analogy actually fits, and where the analogy falls apart? I'd love it worked through on the annoying cases: negation ('the king of France is not bald' — does it matter where the 'not' attaches, because I keep hearing it does), negative existentials ('the king of France doesn't exist' — I have a feeling one of the two views is in real trouble there), and descriptions that pick out fictional or failed things. Then tell me honestly whether my promise/optional-type idea is genuinely a third option or just presupposition failure wearing a different hat.
