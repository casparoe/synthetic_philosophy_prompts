I'm a PhD student in statistics working on causal inference — structural causal models, the do-operator, potential outcomes, the invariance/autonomy literature, the whole apparatus. I've been reading about Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I want to see how much of that position actually survives a translation into my vocabulary, rather than being flattered by it. Please do the translation and, more importantly, the residue report.

First, so we're working with the same target vocabulary: directed acyclic graphs, exogenous and endogenous variables, structural equations and their modularity, interventions via the do-operator, the invariance or autonomy assumption (a mechanism counts as causal to the extent that it stays stable under interventions on its inputs), confounding and collider structure, and the distinction between a model that merely predicts well and one that supports counterfactual queries.

The position, as I understand it: the occasionalist denies that created things have any causal power, or that there are necessary connections among them; what look like causal relations are regularities or habits, and the only genuine cause is God, who directly produces each effect on each occasion. Restate that as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. In particular, I want you to try the most natural formalization I can think of — a single exogenous variable, call it W (divine will), that is a common cause of every observable variable, with the apparent edges among observables being artifacts of a structure in which each observable is a direct child of W and of nothing else — and then interrogate that formalization rather than just asserting it. Does it capture the position, or does it capture a nearby position that happens to share its observable consequences?

Then the residue report. Which parts of the occasionalist position translate cleanly into SCMs, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? I'm especially interested in four things. First, is the denial of necessary connection the same claim as the claim that the mechanisms are not invariant under intervention, or are those two different claims that my framework tends to conflate? Second, does the single-common-cause model say anything over and above a re-description of the same joint distribution — and if it is empirically equivalent to an ordinary DAG over the same variables, in what sense can the occasionalist claim to be saying something about the world rather than about which description we prefer? Third, the miracle argument — the idea that because the connection is not necessary, the effect could occur without the usual cause — reads to me like a counterfactual claim, except that the counterfactual is one that no intervention in my sense could realize; is that a coherent move inside the semantics I use, or is that precisely where the translation fails? Fourth, what does the occasionalist say that my framework cannot even frame, and is that residue a sign that my vocabulary is missing something real (a notion of production, power, or genuine dependence, say) or a sign that the original position is doing less work than it appears to?

I know there's a live interpretive dispute about how thoroughgoing the occasionalism actually is — whether it is a full denial of created causality or something closer to a thesis about divine habit and secondary causation. Don't settle that for me by appeal to authority; tell me which reading my formalization presupposes, and whether the other reading would translate differently.
