I'm assembling a proposal for a small interdisciplinary workshop grant — the sort of thing a university humanities center funds for two days, eight or so invited participants, and a short written outcome — and I want it to be about a question I have been circling for years without ever getting it to sit still. The seed is the MU puzzle from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: you begin with the string MI, you have four rewrite rules, and you are asked to reach MU, which is impossible; the standard proof finds an invariant — the number of I's in any derivable string is never divisible by three — and that proof becomes available only once you stop trying to derive and start looking at the system from outside. The leap that produces the invariant is what people call jootsing, jumping out of the system, and I have a half-formed suspicion that we lump at least three distinct achievements under that one heading: noticing that a search has stalled; finding some feature of the system that happens to be invariant; and recognizing that this feature is the right sort of thing to explain the failure, that it tracks the reason rather than merely correlating with it. My hunch is that almost all of the philosophical interest, and almost all of the disagreement between people who treat jootsing as a genuine epistemic move and people who suspect it is post-hoc storytelling, lives in that third step, and that the MU puzzle is unusually clean for isolating it because the invariant is so obvious once seen and so invisible before.

Please turn this into the proposal document. State the question so that it has a determinate answer — something in the vicinity of: is there a principled distinction between an invariant that merely holds across all reachable states and one that explains why the target state is unreachable, and if so, what marks the difference? — then say why the obvious answers leave it open: the formal answer tells you when an invariant suffices but not when you have found the right one; the computational answer, search first and then reflect on the search, describes the sequence of events without distinguishing lucky correlations from explanatory ones; and the deflationary answer, that it is just creativity, refuses the question rather than answering it. Sketch a method a two-day workshop could actually execute: work through the MU puzzle itself; have participants invent a few small rewrite systems on the spot, some with decoy invariants that hold but do not explain, and see whether the criteria we propose can tell decoys from the real thing without being told in advance which is which; and include at least one case from outside formal systems, perhaps the moment in a debugging session when someone reframes what the program is really doing and the failure suddenly makes sense. Say what a successful outcome looks like — a defensible taxonomy of the three steps, plus at least one non-circular criterion for explanatory as opposed to merely holding invariants — and what would count as failure, including the real possibility that the third step collapses into the second and the distinction I care about is illusory.
