I'm in the middle of a decision theory module and I've ended up in a running argument with a mate about the Allais paradox, and I suspect we're both getting something wrong. The setup we've been using is this: first, choose between (A) £1m for certain and (B) an 89% chance of £1m, a 10% chance of £5m and a 1% chance of nothing. Then, choose between (C) an 11% chance of £1m with nothing otherwise and (D) a 10% chance of £5m with nothing otherwise. Everyone I've asked so far takes A in the first and D in the second, and I'll admit I do to.

My mate's line of defence is basically that certainty is a good in its own right, so any lottery containing a guaranteed branch gets a kind of bonus that a lottery without one doesn't, and once you allow that bonus the A/D pattern is no less rational than any other set of preferences. He reckons nothing in the bare idea of rationality forces you to treat the 89% branch as irrelevant to the first choice, and he adds that when he re-describes the first choice to himself as a two-stage affair — an 89% chance of £1m whatever happens, then a 1-in-11 shot at the second stage — he still wants A, so it can't just be the description doing the work. What I want is an essay on whether the A/D pattern is genuinely irrational. Please weigh the main lines of argument on both sides — the independence axiom, the sure-thing reasoning that seems to drive the standard verdict, the suspicion that the charge of irrationality quietly assumes the two choices should be assessed as a single portfolio rather than one at a time, and whether the pattern looks better or worse if I know in advance that I'll be facing the first choice twenty times over instead of once. Don't sit on the fence at the end: tell me what you think a sensible person should actually pick and why. British English please.
