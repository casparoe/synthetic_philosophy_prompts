I am assembling a handout for a decision-theory reading group on acausal threats, and I would like you to write the decision tree that will anchor it. By an acausal threat I mean a threat whose threatened punishment is not causally downstream of the choice it is meant to influence: your complying and the threatener's punishing are linked only by a shared logical or algorithmic fact — the threatener simulates your decision procedure, or your act and its policy are both fixed by the same counterfactual rule, or the relevant facts are Platonic rather than physical. The recurring failure I want the handout to correct is that readers collapse this into an ordinary mugging, where the mugger's pulling the trigger is causally contingent on your handing over the wallet, and then argue by analogy from the wrong case.

Please produce a decision tree whose internal nodes are commitments a participant can state without already knowing which position they lead to, and whose leaves are named positions. The tree should turn, in roughly this order, on the following distinctions:

1. Whether an agent's policy can rationally depend on your decision procedure rather than on the causal consequences of your act. A participant who denies this — the causal-decision-theory rejection — is a leaf; nothing downstream applies to them.
2. Given that it can, whether the dependence makes the threat binding (a genuine reason to comply) or merely correlates your act with the punishment without giving you a reason.
3. If binding, which response is correct: comply case by case; adopt a standing policy of never complying and absorb the punishment; redesign your decision procedure so that you are not the sort of agent a threatener profits from targeting; or adopt what I will call a surrogate goal — a stand-in objective you genuinely come to hold, chosen so that a threat aimed at it leaves your true values untouched, while in ordinary, unthreatened cases your conduct stays close to what your true values would have recommended.
4. Whether the threatener's own incentives make the threat credible — whether carrying it out costs the threatener anything, and whether the threatener gains from being known as the kind of agent that carries out threats.

Phrase every node as a concrete question or a two-person case, never as "are you a causal or an evidential theorist?"; the participant should be able to answer it and only then learn which position it selects. Each leaf should carry the name of the position if one exists in the literature and, where none does, say so explicitly and describe the view in a sentence. Include a closing paragraph on the combinations of answers your tree cannot represent, diagnosing each as incoherent, unstable, or merely unoccupied, and flag any two leaves your tree fails to separate rather than papering over the gap.
