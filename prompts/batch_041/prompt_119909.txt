I'm in a decision-theory reading group and we keep crashing into the same wall. Someone will say, in that flat way that's meant to end the conversation, something like: 'look, if your credences are coherent then no bookie can construct a set of bets that's a sure loss for you, and if a bookie can construct one, then you're just being irrational — that's the entire Dutch book argument, end of story.' It came up most recently because I said I wasn't sure it's always a mistake to have credences that don't sum to exactly 1, since I don't know whether a credence in a borderline-vague proposition even has a determinate value in the first place, and someone reacted like I'd denied that 2+2=4. I have a nagging feeling there's a huge gap between the boring thing that slogan is definitely true of and the thing people are using it to do, and I can't tell which one is doing the work.

So could you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that 'you can't be rational and be Dutch-bookable' might actually be making? Things in the vicinity: the bare mathematical fact about what a bookie who knows your credences can do to you; the claim that being Dutch-bookable *just is* being irrational, as opposed to being evidence of or a symptom of some deeper defect (and if the latter, what the deeper defect is supposed to be); the purely epistemic reading where the money is a distraction and the real fault is that your credences don't fit together, with the betting story only a vivid dramatization; the reading on which all you get is a practical / means-end kind of irrationality rather than an epistemic one; the reading where the whole thing begs the question because we stipulated at the outset that credences are betting quotients; the diachronic version where the book spans two times and only bites you if you'd have accepted the bets at both; and whatever else belongs on the list. For each reading I want a verdict — theorem, platitude, substantive normative claim, or just false — plus the assumptions it leans on, because I suspect most of the work is being done by things like: that you value money linearly (or that we can pick stakes so the loss matters), that you'll take each bet in isolation at the odds you yourself quote, that the bookie knows your credences, that the bets are actually offered to you, and that you can't simply refuse the combination. And then tell me which reading it is that people are invoking when they say 'you'd be Dutch bookable' to mean 'you're simply irrational,' and whether that reading survives the standard objections or whether my vague-credence case is a genuine counterexample rather than just a weird edge case. I'd like to be able to say, next time, exactly which premise I'm denying when I deny the slogan.
