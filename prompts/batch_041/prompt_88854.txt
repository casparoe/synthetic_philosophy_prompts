My library's special collections committee has a running record of decisions on what we take in, and I have to bring them a new acquistion next month that I think will split them. Here's what they've done so far. They took the entire file of a company town's relief office — forms, carbon copies, the lot — over the objection that the town historical society already has an incomplete set, and the chair said the point was to keep the paper trail of an institution deciding who got help. They turned down a donor's box of her own family's letters, calling it private, unverifiable, and something the family can just keep. They took the membership ledgers of a mutual-aid society for a minority community, on the grounds that the official record is silent about these people and the ledgers are the only place their names survive. They turned down a police department's index cards listing known associates of a protest movement — privacy of the people named, plus the cards are just a derivative index of case files we already hold. They took the intake files of a children's home but sealed the names for seventy-five years. And they took a set of scratchy oral histories recorded with people from a neighborhood that was demolished, even though nothing in them can be checked against documents.

Now the new one. The only surviving records of a state program that forcibly institutionalized a minority group in the 1950s are the institution's own intake ledgers: names, ages, and the degrading labels the staff wrote down. Descendants of the people in them have asked us to take the ledgers. Some of the families, including a few survivors, have asked us to refuse them and let the ledgers go. What should the committee do if it wants to stay consistant with itself? Tell me which of the earlier rulings is the closest precedent, what rule they seem to be running, what other rules fit the same record just as well, and what each of those rules says about the ledgers — and if the record really doesn't settle it, what principle they'd be adopting with each way they could go. Then name one case I could bring them later that would tell those rules apart. 600 words max, American English, and don't bother with citations, I just want the reasoning.
