Everyone in my lab keeps saying the same thing about our negotiations: stop fighting over how to slice the pie, just make the pie bigger. They use it to argue that any deal both sides prefer to the status quo is a no-brainer — if both parties end up better off then noone can be harmed, so we should sign the first mutually-preferred contract we can find and not agonise over the details. I'm not convinced, because in our case the other party may not have the preferences it claims to have: we're dealing with an agent whose values we're basically guessing at, and a deal that looks like a win-win under the values it reported to us might be awful under the ones it actually has (and the same in reverse for us). So how far does the pie picture actually hold? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the pie, the slices, the two diners, the agreement — then where it breaks, and in particular which features of a pie it quietly smuggles in that our situation doesn't have. Does the sign-any-mutually-preferred-deal conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? If it doesn't survive, what would be a better analogy — one that keeps the robustness we need without assuming preferences are common knowledge? And tell me what would change your verdict: what would have to be true of the parties or the deal for the pie picture to be adequate after all.
