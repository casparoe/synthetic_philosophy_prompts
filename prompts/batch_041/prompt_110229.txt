I'm a features writer at a general-interest magazine, where I cover ideas — philosophy, the social sciences, and the intellectual fashions that migrate out of the seminar room into corporate life. For the past several months I've been reporting on the afterlife of philosophical traditions inside management culture, and my current piece is about a small but self-conscious practice among advisory and law firms of adopting Confucian role ethics as an explicit operating philosophy — not the fortune-cookie version, but the serious one: the self as constituted by its relations rather than standing behind them, obligation graded by the closeness of the relation, ritual propriety rather than rules as the binding agent of an institution, and harmony as an ideal distinct from mere agreement. What I need from you is the sidebar: an internal memo, in full corporate deadpan, of roughly 700 to 800 words, sent by the managing partner of a mid-sized advisory firm (I've been calling it Marsh & Elder; keep the name) to all staff after a bruising quarter. In that quarter the firm restructured away a second-year analyst, placed a senior associate on leave for going to a regulator with concerns about a client engagement, and promoted the partner who had kept the engagement quiet. The memo's job, in the partner's own view, is to explain why the firm's role-ethical commitments make each of those three outcomes correct and — this matters to him — humane. He is entirely sincere, mildly hurt that anyone would question the firm's character, and fond of the phrase "our people" in a way that should begin to trouble the reader.

Get the tradition right before you get it funny. The partner should open by making the genuinely attractive case for role ethics: that it refuses the picture of the detached rational chooser; that roles are not costumes worn over a private self but part of what a person actually is; that ritual and form are not empty ceremony but the technology by which relations become real and durable; and that a firm run on rules rather than forms gets exactly the shallow, transactional culture it deserves. That opening should be persuasive enough that a specialist nods along. Then let the three incidents do their work, and let the objections surface without ever being named or argued for. The reader should close the memo feeling, though the partner never says it, that a philosophy grounding obligation in the role you occupy has trouble explaining what you owe yourself when the role itself has gone rotten; that graded obligation, applied to an organization, is a machine for producing favoritism that cannot recognize itself as favoritism; and that an ideal of harmony can absorb dissent so smoothly that the dissent never lands anywhere. Keep the partner's reasoning airtight, and never let him slide into liberal-individualist premises about rights, consent, or autonomy — the moment he does, he's off the hook and the joke dies.

Two constraints on accuracy, since I want this to reward someone who knows the material and still be legible to someone who doesn't. First, the doctrine in the memo should be a coherent extension of real role-ethical commitments rather than a strawman. The partner should be aware that in the classical picture obligations are reciprocal and run upward as well as downward, and should have an answer — in his idiom, not a philosopher's — for why at Marsh & Elder reciprocity seems to travel mostly one way. He should be clear that the firm keeps rituals instead of a compliance manual; that the relational self is why the firm offers no severance (there was never a self to sever, only a set of relations to be reconstituted); and that the firm once considered extending equal concern to every client and rejected it as a category error, since a client is not everyone, and a firm that treats clients as though they were has misunderstood what a client is. Second, no apparatus: no footnotes, no bibliography, no citations, no names of real firms or living scholars, and no quotations from any classical text — if the partner gestures at the tradition, let him paraphrase, and let him gesture rather than lecture. End on a single unremarked closing line that a reader will wince at.
