Two years ago I helped start a Friday reading group — six of us, half from the law faculty and half from the game-design lab — that meets to play a game in which the rules are changed by moves inside the game, including the rules that govern how rules are changed. (It belongs to the family of games usually called Nomic, though what we play is our own house rule-set, drifted far enough from anything published that I wouldn't defend it as canonical.) At the end of this term we're holding a two-day symposium on self-amendment, open to the whole department, and it doubles as a send-off for the professor who founded the group and retires in June. She has argued for thirty years that any system of self-amending rules must contain something that cannot itself be amended — that without a fixed point nothing in the system determines which changes are valid, and that a rule-set with no fixed point is not a rule-set but a rumour. She will be in the front row. I have spent those two years arguing the other way, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks: five to seven minutes, to a room of about forty, roughly half of them designers who have never read a page of legal theory and half of them lawyers and graduate students who have read far too much of it. I am taking a course on constitutional theory this term, which is where I met the idea of a basic norm — a norm whose validity is presupposed rather than enacted — and I still cannot tell whether that idea is a discovery about how legal systems work or a dignified way of giving up.

The episode I want to build the remarks around is one we actually played out in week six, and I'll set it out here so you can use it. Our rule-set fixed a two-thirds majority for any amendment; a motion to lower the threshold to a simple majority passed comfortably under the old rule; a fortnight later a motion to restore the two-thirds requirement passed by a single vote under the new one; and then a motion adding a clause that the amendment rule may never again be amended passed under the restored rule and locked itself in place. What I want the room to feel is the queasiness of that last move: it is valid only if the previous one was, which is valid only if the first was, which is valid only because a rule that nobody ever enacted was treated as authoritative — and the lock, when it finally arrives, is a wall built by precisely the process it claims to stop. I think the real question is whether a chain of valid changes can be valid all the way down with no anchor outside itself, and I want her to hear that as a question in her own vocabulary rather than as my answer to it.

So please write the remarks in full. They have to honour her and her position in a form she would sign her name to, without pretending to agree with her and without letting the tribute curdle into a rebuttal; they must not settle the question, since the whole symposium exists in order to settle it; and they must not take the easy exit of saying that it is all convention and therefore no problem, because she has spent a career treating that move as a dodge and the room knows it. The designers need to follow every step of the episode without a glossary, the lawyers need to follow it without being talked down to, there should be one moment where the whole room laughs together rather than at anyone, and the last line should hand the question to the room rather than land a thesis. After the remarks, add three or four sentences, not to be read aloud, on which parts of the speech are pitched at which half of the room and where I am most at risk of losing one of them.
