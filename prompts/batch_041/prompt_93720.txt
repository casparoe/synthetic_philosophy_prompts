I've been sitting in on a reading group some coworkers run on decision theory (I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, so about half of it goes over my head), and I tried to write out one of the arguments we chewed on because I think it proves way too much. Someone said it's basically Harsanyi's theorem, though I have no idea if that's the right name. Here's my reconstruction:

(1) A society choosing between policies can be treated as a single chooser with a ranking over the outcomes. (2) That chooser is rational -- its ranking is consistent, and when outcomes are uncertain it follows the usual expected-utility rules; in particular, if it prefers A to B whether or not some event happens, then it prefers A to B overall. (3) It only cares about how well off people end up: if a policy leaves everyone at least as well off as another policy and makes at least one person better off, the society prefers it. (Assume everyone's well-being can be put on one common number line so the numbers are comparable.) (4) It's impartial: swapping which person gets which life leaves the ranking unchanged. (5) Since it doesn't care which person it is, it has no reason to think it's more likely to end up as any one member than another, so it behaves as if it faces a fair lottery over who it'll turn out to be. (6) By (2), it picks the policy with the highest expected well-being, and that's just the average of everyone's well-being -- total divided by headcount. (7) So the society always ranks policies by the total and is indifferent between any two with the same total, however lopsided the split. (8) Which means it's indifferent between a world where everyone is equally comfortable and a world where one person lives in luxury while everybody else scrapes by, whenever the totals match. I don't think any morality worth having says that, so something in there has to be wrong.

Two people in the group blamed different steps and both sounded convincing, which is definately why I'm stuck. Can you tell me which step actually breaks, why it looked fine until you push on it, and what it costs to give up -- what else falls with it? If I have to sacrifice something, the parts I'm surest about are the "nobody worse off and someone better off" rule and the idea that everyone counts equally; the parts I'm shakiest on are the equal-likelihood move in (5) and the claim in (1) that a whole society is literally one rational chooser. Please lead with your bottom line -- which step, if any, you'd reject -- in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. American English, please.
