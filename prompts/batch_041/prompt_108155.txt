I'm on an advisory panel for our state health department, and somehow I got handed the job of writing the "precautionary rule" we're supposed to apply to the gene-drive mosquito question — whether to recommend field trials of a strain meant to crash the local malaria vector population. The thing is, the more I stare at the standard move here, the more it looks like it can be aimed at anything. I can say don't release, the ecological fallout is uncertain; or say release, the malaria deaths are certain and the delay is the harm. The other side just says the mirror image back at me. Our chair keeps saying well, it's the precautionary principle, as if that settled which way it cuts. So before I write a word of the actual rule, I want the spec: what would a theory of precaution — as a real decision rule for a regulator, not a vibe — actually have to deliver?

Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each one. The cases I care about: the gene drive itself; a drug with a rare side effect that only shows up at scale; a chemical where we have a bad animal signal and basically nothing else; and a case where the precautionary call turned out to be the thing that killed people — delayed approvals, moratoriums on stuff that later looked fine — because a rule that can't handle that last one is a bias, not a principle. Distinctions I don't want collapsed: risk vs. genuine uncertainty vs. we don't even know what to look for; precaution vs. old-fashioned prevention of a known hazard; reversible vs. irreversible harms; who has to carry the burden of proof and why; and whether a pile of small individually-acceptable risks can add up to something the rule has to catch. I also want the meta-requirements — that it be action-guiding (tells me yes or no, not be careful), that it not be re-aimable at every available option, and that it not quietly smuggle in a cost-benefit calculation while pretending to be a different kind of principle.

Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp, and — the part I actually need — whether some subset of them can't all hold at once. My hunch is that action-guiding and doesn't-just-reduce-to-expected-utility are in a fight, but I dont know if that's a real result or just me being confused. And say which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise-popular versions of the principle. Skip the scorecard of the named versions, I'll do that in the meeting.
