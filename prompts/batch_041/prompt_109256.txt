I am a third-year philosophy student, currently taking a course on ethics and social change, and I have to write a 4,000-word essay on moral revolutions. The lecture gave us three candidates for what ends a practice like slavery or duelling: argument, interest, honour. The honour account was put to us roughly as follows — a practice dies when it becomes dishonourable rather than when it is refuted, with duelling and footbinding as the standard cases, and Appiah's The Honor Code (2010) as the book on that side of the contrast. I want to argue that the three-way contrast is unstable and to put something narrower in its place. I have notes only, and I would rather be told now that the replacement is a redescription than discover it at the end.

My three claims are these. First, the mechanisms cannot be separated: the arguments against slavery that did the work were arguments about what it would be shameful to be caught defending, and the honour revolutions needed arguments, if only to give the shame something to attach to — one cannot be ashamed of a practice one cannot describe as a practice. Neither mechanism explains the timing on its own, and the interest story fails for the same reason: it cannot explain why the practice ended in a public reckoning rather than quietly. Second, what changes at the moment of revolution is the availability of a second-order excuse. Defenders of a dying practice seldom assert its justice; they say they did not choose it, inherited it, that it is the way of the world, that their own conduct within it is decent. My claim is that a practice falls when that excuse-form becomes unusable in public — when it can no longer be said without being lost by the saying of it — and not when the first-order justification is refuted. Third, this is helped by the way people under a bad practice contrive not to know what they know: the planter who never reads the pamphlet, the household that does not ask where the sugar came from. A refuted justification can be lived with for a long time on that kind of arranged ignorance, but not once the arrangement is visible as an arrangement.

I mean to run the argument through abolition, the duel in Britain, and one contrast case in which a justification became unspeakable and the practice carried on regardless; I have not settled which case to use for the last and would welcome a suggestion.

What I would like from you: comments on accuracy first, then on interest. Where have I misdescribed the honour account, given that I am working from one lecture and a summary of the book? Is my second claim a claim at all, or only a redescription of "the arguments finally landed" — and if it is a claim, what would show it false? I have not been able to think of anything that would, which worries me. Is "unspeakable" doing moral work or only sociological work, and does the essay have to say which, and does saying it commit me to the view that moral revolutions are just status competition with a longer time-lag? Which of the three claims is load-bearing, and is the third doing too much work to keep? Finally, where should the contrast case come in — before or after the thesis is stated — so that it reads as a test rather than as an admission? Please write your comments in British English.
