I'm on the programming committee of a small graduate philosophy society, and each year we produce a satirical supplement to our termly newsletter: past editions have included a deadpan travel-guide entry for a country that has fully implemented epistocracy, and a set of terms and conditions for an upload service, and this year the centrepiece is mine. I've spent a few months reading around the long-run violence statistics — the whole genre of books and papers claiming that violence has declined over the course of human history — and I've come to think the interesting object isn't the trend itself but the machinery underneath it: what gets counted, which denominator gets chosen, and what happens when the two disagree. So I'd like you to write that centrepiece as a leaked internal memo from the methodology committee of an invented institute that publishes an annual global violence index, circulated in the week before the press conference at which another record low will be announced. The register should be deadpan bureaucratic — numbered agenda items, faintly apologetic tone, an aside that the press office has already drafted the headline — and the comedy has to come from the conceptual choices themselves rather than from jokes at the expense of statisticians, so that someone who works on this material laughs and recognises the argument rather than wincing.

Roughly 700–900 words, British spelling, no citations and no named scholars or real institutions (the institute, its committee and its critics should all be invented, and any figures should come from its own obviously fictional series rather than from real history). The memo should work through, in whatever order seems natural to a committee under deadline: a revision that moves something formerly counted as violence into a residual category, together with the awkward fact that the revision is itself what produces the record low; the divergence between the per-capita rate and the absolute number of victims, with a toy example in which a smaller and more brutal world outranks a larger and gentler one, and some unease about the fact that the denominator is not independent of what happens to the numerator; the unreliability of the historical baseline, where the early figures rest on sources that would not survive review; the substitution of state violence for private violence, and whether a monopoly on killing should be scored as a reduction; the boundary problem about deaths by omission (famine, preventable disease, neglect) and about animals; and, hovering over all of it, the question the committee cannot settle by majority vote — whether there is any fact of the matter about which of these measures is the right one, or whether this is the sort of concept that changes according to what we ask of it. End with a short minority annex from the committee's own dissenting member, in a drier register, which should leave the reader feeling the force of the objection while the memo itself never concedes it, and never winks.
