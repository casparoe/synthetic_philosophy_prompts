I sit on the professional-conduct panel of my software engineering association, and I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's hearing. The case: two teams at the same company independently shipped the same faulty session-token check. In one, the flaw was exploited and roughly forty thousand users' records were exposed; in the other, the identical flaw never fired because a legacy configuration happened to block the path. The two lead engineers had comparable records and comparable care in review, and neither concealed anything afterward. A third engineer at the same firm knowingly skipped the required security review, but the feature was cancelled before release, so nothing happened. Half the panel wants our sanctions to track the damage done -- the view is that members answer for what their code actually does -- and half says we would then be punishing people for events outside their control, and that the engineer who genuinely chose badly would walk away with no mark on his record at all. I don't have the vocabulary for this and I care more about the memo being right than about it sounding philosophical. Please draft the opinion as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, my recommendation on all three engineers together with the standard I am applying; second, how that standard applies to the engineer whose bug caused the breach and to the one whose identical bug did not, including how we are to judge what a reasonable engineer could have known before shipping without letting the outcome smuggle in hindsight; third, what the panel owes the exposed users as distinct from what it owes the engineers, and why those two ledgers should be kept apart; fourth, the strongest case for the damage-tracking view and the honest reason I am not adopting it; and fifth, a concrete standing rule and a timeline the panel can adopt at this meeting so that future cases of this shape do not get relitigated from scratch.
