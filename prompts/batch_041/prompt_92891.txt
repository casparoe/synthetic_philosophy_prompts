I teach normative ethics, and I keep running into the same frustration in our departmental discussions of wild animal suffering: the exchange is too often short-circuited by the maxim that nature knows best. At a workshop last term on the ethics of wildlife intervention, a colleague invoked it to close off the suggestion that we might have any reason to reduce suffering in the wild, as though the fact that a state of affairs is natural, or that natural processes produced it, settled the question whether it ought to be altered. I want to be able to respond with precision rather than merely observing that the phrase sounds like an appeal to nature in disguise.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims the maxim could be making — the full range, from readings so thin that no one would dispute them, through substantive empirical or metaphysical theses, to readings that are simply false or incoherent. For each, say what the claim amounts to precisely, whether it is defensible on its own terms, and — the part I care about most — whether it would actually deliver the conclusion my colleague wanted: that concern about wild animal suffering is misplaced and intervention is unwarranted. Among the candidate readings I can already see: that nature is a purposive system with ends of its own; that ecosystems are self-regulating and tend toward a beneficial equilibrium, so that interference is self-defeating; that we lack the ecological knowledge to predict the consequences of intervention; and that what is natural is for that reason normative. I suspect there are others, and I suspect that several trade on an equivocation — for instance between being well-adapted and being well-off — that the slogan conceals. Finally, tell me which reading is most likely the one actually doing the work when the maxim is deployed in this context, and whether that reading survives scrutiny.
