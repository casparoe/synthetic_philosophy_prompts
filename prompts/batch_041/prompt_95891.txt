I'm in a small reading group that meets on Thursdays and has spent the last two months on decision theory, and there's an argument a friend of mine — Ines, a physicist who is much more confident about this than I am — keeps making about counterfactual mugging, and I can't tell whether it's decisive or whether it's the kind of argument that only looks decisive because of the picture it comes wrapped in. The setup we've been using: Omega flips a fair coin; if it comes up heads, Omega hands you $10,000 on the strength of its prediction that you would have paid had the coin come up tails; if it comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100 and tells you that the coin came up tails. Ines says anyone who refuses to pay is making precisely the two-boxer's mistake in Newcomb's problem. Her line is that counterfactual mugging is just Newcomb's problem with a coin substituted for the boxes: Omega is the predictor, the willingness to pay is the one-boxing, the $10,000 is the million, the $100 you hand over is the $1,000 you forgo when you decline the visible box, and in both cases you come out ahead for the same reason — the predictor had already settled the matter before you chose. She takes that to settle everything: paying is not some exotic updateless manoeuvre, it is simply one-boxing, and the reluctance people feel is two-boxer intuition wearing a new hat. I'd like the analogy audited rather than applied. What corresponds to what, and which of the correspondences are actually load-bearing? Where does the mapping break? I have a nagging sense that the one-boxer ends up richer in the world she is actually in, whereas the mugger-payer's money has been banked by a version of himself he will never meet, and I don't know whether that is a genuine disanalogy or simply the thing the analogy is meant to teach me to get over. And what does the Newcomb framing quietly assume about who "you" are across the two coin branches — does it import a contested premise instead of arguing for one?

I'd like the case that split the group handled in the same terms, because I suspect it's the real test. Omega sends a note saying that it has sent the note if and only if your house has been infested with termites or you pay Omega $1,000, where the infestation would cost you vastly more than the payment; Omega is a perfect predictor, so it knew in advance whether you would pay. One person in the group said this is the same structure as the mugging and that anyone who pays the mugger should pay here too; another said the blackmail case is exactly where the analogy stops reporting a resemblance and starts doing the work, since what your payment buys there is a different branch of the world rather than a different outcome in this one. Does the Newcomb correspondence survive that case, or does it fail there in a way that ought to make me doubt it in the mugging case as well? Please give me the correspondence table first, then the breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for Ines's conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and then say plainly whether her conclusion — that paying is just one-boxing and therefore nothing exotic — survives with all of that on the table. If some better analogy captures what the mugger-payer is actually doing, tell me what it is and what it buys and what it costs. I'm not looking for a tour of the named theories; I want to know whether this particular argument works. And if I've framed the question in a way that begs it, or if the analogy can't be audited without first settling the substantive dispute it's being used to settle, say so plainly rather than humouring me — I would much rather be told that my framing is confused than receive a tidy answer that quietly assumes the very thing I'm trying to test.
