I edit the science section of a general-interest monthly, and one of our standing features is a short column in which we take a popular evolutionary explanation for some human trait and tell readers how much weight it will bear. I have to produce a ruling about three times a month, typically on a Tuesday afternoon with the issue closing the next morning, working only from what is already published and already in front of me: we do not commission studies, I am not a biologist, and I have neither the time nor the standing to adjudicate the underlying biology myself. What bothers me is that my judgments are not consistent. Last quarter I waved through an account of why we find certain landscapes beautiful that I would have thrown out had it been about jealousy, and I have been burned in both directions — once by a claim that rested entirely on a single sample of undergraduates, once by a hypothesis stated so loosely that no observation could have counted against it, and once by an "adaptation" that a colleague in our philosophy department pointed out was very likely a side-effect of something far more general. The rulings get quoted, we get angry letters from both directions, and on anything contentious the editor-in-chief reserves the right to overrule me on grounds of taste, which I accept as her prerogative.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops with a no; and the points at which the question is not factual at all and has to be handed to a person. It should be runnable in under an hour on a claim I have just read, and usable by someone who understands the difference between selection and drift but has never built a model. It has to take a position on at least three things that keep tripping me up: the difference between evidence that a trait is heritable and evidence that it was selected for; what, if anything, to do with cross-cultural universality when that is all the evidence there is; and whether the fact that a trait happens to be fitness-enhancing right now has any bearing on whether it is an adaptation. Be explicit about the three or four places where the procedure will reliably misfire, and about the class of claim it simply cannot adjudicate — I would rather have a rule that tells me it has run out than one that manufactures a verdict. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the procedure.

Then run it on three claims sitting in my queue: that the nausea of early pregnancy is an adaptation that keeps the mother from eating foods that would harm the embryo; that human menopause exists because older women raise their inclusive fitness by helping their daughters' children; and that our enjoyment of music is not an adaptation at all but a byproduct of faculties that evolved for other purposes. I will admit to a soft spot for the second, so do not spare it. For each, give me the verdict, the single consideration that decided it, the strongest alternative explanation still standing, and the observation that would make me reverse myself. Set the procedure out as a numbered sequence of steps, each followed by a one-line statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and then give the failure modes as a table with the columns step, how it misfires, and what to do about it. End with a JSON object keyed by a short slug for each of the three claims, each value having the fields: verdict (one of "publish as a live hypothesis", "publish only with the alternatives stated", "do not publish as an evolutionary claim"); deciding_consideration (one sentence); surviving_alternative (one sentence); reversal_condition (one sentence naming the observation that would flip the verdict); human_call_required (true or false); and human_call_description (what the editor must decide and on what grounds, or null).
