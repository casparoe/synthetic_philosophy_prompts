I've been reading a fair amount of ancient history on my own time — the Greek material mostly, Herodotus and Thucydides and whatever else I can follow without a teacher — and a friend of mine who did a classics degree keeps explaining the whole enterprise to me through an analogy I can't quite shake off. His line is that reading a source is like taking testimony in a trial: the event is the crime, the text is the witness's deposition, the historian is the jury, and everything we call source criticism is just the rules of evidence. He leans on it hard, and it does real work for him. On his telling, Thucydides is the eyewitness deposed early, whose account we can mostly trust because he says he stuck to what he saw and to witnesses he could check; Herodotus is closer to a rumor passed around an office, a man who reports what he was told and openly declines to vouch for all of it; so where the two conflict we side with Thucydides, and where neither was present we suspend judgment. The conclusion he draws is that the further back you go, the more you are living on hearsay, and that anything like real history is confined to roughly the span a competent witness could cover.

I'd like you to audit that analogy rather than just agree or disagree with it. Lay the correspondence out explicitly — what plays the role of the witness, the deposition, the cross-examination, the tribunal, the rules of evidence, the chain of custody — and then say where the mapping breaks. A few things I've noticed on my own, which may or may not matter: you cannot cross-examine a dead author; much of what we have from some writers survives only because a later writer quoted or summarized them, so the deposition reaches us through someone else's summary; the copies we actually read were made by scribes long afterwards, often centuries later; and in many cases the source was not present at the event at all but is himself reporting a report. I suspect the analogy quietly imports a whole apparatus the historical case lacks — a witness who means to inform a fact-finder, a court with power to compel and confront, a single determinate event to be testified about, and a clean separation between what the witness observed and what the record now says. Please separate the disanalogies that bear on his conclusion from the ones that are mere decoration, and then tell me whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table, or whether the analogy pressed honestly proves too much and would leave us skeptical about the recent past as well.

I work on distributed systems, so my instinct is to reframe the transmission problem in terms I actually understand: manuscript copying as replication with no checksums and no version history, the historian's appeal to independent witnesses as something like parity, and the fragment that survives only inside a later quotation as a record whose provenance is partly lost. Tell me whether that framing does better than the courtroom one, and what it would cost — in particular whether it can still support any verdict at all, or only degrees of confidence. I've also heard the historian described as a detective at a scene where nothing remains but traces, and oral transmission described as a game of telephone; if you think one of these is the analogy to keep, say so, and say what it lets us do that the trial analogy cannot.
