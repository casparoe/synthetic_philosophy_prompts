I am a high school student working on an independent project in the philosophy of language, and I would like a single, carefully worked example rather than a general survey. Please design a thought experiment that drives a wedge between epistemicism about vagueness and supervaluationism, and that shows the wedge rather than merely announcing it. The setting should be ordinary and checkable: one vague predicate (a heap, bald, tall, red — your choice) applied to a finite series of cases that an ordinary person could go through one at a time, beginning with a case everyone agrees falls under the predicate and ending with one everyone agrees does not, where each neighbouring pair is indistinguishable in the respect that matters. I want the two theories to agree on every observable fact and on every statement that is plainly true or plainly false, and to come apart only on a small, specified set of questions: (a) the semantic status of a single borderline instance; (b) whether the corresponding instance of the law of excluded middle is true, and whether its being true requires one of its two halves to be true; and (c) whether the word definitely, applied to a borderline instance, adds anything to the claim.

The verdicts I am aiming at are these. On the single borderline instance, one theory should say that it is true or false but unknowably so, while the other should say that it is neither. On the excluded-middle instance, the two should agree that it is true while disagreeing about whether its truth requires a true half. On definitely, the two should disagree about whether it contributes anything at the borderline. I want the case to generate these verdicts out of the theories themselves, not to stipulate them.

Constraints: no ideal observers, no infinitely precise instruments, and no stipulations about what the speaker can or cannot know; the case must not simply assume that the series does or does not contain a sharp cutoff, since that is the point at issue; the scenario itself should fit within a single paragraph; and do not lean on any particular published version of either theory, so that the case can be assessed on its own merits.

After the scenario, please do the following. Give each theory's verdict on (a), (b), and (c), tracing each verdict back to what that theory says a borderline case is. Identify the one feature of the design that is doing the work, and say what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed — for instance, if the series is shortened to two neighbouring cases, or if the predicate is replaced by a precise one. Then state how a defender of whichever theory comes out worse would try to disarm the case, and whether that reply costs them anything elsewhere.

Because I will be presenting this to people who know the literature better than I do, please keep two kinds of claim apart: what is settled among people who work on vagueness, and what is genuinely contested. Do not present a contested verdict or characterization as the consensus, and flag any point at which a careful adherent of one of the theories would say you have misdescribed their view.
