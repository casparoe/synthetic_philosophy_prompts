I'm writing up a proposal for a small grant — 6 months, basically just me plus maybe a part-time RA — and I want to get the framing tight before I commit to it. Here's the half-formed thing I've got.

Everyone argues about whether 'cancel culture' counts as punishment, and the argument usually goes nowhere because people are really fighting about the word. What I think is actually interesting is a structural feature that nobody seems to isolate: in a paradigm case of punishment there's an identifiable punisher standing behind the act — a court, a dean, a bishop, a boss — and because there's someone to point at, all the usual constraints can attach to them. A rule that was broken, notice, a chance to answer, some proportionality, and a defined end point at which you've paid and can come back. In a cancellation, the sanction is produced by a dispersed crowd. No single contribution is the sanction, most contributions are individually trivial, and the person at the center can't answer anybody because there's no single addressee. So you get something with the *shape* of punishment — condemnation, a status loss, a period of exclusion — but with nobody positioned to carry the obligations that punishment normally brings.

The question I want the project to answer is this: is the dispersal of the sanctioning agent itself what explains what's distinctively objectionable about these episodes, including for people who think the underlying criticism was fair? And can the missing obligations be reconstructed without just handing the power back to a centralized authority?

Why I think the existing positions don't settle it: the two standard camps are (a) it isn't punishment, because punishment requires an authority, a violated rule, and an intention to censure, so this is just the aggregate of private choices, and (b) it is punishment in substance, because the expressive function is identical. Both camps treat the punisher as a given and then ask whether the crowd qualifies. Neither asks whether the *dispersion* is doing independent work. Same issue with the informal-sanction literature and the marketplace-of-private-choices line — both assume each act can be assessed on its own, which is exactly what's in doubt when every individual act is permissible and the aggregate is the harm. That's an aggregation problem philosophy of punishment mostly hasn't touched (it gets discussed re: climate, voting, markets).

Method, roughly: (1) fix a structural account of punishment's constitutive elements so I have a checklist instead of intuitions; (2) run it against cases — a formal sentence, a firing, a consumer boycott, a pile-on, an excommunication, a no-platforming; (3) isolate which elements the dispersed cases lack and ask whether the lack is essential or contingent; (4) examine candidate reconstructions — anonymity norms, a right of reply, sunset clauses on social sanction, limits on employers acting on crowd pressure — and ask whether any of them preserve what made the sanction legitimate in the first place. I also want to read up on outlawry and civil death as a comparison class, since that's a status harm imposed without anything trial-like, and see if the analogy does real work or just sounds cool.

Deliverable would be a paper of maybe 8-10k words plus a short general-audience version. I want the proposal to be honest about failure conditions: if it turns out every element can be recovered by just redescribing the crowd as a single agent — i.e. the dispersal is a redescription rather than a real difference — the thesis collapses, and I should say so upfront. Same if the upshot turns out to be trivial ('be nicer online').
