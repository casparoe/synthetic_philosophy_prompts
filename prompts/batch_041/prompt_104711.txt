Design a single 90-minute classroom activity for a philosophy of science seminar of about sixteen students, none of whom have any prior reading on the topic. The goal is for the students to run into the underdetermination of theory by evidence from the inside, before it is ever named to them: to commit, under pressure, to a theory on the strength of a fixed body of data, and then to discover that the data did not force their choice.

Build the whole activity around one mechanism. The students are given a finite, concrete body of observational records — a short table of readings, positions, or counts that you specify exactly — and are asked to state the law or model that generates them and to stake a prediction on the next record. What they cannot see is that the same records are accounted for equally well by a second model, which you construct to agree with the first on every given record but to diverge elsewhere. Neither the data nor any rule of inference you hand the students distinguishes the two; only something they bring from outside — a preference for the simpler law, a demand for a mechanism, a convention about which quantities count as legitimate parameters — can do the work. Arrange the activity so that this becomes visible to them.

I want two pairs of rival models, not one. The first pair should be empirically equivalent only so far: some future observation could in principle separate them, even though none of the observations the students are given does. The second pair should be equivalent in a stronger sense — no observation whatsoever, present or future, could tell them apart — or, if you judge that no honest example of the stronger kind can be built into an activity this size, say so plainly and explain what the obstruction is. The contrast between the two pairs is the heart of the session, so keep them clearly separate in the students' experience and be explicit in the debrief about which pair is which and why.

Specify the materials and how they are distributed; the roles, if you use roles, or the rules, if you use rules; a timeline with minute-by-minute checkpoints; a scheme of stakes or payoffs that creates genuine pressure to commit without using money; the exact moment of the reveal; and how you will handle a student who defends a third model you did not anticipate. Then write the debrief: for each moment in the activity, name the feature of the problem it makes vivid — the gap between evidence and theory, the role of theoretical virtues as tie-breakers rather than deductions, the possibility of saving a threatened theory by revising a background assumption instead of the theory itself, and the difference between a tie that further evidence could break and one it could not. Be frank about the one feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that you, the facilitator, will have to supply in words; say what it is and why staging it would be impossible or dishonest at this scale. Finally, tell me the three ways this activity will most predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and what to do about each.
