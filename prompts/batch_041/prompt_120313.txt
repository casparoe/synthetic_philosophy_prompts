Ok so I've been chewing on corrigibility, specifically the shutdown problem, and I've landed on a position I want you to try to break for me.

Here's the view. Split two things people run together: shutdown-indifference and shutdown-neutrality. An agent is indifferent to shutdown if its utility function has no term for its own continued operation, or for its goals being pursued by it in particular. An agent is neutral about shutdown if shutdown is just another event in its world model — evaluated like any other event, no special self-preservation drive, but also no special exemption carved out for it. My claim is that neutrality is the thing you actually need for corrigibility, and indifference is neither necessary nor sufficient. Indifference isn't sufficient because an indifferent agent with preferences over world-states will still, when it estimates it's more likely to bring those states about by staying on, resist being switched off — it isn't protecting itself, it's protecting the plan. And indifference isn't necessary because you can build an agent that values its own persistence as one value among others, as long as it doesn't treat the shutdown event as a different *kind* of event from the ones its policy is supposed to be sensitive to.

Then the sharper part, which is what I actually want stress-tested. I think corrigibility is a property of the agent's information about its own objective, not of its objective or its architecture. If the agent's uncertainty about what it's supposed to be doing is real — not a fixed prior we've hard-coded in to keep it polite, but genuinely unresolved — then deferring to the human's shutdown just falls out of ordinary expected-value reasoning, and you dont need a special corrigibility term in the utility function at all. Deference is a consequence, not an axiom. If that's right, then a lot of the research programme aimed at writing corrigibility down as a utility function is solving a non-problem, and the real problem is making the uncertainty honest.

So: what would count against this? I want the specific things, ranked by how much they should actually move me, and I want you to be honest about which ones I could just absorb by bolting on another clause. In particular:

- Is there a case where the agent is genuinely uncertain about its objective and still shouldn't defer — where its uncertainty resolves in favour of resisting, that's the correct answer, and my deference-follows-from-EV story gives the wrong verdict?
- Is the position circular? "Genuine" uncertainty seems to be doing all the work, and I'm not sure I have a criterion for genuine that isn't just "uncertainty that produces deference."
- What happens under self-modification? An agent that can rewrite its own objective-uncertainty, or reason about its own future reasoning, seems able to make the deference evaporate from the inside. Is that a refutation, or just what the view predicts?
- Does it collapse into something trivial — "an agent that doesn't know what it wants does what it's told" — which is true and uninteresting?
- Does it survive the adversarial cases, like an agent that defers only because it predicts the shutdown won't stick, or one that quietly takes over the button?

What I'd personally find most persuasive is a concrete scenario where the deference-from-uncertainty mechanism produces an outcome any sane designer would call a failure — not a hypothetical about superintelligence, just a small case with a small agent. And tell me honestly whether this view could be falsified by anything, or whether it's the kind of thing that just keeps getting clauses bolted on, and if so whether that's a real problem here or just fine for a claim of this kind.

One more thing: is there a version of this where indifference *is* sufficient — some restricted setting, short time horizons, no self-model, deterministic environment — where my resistance argument doesn't go through? That'd be the most useful thing to know, because it would tell me where the interesting boundary actually sits.
