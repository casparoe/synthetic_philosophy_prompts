Right, my mate and I had a proper row about this down the pub last night and I want it settled properly, not with more shouting. Here's where I stand, case by case.

I promised my mate I'd feed his cat while he was away, then forgot, and the cat died. Did I cause the cat's death? Yes. Obviously.

A bloke walks past a canal, sees a toddler face-down in the water, and keeps walking. The kid drowns. Did he cause the death? No. He's a disgrace, but he didn't kill anyone.

The nurse on shift doesn't connect the drip. Patient dies. Did she cause it? Yes.

A storm brings the power line down, the leccy's off for a week, the burglar alarm is dead, and the house gets done over. Did the storm cause the burglary? I want to say yes — the reason the place got robbed is that there was no alarm, and the reason there was no alarm is the storm. But I don't want to say a storm did anything to a house.

Now the one that broke the argument. My mate's cousin cut the phone line to the cottage so that nobody could ring the fire brigade, and then the place burnt down. Did he cause the house to burn? I say yes — he didn't start the fire, he just made sure nobody could stop it. My mate says if that counts, then the bloke at the canal counts too, because he also failed to stop a death.

Then the bridge. The inquest said it came down because there was nothing in the design to let the steel expand in the heat. Not a person, not an act — a gap. I still want to say the gap caused the collapse.

And one more, because it keeps nagging me: there were two of us on the rota to lock up the shop, we each assumed the other had done it, neither of us did, and the place got robbed overnight. I say we both caused that, even though you can't point at either of us and say it wouldn't have happened if only he'd done it.

My mate's rule is the but-for one: you caused it if it wouldn't have happened had you acted. But that makes the man at the canal a killer, and the storm the cause of the burglary, and honestly it makes all of us the cause of everything we never got round to preventing.

So — is there a principle that gets my verdicts right, or nearly all of them, without swallowing the whole world? I want it stated properly, not vibes. Tell me what each clause is doing and which case forces it. If a clean principle costs me one of my verdicts, say which one I should give up rather than bolting on another clause to save it; and if no single principle does the job, give me the two best and what each of them costs. I'm not intrested in a list of everything anyone's ever said about this — I want the thing itself, and I want it to tell me what to say about a case I haven't listed: a surgeon who decides not to operate and the patient dies, where operating would've given them maybe a fifty-fifty chance.
