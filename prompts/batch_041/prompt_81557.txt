I retired two years ago from a career in soil science — forty years of it, mostly at agricultural research stations — and since then I've been working my way through philosophy, which is a strange thing to do at 68 and my wife is convinced I've lost it. A friend from my old institute, who now does tech-policy consulting, uses it to argue we shouldn't panic — farming went from employing most of the country to a rounding error and nobody starved, the workers found other things to do, the economy grew, so it'll be the same with lawyers and radiologists and programmers. I know the farm side of that story alot better than he does, and I think he's using it too fast, but I can't articulate exactly where it slips. So give me the audit. What maps onto what — I want the correspondence table, the tractor, the field, the farmer, the farmhand, the horses, the surplus crop, the city — and then where the mapping actually breaks. Which breaks matter for the specific conclusion he's drawing (don't worry, labor will reallocate) and which are just noise? Does his conclusion survive the breaks, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time?

The part that nags at me is that in my own field the tractor didn't merely do the same job faster. It changed what a farm was, who owned it, who got to stay on the land, and it turned a whole category of skilled work into unskilled work and then into no work at all — and I'd want the mapping to say something about that, or admit it can't. My nephew in finance says the better analogy is horses: they were the motive power of everything and then they were pets, and there was no retraining program for them. Tell me whether that's a fair amendment or a cheap gotcha. And here's the bit I actually care about as a beginner philosopher: the tractor analogy treats labor as a quantity of work done — horsepower, man-hours, a flow of energy into a field — and I've been reading enough by now to suspect thats the whole trick. If what I care about is whether work is where a person finds meaning and standing and a place in the world, does the analogy have anything to say at all, or is it a category mistake from the first sentence? So finish by telling me which features of the farm story are being quietly imported into the AI story that the AI story doesn't have, and what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
