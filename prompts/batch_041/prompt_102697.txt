Question I set: "Suppose a detective has strong first-order evidence that the butler did it, but then learns that she reasoned about the case while severely sleep-deprived, and that her track record on sleep-deprived cases is barely better than chance. She keeps believing the butler did it, and she also believes that this belief is irrational. Is she epistemically akratic? Is that ever rationally permissible? Distinguish first-order from higher-order evidence, and say what a conciliationist and a steadfast theorist would say about her case."

Student answer: "Higher-order evidence is evidence about the evidential force of your first-order evidence — like evidence that you're drunk or that your reasoning is unreliable. First-order evidence is the stuff that bears directly on whether the butler did it: fingerprints, alibis, and so on. In this case the detective's first-order evidence supports the butler's guilt, while the sleep-deprivation info is higher-order evidence against the reliability of her reasoning. Epistemic akrasia is believing p while also believing that believing p is irrational. The detective is akratic: she believes the butler did it and believes that belief is irrational. A conciliationist says she should lower her confidence in the butler's guilt, because the higher-order evidence shows her first-order reasoning is unreliable. A steadfast theorist says she can keep the belief, because the first-order evidence is what it is, and higher-order evidence about her own reliability doesn't change the probability of the butler's guilt. On whether akrasia is ever rational: conciliationists say no, because if you believe your belief is irrational then the belief is unjustified, so you shouldn't hold it. Steadfast theorists, like the level-splitting view, say yes: you can rationally believe p and also rationally believe that your belief in p is irrational, since the two beliefs are at different levels. The key mistake is thinking higher-order evidence always defeats first-order evidence; it only does so if the higher-order evidence is stronger. So the detective is akratic, but not necessarily irrational."
