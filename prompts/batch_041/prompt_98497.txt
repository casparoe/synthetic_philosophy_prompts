I have no philosophical training and have arrived at this question on my own, so I want the result to be a working document rather than a survey of what other people have written. Two people I know have been arguing for about two years about what to do when someone you have no particular reason to think is your inferior disagrees with you. One of them holds that, other things equal, learning that a peer disagrees with you should move you toward their view -- that the bare fact of the disagreement is itself a reason to doubt you were right. The other holds that you may reasonably keep your view, because you can only assess their judgment through your own and treating their conclusion as equally good evidence against you looks like a kind of self-undermining capitulation. They are no longer making progress against each other, and I would like you to write the joint statement the two of them could both put their names to if they collaborated honestly for a weekend.

I want the following, and I want the drafting to do the real work:

First, the claims both sides can sign. These should be substantive rather than truisms -- claims about what disagreement is, about the difference between first-order reasons to think a proposition true and reasons that concern how good you are at judging such propositions, about what a peer is, about whether the two of them share any standard for when a belief is reasonable, and about what would count as resolving their dispute. Number these.

Second, the exact points where they still part. State each one in wording that the side holding it would actually accept as its own position, and equally state the other side's position in wording its holder would accept. If any of their apparent disagreement turns out to be about words -- two different things being meant by reason, or by peer, or by should -- say which, and say how you would phrase it to make the verbal part disappear.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each crux, give a case or an argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about how that case comes out. I am most interested in any crux where the two sides make genuinely different predictions that could in principle be checked.

To keep this from floating free, please use two concrete cases throughout. The first is the one they keep returning to: two people disagree about a question with a determinate answer neither can look up, each regards the other as roughly as careful and as well informed as themselves, and each also has a private sense that the other is going wrong in a way that is, by its nature, invisible from the inside -- a blind spot neither can detect in himself. The second is the opposite shape: one of the two has a clear track record of being wrong more often than the other on this kind of question, and they both know it. I want to see whether the collaboration's agreed claims and cruxes survive both cases or come apart between them.

A few constraints. Do not tell me who wins; the point is a document both would sign, not a verdict. Do not pad it with names, works, or reading suggestions -- I cannot check any of those and they are not what I need. Write in plain language a non-philosopher can follow, keep the agreed claims to things that actually carry weight, and where you find that a crux cannot be settled by any case the two of them would both accept, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a test.
