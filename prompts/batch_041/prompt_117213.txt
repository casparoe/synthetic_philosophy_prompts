I have been reading about the early Cold War on my own — the wartime conferences, the unravelling of the Grand Alliance, the quarrels over Poland and Germany, the Marshall Plan, the Berlin blockade — and in almost every conversation I have about it, somebody eventually says, as though it settled matters, that the Cold War was inevitable. What strikes me is how many different jobs those three words are made to do. One friend means only that two victorious powers with incompatible visions of the post-war order were bound to end up in some sort of stand-off, which seems close to a truism. Another means something much stronger: that no particular decision by any particular statesman — not Stalin's handling of Eastern Europe, not the American choice to keep the bomb to itself, not the division of the Balkans into spheres — made any difference to the outcome. A third means that nobody was really to blame, because the causes were impersonal: geography, the power vacuum in central Europe, the security dilemma, ideology. And a fourth seems to mean nothing more than that, looking back, we cannot now picture it having gone otherwise, which I suspect is hindsight dressed up as history.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on who was to blame, and certainly not a reading list. I want the slogan itself taken apart. Set out as a menu the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making: which agents' choices are supposed to have been closed off, which alternatives are supposed to have been unavailable, which counterfactuals have to be true for the claim to hold, and at what level of description — a particular decision, a policy, a whole social order — the inevitability is meant to operate. Take the readings from those that are almost trivially true, through those that are substantive and defensible, to those that are false or merely confused, and for each say whether it is a claim about the world at all or only a claim about our inability to imagine the alternatives. Then tell me which of those readings would actually support what my friends plainly want the slogan to support, namely that asking who started the Cold War is the wrong question — and which reading they are most likely reaching for when they say it in the middle of an argument. I would like you to be careful here, because the wrongness of a question can mean several things: that it cannot be answered, that it can be answered but does not matter, or that it presupposes a false picture of how historical outcomes come to have authors. Which inevitability reading delivers which of those conclusions, and does any of them deliver the conclusion without proving so much that it would dissolve blame for almost anything?
