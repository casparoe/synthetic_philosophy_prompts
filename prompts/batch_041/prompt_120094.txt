I'm an undergraduate in an applied ethics course, and our current unit is on the ethics of eating animals. The instructor has us argue over a set of hypothetical supply chains rather than real ones, so that we can't deflect with disputed statistics — the welfare and mortality figures below are stipulated for the exercise, not claimed as fact. I've been recording my own verdicts on them as we go, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I don't especially want to be told what to think; I want to know whether a single criterion sits underneath these reactions, what it says about the cases I haven't classified, and which of my reactions I'd have to give up for the set to be coherent.

Assume the systems below are otherwise equal in environmental cost and in nutrition per serving. Here are my verdicts.

1. A steak from a cow that grazed on pasture for six years and was killed with a captive-bolt gun: wrong, but mildly so — the sort of thing I might do unthinkingly at a barbecue.
2. A chicken from a conventional barn, killed at six weeks after a life of crowding and debeaking: wrong, and clearly worse than the steak.
3. Wheat, soy, and rice, which under the instructor's stipulation kill a number of field rodents and invertebrates per hectare at harvest: fine.
4. Farmed oysters: fine.
5. Wild-caught tuna: fine, though I feel a flicker about it.
6. A protein bar made from cricket flour: fine, possibly better than fine.
7. Lab-grown beef: fine.
8. Eggs from well-kept backyard hens: fine.
9. Venison from a deer killed by a car: fine.
10. A whole pig raised outdoors on a small farm: wrong, and I place it between the chicken and the steak.

Now the pair I can't reconcile. Set the steak and the chicken side by side and my gut says the steak is the graver wrong: the animal is larger, it lived longer, more is taken from it. But set them side by side as suffering caused per serving and the chicken looks worse, because a chicken's life is short and wretched and one bird feeds far fewer people than one cow. I hold both reactions, they cannot both be right, and I don't know which to drop.

A related worry: several people in the seminar have argued that if I count the field animals killed in crop cultivation, then wheat and soy are not obviously better than the steak and may be worse per calorie. My instinct is to say crops are fine, but I can't tell whether that instinct is a considered judgment or just that the field animals are invisible to me.

The tentative principle I have been running, and which I am fairly sure is too crude, is this: a food is permissible in proportion as it causes less intense, deliberately inflicted suffering per unit of protein, where suffering is weighted by how likely the animal is to be sentient — and, I have been assuming, only animals we farm count. I suspect that last clause is a fudge.

Please do the following. State the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures the verdicts, marking for each clause which case forces it. Tell me what each candidate principle predicts for three cases I have not classified: a beef system in which the cattle graze marginal land that could not be cropped and whose grazing keeps a grassland from being plowed; a chicken engineered so that it cannot suffer, raised and killed as usual; and a wild-caught octopus, stipulated to be as sentient as a pig. Finally, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts cannot survive, and what the least revision would be.
