I have spent an unreasonable amount of time in the comment sections of decision theory and quantum foundations blogs, and the one argument I have never seen written out properly is the fight between two people who agree completely about the ontology of branching and disagree completely about what that ontology licenses. I want that fight as a dialogue, worked through cases rather than slogans.

The characters. Both accept the Everettian picture in full: the wavefunction never collapses, every outcome of a measurement is realized, and no branch is less real than any other. Neither is a Copenhagenist in disguise, neither thinks the Born rule — the rule that credence tracks squared amplitude — is false as a rule of thumb for prediction, and neither is a strawman for the other to knock over.

The first, call her the Measure, holds that the Born rule is a theorem about rationality. On her view, an agent who knows the wavefunction is rationally required to set her credences to the squared amplitudes, and this follows from ordinary decision-theoretic constraints — a preference ordering, dominance reasoning, consistency across time and across branches — with probability never assumed anywhere in the premises. She thinks self-locating uncertainty is a genuine kind of uncertainty and the correct description of the branching agent's situation.

The second, call him the Ledger, accepts every word of the physics and none of the norm. Squared amplitudes are real features of the wavefunction, but nothing about them can rationally bind anyone's credences. If every outcome is realized, then what will happen to me has no determinate answer, and the honest description of an agent's situation is a count of copies of her distributed across the branches. What she does with those copies is a question about what she cares about, not about what she ought to believe. He thinks the Measure's derivation recovers the Born rule only by assuming something equivalent to it, and that the circularity is not a technical gap but the whole story.

What they have to argue about — at least these four things, in this order, with each reply answering the previous point rather than restarting:

1. Duplication. An experimenter is put to sleep; a measurement is performed; on one outcome she wakes once, on the other she wakes twice, with her memory of the earlier waking erased in between, each waking subjectively indistinguishable from the other. On waking she is asked for her credence that she is in the single-waking branch. Both characters must derive a number rather than assert one, and then each attacks the other's derivation at its weakest joint. Then change the setup so that the two outcomes are not equally weighted — one branch twice as substantial as the other — and say what, if anything, changes for each of them.

2. The counting problem. The Ledger's position needs a definite number of branches to count. The Measure presses: that number depends on how the state is carved up, since any branch can be rewritten as any number of finer, mutually decohered sub-branches, so the count is not a fact about the world until a factorization has been chosen, and a norm of rationality cannot rest on a bookkeeping convention. The Ledger gets the strongest reply available to him — decoherence, stable records, a privileged factorization, something else — states it precisely, and the Measure then attacks that specific reply.

3. Recoherence, a case I have not seen worked out and want them to do the work on. Suppose the experimenter knows the measurement is not irreversible: after a delay the apparatus is subjected to a process that undoes the decoherence, so the branches interfere and recombine into one. While the split lasts she is offered a bet on the outcome. What credence does each of them tell her to use, what happens to her betting behavior across the recombination, and does the Ledger's view survive the fact that the number of copies of her goes from two to one without anyone dying? If he is forced to weight the copies by something other than raw counts in order to give sensible answers here, he should say so, and the Measure should say what that concession costs him.

4. Diagnosis. Have them locate the disagreement precisely: is it about the metaphysics of branching, about the theory of rationality, or about what an agent may rationally care about? If the Ledger's position collapses into the claim that a rational Everettian is permitted not to care about her other branches, the Measure should say whether that is a substantive thesis or an evasion, and the Ledger should answer.

Ground rules: no citations, no attributions, no as-so-and-so-has-argued — I want the arguments themselves, in plain language, with every technical term introduced before it is used. At least one character must concede something specific or narrow a claim under pressure. Do not end with a forced synthesis; if the disagreement survives, let it survive, but make clear what would have to be true for each side to be wrong.
