I'm a science journalist and I've been working on a long feature about analogy — how much of thinking is really just seeing one thing as another — and somewhere in the reporting I stoped being a reporter and started believing the thesis. Before I file it I want somebody to tell me whether the version of it I've actually committed to in my head is a position or just a pile of things that each sound good on their own.

Here's where I've landed.

1. Analogy isn't an occasional flourish, like calling the atom a solar system. It's the core of thinking, full stop — it's the thing that's happening constantly underneath everything else, and logic and deduction and planning are built on top of it rather than sitting next to it. Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences got me here and I have not found my way back out.

2. And I'd take it a step further, or maybe this is just what they mean: categorizing something just is seeing it as like something else. Which means concepts don't have definitions or essences. A concept is a fluid halo — a bunch of remembered instances weighted by whatever's salient right now, plus a lot of half-conscious pressure about what matters. So whether something falls under a concept is always a judgment call, never a lookup, and it shifts with context. Same word, different situation, different category.

3. There's no literal level underneath any of this. Calling a use of a word 'literal' just means the analogy is so worn down that nobody notices it's an analogy anymore. So there's a continuum running from trivial analogy to deep analogy with no principled place to draw a line and say past here it stops being analogy and starts being plain meaning.

4. But here's what I also believe. Some concepts really are exceptionless and rule-governed. Prime numbers. The rules of chess. What a specific clause in a contract obligates you to do. Nobody gets to decide by vibe whether 91 is prime; there's no context in which 91 is prime, no culture, no era of history. Same for whether a bishop can jump over a pawn.

5. When I want to know whether something is actually thinking, my test is this: does it make an analogy that's both surprising and apt? You couldn't have predicted it, and once you see it you can't unsee it. That's what I point at when I say a person is smart, or that a scientist is a genius.

6. Meanwhile the LLMs, which churn out surprising-and-apt analogies all day long, are not thinking. I dont think there's anyone home in there. I know that makes me sound like a snob and I don't care.

So: does this hang together? I have a feeling at least two of these can't all be true at once, and I also suspect at least one of them is really another one wearing a different hat, and I'd rather not burn five thousand words solving a problem that dissolves the second you say it out loud. So do this for me. Rank the real conflicts by how bad they are. For each one, tell me the cheapest commitment to give up and what it costs me — and don't tell me to "hold a more nuanced view," tell me concretely what the repaired position would say about a case. Then tell me the single change that cleans up the most at once. And if it turns out I'm fine as is, say that too, because I have a deadline.
