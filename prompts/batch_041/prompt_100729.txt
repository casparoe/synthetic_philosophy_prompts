I'm an undergraduate in a course on twentieth-century existentialism, and next week I'm responsible for leading the seventy-five-minute discussion section on Sartre's account of freedom and bad faith. The assigned reading is the relevant stretch of Being and Nothingness, but I've been told not to lecture and not to spend the hour walking through the text's architecture; the students will have read it, badly, and my job is to make it land. What I want from you is not an explanation of the material but a teaching plan: the order in which the ideas have to be introduced so that each one is doing work the next one depends on, the single case I should build the whole session around, the misunderstandings that will form in the room and exactly where in that order I should head each one off, and the question I should put to them at the end to find out whether the idea has actually landed rather than merely been repeated back to me.

A few constraints on the case. I don't want to use the café waiter. I understand why it's the canonical illustration, but everyone in the room has already met it and it has hardened into a set piece they can recite; I want a case that does the same structural work but that they have to think through along with me. It has to be a case where the person's situation genuinely constrains them — real obstacles, real history, no costless exit — so that the session doesn't collapse into the lazy reading on which being free just means being able to do otherwise. And it has to be a case where what the person is doing is best described as a way of relating to their own situation rather than a belief they hold, so that we can see why Sartre treats bad faith as a matter of the shape of a life rather than a false proposition someone assents to.

Two things I specifically need the plan to solve. First, there's a circularity I can't get past on my own: bad faith seems to require someone who is at once the one deceived and the one deceiving, which sounds like it needs a divided self, and Sartre's own picture seems to rule that out. I want to know whether this is a real problem, a misreading, or something he simply accepts and turns into a feature, and where in the sequence it should surface so that it sharpens the session instead of derailing it. Second, I want to handle the objection that will come up in the first ten minutes from the psychology students in the room — that talk of choosing one's attitude is naïve in light of everything we now know about how behaviour is caused — and I don't want to deflect it with a shrug about different senses of freedom; I want it to arrive at a point in the sequence where the class already has the vocabulary to see exactly what is and isn't being claimed.

The misconceptions I already expect, in no particular order: that bad faith is just lying to yourself, or self-deception in the ordinary sense; that radical freedom means you can do anything you want regardless of circumstance, so that Sartre has nothing to say about oppression or constraint; that if you are responsible for everything about yourself then you are to blame for your own suffering, which is what makes the view cruel and obviously false; and that the whole thing is refuted the moment someone mentions determinism. Tell me which of these are actually the same mistake wearing different clothes, and which order they will really arise in — the order I should be ready for, not the order that would be tidy.

Finally, tell me what to leave out and say that I'm leaving it out, and why that is the right call at this level rather than a cop-out.
