I'm a second-year PhD student working on epistemology and I've wrote a draft of the central section of a short paper on the surprise exam. Could you give me comments on it? I'm not after line edits — I want to know whether the diagnosis actually holds up. Draft below.

The setup I'm using: on Friday afternoon the teacher tells the class there'll be a surprise exam next week, on one of the five days, and that on the morning of the exam they won't know that it's that day. The students reason that it can't be Friday, because if Thursday evening comes and there's been no exam, they'd know it's Friday. Then it can't be Thursday, since Friday is ruled out and the same argument applies, and so on down the week — so there'll be no exam at all. Wednesday comes, they're surprised, and the announcement was true.

My twist is to add an envelope. Same announcement, plus: the teacher says the day is written on a card sealed in an envelope on the desk, and the students are free to open it whenever they like. Nothing else changes. The backward induction is untouched — they can still work out that it's Wednesday by pure reasoning, and if they do, the exam won't be a surprise. But if they simply don't bother doing the reasoning, the exam is a surprise, even though the day was knowable in principle from the start.

The moral I want to draw is that the word 'surprise' in the announcement is doing two jobs. Read as 'you cannot know the day', the envelope case makes the announcement into something the students can falsify just by doing the induction, which is odd, since it makes a claim about next week hostage to how they spend the weekend. Read as 'you will not in fact know the day', the announcement's truth depends on how the students conduct themselves, and their own clever argument turns out to be the thing that makes it false. I'm calling this epistemic brinkmanship: the announcement isn't a prediction about the week so much as a constraint that the students can breach simply by reasoning about it.

The bigger claim is that the usual framing gets the order of explanation wrong. People treat the surprise exam as a puzzle about backward induction and common knowledge. I want to say what's really doing the work is that the announcement tries to be both a fact about the students' epistemic state and something they can act on. Facts about what you know aren't action-guiding in that way. So it isn't a paradox of knowledge or of induction — it's a paradox of a self-referential instruction. I know this is adjacent to things people say about self-fulfilling predictions, but I think the envelope does something different: here the truth value doesn't flip with how the world goes, it flips with what the students choose to think about.

What I'd like from you: whether the envelope case genuinely adds anything beyond the standard paradox, whether the diagnosis is right or just a relabelling of the familiar knowledge/action point, whether 'epistemic brinkmanship' earns its keep as a term, and where I'm most likely to be attacked. Please give it as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion.
