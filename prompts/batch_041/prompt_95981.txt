I teach a comparative philosophy seminar that is cross-listed with religious studies, and next week I have a single ninety-minute session in which to introduce anekāntavāda, the Jain doctrine of the many-sidedness of reality, to twelve students who are mostly analytically trained and who arrive either with no exposure to Jain thought at all or with the settled impression that the doctrine amounts to relativism. Plan the session for me rather than delivering it. Begin with the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the ones before it: where the ontological claim — that a thing has indefinitely many aspects — must sit relative to the epistemological doctrine of standpoints (naya) and to the semantic apparatus of qualified predication (syādvāda, with its seven modes), and whether the seven modes can be presented before the standpoints without collapsing into a parlor trick. Then specify the single example that should carry the session. I have been tempted by the blind men and the elephant, but I suspect it teaches the wrong lesson; tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack, and what you would put in its place. Then give me the misconception map, in the order the misconceptions will actually surface: that many-sidedness means every view is equally true; that syādvāda is a proto-fuzzy logic or a machine for hedging; that the seven modes are simply Nāgārjuna's tetralemma under another name, since several students will have just read him; that 'syāt' is best rendered as 'perhaps,' which turns qualification into uncertainty; and that a doctrine which can accommodate any predicate is unfalsifiable and therefore empty. For each, say where in the sequence it must be headed off and what the students must already have understood for the correction to take hold. Close with one question I can put to the class at the end, built on a live disagreement from outside Indian philosophy — the dispute over whether a fetus is a person, or over whether addiction is a disease — that will show me whether they have grasped standpoint-indexing or merely learned to say that everyone is partly right. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately omit at this level, and what I should say out loud in order to omit it.
