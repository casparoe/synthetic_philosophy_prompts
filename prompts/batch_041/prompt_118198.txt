Here is my opening move. I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, so if I fumble the vocabulary, press me on it instead of quietly repairing it. Consider a race in which several firms could each adopt a costly standard that benefits everyone, but where the benefit falls on people who cannot pay for it — say, a safety property that protects third parties rather than the firms' own customers. I want to claim this defeats every escape route you listed, and not for the boring reason that the firms are selfish. The interesting part is the demand side. The usual reason a large market ratchets standards upward — call it the "California effect" — is that a big, wealthy buyer bloc refuses to buy the dirty product, so laggards must comply to keep market access. But that mechanism presupposes that the buyer can distinguish the dirty product from the clean one. Here the relevant quality is what I have seen called a credence good: the buyer cannot assess it even after consuming it, and the third parties who are harmed are not buyers at all. That severs the demand-side ratchet. The firms, meanwhile, face a one-shot, many-player, effectively anonymous interaction over a resource that is the entire planet, so there is neither exit nor a repeat game. My claim is that this is a genuine trap: the individually dominant move is to skimp, no buyer can reward the firm that does not, no harmed party can pay the firm to stop, and no firm can unilaterally internalise the externality because its competitors will simply undercut it.
