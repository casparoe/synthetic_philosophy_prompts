I've been co-teaching a graduate seminar on partiality and impartiality, and a long-running disagreement with my co-instructor has now escaped the classroom and colonized our lunches. His position, roughly stated, is that patriotism is straightforwardly a virtue and that the standard critique is confused at the root: it treats patriotic partiality as though it were a free-floating preference that must be justified to the impartial standpoint, when in fact it belongs to a different logical category altogether. On his account patriotism is a species of role morality — the citizen stands to her compatriots roughly as a parent stands to her child, and we do not demand that a parent justify loving her own child more than a stranger's, because the special relationship is itself the source of the reason. I have been pressing him on whether the parental analogy can bear the weight he places on it. One does not choose one's country, and one cannot exit it cheaply; the goods at stake in international partiality are frequently rival and zero-sum in a way that a parent's devotion to her child is not; and the foreigner who bears the cost of our partiality is a moral equal who has done nothing to forfeit her claim. He replies that I am importing an impartialist framework in order to convict his view of failing to satisfy it, which he calls begging the question with extra steps.

What I'd like from you is a written intervention in this dispute, and I want it to land somewhere rather than hovering above the fray. Write an essay on whether patriotism is a virtue or a vice — and reach a verdict, even if the verdict is qualified or conditional, rather than concluding that reasonable people differ. Two distinctions will help you keep your footing. First, separate patriotic sentiment, the affective attachment one may feel to a place, a history, or a people, from patriotic partiality, the distributive and political practice of giving compatriots priority in goods, opportunities, and sacrifices; I am interested in whether these can come apart, and whether either can be defended without the other. Second, treat the role-morality defense as a reconstructed target rather than a position you need to source — you may attribute it to my co-instructor, and you need not worry about who has actually held it. Then do the real work: adjudicate the parental analogy by isolating exactly which features do the justificatory work and testing whether the citizen–compatriot relation possesses them, and ask whether a defensible patriotism must be conditional — say, restricted to non-rival goods, or licensed only where the institutions one is partial toward are themselves just, or bounded by some threshold of cost imposed on outsiders. A verdict of the form "patriotism is a virtue under conditions X and a vice otherwise" is perfectly acceptable, provided you specify X and defend the line. Please argue rather than survey: no citations, no reading list, no appeals to who has said what — the arguments themselves are the thing, and I care far more about whether your conclusion survives the strongest version of the opposing case than about whether it is the conclusion I happen to favor. Around 1,200 to 1,800 words.
