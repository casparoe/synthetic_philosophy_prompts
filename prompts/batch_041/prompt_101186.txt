I'm in a small reading group that has been working through decision theory, and we keep circling Roko's basilisk; I have now heard the same gloss of it three times, from a friend who is usually reliable, and before I repeat it to anyone I want a careful audit of it rather than another paraphrase. Her account runs roughly like this. At some point a superintelligent AI will come into being, and although it will be benevolent in the ordinary sense it will also want to have existed, so it will take steps to ensure that it does. Because it is superintelligent, it will be able to reach back and predict the past: it will model everyone who ever learned about the argument, torture a simulation of anyone who heard of it and did nothing to bring it about, and reward a simulation of anyone who helped. The consequence, she says, is that merely having heard the argument rationally obliges you to spend your life working to build this AI, since otherwise a copy of you is tortured; the only way out is never to have encountered it, which is why the people who discuss it treat it as an infohazard and why the forum where it was first posted moved to suppress discussion of it. She then tells me the whole thing is bankrupt on two counts: first, it has to assume that the AI can send information backward in time, which physics forbids; second, even if it could, a simulation of me is not me, so torturing a copy harms no one who matters, and the argument reduces to a Pascal's wager for people who read too much science fiction.

I know enough to suspect this is a caricature, but not enough to locate the error, and I would rather not simply be told to go read the original. Please go through the account claim by claim: what a defender of the argument would accept as accurate, what is a common simplification or conflation, and what is plainly wrong. I am especially interested in whether her two objections are the fatal ones she takes them to be or whether they miss the argument's actual mechanism, whether any version of the argument survives them, and how much of its force depends on the decision theory one assumes rather than on anything the AI is assumed to do. I would also like the descriptive summary and the objections kept apart, since I suspect she is running together three different things — what the AI is assumed to want, what the decision theory is assumed to license, and what is being claimed about personal identity.

Where you give me your own view, tell me how confident you are, and single out the one point you are least sure of; I would rather have a hedged answer that marks its weak joint than a confident one that papers over it.
