I'm trying to get clear on what a very common kind of measurement in comparative politics is actually committed to. The widely used cross-national democracy ratings — the ones that score a country on separate components such as free and fair elections, freedom of expression, associational and organizational pluralism, checks on executive power, and the rule of law, and then aggregate the component scores into one overall number or band — mostly work by compensation: a country can make up for a poor score on one component with a strong score on another, so a weakened legislature or a captured judiciary can be offset by vigorous elections and the total still lands in the "democratic" range. A smaller number of rating schemes use a floor or veto rule instead, where dropping below a threshold on any single component caps the overall rating no matter how well the country does elsewhere. Meanwhile the same body of work uses these numbers to say that a country is "backsliding" — one that scored well a decade or two ago now scores lower — and to rank such countries against each other on a single scale.

My question is about the theory of democracy this practice presupposes. Does the compensatory aggregation rule commit the practitioner to the view that the goods of democracy are substitutable for one another, and that democracy is a single scalar quantity along which all countries can be placed? Which rival conceptions does it quietly rule out — for instance, a minimalist view on which competitive elections are close to sufficient, or a view on which certain rights and constraints are lexically prior and simply cannot be traded off against electoral performance? I notice an apparent inconsistency I can't resolve: the people who build and use these indices are often the same people who insist that democracy is an essentially contested concept with no single correct definition, yet the scoring practice behaves as though there were one measurable thing there. Reconstruct the implicit conception as best you can, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several quite different theories of democracy would license the same scoring behavior. Then tell me what would have to change in how "backsliding" is identified and compared if the compensatory rule were replaced by a conjunctive or floor-based one; would backsliding stop being a matter of degree and become a threshold event, and would the "who decides" problem — the reliance on expert coders to fix the components and their weights — look any different under the two rules?

If I've got the framing wrong — if these ratings don't really presuppose any single conception, or if I'm conflating the measurement apparatus with a substantive theory of democracy when they should be kept apart — please say so directly rather than working around it, and tell me what the better-posed version of the question would be.
