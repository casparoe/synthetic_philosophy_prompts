I retired last year after thirty-odd years in a crystallography lab, and I've been trying to make up for lost time by reading philosophy, mostly badly. Our reading group at the library has worked its way round to Parfit, specifically the part where he asks whether you'd step into a machine that scans you, destroys the body, and rebuilds you atom for atom on Mars. I went in assuming the interesting question was whether the fellow who steps off on Mars is *me*, and Parfit keeps telling me that's the wrong question — that what matters in survival is something thinner, connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause, and that when the machine makes two of me, both of them should feel about it the way I'd feel about simply surviving. I can just about follow the argument on the page, though I notice I don't believe a word of it, which I gather is itself a datum he'd want explained.

What I can't square it with is Nolan's The Prestige, which we watched the other night. You'll remember the machinery of it: Angier gets a device from Tesla that produces a duplicate of him across the theatre, and every night one of the two drops through the trapdoor and drowns in the tank while the other takes the applause — and my recollection is that he tells Borden he never knows, stepping in, whether he'll be the one who drowns or the one who bows. On Parfit's account that shouldn't be a tragedy at all: whoever walks out the far side is continuous with the man who walked on, so Angier survives in the only sense that matters, and drowning the spare each night ought to be about as weighty as trimming a fingernail. But the film plays the tank as a horror and as a sort of suicide, and the whole third act turns on his willingness to keep doing it. And then there's the twist, which I think is more interesting than the film quite realizes: Borden turns out to be identical twins sharing one life and one wife between them, which is exactly Parfit's other case, the one where a man is divided rather than copied, and the movie treats it as a devastating secret rather than as a benign branch of one person into two.

So what I'd like from you is a proper reading of the film against the philosophy, not a summary of either. Lay out the correspondences first — which character and which scene carry the teletransporter case, which carry the division case, what plays the role of the scanner and of psychological continuity — and then be honest in both directions. Where does the film actually vindicate Parfit, if anywhere? Where does it resist him, and is the resistance anything more than the brute first-person horror he'd say we're supposed to get over? The twins seem to me to cut against him in a way the drowning doesn't, since two men sharing one name and one marriage is not obviously the same thing as one man branching into two equally glad survivors, and I'd like you to say whether that's a real problem for him or just my sentimentality. Also tell me whether the film dramatizes something the argument can't reach — the business of being the one in the tank rather than the one on the balcony, the fact that from the inside somebody has to be the copy — and whether that's a point in the film's favor or a confusion it's trading on. If it helps, point me at the two or three scenes I should make the group watch twice, and finish by saying what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone doesn't. I don't need citations, just the reading.
