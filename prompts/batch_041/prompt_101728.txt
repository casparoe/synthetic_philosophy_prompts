I'm a doctoral student in political theory working on the ethics of armed intervention, and I want to pressure-test the current debate by reconstructing an older position within it. Take John Stuart Mill's 1859 essay on non-intervention at its strongest — the claims that a people must win its own freedom rather than have it bestowed from outside, that institutions cannot be imposed on a society, and that the chief legitimate ground for interference is to counter another power's intervention — and ask what he would have concluded about three cases that postdate him: NATO's 1999 intervention in Kosovo, undertaken without Security Council authorisation; the 2003 invasion of Iraq, whose humanitarian justification was advanced only after the fact; and the 2011 intervention in Libya, authorised by the Security Council but widely accused of exceeding its mandate. Would he have accepted the now-common view that an intervention can be unlawful under the Charter and yet morally justified, or would he have read that distinction as a licence for self-serving intervention dressed in humanitarian language? And how would he respond to the proposal, floated in the debates that followed Libya, that interveners should be held accountable for how they exercise an authorised mandate, with military force sequenced after non-military measures?
