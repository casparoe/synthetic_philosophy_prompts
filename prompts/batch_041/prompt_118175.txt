I'm a novelist, about a hundred pages into something whose narrator is a man who had his corpus callosum cut when he was a kid — the epilepsy surgery, the one where they sever the bridge between the two halves and the patient walks out looking completely normal, and then someone flashes a word into the left field of view and he can't say it, or his left hand does something and his speaking half offers up an explanation that's obviously confabulated on the spot. My narrator has convinced himself he's got a bulletproof answer to every doubt: whatever else is going on in there, he says, at least he knows he's thinking, and a thought needs a thinker, so he knows he exists. Which I gather is the cogito, and I need to know exactly how much of that my guy is actually entitled to. Not the history, not a reading list — take the argument apart: what has to be true at each step (that thinking is going on, that the thinking is his, that there's one of him, that the I in question is a thing of some particular kind), which of those steps actually gets paid for and which are just helped himself to, and where the whole business goes quiet. I've also heard there's a reading on which it isn't an inference at all but a kind of performance — that you can't do the doubting without a doubter, so there's no premise to be missing — and I want to know whether that's a rescue or a dodge.

Then run it the other way, because that's the part I care about. Take the split-brain findings as a real episode the cogito has to answer for: two streams of experience in one skull, the left one narrating and making up stories about what the right one just did, the unity of the thinker looking more like a committee that votes and then publishes a single set of minutes. What does that do to the certainty — does it show the cogito proves less than advertised, only that thinking is occuring somewhere in here and not that there's one persisting somebody it belongs to, or does the performance reading survive because the doubting is still being done and a performance needs a performer? I keep going back and forth on whether the move from thinking-is-happening to I-exist is an inference with a smuggled premise or no inference at all, and I'd like you to pick: defend one reading and say what it costs, don't just hand me the menu. Same for the older move from I can conceive of myself without a body to I'm really distinct from it — does the split-brain material make that better or worse? And be honest about where the argument simply has nothing to say: about the identity of the thinker over time, about what would make two streams into one person, about whether my narrator's certainty is even about him. If the truth is that the cogito gets you something real but thin — that there's experience here, first-personally undeniable, and not a whole lot else — say so plainly and tell me which of Descartes's prizes (the simple nature, the immaterial soul, the real distinction) he still gets to keep and which he drops. Finish with a practical note, since I have to write the scene: what's the strongest thing my narrator can say out loud that's genuinely licensed, and which line would make a philosopher friend wince.
