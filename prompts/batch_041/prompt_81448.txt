I staff the tribunal our diocese convenes when someone claims a healing through the intercession of a candidate for sainthood, and my job is to decide which claims get a full investigation and which we decline. We see perhaps twenty claims in a decade, a decline is effectively final for the claimant, and our stated criteria are that the cure be complete, lasting, and medically inexplicable. Write me a decision procedure my team can actually run — a checklist in order, not a discussion — built out of Hume's argument about miracles rather than out of general skepticism. I need the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the step where the procedure stops with a no, and the step where it hands the decision to a person because what remains is a question of faith rather than of evidence. Keep straight which parts of Hume's case bear on the evidence for this particular report and which bear on the credibility of reports of the miraculous in general, and include his concession that testimony can be credible when rejecting it would require believing something even more extraordinary. Then run the procedure on three claims: a tumor that disappeared after a pilgrimage, with before-and-after scans and three physicians willing to call the recovery unexplained; a claim resting on a single elderly witness devoted to the cause, who stands to gain nothing; and a healing of the same illness reported at a shrine belonging to a different religion. Our team is me, a consulting physician, and a canon lawyer; our recommendations go to the bishop, who signs off. Tell me where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure cannot do. Please write in American English.
