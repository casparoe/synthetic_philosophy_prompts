My reading group has been stuck for about six months on a fight between me and a friend, and I want to finally write the document we should have written ages ago. I'm the covering-law person: I think explaining a historical event is showing that it had to happen, or probably had to happen, given the antecedent circumstances plus general laws, and that what we call a narrative is at best a convenient string of such explanations with the laws left implicit. She thinks that's backwards. On her view historical explanation is narrative all the way down: what explains is the tracing of connections, the way a story makes a sequence of events intelligible, and any laws you dig out of it are either trivially true or artifacts of the reconstruction. We've read the usual stuff, we meet Thursdays, the coffee in that room is a war crime, and we have gotten exactly nowhere. So: draft the joint statement the two of us could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be non-vacuous (not just "we both think history is hard"), the disagreement entries should be written so each side would accept the wording of its own view, and the cruxes should be named precisely — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of us.

A few things I want built into the document, because they're the cases we keep circling and I'd like to see them handled rather than gestured at. First, a made-up test case (don't dress it up as real history): a provincial bank fails in a single week while three banks in the same town with near-identical books survive. There's a clean general law available — something like, depositors withdraw when they expect others to withdraw, given a trigger — and there's also a story: one rumour, one frightened clerk, one badly timed telegram, a director who happened to be out of town. I say the law does the explanatory work and the story is garnish; she says the story is the explanation and the law is just a redescription of it. Second, the four things a narrative might be doing, and I want each of us to take a seperate stand on all four: (i) chronological packaging, (ii) tracking causal chains through contingent connections, (iii) constituting the explanandum, so that there's no pre-narrative "the event" to be explained in the first place, (iv) making the past significant or orienting us rather than causing it. Third, a paired test we both agree in advance to run: one case where the historian's explanation contains no generalisation at all and yet feels complete, and one where the generalisation is fully explicit and the explanation still feels unsatisfying. For each, write down what each of us would predict, and what result would actually count against our own side. And tell us honestly which part of our fight, if any, is just about words — say whether "law" is doing the work as a true universal, a lawlike generalisation, or just whatever generalisation you need to get the explanation to go through, and whether "explanation" is being used for a product or for an activity.
