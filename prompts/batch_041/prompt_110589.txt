I am a novelist, and for the book I am now writing I have engaged a documentary historian to keep me from asserting, in the narrative's own voice, more than the sources will bear. Her rulings come in three grades: I may state a thing flatly, I may put it only in a character's mouth, or I must leave it out. Five rulings so far, and I am trying to work out the rule she is actually running, because her sixth bears on the hinge of my third act.

First: an annalist of the losing side, writing within a decade of the battle, reports that his own lord quit the field before the end and that his own men began the killing at the ford after the enemy had surrendered. Nothing else about the ford survives. She let me state both.

Second: the victor's chancery, writing a generation later, claims the victory and that the war ended in that one campaign. Character's mouth only.

Third: the panegyrists of both sides claim the ford for their own lord, and no third account survives. She struck the engagement from the book entirely.

Fourth: a compilation written two centuries later by the house that held the founder's tomb says the founder's line was foretold and that the rival line descends from a slave. Struck.

Fifth: another compilation of the same date preserves, from a lost register, the acreage of a land grant, a detail no one's quarrel turns on. Character's mouth only, on the ground that the register is gone and the compiler is the sole channel to it.

Now the case I care about. A chronicle from a neighbouring kingdom, in another language, composed about a hundred and fifty years after the founder's death. Its patron's title to a border province rests on the founder's conquest having been a lawful settlement; the chronicle nevertheless reports that the founder's army killed the people of the town of Brinden after they had opened the gates, and did so on the founder's own order. The founder's own annals never mention Brinden, and nothing in the foreign chronicle can be traced to them.

Will she let me state it? Set out the rules that fit her five rulings, say what each predicts here, tell me which of those rules her record cannot yet separate, and which further case would separate them. This is, I take it, the oldest argument in the discipline, whether the historian reports what is said or cross-examines the witness, and I would like to know which side she is on.
