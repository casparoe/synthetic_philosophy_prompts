I'm a philosophy graduate student sitting in on a course on the metamathematics of set theory, and I've hit an argument I genuinely can't decide whether to resist. A friend of mine — sharp, but no set theorist — insists that the independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC settles the philosophical question once and for all. His reasoning runs like this: Gödel showed that CH holds in the constructible universe, Cohen showed how to force it to fail, and neither of the resulting models of ZFC is privileged over the other; the two are simply different, equally legitimate pictures of the set-theoretic universe. On his view, asking whether the reals "really" have an intermediate cardinality is like asking whether the parallel postulate is "really" true — a pseudo-question that dissolves once you grasp that axioms define structures rather than describe one antecedently given world. He goes further and says that the long search for new axioms — large cardinals, forcing axioms, and the like — that Gödel and others hoped would settle CH is a category mistake, and that anyone still speaking of CH as having a truth value is in the grip of a discredited Platonism.

So here is what I want to ask. Given that CH is independent of ZFC, and that both CH and its negation have models, is my friend right that there is simply no fact of the matter — that "CH is true" is neither true nor false, but only true-in-this-structure and false-in-that-one, exactly as "the parallel postulate holds" is true of Euclidean and false of hyperbolic geometry? And if he is right, what is left of the idea that set theory might yet discover a determinate answer to the size of the continuum? I'd like a clear verdict rather than a survey of positions, in a single paragraph of no more than 250 words.
