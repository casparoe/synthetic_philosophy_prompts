I'm in a weekly reading group on the measurement problem, and for the past two months we've been circling the probability question for no-collapse quantum mechanics: if the linear dynamics is all there is and every outcome of a measurement is realized in some branch, what could it mean to call one outcome more probable than another, and what licenses an agent to act on the squared amplitudes rather than on anything else? Every proposal we've looked at seems to do one of two things — quietly assume the answer somewhere in the setup, or produce something that reads like a claim about rational preference rather than a claim about the world. Before I read any further derivations I want the specification of the problem itself, so that I can tell whether a given proposal is meeting the demands or changing them. Write me the desiderata that any adequate account of probability in a no-collapse, branching setting must meet, with the consideration behind each requirement, and be explicit about which of those requirements are genuine constraints and which are merely the preferences of one camp dressed up as constraints.

I want a case behind each item rather than a bare label. The account has to get the standard experimental statistics right, obviously, but I care more about the harder cases. It should handle a situation where the number of decohered branches and the squared amplitudes come apart: suppose a measurement has three mutually exclusive macroscopic outcomes, with amplitudes one over the square root of two, one half, and one half, so that weighting by branch count gives each outcome equal weight while weighting by amplitude gives one half, one quarter, one quarter — a theory has to say which of these is right and why the other is wrong, and it has to do so without defining the answer into the derivation. It should handle an agent who has computed the universal state and therefore knows that every outcome occurs, so that nothing is uncertain to her in the ordinary sense; the account owes me a statement of what she should believe, what she should pay for a bet, and whether her answer before a branching event may legitimately differ from the answers of her successors after it. It should handle a repeated survival-type measurement, where the question is whether the agent who finds herself still present should treat that as evidence about anything at all. It should handle the limiting case of zero amplitude, and more generally the passage from a continuous amplitude to the discrete fact that a branch does or does not exist. And it should handle a universe with infinitely many branches, where the relative frequency of outcomes along a branch has no limit and counting is simply unavailable. Alongside the cases, the account has to respect distinctions I would rather not see collapsed: between a credence and a physical weight attached to branches; between being uncertain and merely not knowing which branch one is on; between a result derived from the dynamics and a further postulate added alongside it; and between what is true of a branch and what is true of the agent in it. It also has to be consistent with two standing facts — that decoherence, not the interpreter, supplies the branch decomposition on which the whole probability story depends, and that the account has to survive the move to relativistic quantum field theory, where branch counts are notoriously ill-defined.

Once you have the list, mark which requirements are non-negotiable given the purpose of keeping the linear dynamics and adding no collapse, and which are the preferences of a particular camp. Then flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see, because I suspect the list itself is a result. In particular, tell me whether one can consistently have all three of the following: that probability is derived from the deterministic linear dynamics alone; that probability is an objective feature of the world rather than a report on an agent's information; and that nothing is added to the theory beyond the wavefunction and its equation of motion. Say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and whether any requirement on your list is really just one camp's preference wearing the costume of a constraint. I don't need reading lists, references, or citations — I can find the literature myself — and skip the scorecard against particular proposals, since I'll do that in the group. What I want from you is the standard itself, argued for rather than merely enumerated.
