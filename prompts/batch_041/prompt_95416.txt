A researcher whose talk I attended explained pain asymbolia with a comparison I have not been able to shake. Pain, she said, is like a fire alarm: in these patients the smoke detector is intact — they can report the location and intensity of a noxious stimulus about as accurately as anyone else — but the bell that makes you act has been disconnected, which is why they say it hurts and yet do not mind. She drew the moral that what makes pain bad is the second, detachable part and not the pain itself, so pain is not bad in its own right. I want an audit of that comparison, not a general essay on asymbolia. Lay out what corresponds to what, where the comparison breaks, which features of the alarm it quietly imports that pain may not have, and whether the conclusion it was used to license survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Separate the breakages that matter for that conclusion from the ones that do not, and say what a better analogy would be, if there is one.

Please give the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The mapping** — a correspondence table, with a sentence or two on the work each row is doing (roughly 150–250 words).
2. **Where it breaks** — the disanalogies, ranked by how much they matter (roughly 350 words).
3. **What the source smuggles in** — features of alarms that the comparison transfers to pain without argument (roughly 200 words).
4. **Does the conclusion survive** — a direct verdict on whether the analogy can carry the claim that pain is not bad in itself (roughly 250 words).
5. **A better analogy, if there is one** — or an argument that none is available (roughly 150 words).
