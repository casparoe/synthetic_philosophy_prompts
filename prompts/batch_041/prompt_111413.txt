"The first incompleteness theorem says that any formal system which is consistent, whose axioms can be effectively listed by a machine, and which is strong enough to express the arithmetic of the natural numbers, is incomplete: there is a sentence in its language that it can neither prove nor refute. Arithmetic is the threshold because you need enough of it to code up the syntax of the system itself — formulas, sequences of formulas, proofs — as numbers.

The key device is the diagonal lemma. For any formula P(x) with one free variable, the lemma gives you a sentence G such that the system proves the biconditional G ↔ P(the numeral for G's Gödel number). Take P to be the provability predicate, and you get a sentence which says, in the language of arithmetic, that it is not provable. If the system is consistent then G is not provable; if it is ω-consistent then G is not refutable either (Rosser later got this down to plain consistency, using a cleverer sentence). And since G says that G is not provable, and G is indeed not provable, G is true. So the theorem is about provability, not truth: the sentence isn't undecidable in the sense of having no truth value.

On the passage. For an axiomatised scientific theory the point goes through in the same way: once you write the theory down as a formal system rich enough for arithmetic, there will be consequences of the theory that it cannot derive. For a computer program, likewise — any program equivalent to such a system inherits the limit. Legal codes are a different matter: a legal code isn't a body of arithmetic truths, and it's not clear what would count as a truth of its own domain that it fails to derive, so the passage is trading on a loose analogy there.

For the mind, this is the argument Gödel himself gives at the end of his 1931 paper, and it is now usually associated with Lucas and Penrose. The machine, being a formal system, has a Gödel sentence it cannot prove. But we can see that the sentence is true: we run through the argument and observe that the sentence says something that holds. So we can know something the machine cannot, and the mind cannot be a machine. Someone might object that we could simply add G as a new axiom, but that only produces a new system with a Gödel sentence of its own. Or they might say we could take all the true sentences of arithmetic as axioms and get a complete system; true, but such a system's axioms can't be effectively listed, so the theorem simply doesn't apply to it, and that isn't a way round the theorem. The second incompleteness theorem says that a system of the relevant kind can't prove its own consistency; that's a further limit, though the argument about the mind doesn't really need it, because we can see the consistency of the machine's system from the outside.

So the passage is broadly right. The theorems are a genuine limit on formalism, and the mind's ability to see the truth of the machine's Gödel sentence is a real asymmetry. The only thing I would add is that this doesn't show the mind can prove everything either — we may have blind spots of our own."
