I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I have spent months trying to draft a piece on a dispute in metaphysics that I keep getting wrong in the retelling, so I want to settle my own view before I write it. The dispute is about what we are doing when we say that something could have gone differently for a particular person. On one account, a person exists in only one possible world, and when I say that I could have been a doctor I am really talking about a counterpart of mine in another world, someone similar enough to stand in for me in the modal claim. On the other account, I am the very same individual across many worlds, differing in my properties from world to world. I have drifted toward the counterpart account, largely because it seems to let me say that my life could have gone otherwise without pretending that I would still be me after any amount of variation, but I have also noticed that the same treatment could be turned on my earlier and later selves, which would make them counterparts of me rather than me, and I cannot tell whether that is a consequence to welcome or a reductio. I am a journalist, not a metaphysician, and I suspect I may have talked myself into a view that cannot be argued out of. Give me a map of what would actually count against the counterpart account: exactly five numbered items, ordered from the most to the least damaging, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing summary, each item stating plainly whether a committed proponent would have to concede the point or could absorb it as a cost, and whether the rival account is immune to the point. If the view has been built so that nothing could count against it, put that in one of the items rather than saving it for the end. The consideration I would personally find hardest to answer is one showing that the account cannot say what it was meant to say, namely that the possibility it hands me is not really mine. Write in American English.
