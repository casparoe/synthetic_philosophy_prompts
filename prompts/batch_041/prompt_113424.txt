I spent 35 years as a plant physiologist before I retired, and for the last two years I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which has been more humbling than the science ever was. Right now I'm stuck on the therapy/enhancement distiction — the line between fixing something that's gone wrong in a person and just making them better than normal. Before I read any more arguements about where the line falls, I want to know what a theory that draws this line is even supposed to deliver. So please write me the desiderata.

I mean the full spec: what any adequate account of the distinction has to be able to do. Which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, what facts about biology and medicine it can't contradict, what it has to say about who decides — each requirement with the case or consideration behind it. Then tell me which of the requirements you think are genuinely non-negotiable and which are really just the house prejudices of one camp, and — the part I most want — whether some subset of them can't be satisfied all at once. My suspicion, as a scientist, is that the list is overdetermined in a bad way and the real result is a no-go, but I'd rather you show me than just agree with me.

Some cases I keep tripping over, no particular order: vaccines, which prevent a disease you don't have yet; statins for someone with genuinely high cholesterol vs. the same pills for someone sitting at the population average; Ritalin for a kid who plainly has ADHD vs. the same pill for his friend who just wants to study longer; growth hormone for a very short kid with no pathology anyone can name; cochlear implants, where part of the Deaf community insists there's nothing broken; laser eye surgery, which takes you from below-normal sight to better-than-normal in one step; a drug that dulls the emotional charge of a traumatic memory; gene edits that lower disease risk vs. edits that only add height or muscle; and the one that broke my brain, that near-sightedness has become so common in some countries that what counts as normal vision looks like a moving target that depends on how much reading kids do. If the normal/abnormal line is carrying all the weight, and that line is part statistical and part environmental, I don't see how the theory stays at once precise and value-neutral. Maybe I'm missing something.

No reading list please — I can't keep up with one and I don't have library access anyway. I want the requirements themselves, argued for, plus your honest call on which ones can't be had together. If it's easy, close with a line or two on how the usual camps do against your list: the ones who say the distinction is a fiction, the ones who defend some normal-function line, and the ones who want to scrap it for a welfare test. A table is fine if that's easier. I'm not trying to settle policy with this, I just want the yardstick before I start measuring theories with it.
