I write a column on science and ideas, and I have been reporting on the unilateralist's curse—the pattern where each of many actors can take an action that would be good if only one did it but bad if many did, so that individually reasonable choices add up to a bad outcome. I want to pin down my own verdicts on a set of cases before I write the column, because I suspect they do not all hang together. A lone researcher who can synthesize a dangerous pathogen: I think publishing is wrong if several others are close to the same result, but permissible if she is genuinely the only one who could do it and the defensive payoff is large. An engineer at one of many labs that could each release a similar open-source model: I think each lab should refrain, yet if she is confident the others will release anyway, I feel she may as well release too—though I am uneasy about that verdict. A doctor with a controversial but well-supported finding: publish if the evidence is strong, not if it is weak, even though a single weak publication does little harm on its own. An activist who can unilaterally block a pipeline: acting is permissible when she is likely to be pivotal, not when she is merely one more body in a crowd. A person considering whether to raise a friend's drinking problem: she should raise it if she has a unique relationship with him, not otherwise. A donor to a matching campaign: giving is fine if her gift is the one that triggers the match, not if it merely adds to an already overfunded pool. A council member who can leak a report: leaking is permissible if it reveals serious wrongdoing, not if it is merely embarrassing.

The tension I keep running into is between the engineer and the activist or the donor. In the engineer's case, my sense that she may act if others will act anyway looks like a concession to futility, and in the activist's and donor's cases I treat the same thought—others will act anyway—as no license at all. A game theorist I spoke with suggested the curse is really about actions being substitutes, and that the interesting case is when they are complements, where the good outcome requires many actors rather than one; I want to know whether that distinction does any real work here, or whether the principle should instead be stated in terms of pivotal-ness, expected marginal value, or a norm of restraint that each actor should follow regardless of what she expects others to do. I also cannot tell whether the right unit is the individual action, the rule the individual acts on, or the outcome the group produces.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best captures this pattern. State each precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I have not listed—for instance, an action that is good only if many people do it, an action that is irreversible, or a case where the actor's uncertainty is about whether others are even aware of the option. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate; if I have to give one up, tell me which and why. I do not need citations or a reading list—I am after the principle and the cases that force it.
