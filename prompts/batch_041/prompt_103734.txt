You are a philosopher with a background in logic and the design of self-governing institutions. Please think this through carefully before answering, and start by saying what you take the underlying structure of the situation to be.

I sit on the founding committee of a small worker-owned bakery, and we are drafting our own bylaws. We want them to be genuinely self-amending: the members should be able to change any rule, including the rule that specifies how rules are changed, using whatever procedure that rule currently prescribes. A lawyer friend warns that this is circular — the procedure for revising the amendment rule is itself something the amendment rule governs — and that unless something at the root is simply not up for revision, the whole scheme has no bottom. Some of us want to declare the founding committee's original document unchangeable at the root and derive everything from it; others insist that nothing should be off-limits, on principle. I have also noticed that the person who drafted our first version wrote the amendment clause so that it happens to require his own signature, which now looks less like an oversight than a design choice.

What should we do? Is the circularity my friend fears real and fatal, or can it be made to work — and if so, what has to sit outside the loop for it to work? Be concrete about the design you would recommend and about the status of whatever does the authorizing.
