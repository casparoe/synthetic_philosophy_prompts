I'm writing a novel set between 1838 and 1901, and its spine is a single family whose fortunes track a conviction I gave to its matriarch: that history is going somewhere, and that it is going somewhere just. I built her an argument for it, and I built it too well. She now says things I did not intend her to be able to say, and I find I cannot make her wrong without also making her stupid, which she is not.

The argument, as she states it in the fourth chapter, runs:

(1) Some moral changes are real and irreversible. The trade in human beings was once unremarkable and is now unthinkable; whatever else changed, that did not change back.
(2) A sequence that cannot run backward is a sequence with a direction.
(3) A direction that has survived every reversal it has met is a tendency of the thing itself, not an accident of the weather.
(4) If it is a tendency of the thing itself, it will carry on carrying us forward.
(5) Therefore the injustices she will not live to see undone will be undone.
(6) Therefore her own losses — the brother, the shop, the years — are not merely borne but spent, contributions to a debt that will be paid.
(7) Therefore a person who knows all this and still despairs has simply failed to do the arithmetic.

I believe every step up to (5) is doing something I can defend, and (7) is monstrous — it tells the grieving that their grief is a math error, and it would license my matriarch to tell a starving tenant to be patient. But when I try to cut the argument, every cut takes something I need. If I deny (3), I lose the difference between progress and mere change, and then I have no novel — only weather. If I deny (5), I seem to be saying that the only reason to fight is that one happens to feel like it, which is beneath her. And (6) is the only thing that lets her survive the book.

I want a diagnosis I can put in the mouth of the younger generation in the last act — a real one, not a novelist's fudge. Tell me which of these numbered steps I should have her granddaughter reject, why it looked airtight to a serious person in 1870 (not a fool, not a saint), and what it costs to reject it: which of the other steps it drags down with it, what has to be rebuilt in its place, and what the rebuilt position can no longer say. I would rather keep (1) through (3) than keep (6); price that choice for me, and price the alternative. Also tell me whether the flaw is in the reasoning or in the metaphysics — that is, whether she is making a mistake or whether the world simply does not cooperate with the argument — because the two give me very different last chapters. If any step is really two steps wearing one coat, say which and where the seam is.
