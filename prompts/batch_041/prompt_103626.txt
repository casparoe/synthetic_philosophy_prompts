I am a competition and regulatory economist by trade — fifteen years on natural-monopoly infrastructure, first at a sectoral regulator and now advising on price controls and franchise design — and I have lately been dragooned onto my city's police and crime scrutiny panel, where the argument between reform, defunding and abolition is one I can follow as a citizen but cannot yet parse with the tools I actually trust. At the last meeting the chair insisted that nothing was being defunded, only reallocated; a campaigner on the other side said the force is not a service at all but a relation of domination, and that asking how to run it better is like asking how to run a better protection racket; a third member said the point is simply that the people paying for it never agreed to buy it. My instincts run to natural monopoly and contestability, barriers to entry, regulatory capture, universal service obligations, price-cap and yardstick regulation, franchising and competitive tendering, and public-choice accounts of budget-maximising bureaux. I am fluent enough in these that you need not explain them back to me, and I would rather you used them at full strength, including the places where the standard models are themselves contested.

Please take the three positions in that debate — reform, defund, abolish — one at a time, and restate each as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary: who is the provider and who the principal, what exactly is the good, where the barriers to entry lie, how the incentive constraints and the information asymmetries bite, and what the proposal would amount to as a regulatory intervention (a service-standard tightening, a budget reallocation, a de-franchising, a liberalisation of entry, or something else). Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the framework at all? I am particularly interested in anything the economics can only accommodate by quietly redefining it — the suggestion that what is supplied is not a service but an authorisation; that the demand for it is itself produced by the supplier, through the very decisions about what to criminalise; that the relevant public cannot exit, cannot choose an alternative and has not consented; and that the institution might be constitutive of the harm rather than merely an inefficient remedy for it. Finally, tell me whether the residue looks like a defect in my framework or like a sign that the original claims were less clear than they sounded, and whether my hunch — that this is natural-monopoly regulation with a story about legitimacy bolted on — is defensible or is precisely the kind of smuggling I ought to be suspicious of. Please answer in British English.
