A friend of mine who teaches in the same department and I spent most of last Thursday evening arguing about how to teach the grue puzzle, and I would like to settle the disagreement by actually building the session rather than by continuing to argue about it. Her view is that the puzzle has to be explained before it can be felt: you tell the students that "all emeralds examined so far have been green" and "all emeralds examined so far have been grue" — where a thing is grue when it is examined before some fixed future moment and is then green, and when it is not examined before that moment and is then blue — are supported by exactly the same evidence, and you then discuss what, if anything, licenses the preference for green. My view is that this is backwards: the puzzle only bites for someone who has already committed herself, publicly and in writing, to one of the two predicates, and who then discovers that nothing in the evidence distinguished her choice from its alternative. I teach the second-year seminar on scientific method — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, no reading assigned beforehand, no laptops needed — and I want the whole session to be an activity of the second kind, in which the students do the committing before they know there is anything to commit to. What I want them to come away with is not the slogan that the evidence is neutral, but the experience of having been neutral without noticing, plus a clear view of the fact that the asymmetry they will want to appeal to afterwards — that one of the predicates is the one we actually use, the entrenched one — is something their own practice supplied rather than something the world handed them.

I would like the activity to stage that second point, not merely mention it. My rough thought, which you should feel free to replace if you have something better, is a betting game on a deck of cards in two colours, in which each participant must play a rule rather than a guess, where the permitted rules include the familiar one and several gerrymandered ones built out of a before-and-after clause; points are scored for correct predictions, and the points are worth nothing but a place on a visible leaderboard. The crucial move is a licensing stage partway through: before the last rounds, the group has to vote on which rules the room will allow anyone to bet with, and the rules they will license, I predict, are the ones whose description reads smoothly, while the ones with the before-and-after clause will be treated as tricks. I want the debrief to make it plain that what did the work in that vote was the participants' prior fluency with one vocabulary and not any feature of the cards, and to be scrupulously neutral about what follows from that — I do not want the session to end with the students being told that entrenchment solves the problem, or that it fails to, since the point is to leave them holding the question. The debrief should also be the first moment at which they hear the puzzle's name. Please give me the rules, the roles if you need any, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief script with each of its moments tied to a specific piece of the puzzle.

Two further things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the room — I expect at least one student to object that grue is not a real predicate because it is defined in terms of green, and I expect another to try to make the vote a matter of taste, and I would rather have a plan for both than improvise. And tell me honestly which part of the puzzle the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply myself: I suspect it is the position of a single inquirer who has no community of speakers to lean on, but I would like your judgement rather than mine.
