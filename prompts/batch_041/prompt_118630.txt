I'm a third-year philosophy student and I'm taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility; next week each of us has to circulate a position statement and then defend it in the tutorial, so I'd rather find out now where mine is soft than in front of everyone. Here, roughly, is where I've landed. (1) I think the Basic Argument is sound: you act as you do because of the way you are, so being responsible for an action would require being responsible for the self that action flows from, and being responsible for that self would require having shaped it, which only pushes the question back to an earlier self or else bottoms out in a self you simply found yourself with. (2) I don't think any of this turns on determinism; the regress seems to run just as well in an indeterministic world, so I'm unmoved by libertarian escape hatches, agent causation included. (3) I don't think that leaves me without any notion of freedom at all, though: I think we really are self-determining in the everyday sense — what we do flows from our own reasons and our own character — and I'm tempted to say that this is all that freedom was ever worth wanting, and that it's desert, not freedom, that has to go. (4) I've also come to think that two things get run together under the word responsibility: being answerable, in the sense that demands and sanctions are fitting responses to what one does, and merely having one's deeds attributable to one, in the sense that they express what one cares about. I take the Basic Argument to demolish the first and to leave the second completely untouched — which is why I think character appraisal is entirely in order: some people really are admirable and some really are contemptible, and saying so isn't a mistake that a clearer head would drop. (5) I want to keep blame and praise as forms of moral address, telling someone what they've done and asking them to answer for it, rather than as instruments of conditioning or as hangovers from a superstitious notion of desert. (6) And I take it as obvious that deliberation survives all of this: when I weigh reasons, something real is going on and it makes a difference to what I do, and the fact that I didn't choose the machinery I deliberate with seems to me neither here nor there.

What I want from you is an audit, not an exposition. Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? I suspect at least one pair looks worse than it is, so please separate the ones that are genuinely incompatible from the ones that only appear so because of a distinction I haven't made explicit, and for the genuine ones say which commitment has to give and what it costs to give it. Rank the tensions rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each verdict say how confident you are that it's a real tension rather than a merely verbal one — and at the end, tell me which single judgment in your answer you're least sure of.
