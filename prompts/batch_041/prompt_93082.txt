In three weeks my department is holding a small festschrift symposium and a closing banquet for my doctoral advisor, who is retiring after thirty-six years. I am the one giving the toast at the banquet, and I would like it to say something true about her work rather than something merely nice about her.

Here is the room. About forty evolutionary biologists, most of them working on social evolution, roughly evenly divided between people who think group-level adaptation is a genuine phenomenon worth modelling in its own right and people who think the whole apparatus — trait-group models, "for the good of the group" talk, group heritability — is either mistaken or a redescription of selection at the individual and genic levels. Several of them have been arguing with her, and with one another, since the nineteen-eighties. Also at the tables: her husband, who is a tax lawyer; two of her children; and her sister, who has been a hospice nurse for thirty years and once told me she has never understood what her sister actually did for a living.

What I want the toast to do. Her whole career turns on one question — at what level does something like a contribution or an adaptation live? — and I want the toast to answer that question about her, not to decorate itself with it. I would like the honest answer to be some version of: the group-level phenomenon is real, the genic description is also correct, and a large part of the old fight was about which description is doing explanatory work rather than about the facts. I do not want that delivered as a platitude in which everyone turns out to have been right. If the technical material helps — the difference between selection of a thing and selection for it, the Price partition against contextual analysis, the distinction between the two quite different things people have called group selection, the equivalence between a kin-selection accounting and a group-selection accounting — use at most two of them, and only where they are load-bearing. Do not explain group selection 101 to this room; half of them could give that lecture. And do not quote anybody: no lines from books or papers, no "as she always said." Paraphrase everything.

Five minutes, seven hundred words or so. It has to be warm enough that her sister laughs in the right places and understands the shape of the thing by the end, and careful enough that the colleague in the second row, who refereed her first big paper and recommended rejection, can stand up afterwards and embrace her without either of them feeling that I have just re-litigated it.

On that last point: if the picture of the debate I have sketched above is itself a distortion — if "two descriptions, one fact" is mush, or if the two camps mean different things by group selection so that my both-sides gesture is really just an equivocation — then say so at the top rather than writing around it, and write the toast only on the version of the story you would actually defend. I would rather hand over something shorter and honest than something graceful that smuggles in a verdict. If the task cannot be done well as posed, tell me that too.

After the toast, give me a JSON object with these fields:

- "contested_lines": array of objects, each with "line" (the phrase as it appears in the toast), "why_contested", "who_would_object" (which camp, and why), and "neutral_rewrite" (a wording that would not have taken a side, if one exists).
- "level_commitments": object with keys "gene", "organism", "group", "species or clade", each mapped to exactly one of "asserted", "presupposed", or "left open" as the toast stands.
- "verdict_on_my_framing": one short paragraph on whether the story I told you at the top is defensible, oversimplified, or confused, and what I should change.
- "left_unsaid": array of objects, each with "what" (something I might have wanted in the toast) and "why" (why it could not go in without committing us).
- "for_the_nonspecialists": array of objects, each with "line" and "what_it_does" (what that line is doing for the people at the table who are not biologists).
