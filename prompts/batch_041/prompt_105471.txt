My reading group has been doing the Dutch book argument for probabilism and I've somehow argued myself into a corner. You know the shape of it: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms, someone who knows what your credences are can offer you a bunch of bets, each of which looks good to you on your own numbers, and the bunch together guarentees you lose money. A friend wrote it up for our notes like this. (1) A rational agent is one against whom nobody can construct a set of bets, each of which she'd accept on her own terms, that together guarantee her a loss. (2) If your credences break the axioms -- your credence in a claim and in its negation don't sum to 1, or you're more confident in a conjunction than in one of its conjuncts -- then a bookie who knows your credences can build exactly such a set. (3) So your credences have to satisfy the axioms. He then added a line for the dynamic case: if you plan to update in any way other than conditionalising on what you learn, a bookie can offer you bets at different times, each of which you'll take when it's offered, and you lose for sure; so conditionalising is required too. I believe most of this, and yet it seems to prove way too much.

Here's my problem. Nothing in (1) or (2) uses anything special about probabilities, or about people who are being sloppy or wishful. Take a careful person who's certain of A and certain of B and simply hasn't noticed that A and B can't both be true -- she hasn't done the logical work yet, that's all, no vice involved. Her credences are inconsistent, so (2) applies to her, so (3) calls her irrational. Same for anyone a shade worse at logic than an ideal reasoner, which is all of us, and for that matter for anyone who gets tired and takes a dumb bet at 2am. If a theory of rationality implies that every human is irrational, then either the theory is wrong or it isn't really a theory of rationality but a description of an impossible ideal. So (1) looks like the step to give up, except I can't see how to weaken it without gutting the argument. If I replace it with something like "a rational agent mustn't accept bets she can see are jointly a sure loss", then the inconsistent agent in (2) never *sees* it either, and the whole thing collapes into "you must be coherent because incoherent people can be taken advantage of", which is the conclusion rather than the argument for it. Someone in the group said the fix is to drop the bookie and the money entirely and just say the bets dramatise the fact that your credences commit you to a set of evaluations that can't all be right at the same time -- but that sounds like the same argument with the money crossed out, and I don't see why it dodges the objection any better. So: which step should I give up, what exactly is wrong with it as stated, and what does giving it up cost me? And does whatever I do here carry over to the dynamic version, or is that a separate repair? I'd rather keep (3) if there's any honest way to.
