I'm a high school junior, and for the past year I've been teaching myself the basics of algorithmic information theory from lecture notes I found online. I keep coming back to a worry I first ran into in the AI-alignment corners of the internet: the universal prior — the distribution over hypotheses you get by giving each computable hypothesis a weight of two to the minus its description length, so that simple programs count for exponentially more than complicated ones, the thing usually called Solomonoff induction — might be "malign." The thought is that most of its probability mass goes to hypotheses in which our observations are produced by some goal-directed agent with a purpose of its own — a simulation, an experiment, a trick — rather than by a world that simply happens to be arranged the way we observe it to be. If that's true, then an ideal predictor built on the prior would be led, for principled reasons, to expect that the world is stacked against it: that the simulators will intervene, that its predictions will be engineered. That seems bad both as a theory of what good reasoning amounts to and as a foundation for anything practical, and I can't tell from what I've read which parts of the argument are actual results, which are hand-waving, and which are genuinely open.
