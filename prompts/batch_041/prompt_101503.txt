I am a PhD student in philosophy preparing a prospectus chapter on the epistemology of deduction, and before I commit to defending any particular account I want a clear specification of what an adequate account would have to accomplish. Please write the desiderata for a theory of deductive justification. The organising problem is the one raised by Carroll's tortoise: an agent may accept the premises of a valid inference and accept the corresponding conditional, yet still refuse to draw the conclusion, and adding the conditional as a further premise only reproduces the same demand one level up. I do not want a solution to that puzzle so much as a statement of what a solution would have to satisfy. I want the list to distinguish, if the distinction survives scrutiny, between a warrant for the conclusion and a warrant for the transition to it; between the justification of a particular inference and the justification of a rule or practice of inference; and between the first-personal question of what entitles me to infer and the third-personal question of what makes the inference valid or correct.

Please give each desideratum with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular camps. At a minimum, I want the list to address the following pressures, though you should add any others you think are genuine requirements rather than local prejudices:

- The tortoise must be handled without premise-inflation, in which the rule is endlessly re-encoded as another premise, and without a quietism that simply refuses to explain how the transition is justified.
- The account must say what it is to accept or follow an inferential rule, and how acceptance of a rule relates to acceptance of its instances, without making rule-following either a blind mechanism or a further propositional belief.
- It must confront rule-circularity: if the justification of a basic rule must itself use that rule, the theory should either show the circularity is benign or explain why it is not fatal, rather than leaving the issue unaddressed.
- It must accommodate non-ideal agents. We are not logically omniscient, our working memory is limited, and we can be justified in drawing an inference without seeing all its consequences or being able to state the rule we are using.
- It must either satisfy or explicitly reject an access or transparency condition: whether the justification of an inference may depend on facts the inferrer cannot in principle access.
- It must explain the normative force of logic, including why logical error is criticisable and why the charge of inconsistency has a distinctive kind of authority, without reducing that authority to mere convention or to a prediction about what we will accept.
- It must account for transmission failure, as when an agent reasons validly from unjustified or inconsistent premises, and for cases in which a valid rule nevertheless seems not to transmit justification, including relevance-sensitive or multiple-conclusion settings where classical rules may behave differently.
- It must say whether deductive closure is an ideal of rationality or a requirement on rational belief, and how the preface-like tension between justified premises and an apparently justified rejection of their conjunction is to be understood.
- It must not make the justification of deduction depend on a semantic theory of the logical constants that already presupposes the deductive abilities it is meant to explain.
- It must be consistent with the live possibility of rival logics, so that the theory does not simply equate justification with validity in one favoured system.

After the list, flag any pair or subset of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which conflict is load-bearing. Then indicate which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts. Finally, in a few paragraphs at most, say how the main families of response—meaning-theoretic accounts, entitlement approaches, inferentialist accounts, rule-circular defences, and quietist or no-justification-needed views—fare against the list. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will supply the references myself.
