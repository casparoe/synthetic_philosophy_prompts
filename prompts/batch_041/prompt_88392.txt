I am preparing a short essay for the opinion section of a general-interest philosophy magazine, arguing that the contemporary Stoic revival has misrepresented the dichotomy of control, and I would like your comments before I revise. I am not looking for a summary of the draft or for encouragement; I want to know where it is wrong, where it is dull, and where it will not survive contact with a reader who knows the material. The draft is below.

The Stoic revival has settled on one idea, and it is the wrong one to settle on. Everything, we are told, falls into two columns: what is up to us and what is not. Epictetus opens his handbook with precisely this division, and the popular literature since has repeated it as though it were the heart of the philosophy rather than one piece of advice inside it.

My first claim is that the two-column picture is an artefact of translation. What Epictetus divides is what is ours and what is not ours; the word control is the choice of English translators, and the modern reader, hearing it, imports a picture of causal leverage that the Stoics did not have. A doctrine of what is ours becomes a doctrine of what we can operate on, which is why it now reads as engineering advice.

My second claim is that the doctrine only worked inside a cosmology we have thrown away. For the Stoics the cosmos is a single rational animal, everything that happens is fated, and providence arranges matters for the best. Take away fate and providence and the two columns no longer amount to a philosophy; they amount to a technique for lowering your expectations, which is what the airport bookshops are in fact selling.

My third claim is constructive. I want three columns, not two. First, what is settled by my assent: my judgements, my intentions, my character. Second, what is settled by my effort but not by my will: whether the manuscript is finished, whether the patient lives, whether the argument persuades. Third, what is settled by neither: the weather, the past, the year of my birth. The received dichotomy merges the second and third, and that is exactly why it reads as quietism. It tells you not to care about outcomes, whereas the Stoics cared enormously about outcomes and merely refused to call them good or bad.

I close with the thought that the Stoics wanted emotion extinguished, and that a philosophy promising the disappearance of grief, fear and love is either impossible or monstrous; and with the observation that a doctrine whose chief attraction is that it flatters the professional reader's existing wish not to be bothered should be suspected for that reason alone.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary. Across the five, tell me which of my historical claims about Epictetus and the Stoics are false or badly overstated, and in particular whether the third claim is a genuine improvement or a rediscovery of something the Stoics already had; say whether the closing paragraph can be saved or should be cut; and say whether the piece is interesting to a reader who already knows the material. One of the five points must state how confident you are in the comments you are giving and name the single point you are least sure about.
