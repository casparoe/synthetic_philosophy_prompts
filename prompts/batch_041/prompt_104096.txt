I'm an undergraduate currently taking a course on Foucault, and I want to pin down a pattern I keep running into at my internship with a municipal social-services office, because I suspect it has a name and I can't tell which of the concepts we've been reading it actually is. The office was made to start logging every client contact in a new case-management system built around fixed categories — "engaged," "resistant," "at risk" — and within months the caseworkers were spending their visits hunting for evidence that would let them fill the categories, describing clients to one another in the system's words, and competing over their dashboards, even though nobody had been ordered to improve any numbers and no one's pay depended on them. The part that seems to matter is the combination: the categories were introduced purely as a reporting convenience, they quietly changed what the staff noticed and did, they made the staff monitor themselves and each other so that the manager rarely had to intervene, and the aggregate of all this recorded behaviour was then cited by the city as an objective picture of "client need." Is there an established concept for that whole loop? It seems to sit near disciplinary power and the panopticon, but those read to me as being about a gaze that produces compliant subjects, whereas here the knowledge categories, the self-policing, and the resulting "facts" look like a single mechanism rather than three that happen to co-occur. If there is no single name, please coin one. What I need is the carving, not the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, which of the nearby concepts fits best and where each of the others falls short, and where the edges of the thing are — for instance, whether it still counts when the measurements are genuinely accurate, or when the people being measured never learn the categories. Give it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
