For a feature I'm writing on medicine's idea of disease, I spent an afternoon with a physician-philosopher whose position is that disease is a biological fact rather than a social judgment, and he reached for the same comparison again and again: a body is a machine, and a diseased organ is a part that has stopped doing its job. A heart that cannot pump is broken the way a fuel pump that cannot pump is broken, he said, whether or not anyone minds. He was ready for the obvious objections — machines are uniform and bodies vary, machines do not heal themselves and bodies do — and waved them off as differences of degree. What I need from you is not a verdict on his position but an audit of the comparison he used to reach it. Set out the correspondences: what plays the part of the machine, the part, the job, the designer, the specification, the malfunction, and, since he never mentioned it, the manual. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, which of the breaks matter for the work he is putting the analogy to and which are merely decorative, and whether his conclusion — that disease is value-free — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, say what comparison would do better, and finish by telling me what would move you off your verdict: which argument or consideration would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
