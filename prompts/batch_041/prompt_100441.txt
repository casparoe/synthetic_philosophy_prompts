Last night I had a long, exhausting argument with a close friend — it started over dinner and ran well past midnight — and I would like an outside view on it. The subject was my younger brother, who borrowed a substantial sum from me three years ago, sank most of it into a business venture he had no business starting, and has never once acknowledged that he did anything wrong. Whenever I raise it, my friend takes my brother's side in a way I find oddly bloodless. His line is that my brother's conduct is simply the output of his history — a chaotic upbringing, a temperament he never chose, habits laid down long before he could reflect on them — and that being angry with him is therefore on a par with being angry at the weather. He calls his own approach 'the maintenance view of people': you do not blame a machine for malfunctioning, you diagnose it and repair it, and it would be absurd to hold a grudge against a car for breaking down. What irritates me most is that he is entirely consistent about it. He says he has largely stopped feeling guilt himself, and that when he does something he regrets he treats it as a bug to be fixed rather than a fault to be atoned for; he applies the same treatment to his own irritation with me; and he thinks everyone would be better off if we all did likewise. He also pointed out, correctly, that I cannot choose to stop being angry any more than I could have chosen to start, which he takes to show that my anger is not something I could be answerable for, and so not something that could ever be owed to my brother as a matter of fairness.

My instinct is that he has missed something important, but every time I try to say what, I end up sounding merely sentimental. It seems to me that if I took his view I would not simply be calming myself down — I would be withdrawing from my brother in some way that matters, and that the very thing he wants me to surrender, the sense that my brother owes me something, is what makes our dealings a relationship between two people rather than an interaction between two weather systems. Yet I cannot tell whether that is a genuine insight or just a flattering defence of a grievance I happen to enjoy. He presses me hard on the practical question: what does my anger actually do for anyone? It does not recover the money, it does not improve my brother, it makes family gatherings worse, and he claims the evidence shows it is bad for my heart. He also asks why, if I truly think he is answerable for what he did, I am so reluctant to describe exactly what would discharge the debt — a sum repaid, an apology, a change of conduct — rather than simply wanting him to feel as bad as I did.

So I would like you to adjudicate. Is my friend right that his maintenance view is the mature response, and should I take his advice and try to cultivate it in myself? If he is wrong, what precisely is lost by the view, and is there a coherent middle position — can I hold my brother to account without nursing the anger, or are those two things so bound up that trying to separate them quietly dismantles the former? And what should I make of the consistency of his position, extending as it does to guilt, to self-reproach and to his own irritation: is that a sign that he has seen something the rest of us are too invested in our grievances to see, or a sign that he has quietly stopped participating in something? Please be explicit about how confident you are in your verdict and single out the point in your answer about which you are least sure. Write in British English.
