I belong to a small political philosophy reading group, and at our last session two members — Ravi and Freya — spent most of the hour in a sustained argument about the puzzle commonly called the liberal paradox. The puzzle, as it was stated in the room, is this: take a very thin liberalism (each person is to be decisive over at least one pair of alternatives that concerns only their own life), add the weak Pareto principle (if everyone prefers one outcome to another, so does society), allow the domain of preferences to be unrestricted, and the three cannot be satisfied together. I was the bystander throughout and have no stake in the outcome. I have written up their exchange as faithfully as I can, and I would like you to referee it.

Ravi: This is a genuine impossibility theorem, not a parlour trick. It shows that any framework which grants even minimal liberalism and even minimal respect for unanimity, while letting preferences range freely, is internally inconsistent. So it is a real dilemma: something has to give, and whatever gives will be one of liberalism's own commitments. Anyone who calls themselves a liberal and also thinks collective decisions should track what everyone wants has to answer this.

Freya: It is an artefact of a formalism that permits meddlesome preferences — preferences about other people's purely private choices. Liberalism never promised anyone sovereignty over anyone else's life, only over their own. Restrict the domain to exclude the meddlesome cases and the inconsistency simply evaporates. The theorem refutes a strawman liberal, not liberalism.

Ravi: But that restriction is the substance of the problem, not a solution to it. Declaring certain preferences out of court is itself a substantive and contestable restriction on the domain: it tells us which of a person's concerns are allowed to count as reasons. And the meddlesome case is not exotic — people care deeply about how others live, often for reasons they would defend. There is no formal criterion of 'meddlesome'; the label smuggles in a normative judgement and then presents itself as a technical fix.

Freya: Then what the result shows is a limit on one particular rendering of rights, not on liberalism. If you encode a right as unconditional decisiveness over a pair of alternatives, you have built a machine that must also aggregate preferences over that same pair, and of course it jams. Model rights differently — as side-constraints that fix the feasible set before aggregation begins, or as conditional claims of the form 'I decide, provided the rest of the profile is as specified' — and the jam disappears, because the aggregator is never asked to swallow a right whole.

Ravi: Yet every fix you offer either abandons the unrestricted domain or demotes a right from a decision into something the aggregator may trade away. A conditional right that lapses the moment someone else's preference happens to conflict is a right-when-convenient, not a right. So you have not dissolved the paradox; you have chosen one horn of it and are calling the choice a solution.

Freya: Every impossibility theorem says something has to go — Arrow's does too, and no one concludes that democracy is incoherent. The theorem is a map of the trade-offs, not a proof that a destination is unreachable. Which axiom you relax is precisely where the philosophy lives; the theorem only tells you that you cannot have all of it for free.

Ravi: There is an asymmetry, though. In Arrow the relaxable axiom — say, independence of irrelevant alternatives — is a technical nicety. Here the axiom you would relax, namely that a person gets to decide something purely about their own life, is the entire moral content of the position. So this is not 'just another trade-off'; it is a trade-off that cuts into the core commitment.

Freya: That assumes the core of liberalism is 'decisive over a pair of alternatives'. If the core is instead something like 'never to be treated merely as a means to others' ends', the theorem does not even touch it. You have identified liberalism with one formal rendering of it and then reported the death of the rendering as the death of the thing.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the eight moves, tell me who has the better of it and why, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and be explicit about which claims are claims about the formalism and which are claims about liberalism itself. Then tell me what both of them are missing. I would rather have a verdict that flags its own uncertainty than one that merely sounds confident, so mark where the argument genuinely turns on a contested interpretation rather than on a demonstrable point. And if you think the task as I have posed it cannot be done well — for instance, if the two of them are really answering different questions and a move-by-move verdict would be a false precision — say so plainly rather than manufacturing a scorecard. Please write in British English.
