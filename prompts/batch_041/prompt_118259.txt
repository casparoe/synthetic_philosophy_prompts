So I've been chewing on the 20 July plot for weeks now — Stauffenberg, the briefcase bomb under the map table at the Wolf's Lair in '44, Hitler walking away with his ears blown out and his hair singed, and then everything in Berlin going sideways in a few hours because nobody could get moving. I don't need the history, I know it: the delay waiting for confirmation he was dead, the Valkyrie orders going out hours late, the courtyard at the Bendlerblock that night. What I want is to put the whole thing up against Aquinas's thinking about tyrants and see what falls out.

Here's my grasp of him, correct me if it's off. He isn't one of those people who says a bad ruler is untouchable. A ruler who's turned into a tyrant can be dealt with, but the one who should do it is the community, or some public authority standing above the ruler — not any private person who decides to take it on himself. And he worries a lot about what happens if you try and fail: the tyrant comes back crueler, the people who took the risk die, and everybody left pays for it. So the caution is baked into the position, not bolted on. I also have a memory of him saying the man who kills a tyrant to free his country is praised and gets a reward — though honestly I might be mixing him up with Cicero going on about the men who killed Caesar. Tell me which one it is.

Now the part I actually want to argue about. There's a distinction I've used in my own head for years and I've no idea if it has a name. You can be a tyrant because of how you got there, meaning you had no business holding the job, or a tyrant because of what you do with the power once you've got it. Hitler is awkward for the first kind, because he got the chancellorship through the front door and then pulled the whole structure down around him from the inside. He's a tyrant many times over by the second kind. So does Aquinas's arguemnt need the first kind to get off the ground at all, or can the second kind by itself justify killing him? And if it needs the first kind, does his view end up saying that a monster who arrives by the correct procedure is untouchable, which can't be what he meant.

Then the question of who gets to do it. He points at the community, but under a tyranny the community is exactly the thing that's been taken apart — no parliament, no court, no superior lord to appeal to, and the army swearing oaths to the man himself. So the only agents available are private people, which is what the plotters were: a colonel, some generals, a few civil servants, and their plan was to seize the government too, not just kill him. Does the standing question come down to who they were — some of them had been deep in the regime for years before they turned — or to whether they were acting for anybody other than themselves? Can a handful of insiders count as acting for the community, or is that just self-appointed people telling themselves a story?

And then the reprisals. His worry about making things worse looks vindicated here: the failure cost hundreds of lives and the war ran on another ten months. But is that a moral arguemnt or a prediction? If it's a prediction, it can be wrong in a given case and tells you nothing about what's right. If it's a moral argument, the theory ends up saying that a sufficiently strong tyrant should be left alone, which sounds like it rewards the crime. And it sits badly next to the praise-and-reward bit, since that seems to make the killing honorable even when it fails and everyone dies.

So: run the episode through his framework properly — who the agent is, what standing they have, what counts as a tyrant, what weight the likely aftermath carries — and then tell me where the framework runs out of road and where the episode actually breaks it. My suspicion is that it never had a grip on this kind of case, because it always assumed some legitimate authority was still around to do the deposing, and the whole point of a modern tyranny is that there isn't one left. If that's right, say what's left of the position once you admit it.
