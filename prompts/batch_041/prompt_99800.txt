I am a junior at a public high school and the editor-in-chief of our student newspaper, which goes to press every other Friday; on the Thursday night before we print I am the last person to read every page, and lately the hard decisions have all been of a single kind. Our faculty adviser has warned us that the administration reads the paper closely, that an outright error can be corrected and forgiven, but that a story which is technically accurate and still leaves readers with a false impression is the sort of thing that ends a paper's existence, and I have spent a while trying to think this through rather than just developing a feel for it. What I keep returning to is the distinction philosophers draw between lying and merely misleading: a lie is a false statement made with the intent to deceive, whereas a misleading statement can be perfectly true and still do the work of a lie by exploiting what the reader will assume. Out of that I have built a small vocabulary for our staff, and I would like you to test it and correct it if it is wrong. Every sentence, on this view, has an assertion (what the paper would actually be committing itself to if anyone challenged it), a steer (the belief it nudges an ordinary reader toward without ever asserting it), and an escape hatch (some reading, however strained, under which the sentence comes out true). My working hypothesis is that a sentence functions as a lie when its escape hatch is one no reasonable reader would ever take, and is merely misleading when a reasonable reader could have taken the honest reading but probably would not have.

Please turn this into a decision procedure that my section editors, who are sophomores with no philosophy background and about twenty minutes to spare on a Thursday night, could actually run at the copy desk. It should give the questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, mark the point where the procedure simply stops with a no, and mark the point where it has to hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than of what the sentence means — our adviser signs off on any correction, so I need to know which calls are hers and not mine. Then run the procedure on three sentences from recent issues. First, from our cafeteria contract story, the sentence "the district has not raised meal prices since 2019," which is true, printed directly under a headline about families struggling to afford lunch, in an issue that never mentions the district's 2022 cut to portion sizes. Second, from our schedule-change story, "the principal declined to comment," which is true only in the sense that nobody from the paper ever asked her. Third, my own headline from the last issue, "Board votes unanimously to keep the library open," where the vote was in fact unanimous among the four members present, with three absent. I suspect the procedure will tell me to pull my own headline, and I would rather you told me that plainly than softened it. I also want an honest account of where the vocabulary of assertion, steer and escape hatch breaks down — I already suspect that the principal sentence has no steer at all but a hole where a fact should be, and I do not know whether that is the same failure or a different one. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write a long email to a colleague.
