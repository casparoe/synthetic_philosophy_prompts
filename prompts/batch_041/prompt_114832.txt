What I keep running into is a pattern in how people argue from an extreme hypothetical, and I can't tell whether it has a name or whether I'm just seeing the same trick twice. The pattern is this. Someone wants to discredit an aggregative or consequentialist way of adding up what's good, and instead of arguing against the principle directly, they introduce a hypothetical creature defined entirely by one parameter pushed to an extreme. In one version the creature's capacity for the good in question is stipulated to be so enormous that the sum says we should hand it everything and let everyone else go without; the creature itself need not be described as happy or as deserving, only as capable of a very large amount of the relevant good. In another version the extreme isn't in any one creature's capacity but in how many creatures there are or could be, so that a vast number of lives barely worth living is said to outweigh a smaller number of excellent lives. In a third the extreme is in the quality of the lives being added, so that the arithmetic appears to favor adding lives that are, by the theory's own lights, miserable to the point of being worse than nothing. The surface verdicts differ, but underneath it seems to be one maneuver: find whichever quantity in the theory can be made arbitrarily large or arbitrarily small, drive it to its limit, and report that the theory now says something monstrous.

Here is what makes me unsure it's a single named thing. In the first version the absurdity comes from a being's *capacity* to be benefited; in the second and third it comes from *numbers* or from the *baseline quality* of lives, with no extraordinary being anywhere. And in some tellings the counterexample is meant to show the theory is false, while in others it is meant only to show that the theory's verdicts become counterintuitive, which the defender can accept as a bullet to bite rather than a refutation. I also notice that defenders have a stock reply -- that the extreme case is impossible, or physically unrealizable, or too improbable to matter for practice -- and I can't tell whether that reply is a legitimate restriction on which hypotheticals count or just a way of refusing the example.

So what I want is not a label for the first version alone. I want to know: is there an established concept or literature for this whole family of arguments, the ones that refute or embarrass a theory by taking one of its own quantities to a limit? If the first version has a name of its own, fine, but I care about the genus, not just that species. Tell me what the nearest established concepts are and how the thing I've described differs from each -- in particular, how it differs from a mere appeal to an intuition pump, from a reductio, and from the general complaint that a theory has absurd implications in far-fetched cases. Then tell me where the edges are. Does the pattern require that the quantity be genuinely unbounded, or does an arbitrarily large finite value do the same work? Does it count if the extreme case is metaphysically impossible but still conceivable, or only if it's possible? And is there a principled line between using such a case to *refute* a theory and using it only to *cost* the theory an intuitive verdict -- or is that distinction itself part of what needs naming? If nothing established covers the whole genus, coin a term for it, define it, and give me the boundary cases that test the definition, including at least one case that looks like the pattern but that you think should be excluded.
