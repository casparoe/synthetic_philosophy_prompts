I'm a science journalist — I write a weekly column about the places where research runs into philosophy — and for the last two years I've also sat on the grants committee of a small independent foundation that funds long-horizon work, which is how I've ended up with a vote on Thursday that I'm genuinely torn about. Our biggest grantee is a forecasting outfit that models resource constraints a few decades out. When they first came to us they did something I now realize was brave: they put a date on it. The proposal said, in substance, that if the land-and-fertiliser constraint they were tracking hadn't shown up in prices by 2019, the core mechanism was wrong. 2019 came and went, nothing showed up, and now their renewal says the mechanism is fine and the date was just early — the pressure is arriving in other places (lease values, fertiliser dependence, a couple of import bills they wave at us) — and they want three more years and more money. Half the committee says this is the oldest move in the book: a dated prediction that didn't happen is a failed prediction, and 'right but early' is a rescue you can run forever because you can always slide the date. The other half says the opposite, and I keep half-agreeing with them: a theory that sticks its neck out and misses on timing is doing something different from a theory that never stuck its neck out at all, and we'd be punishing exactly the behaviour we claim to want from forecasters. And honestly its keeping me up, because there's a third thing in their submission I can't classify: they say part of the reason the crunch didn't arrive on schedule is that two ministries read the earlier report and changed policy. Either the model was vindicated by its own consequences or it has quietly grown a shield, and I can't tell which.

Everyone on the committee keeps reaching for Malthus and it's driving me a little nuts, because the same example gets used for both sides. The sceptics: there's your cautionary tale, a man who said population would outrun food, missed, and whose name still gets stapled to every resource panic a century and a half later, which tells you something about how long a bad prediction can stay in circulation if it's vivid enough. The defenders: no, Malthus is the case of a claim that was right in structure and wrong in timing — the reason the constraint isn't visible is that we've spent two centuries living inside the answer to it, in yields and trade and the demographic transition — and you don't get to call that a failure just because the clock was off. I don't need the textbook lecture on falsification, I've read enough of that, and I'm definately not asking for a bibliography — nobody in that room wants footnotes. What I want is for someone to actually draw the line, in a way I could write into a grant decision: what separates a legitimate 'the timing was off' from a rescue that just protects the theory from ever being wrong? Is it that the auxiliary assumption being blamed — the date, the price channel, the technology that intervened — has to be independently testable on its own? Is it that the rescue has to predict something new rather than absorb something old? And what do I do with the reflexivity argument: if the model never predicted that its own report would move ministry policy, is the policy change evidence for the model, or is it a new theory wearing the old one's clothes? I have half-formed opinions about all of this and I'd rather have someone who has thought about it tell me where I'm wrong.

So: draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. I need a concrete recommendation — renew as proposed, renew with conditions, or decline — with a timeframe attached (a date, an observable, whatever the right trigger is), the reasoning that gets me there, and a serious engagement with the strongest version of the case I'm not making; the sceptics are not idiots, and if you strawman them I'll get eaten alive in the room. Tell me plainly which of my questions can't be settled on the evidence in front of us. Then, because twelve people will skim this, close with a JSON object with exactly these fields and nothing else in it: recommendation (one of 'renew as proposed', 'renew with conditions', 'decline'); bottom_line (the recommendation in one sentence, plain enough to read aloud); review_trigger (the concrete date plus observable event at which we should look at this again); conditions (an array, each phrased so it could go verbatim into the renewal letter); pre_committed_falsifiers (an array of things the group must state now, in advance, that would count against the core mechanism — the kind of statement they'd rather not make); verdict_on_right_but_early (one of 'legitimate', 'illegitimate', 'undecidable on this evidence', plus one sentence of why); verdict_on_the_policy_change_argument (one or two sentences on whether 'our report changed policy' is evidence for the model or a shield, and what would settle it); strongest_case_against (the best argument for the option you didn't pick, in your own words, no hedging); what_would_change_your_mind (the specific evidence that would flip your recommendation); and unresolved (an array of questions you can't answer on what's in front of you).
