I serve on the allocation committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds long-horizon, high-variance work, and at Thursday's meeting we vote on a proposal from one of our program officers that I have agreed to write the opinion on. She wants us to adopt what she calls evidential cooperation — cooperation that runs through evidence rather than through any causal channel. Concretely, she proposes a standing rule setting aside a fixed share of each year's budget (she says five percent) for expenditures whose payoff depends on other agents taking similar actions, where those agents are ones we cannot contact, bargain with, monitor, or even identify, and where nothing we do can affect what they do. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it, runs like this: the universe is large enough that there very likely exist many other decision-makers whose deliberations resemble ours; my choosing a policy is therefore evidence about how such agents choose, since in choosing I am sampling from the class of decisions made by agents like me; so the expected value of cooperating can be substantial even with agents whose existence I will never confirm and whose conduct I cannot influence. She has even lined up a first expenditure under the rule — a pool of money released only if a dozen or so other institutions make matching commitments, though she is happy to say the relevant others may include alien civilizations and future machine agents, which is roughly where our treasurer stopped taking notes. The treasurer thinks this is a category error dressed in decision theory, the chair finds it inspiring, and the rest of us would like to know whether we are being asked to spend real money on a proof of our own virtue.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. I need a clear bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — where any conditions are concrete enough to drop into the motion, including what the money would actually be spent on, what would count as the rule's failing, and whether and when we should revisit it; and I need reasons the program officer could engage with rather than dismiss. Be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side; the treasurer is not a fool and I would rather not paper over his objection. Two things in particular I want addressed, because they are where my own thinking stalls. First, whether "my choice is evidence about theirs" survives when I cannot specify the reference class of similar agents: what is supposed to fix that class, and how much of the argument's force is lost if the class is vague, contested, or might contain only us? Second, whether this is anything more than Kantian universalizability with a probability estimate attached, and whether that difference matters for a decision about an actual budget. If the argument is sound, tell me what would break it in practice and how we should guard against that; if it is not, tell me what a defensible version of the program officer's proposal would look like, since I would rather amend the motion than kill it. Please write in American English.
