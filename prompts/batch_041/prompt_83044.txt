I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on whether large language models genuinely understand the language they produce, and I want the criteria of assessment fixed before anyone in the room starts issuing verdicts. You are a philosopher of mind and language who works on the foundations of artificial intelligence. Write me the specification: what a theory of linguistic understanding would have to deliver in order to settle, rather than presuppose, the question of whether a system trained only to predict the next stretch of text — with no senses, no effectors, and no commerce with the world beyond a corpus — understands the language it handles. Think the problem through before you commit to anything; I would rather have six requirements that survive scrutiny than twenty that do not.

The standard is adequacy for adjudicating arguments of the kind that actually get made in this dispute, which means adequacy for cases and results, not for the literature. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or attributions to settle anything; I cannot check them and I do not need them, and a requirement that only bites because some authority held it is not a requirement.

What I want, in order: first, the requirements themselves, each stated so that I could tell whether a candidate theory satisfies it or not, and each accompanied by the case, result, or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste. Second, a verdict on the list — which of these are non-negotiable for any theory worth the name, and which are commitments of one camp dressed up as neutral criteria. Third, and this is the part I care most about, whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, with the argument for why, since a clean impossibility result would be worth more to the seminar than any verdict on the models.

The terrain the theory has to cover, as I see it, includes at least the following, though I expect you to tell me which of these are genuine constraints and which are distractions. Behaviour: the production and interpretation of sentences no one has written before, paraphrase and translation, and the correction of a term's use in light of new information. Failure patterns: answers that flip when an irrelevant detail is changed or the premises are reordered; systems that will affirm that one thing entails another but not the converse; acute sensitivity to phrasing and formatting. The comparison cases: a person who learned a language only from texts and never from use, and a person blind from birth who handles colour vocabulary competently — the theory must say whether it is entitled to treat these differently from the model, and if so on what principled ground. The Chinese Room case, which the theory must adjudicate rather than dodge, including the tangle about whether the thing that fails to understand is the person, the room, or the whole arrangement. The individuation problem: whether the bearer of understanding is the model by itself, the model together with its prompt, the model with retrieval and tools attached, or the model with a human in the loop, since the intuitive answer shifts across these and the theory has to pick. Substrate neutrality, which seems required if the theory is not to beg the question against silicon, together with whatever prevents the same permissiveness from admitting a lookup table or a thermostat. Gradedness: whether understanding comes in degrees, and whether the theory can distinguish competence in a narrow register from competence in language as such. The training signal: whether next-token prediction is the wrong kind of process to yield understanding in principle, and what would have to be true for it not to matter. Counterfactual tracking: the thought that a system understands a term only if its use of it would vary appropriately with the world, and whether that can be stated without smuggling in causal contact. And the distinction between retrieving a memorized answer and predicting the outcome of a setup the system has never seen described.

Then, briefly, how the main families of view do against the finished list: behaviourist or dispositional accounts, grounding-first accounts, functionalist and computationalist accounts, degree-theoretic accounts, and the deflationary line that the question is ill-posed or interest-relative. Keep this part short; the criteria are the deliverable.

Be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus — where a case or a distinction is disputed, say so and say what the dispute turns on. Flag anything you are unsure of rather than smoothing it over.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered list set out as a list, no bold, no tables. If you need to refer back to a requirement, name it in the sentence.
