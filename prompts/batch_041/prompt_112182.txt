I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on self-reference and the self, and for the next session I want a model dialogue that my students can pull apart: a written exchange between two philosophers, both of whom are good at their jobs and neither of whom is a strawman, each trying to get the other to give up a position that is genuinely defensible. Please write that dialogue.

The first speaker defends the account Hofstadter builds out of self-reference. On this account the self is a strange loop: a symbol within a symbol system that ends up standing for the very system that contains and produces it, a level-crossing pattern in which the system's top level turns back on its own bottom level. He insists that the resulting self is not a soul, not a ghost and not a mere manner of speaking, but a pattern that is real at its own level in the way that the temperature of a gas is real over and above the molecules, and that it exerts genuine causal influence downwards on the level beneath it, so that the self, rather than being an idle shadow cast by the neurons, is part of the reason the body does what it does. He also holds that the loop must be grounded — anchored all the way down in the machinery — and that this is what distinguishes a strange loop from a merely vicious tangle of self-reference; and he thinks selves come in degrees, with no sharp threshold, so that a dog has some of a self, an insect very little, and a person's self is partly composed of the selves of those they have been close to, so that a portion of one person's self can survive inside another's head.

The second speaker grants the whole mechanism and denies the conclusion. She is happy to agree that we model ourselves, that the modelling feeds back into what we do, and that this is indispensable for explaining human behaviour; what she denies is that any of it delivers a self over and above the causal structure. Her objection is not the lazy one that the strange loop is a metaphor. It is that the strange loop is a shape that causal structures can take, and calling a particular shape a self does no explanatory work: either the grounding condition is trivial, in which case thermostats and self-referential sentences qualify, or it is demanding, in which case she wants to know why that is the right demand rather than an arbitrary one. She also has a positive account of her own to offer, not merely objections — something to the effect that the self is a regulatory construct we cannot do without, whose reality is practical rather than ontological, and that this is enough to explain everything the realist wanted explained.

Now make them argue. Each must answer the other's actual point rather than sliding past it, and each must be allowed their best resources. At a minimum the exchange should reach the grounding condition and whether it does any work; the degrees-of-self claim, and whether a notion with no threshold can support a realist claim at all; whether the self's causal influence on the neurons amounts to anything over and above the microphysical story, or is either identical with it or superfluous; the suggestion that one person's self can be partly instantiated in another's head, and whether that shows the self is a portable pattern or shows that the concept has gone slack; and the worry that a symbol standing for the system that contains it must be read by something, which threatens either a homunculus or an infinite regress. Give me at least five exchanges from each side, keep each speech short enough to be read aloud — a paragraph or two — and let each speaker make one concession that genuinely costs them something. Do not resolve the dispute tidily: end with the disagreement sharper than it began, or with a reformulation from one side that the other immediately identifies as a retreat. Invent names for the two of them, and include no footnotes, citations or quotations; I want the arguments, not the literature.
