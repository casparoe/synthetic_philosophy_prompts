I write the ideas column for a monthly magazine, and I'm assembling a long piece on the old quarrel between the great-man view of history and structural explanation — the quarrel that always circles back to the counterfactual of whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. For a sidebar, I want a decision tree that readers can actually work through, so that they arrive at a named position instead of the vague intuition they began with. Please build it as a flowchart of five to eight branching questions, each stated as a concrete claim or a small historical case that a reader can answer without already knowing which label it leads to — nothing on the order of do you think individuals or structures drive history. The leaves should be the positions themselves, each with a one-line statement of its central commitment and a sentence naming its most awkward implication, and the branch points should be questions on which the positions genuinely come apart rather than a roster of names in disguise. The magazine reaches a general but well-educated readership, so keep to positions and labels a reader would recognize from ordinary intellectual life, and mark a combination of answers as unnamed where it corresponds to a view nobody has actually defended.

I would like the tree built from the logical space rather than from the roll of famous historians, and the nodes I care most about are these: whether the counterfactual question is even the sort of thing that has a determinate answer, or whether asking it presupposes a false picture of how events are individuated; whether a regime of this kind can survive a change of occupant — that is, whether Nazi Germany is identified by its leader or by its structures; whether an individual's contribution is substitutable, in the sense that the structural role would have been filled by the next available candidate with broadly the same result; and whether the leader's idiosyncratic psychology, as opposed to the office, carries any of the explanatory weight. I have been using three rough labels in my own notes, and I would rather you test them against the tree than assume them: displacement, where a substitute occupies the same slot and the outcome is broadly reproduced; diversion, where the structural pressures produce a different but comparably awful outcome; and disruption, where removing the individual changes the trajectory so thoroughly that the later outcome is not recognizable as the same event. Please anchor the branches in a handful of small counterfactuals a reader can hold in mind — the leader is killed in the 1923 putsch; a rival faction wins the internal purge of June 1934 and the party goes another way; the Reichstag fire never happens and the emergency powers are never granted; the assassination plots that came close in the late 1930s succeed. The leaves should include at least the view that the individual was decisive, the view that the structural slot was decisive, the view that the outcome was overdetermined, the view that the counterfactual has no determinate answer, and the view that the question is malformed because the regime is individuated by its leader. Where a node cannot separate two positions, say so rather than inventing a distinction; where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. No reading lists, citations, or attributions, please — I am after the anatomy of the disagreement, not its bibliography, and I would like the whole thing short enough to run as a printed sidebar.
