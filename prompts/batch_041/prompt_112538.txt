You are a philosopher of mind and a philosophical psychologist with a specialization in the phenomenology of pain, hedonic valence, and the conceptual foundations of welfare. Work carefully and self-critically; do not rush to a neat diagram. I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on whether pain is bad in virtue of what it feels like, and I want a classification instrument rather than an essay.

The instrument should be a decision tree that begins from a single starting commitment the reader already accepts — that pain asymbolia is a real clinical phenomenon, i.e. that after certain brain lesions a patient can sincerely report feeling pain while denying that it hurts or bothers them, and can fail to withdraw from or otherwise show the usual affective response to the noxious stimulus — and leads that reader, through a series of diagnostic questions, to a named position on why pain is bad, or on whether there is a single fact of the matter about that.

Before drawing the tree, brainstorm in a few lines: enumerate the candidate loci of pain's badness (the sensory experience itself; its affective or hedonic quality; the subject's desire, aversion, or caring; the body's demand for action; the evaluative content the experience carries), and note which of these the asymbolia case puts pressure on. Then build.

Requirements for the instrument:

1. Each internal node must be phrased as a question the reader can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to — a concrete case, a claim, or a distinction, not "do you think pain is intrinsically bad?". Every node must be one on which the downstream positions genuinely come apart.
2. Use 8 to 12 internal nodes and 8 to 10 leaves. Each leaf carries a descriptive name for the position plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the case or intuition it handles worst.
3. The tree must include a node on the distinction between a pain's being bad and its being bad for someone, and must place the positions that separate at that node.
4. At least one leaf must be a position on which asymbolic patients are in a state that is genuinely bad but not bad for anyone, and at least one must be a deflationary position on which the question "is the pain bad?" is either ambiguous or empty. Include the view that pain's badness consists in its issuing a demand for protective action, and the view that asymbolia is not a case of pain at all.
5. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the positions in it. Where a combination is unoccupied, mark it and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
6. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say why rather than papering over it.
7. Where you attach a proper name to a position, do so only if you are confident of the attribution; otherwise give a descriptive label. Do not invent citations, dates, or attributions.

Close with a short epistemic note: state which two or three nodes you are least confident carve the space correctly, which of your leaves you suspect is doing the most violence to the position it names, and what argument or empirical finding would force you to redraw a branch — i.e. what would change your mind about the tree's shape.
