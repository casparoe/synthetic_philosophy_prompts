I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a manuscript where a character — a behavioral geneticist, or rather the fictionalized composite of one — has to deliver an argument that I find uncomfortably persuasive, and I want to know precisely how much weight it can bear. The argument runs like this. Across a great many countries, the more egalitarian a society is on the usual indices — economic opportunity, political representation, educational access — the more sharply men and women sort into different occupations, different fields of study, and different self-reported preferences, with the widest gaps appearing in the most egalitarian places. My character's conclusion is that once the cultural and institutional barriers are lifted, the underlying dispositions are finally free to express themselves, and that the persistence and widening of the sorting is therefore evidence for a substantial innate component rather than against one. I have been living with this argument for months and have not been able to shake it; I would like you to help me see exactly where it is exposed. Tell me what would count against it — not the general gesture that culture is complicated, but the specific empirical patterns, measurement failures, alternative explanations, and possible cases that would genuinely undermine the inference, sorted from those that would be close to fatal down to those that would be merely awkward.

I would also like to understand how the argument looks from inside its own defense. Which of these considerations would a committed proponent of my character's inference concede as counting against it, and which would they simply wave away — and is there a version of the argument that survives every concession worth making? I am especially interested in whether the inference from a cross-national correlation to a claim about innate dispositions is doing real explanatory work or has been quietly qualified into something no evidence could touch; if it is insulated, I need to know whether that is a defect of the argument or merely a fact about the kind of claim it is. Include, please, the points that matter most to the two halves of the story I am trying to tell: the measurement half (what exactly is being compared when we compare gender gaps across countries, and whether the standard equality indices capture the thing the argument needs them to capture) and the inference half (what has to be assumed for a larger gap in a freer society to license a conclusion about nature rather than about the particular freedoms and constraints that rich, egalitarian societies happen to distribute). Finally, tell me honestly which single consideration you would find most persuasive if you were the one who had to be moved, and why — I would rather have the one that cuts deepest than a tidy list.
