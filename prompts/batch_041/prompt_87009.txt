Alright, I need help writing a toast and this is the one place I can ask where people will actually get it.

My best friend is retiring next month and I'm giving the toast at his dinner. Background: he is the doomsday argument guy. Not in a gloomy way — he's the most cheerful person I know — but the self-sampling thing has been his running bit at parties for twenty years and at this point its basically his personality. I've been reading LessWrong since like 2010, we met at a meetup arguing about exactly this, so the friendship is kinda built on it. Quick version for anyone who's forgotten: you should reason as if you're a random one of all the people who will ever live, and since you're here this early in the sequence there probally aren't all that many more of us coming. That's it. He loves it.

Here's the tricky part. The room is split. Half are our meetup people who know this cold and will catch everything. The other half are his family and the guys from his old office, who've never heard any of it and will glaze over the second I say reference class. Also his mom will be there, and his daughter made me promise I would not make it about the end of the world — she'll panic. So no doomsday, no extinction, no humanity dying out, none of that language.

What I actually want the toast to do is run the argument on the room, live, without ever naming it. Get everyone to feel, for about ten seconds, that tonight is a random draw from all the dinners this group will ever have, and that this is somehow supposed to tell them how many are left. Then pull the rug out with his own objection, which he calls the everything-is-almost-over problem: the same move says you're near the end of your marriage, near the end of your job, near the end of this book, near the end of tonight, and the move only ever feels compelling as long as you don't notice you're making it about a hundred sequences at once. If one trick puts you at the end of everything simultaneously, it isn't telling you anything about any one thing. He thinks that's the whole ballgame and honestly I do too.

So: five minutes, warm and funny, no lecture, nobody needs to know what a prior is. But it has to actually do the argument rather than reference it — his family should be able to state the logic themselves by dessert. And the objection has to land as a gift to him, not a takedown. Write me the toast, then give me a short list of which sentences are load-bearing so I can trim if I'm running long.
