A seminar-mate defended a claim about war with a one-liner I can't get out of my head: war keeps a people from going soft. When pressed, she said it was the Hegelian point that a nation's ethical health needs the friction of conflict the way seawater needs wind, and that a long peace leaves a people stagnant and corrupt. Two other people immediately agreed, one of them adding that this is why a state that never fights eventually loses the loyalty of its citizens. I suspect at least three different claims are being run together, and that at least one of them is doing all the real work while the others are decoration.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Take the slogan as she stated it — prolonged peace degrades a people, war restores or preserves its ethical health — and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I care about at least these axes of variation, and you should add any others that genuinely cut:

- What is supposedly kept healthy: the moral character of individual citizens, the vigor of a state's institutions, the solidarity or "ethical substance" binding a people together, or the state's status as a genuine actor in a world of other states that must recognize it.
- Whether war is claimed to be causally necessary, merely causally useful, or only revelatory — a test that shows what a people already is rather than something that makes it better.
- Whether the claim is about war as such, about the threat of war, about permanent mobilization short of fighting, or about the specific experience of being defeated or of winning.
- Whether "health" is a functional metaphor (the people keeps working as a people) or a moral predicate (the people is better, and its members more virtuous).
- Whether the claim survives if you hold the scale fixed: a small polity with a permanent external threat that never materializes, versus a large one that fights a limited war every generation.

To force the readings apart, please include a diagnostic case for each one — a scenario in which that reading predicts something the adjacent readings deny. Use at least these three cases, and invent more if they help: (i) a state that maintains total military readiness and constant external tension for a century but never actually fights; (ii) a state that fights a long, indecisive, casualty-heavy war and emerges materially ruined but with its institutions and civic loyalty intact; (iii) a state that never fights but whose citizens remain highly mobilized around a non-military collective project. Say for each reading what it predicts in each case, and flag any pair of readings that your cases cannot separate, with a diagnosis of why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Also tell me which readings are platitudes dressed as profundity, which are substantive and defensible on their own terms, which are straightforwardly false, and which reading the seminar-mate most likely meant given what she was using the line to do. Note explicitly where a reading only works if you smuggle in a particular theory of what a "people" is or a particular theory of state recognition, since I think that is where the real disagreement is hiding.

Format the answer as a single JSON object with these fields:

- "readings": an array of objects, each with "id", "label", "precise_claim" (one or two sentences, no hedging), "scope" (what the health predicate attaches to), "modal_status" (necessary / useful / revelatory / other), "status" (one of "platitude", "contentious-but-defensible", "false", "unclear"), "diagnostic_case_predictions" (an object keyed by case name, each value one sentence), "nearest_neighbor" (an id), "separated_by" (the case that distinguishes them, or "not separated" plus a reason), "supports_seminar_use" (boolean), and "supports_seminar_use_explanation".
- "unoccupied_combinations": an array of objects with "combination" and "diagnosis".
- "summary": an object with "most_likely_intended" (a reading id), "most_defensible" (a reading id), "most_trivial" (a reading id), and "hidden_commitment" (a sentence naming the theory of peoplehood or recognition the argument needs).

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I want the conceptual map, and I'd rather you reason it out than gesture at sources.
