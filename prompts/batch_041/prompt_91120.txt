I teach a graduate seminar in metaphysics, and the unit on modality founders every year in the same way. I set out counterpart theory and the rival view on which a single individual simply exists in more than one possible world, and within two sessions the room has split into two camps that talk past each other for three weeks, each convinced the other is confused; the students leave thinking the whole dispute is a turf war about notation. I have decided the fault is in how I stage it. This year I want to open the unit not with a debate but with the document that two honest partisans could have produced if they had spent a semester working together in good faith: a joint statement that a committed counterpart theorist and a committed defender of genuine transworld identity could both sign. I will circulate it as a model, so it has to survive both readings. A counterpart theorist should find their own position stated in it and should not be able to complain that the author has quietly assumed the rival view; the same, symmetrically, for the defender of transworld identity. The document must not adjudicate between them, and it must not be so hedged that it says nothing. I am not asking for a survey of the literature and I do not want citations or a reading list; the interest is in the two positions themselves and in how far two people who hold them can actually get.

Two things I would like the statement to take seriously, because I suspect they are where the real work lies. First, I have a hunch that a good deal of the apparent disagreement is not about how things stand in other worlds at all, but about how to read the formalism and about what work the word identity is being asked to do; if part of the dispute is verbal, I want that part identified and the residual, substantive part made exact. Second, there are two cases I have been chewing on, and I would like the joint statement to say in advance what each side predicts about them, so that my students can see what is genuinely at stake. The first is what I have been calling the violinist case: a colleague of mine at twenty-five insisted, with reasons, that she could have been a professional cellist; at fifty-five, her hands ruined and forty years of not practising behind her, she says with equal conviction that she could never have been one. The second is a counting case: take a world qualitatively just like ours except that two people who are strangers here — call them A and B, whose lives never touch — have swapped their entire histories, so that the qualitative pattern A's life has here is played there by a person occupying B's position, and conversely; then ask how many people in that world are A. I expect the two sides to say very different things about the violinist case, and to find the counting case uncomfortable in different ways, but I may be wrong about which of these is a crux, so say so if I am.

Please structure the response as follows. First, a Framing section of about a hundred words on what the dispute is about, stated so that neither side would object to the description. Second, an Agreed claims section: a numbered list of roughly ten single-sentence claims both sides can sign, running from the formal to the interpretive — what the modal operators do, what does and does not follow from the necessity of identity and the rigidity of names, what each view is committed to denying, and what neither view needs to deny. Third, a Residual disagreements section in the form of a table, one row per issue, with a neutral name for the issue in the first column and then, in separate columns, the counterpart theorist's position and the transworld identity theorist's position, each written in wording its own side would accept. Fourth, a Cruxes section: for each of the two or three sub-questions that actually separate them, say what would move each side, and record each side's prediction about the violinist case and the counting case before either side has a chance to rationalise the result. Fifth, a Verbal or substantive section, saying which disagreements are terminological, which are not, and why. Sixth, a Bottom line section of about a hundred words on how much is really at stake for a reader who is not already invested in the dispute.
