A friend and I run a small decision-theory reading group, and we have now spent five sessions failing to make any headway on the St. Petersburg game. The mechanics are not in dispute: a fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads, and if that happens on toss n you receive 2^n units, so the expected payoff is the infinite sum of one unit per toss. Nor is anyone's willingness to play in dispute: none of us would pay more than a few units for a ticket. What astonishes me is that the disagreement survives everyone having stated their view in full. One member holds that the paradox dissolves the moment you notice that utility must be bounded, so the expected utility of the game is finite after all. A second holds that the real work is done by feasibility: no counterparty has infinite reserves and no one can be paid across an infinitely long run of trials, so the game as described is not an object that can be instantiated, and the puzzle is a fact about an impossible case. A third takes the game to show that expected-value maximization is simply the wrong theory of rational choice, and that a primitive aversion to low-probability catastrophes, or to variance, should replace it. A fourth insists the expected value really is infinite and that our reluctance to play is a psychological fact about us rather than a normative fact about the game. A fifth suspects the trouble lies in the probability space itself: that an infinite sequence of independent fair tosses is not a well-defined object, or that the relevant expectation is undefined rather than infinite. Nobody has shifted an inch.

What I want is not a verdict on who is right but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this one dispute, or several questions travelling under a single name — a question in the mathematics of infinite expectations, a question about which utility function we actually have, a question about whether an unrealizable case can constrain a theory of rationality, and a question about what a rational agent is required to do? My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that a good part of the impasse is verbal: some of us mean by the value of the game its expected payoff and others mean what it would be rational to pay, and once those are separated the residue is a narrower but genuine dispute about bounded utility and about whether infeasibility is a legitimate idealization constraint. If that hypothesis is wrong, say what the disagreement actually is, and be specific about which of the five positions are answering different questions rather than contradicting one another. I would also like the diagnosis to earn its keep on three variants we invented ourselves, none of which I have seen discussed: (a) a truncated game in which the counterparty's reserves are finite but so large that the truncation point lies beyond any number of trials anyone could plausibly run; (b) a game whose prize is, by stipulation, a good the player values linearly, with no diminishing marginal utility, so that bounded-utility replies are unavailable by construction; and (c) an epistemic variant in which each toss doubles the number of independent pieces of evidence you would receive for some hypothesis, and you must decide whether to commit to believing it now. For each variant, tell me whether your diagnosis predicts that the paradox survives, and what that reveals about which of the five positions is doing the explanatory work.

Finally, be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on the topic and which are genuinely contested; do not present a contested position as the consensus, and flag any point where you are reconstructing a view rather than reporting one. If you think the whole question is shallower than we take it to be, say so and give your reasons, with some indication of how confident you are and what would change your mind.
