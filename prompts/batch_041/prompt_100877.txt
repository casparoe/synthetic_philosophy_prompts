I've been sitting on our hospital's ethics committee for about six weeks now—I got drafted because I teach intro bio and they needed a non-clinician—and its been a crash course in moral philosophy. Last month we had to allocate two ECMO circuits during a surge. The committee went with the rule that saves the most lives, so five patients got them over one. I was fine with that until a colleague, who reads alot of philosophy, said our rule proves too much. He wrote out this argument on the whiteboard and now I can't stop chewing on it. I'd like you to help me figure out which step to give up and what it costs.

His argument:

(1) If the number of lives saved is a morally relevant consideration, then saving five lives is morally better than saving one life, all else equal.
(2) If saving five lives is morally better than saving one life, then, all else equal, we ought to save five rather than one when we can.
(3) If we ought to save five rather than one when we can, then we ought to bring about the five-saved outcome even when the only available way to do it is to kill one innocent person and use their organs to save the five.
(4) We ought not to kill one innocent person to save five.
(5) So the number of lives saved is not a morally relevant consideration.

I can't accept (5). I mean, I really can't—it would mean the ECMO decision was a coin flip, and I don't believe that. But (4) seems rock solid, and I don't see how to deny (1) without saying numbers don't matter at all. My colleague says the argument is airtight and that anyone who wants numbers to count has to give up (4). I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying. Also, if I reject a premise, what do I say about cases where the only way to save five is to redirect a runaway trolley into one? That seems different, but I need to know why.

Please give me:
- The weak link (about 150 words): name the step you'd reject and state precisely what is wrong with it.
- Why it looked fine (about 200 words): explain why that step seemed plausible, and why the argument is tempting even if it's flawed.
- The price list (about 300 words): for each of the other premises, say what it would cost to give it up instead, and what you'd have to accept. Include whether there's a reading of the argument on which it's valid and the real question is which premise is least costly.
- Where I land (about 100 words): your verdict on whether numbers count, and what our committee should say the next time someone brings this up.

A couple of things: don't give me a bibliography or cite papers—I can't look anything up right now and I don't want names I'll just nod along to. Just make the distinctions do the work. Also, I'm not a philosopher, so keep the jargon to a minimum, but don't dumb it down.
