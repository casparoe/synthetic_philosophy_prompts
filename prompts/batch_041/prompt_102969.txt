For the better part of forty years I did experimental work in the life sciences, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology in earnest — somewhat late in the day, I admit, and with the autodidact's habit of wanting every dispute cashed out in terms I can actually get a grip on. I keep returning to the argument that Gould and Lewontin made against what they called the adaptationist programme: the charge that biologists too readily treat every trait as an adaptation and neglect the alternatives, namely developmental and phylogenetic constraint, drift, and traits that are mere byproducts of other traits — their spandrels. I am largely sympathetic to the charge, and yet I have never been able to see precisely where the two sides are supposed to disagree, because it seems to me that the critic's assertion ("this trait is a byproduct, not an adaptation") is no easier to falsify than the adaptationist's assertion that it is an adaptation. If that is right, then the quarrel is not really about testability at all but about the base rate one assigns to adaptation as against byproduct, and hence about what it is reasonable to assume when the evidence is thin.

I would like you to write a dialogue in which this suspicion is worked out rather than merely announced. The two speakers should be an unrepentant adaptationist and a critic in the Gould–Lewontin tradition — equally sharp, neither of them a strawman, each genuinely answering the other's last point rather than restating a position. Let the exchange move from the familiar accusations (Panglossianism, unfalsifiability, the neglect of constraint and of non-adaptive alternatives) toward the deeper question of whether their disagreement is at bottom empirical — a dispute about frequencies in nature — or conceptual, a dispute about what "adaptation" means and about what is required to explain a trait. Have them use one concrete trait as a running example — the human chin, if you like, or another you find apt — but do not lean on the literature or citations; I want the structure of the argument, not a bibliography. Let the dialogue end without a forced victor, but with each speaker naming precisely what would have to be true for the other to be right.
