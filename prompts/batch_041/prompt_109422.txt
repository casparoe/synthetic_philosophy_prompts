An old puzzle about letterforms asks what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Two answers are on offer. The structural answer holds that a shape is an 'a' just when its skeleton has the right abstract structure — a closed counter joined to a stem, a particular junction pattern and terminal count — so that identity is discrete and all-or-nothing, and legibility should collapse at a sharp threshold: break the counter and the 'a' is gone. The prototype answer holds that there is no invariant core: 'a'-ness is graded similarity to a learned central tendency in a perceptual feature space, with the features and their weights themselves learned from a community's writing and reading, so that each letter has something like a deformation budget — unlimited slack along some dimensions, almost none along others. Compare the two accounts and adjudicate between them on three phenomena: (a) the instant legibility of a wildly stylized 'a'; (b) the fact that a tiny deformation can destroy legibility while a large one leaves it intact; and (c) the fact that the 'a' of one script or era can look almost nothing like the 'a' of another, yet both count as the same letter. Then say whether the accounts are genuine rivals or merely partial descriptions of one process, and whether either needs a further ingredient — for instance, that a letter's identity is a social or historical convention that perception merely tracks, rather than a fact about form or about perception. Format the response as four titled sections: 'The two accounts' (~200 words), 'The three phenomena' (~300 words), 'Rivals or complements' (~250 words), 'Verdict' (~150 words).
