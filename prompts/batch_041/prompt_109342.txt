I am trying to get clear about a family of arguments that invert the direction of explanation — arguments in which a competence of some kind is accounted for without any comprehension, and in which the vocabulary of comprehension nonetheless proves very hard to give up. Darwin's treatment of natural selection and Turing's treatment of machine intelligence are the two cases I keep returning to, and I have hit a snag on the Darwin passage.

Here is the passage (Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859, chapter 4):

"How will the struggle for existence, discussed too briefly in the last chapter, act in regard to variation? ... Can it, then, be thought improbable, seeing that variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, that other variations useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life, should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations? If such do occur, can we doubt (remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive) that individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind? On the other hand, we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed. This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

What I need help with:

First, the last sentence has the form of a naming or a stipulation: Darwin attaches a label to a preservation and a rejection. But the sentences before it read as an argument, and their apparent conclusion is that advantageous variations will be preserved. If that conclusion is already packed into the name, where exactly does the argumentative work get done, and is there a circularity in the step from "would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind" to "this preservation of favourable variations ... I call Natural Selection"?

Second, "preservation," "rejection," and "rigidly destroyed" import agency: preserving and rejecting are things agents do. Darwin's point, as I understand it, is that no agent is required — the outcome is a residue of differential survival. So is the agency-laden idiom a dispensable expository convenience, or does it carry explanatory content that a neutral redescription such as "differential reproduction" loses? If it is dispensable, why has the personified idiom proved so stubborn?

Third, I want the comparison with Turing made carefully rather than by slogan. In the machine case, a question about an inner state — thinking — is displaced onto a question about performance in a game. In the Darwin case, a question about design — how did this fit arise? — is displaced onto a question about the differential survival of variants. In both, something that appears to require comprehension is accounted for by a process that does not comprehend anything. But I suspect the parallel breaks: in Turing's case the comprehension is precisely what is in dispute, whereas in Darwin's case it is the designer's comprehension that is being explained away, and the fit itself is not in dispute. Is that the right place to locate the disanalogy, or does the real difference lie elsewhere — for instance, in whether the displacing question functions as a criterion for the original question or as a replacement for it?

Please work through the passage closely and in its own terms before bringing in the comparison. I am not asking for a survey of the secondary literature and I will supply references myself. Say clearly where the passage is doing something that a modern paraphrase obscures, and where the modern paraphrase is simply an improvement.
