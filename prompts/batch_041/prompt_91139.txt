I've been going back and forth on the definiton of death alot lately and I keep sliding between two senses of one word, and I'd like someone to actually lay out the conceptual issue for me rather than give me the standard brain-death-vs-cardiac-death rundown.

Take two ICU patients. First one: whole brain gone, brainstem included, ventilator doing the breathing. Everyone agrees she's dead. But the reason we can't bring her back is that we don't have the tech — nobody thinks there's a law of nature stopping us from regrowing a brainstem, we just can't do it yet. So when we call that loss 'irreversible,' we seem to mean 'can't be reversed with what we've got.' Second one: heart stopped, and everyone in the room knows perfectly well we could restart it, we've simply decided not to, because the family called a halt or because the transplant team is standing by. A few minutes later we declare death and wheel him off. Here 'irreversible' doesn't mean can't, it means won't, and what makes it true isn't a fact about his body, it's a decision somebody made.

Someone in my reading group floated that we should drop 'irreversible' and say 'permanent' instead — permanent meaning the loss isn't going to be reversed, because nobody is going to try — and that this treats both patients the same way. Sounded neat to me for about a day, then it started to feel like it was smuggling in a judgment about when it's okay to stop trying.

So: is irreversibility one concept here or two? If the whole-brain criterion leans on 'we can't, yet,' does that make brain death a moving target that will get revised as medicine improves, and is that a bug or just how it works? And how can the definition of death be partly a matter of what we've decided to do, when it's supposed to be a fact about an organism — or is the idea that it's a plain biological fact the thing that's confused in the first place?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no summing up at the end.
