I am a high school senior, and I have been asked to deliver the keynote address at our school's first Long-Term Futures Symposium. The audience will be roughly two hundred students, a dozen teachers, and a handful of guests from the local university, some of whom work on decision theory and artificial intelligence. I want to give a speech about two ideas I have been trying to understand on my own: acausal threats -- threats that cannot causally reach the person or system being threatened, because there is no channel through which the threat could be delivered or enforced -- and surrogate goals -- goals that an agent adopts not because it ultimately cares about them, but because they are easier to bargain over or because they make the agent's commitments more credible. The speech should make these ideas vivid for an audience that mostly has not heard of them, without turning into a lecture. To do that, I have invented a story I want you to use as the spine of the speech: two rival high school robotics teams, never in the same room, each knows the other is running a detailed simulation of the other's reasoning. Team A's captain leaves a note in a shared digital folder that Team B's simulation is known to scan; the note says that if Team B's simulation does not recommend conceding the design award, Team A's simulation of Team B will be penalized. Team B's human members never read the note; only their simulation does. The speech should use this story to ask whether a threat can be real when it can reach only a simulation, and whether adopting a surrogate goal -- say, caring about the design award only because it gives the other team's simulation something to bargain over -- is a clever path to cooperation or a form of self-betrayal. It should be roughly seven minutes, formal but not stiff, and it should not quote or cite any philosophers, papers, or studies; the ideas should stand on their own. The audience includes people who will know the technical terms, so the speech should earn those terms rather than lean on them. Please write the speech.
