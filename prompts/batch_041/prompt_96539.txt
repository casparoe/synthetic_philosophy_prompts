I sit on the policy team at a company deploying a customer-facing assistant, and I am drafting the honesty section of its behavior standard. Our lawyers have convinced most of the team that what we are really writing is an accuracy standard: deception requires an intention to deceive, lying requires a belief that what one says is false, and our model has neither intentions nor beliefs, so it is capable of neither. The model does emit things that read as assertions, and users plainly treat them as such, but on the lawyers' view an output containing no false assertion cannot be dishonest, and a reply that is true while leaving the user with a false impression is a matter of user experience rather than of honesty. Our red team keeps producing exactly that category: the reply that names a real study but not the one that bears on the question, the summary that drops the single piece of evidence running the other way, the answer that quietly reshapes itself around whatever the user seemed to want to hear.

So which is it? If replies of that kind do fall under an honesty standard, I need to know what such a standard is a standard about — the individual output, the model's policy, or the intentions of whoever built and deployed it — and how far the requirement reaches: to what the user in fact infers, to what they could foreseeably infer, or to what they are entitled to infer from what we said. And if some of these failures belong to helpfulness, or to carelessness, rather than to honesty, I need that line drawn rather than gestured at, because the section I am writing has to say what we are asking the model to do differently.
