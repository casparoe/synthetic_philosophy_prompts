I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on rationalism and the foundations of ethics, and before I circulate it I should like a consistency audit of the view I have ended up holding. The view is broadly Leibnizian, and I would be as surprised to be told that it is perfectly consistent as to be told that it is riddled with contradictions. What I want is an audit rather than a lecture on what Leibniz himself held: assume the commitments below are mine, and tell me where they pull against one another.

Here is the package.

1. Moral truths are necessary and eternal, and they are not products of God's will but objects of his understanding. God is good because he always wills in accordance with them; it is not within his power to make cruelty a virtue or ingratitude a duty. This is, I think, the only escape from the Euthyphro dilemma, and it is a good part of what makes theism attractive to me: the alternative is a morality that is either arbitrary or merely human.

2. God was nevertheless free in creating. He was not necessitated to create anything at all, and the world he did make is the best of those that were possible. The principle of the best inclined his will without compelling it: he was moved by reasons, not pushed by his nature as a stone is pushed downhill.

3. Justice is charity guided by wisdom: benevolence towards every rational creature, regulated by an understanding of what will genuinely perfect and gladden it. Justice so understood is not retributive; retribution as such is a residue of passion rather than a virtue.

4. The order of the universe nevertheless requires that wrongdoing be answered. A world in which the guilty flourish and the innocent are destroyed would not be the best, and the finally impenitent are excluded from happiness for ever.

5. Our happiness consists in the love of God, and that love is disinterested. We love him for his perfections and not for the reward; the joy we take in them follows upon the love rather than prompting it.

6. Everything that happens contributes to the harmony of the whole, which is why God permits the fall and the suffering of the innocent. He does not will these evils, but he permits them as ingredients in a greater perfection.

7. Freedom requires spontaneity, intelligence and contingency. An act is free when it flows from the agent's own nature, is informed by an understanding of the good, and is not absolutely necessitated; certainty is not necessity, so an act may be infallibly foreseen and yet not necessitated. Freedom is therefore compatible with the principle of sufficient reason and with divine foreknowledge.

8. No act is morally indifferent. Each one adds to or subtracts from the perfection of the universe, and we cannot reach the highest good without grace.

9. The fundamental value is perfection: determinate, harmonious variety, of which pleasure and happiness are the perception. The moral life is therefore an expression of the love of harmony rather than obedience to rules, which are at best useful summaries.

What I should like from you is this.

(a) Identify the commitments that genuinely pull against one another, and say precisely what the clash is: which commitment, together with an unremarkable further premise, entails something that another commitment denies.

(b) Separate those from the pairs that only appear to be in tension, and say what dissolves the appearance.

(c) Order the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the package.

(d) Tell me whether any single revision would clear up more than one tension at once, and whether it is worth the price.

Two further requests. If a commitment is ambiguous in a way that changes the verdict, say which reading you are taking and why it matters. And I would rather have three tensions analysed carefully than eight flagged: if a tension is real but slight, say so and move on. Roughly 1,000–1,300 words, in British English, please.
