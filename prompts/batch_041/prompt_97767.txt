I've been reading about the laws of war for a history project, and I keep running into a tension I can't resolve on my own. Under the body of international law that governs armed conflict, a soldier who kills an enemy soldier in battle commits no crime, and this immunity holds no matter which side he fights for: a soldier defending his country against an invasion and a soldier marching in the invasion have exactly the same legal permission to kill, so long as neither commits an atrocity like deliberately targeting civilians or torturing prisoners. Yet the same body of law treats the war itself as a matter of individual criminal responsibility, since international tribunals have prosecuted political and military leaders for the crime of waging aggressive war, which means the law does hold someone answerable for the injustice of a war of aggression. And there's a third piece that puzzles me even more: soldiers are expected to disobey orders that are manifestly criminal, an order to shoot unarmed villagers being no defense, which seems to assume that an ordinary soldier is capable of recognizing serious wrongdoing and is blameworthy when he fails to. So the law treats a soldier as a competent moral judge of the order he is given, but not of the war he is ordered to fight.

What philosophical commitments does this whole practice presuppose? Does combatant immunity presuppose that soldiers on both sides are moral equals, or merely that individual soldiers cannot reasonably be expected to judge their cause, or only that the law finds it useful to draw a line somewhere and stop? Which theories of responsibility would license the practice, and which does it quietly rule out, for instance the view that a soldier fighting for an aggressor does something wrong simply by killing, even if he commits no war crime? Are the three parts consistent with one another, or does the leader's liability for aggression sit uneasily with the soldier's immunity? And if we replaced the presupposed view with the rival view that unjust combatants are not morally permitted to kill at all, what would have to change in the practice, would we have to put ordinary soldiers on trial too, or is there some division of responsibility that could survive the revision? Please reconstruct the implicit theory the practice seems to need, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
