Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm TA-ing a final-year course on the ethics of war and for the last week I want to set something other than another essay — a satirical piece that forces the students to actually run the machinery instead of gesturing at it. Here's the target. The traditional view, usually called the moral equality of combatants, says that soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on a par: they're permitted to kill each other because they aren't the ones who decided the war was just, and the convention that treats them alike is worth having precisely because it puts a lid on what war can do. The revisionist critique says that's a mistake — whether you're permitted to kill depends on whether your side's war is actually just, so an unjust combatant who kills has done something seriously wrong even if he's brave, dutiful and completley ignorant of the facts. Write me the mandatory online compliance-training module for a private military contractor that has swallowed the revisionist view whole and staffed its HR and legal departments with people who still believe the traditional one. Deadpan corporate register, roughly 800–1000 words, and the jokes have to come from the theory being right, so that someone who knows this literature laughs rather than winces — but someone who hasn't taken the course should still be able to follow it.

What I want in it, in some order: a bland preamble about "our values"; a section on the Subjective Justification Attestation every contractor signs before deployment, including what happens if you tick "I am not sure the Client's cause is just"; a section on the internal "moral liability" ledger the risk team keeps, sitting next to — the legal department insists, not against — the clause guaranteeing that all combatants get identical pay, identical medals and identical legal cover whatever the war's status, on the grounds that the two things "operate at different levels of analysis" (I want that line doing real work, not just sounding corporate); something on what to do if you decide mid-tour that the war is unjust, given that nobody at the company is allowed to tell you whether it is, because that's "Client-side"; and a four-question multiple-choice quiz at the end with an answer key, where the options have to encode genuine positions — duress and mistake, the difference between being liable to be killed and being permitted to kill, the soldier on the just side who still commits an atrocity, the poor guy who never fires a shot. Somewhere in there let the innocent-threat-style cases surface, the driver who blacks out at the wheel sort of thing. I was arguing about this with a friend who does defence procurement law and she said it would never get past compliance, which is sort of the point. Both of the standard objections — that this is a machine for laundering the unjust side's killing, and that it makes a private's moral standing hostage to facts he has no way of knowing — should be visible in the document without anyone in it ever stating them. Invent the company and its paperwork; don't use a real firm, and don't put words in any living person's mouth.
