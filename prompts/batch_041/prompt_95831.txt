My friend and I have now spent three evenings arguing about whether appeals to the will of the people amount to anything more than a dignified way of describing whoever is currently winning, and somewhere in the second evening he produced the following argument. I have not been able to find the bad step, and I am no longer sure there is one.

(1) A decision binding on an entire political community is legitimate only if it expresses the will of the people.
(2) The will of the people is not simply whatever a majority votes for. If it were, it would make no sense to say that a majority had been deceived, or had voted against its own interests, or had been stampeded into a decision it would reverse on reflection. We say these things, and we are not always wrong to.
(3) So the will of the people is what the people would will under better conditions than the actual ones: full information, unhurried deliberation, and attention to the common good rather than to factional advantage.
(4) Actual majorities meet those conditions only sometimes, and sometimes fail them spectacularly.
(5) So an actual majority can act against the will of the people, and its decision can be illegitimate.
(6) When a minority is better informed and more reliably oriented to the common good than the majority is, that minority's judgment stands closer to the will of the people than the majority's does.
(7) So the will of the people can authorize a minority to set aside what a majority has decided.
(8) But a principle of legitimacy under which any confident minority may override a majority is not a principle of legitimacy; it is a permission slip for whoever can seize the microphone.
(9) Therefore either (1) or (2) is false, and talk of the will of the people cannot do the work we ask of it.

He added that the same argument, run with one person in place of a people, shows that a person's real will can be invoked to override what she says she wants, which he takes to be absurd. I said I was not so sure, since I think an advance directive I signed while lucid should bind me after I have forgotten signing it. We left it there.

What I would like to keep are (2) and (8) together, which may be the whole problem. Without (2) I seem to lose the only vocabulary I have for saying that a majority can wrong the very people it claims to speak for — that the people cannot will the destruction of a minority in its midst, however loudly the majority votes for it. Without (8) I seem to have licensed exactly the thing I was afraid of.

So: which step is doing the damage, why did it look innocent when he wrote it down, and what do I have to give up to escape the conclusion? And does whatever you say carry over to the individual case he raised?
