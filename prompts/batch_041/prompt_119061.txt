I'm doing the "which diet kills the fewest animals" session in my applied ethics module next Thursday — ninety minutes, final-year undergrads. Most of them are already animal-welfare people; two or three role their eyes at the wild-animal stuff. Every year I get to the end and realise I've performed the argument at them instead of building it, so plan the teaching for me: the plan, not the argument.

What has to land: once crop cultivation is on the table (field animals, mice, insects, whatever the combine goes through), "eat plants, obviously" stops being obvious, and it's genuinely arguable that a grass-fed beef diet kills fewer animals per calorie than a vegan one, with chicken coming out worst of the lot. And the thing I suspect is why the session never lands — the comparison is never settled by the science, because the unit of comparison is a moral choice (deaths per calorie? per hectare? per animal? per year of suffering?) and so is the baseline (would those animals have existed otherwise, and is bringing them into existence a benefit or a harm?).

So, concretely:

1. The spine — the order the pieces have to go in, what depends on what, starting with what has to be true in their heads before the beef/beans comparison even reads as a question, and where the hinge is: the single move that, if it doesn't land, makes everything after it noise.

2. The carrying case — the one example that carries the whole thing, what it has to have and what it must not have. If cow-versus-chicken really is the right carrier, fine, but tell me why it beats the alternatives.

3. Misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface in the room, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. I'm expecting "plants don't hurt anything," "deaths in nature don't count," and "so we should all eat beef, great" — give me the ones I'm not expecting.

4. The check — one question I can put to them at the end that shows whether they've got it rather than just been provoked.

5. What I openly refuse to do at this level, and say so.

No reading list, no citations, no numbers I can't check (I couldn't defend field-mouse-deaths-per-hectare even if I wanted to, so keep the magnitudes qualitative). And dont hedge into "well, it's complicated."

Give it to me as those five sections in that order, roughly: The spine (500 words), The carrying case (250), Misconceptions in the order they'll show up (350), The check (100), What I'm leaving out (150).
