I'm a philosophy prof and I teach a grad seminar on the justification of deduction, and every year the Carroll tortoise dialogue lands with a thud because the students can see the regress coming from a mile away and then they just say so it's circular and move on. What I want is a poem I can put on the handout for the week, something in the tortoise's voice that makes the regress feel like a trap that closes one step at a time rather than a cute paradox. I've been calling it teh tortoise trap in my notes: the point isn't that the tortoise is being obtuse, it's that every time you try to supply what she's missing—a premise, a conditional, a rule, a metarule—she accepts the new thing and then asks you to make the transition one more time. I want the poem to track that structure: premise, conditional, modus ponens as a rule, the demand that the rule be written down as another premise, the regress, and then the moment where you realize the rule was never a premise but a practice or a norm of inference. And I want the form to do the work.

Please write it as exactly five numbered sections, each a short stanza of four lines, with a refrain that shifts from grant it to write it down to not yet—the repeated line should feel like the same demand being made again under a new name, so the reader feels the step-by-step closing of the trap. Don't make the tortoise a troll or a villain; she should be polite, patient, and maybe a little sad, because she's asking for something the logician can't non-circularly give. In the last section I'd like the poem to turn toward the idea that accepting a rule is more like going on in a practice than assenting to one more proposition, but I don't want that as a thesis statement—let the turn happen in the line break or the refrain if you can. No quotes from the original dialogue either; I want the poem to stand on its own. Give it to me as exactly five numbered sections, no intro, no closing note.
