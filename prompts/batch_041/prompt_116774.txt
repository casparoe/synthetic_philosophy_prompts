ok so I keep running into the same move in constitutional theory and in rationalist-adjacent writing: a constitution is just a Ulysses contract. We're Ulysses, the sirens are our own future majorities (or our own future passions), the mast is entrenchment, the ropes are judicial review, and so there's no real tension between binding ourselves and democracy, because self-binding is exactly what a rational agent does when he knows he'll be weak later. Elster's Ulysses and the Sirens is usually the citation, or a stand-in for it.

Act like a political philosopher who actually knows both the Elster/precommitment material and the constitutional-theory literature, and audit the analogy instead of just restating it. First brainstorm the correspondence table out loud: who or what plays Ulysses, the crew, the ropes, the wax, the sirens, the ship, the destination? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into the ones that bear on the conclusion people are trying to get (precommitment justifies entrenching rights against majorities) versus the decorative ones. I'm especially interested in: there's no crew in politics, so who's holding the rope, and what happens when nobody is external to the person being tied? Ulysses binds one future self for an hour, a constitution binds strangers generations later who never agreed to the voyage; and in the Ulysses case everyone agrees what the agent's real good is, whereas political disagreement is often about ends, not just willpower. Also who ties the binder -- if the founding generation gets to define what counts as the later generation's real interest, isn't that just one faction winning and calling it akrasia?
