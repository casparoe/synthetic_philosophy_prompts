I'm putting together a handout for a small discussion group that keeps having the same fight about whether violence is going down, and I want us to stop re-litigating it in prose and just build a decision tree instead. The fight always goes the same way: someone says violence is obviously declining, someone else says that's only true if you define violence as men with guns killing each other in wars, and then we spend an hour on definitions and never get anywhere. I want a tree that makes the definitional choices explicit and sorts us by which ones we actually make.

So: build me a decision tree / taxonomy of positions on whether the world is becoming less violent. The branch nodes should be the questions that really do split the positions, phrased so a person can answer them without already knowing which camp their answer puts them in. Build the structure out of the logical space first and locate the positions in it second — don't just hand me a list of camps.

The branches I care most about, roughly in this order:

- whether violence requires an identifiable agent doing something, or whether deaths from poverty, blockades, priced-out medicine and plain neglect count too (and if they do, whether that makes "violence" so broad the word stops doing any work)
- whether intent matters: a bombing that kills civilians on purpose vs one that kills them as a side effect vs one that kills them by making the water undrinkable
- what goes in the numerator: deaths, injuries, years of life lost, fear, or coercion with no deaths at all (a population under occupation that isn't being killed much — is that violence? a credible threat of nuclear annihilation?)
- what goes in the denominator, and why: per living person, per person-year, per adult, as a share of all deaths, per dollar of income, or just absolute counts. Somebody in our group thinks "share of all deaths" is the honest measure and I can't tell if that's a real position or a rhetorical move.
- what the baseline for comparison is: 1500s Europe, pre-state societies, the peak of the world wars, a hypothetical stateless present. I know the pick changes the answer; I want to know whether it changes the position or just the number.
- whose deaths count: combatants and civilians, our side and theirs, people alive now and people not born yet, humans and animals.
- whether we're asking about the trend in the typical event or the risk of one enormous event. A world where small wars get rarer and one big war gets more likely is a different world from one where everything shrinks together, and I don't think most people have a view about which one they mean.
- and at the bottom, the meta-question: is there a fact of the matter here at all, or does the answer just fall out of the definitions, or is it a normative question about which definition we ought to be using?

Rules: no author names and no citations anywhere, descriptive labels for the positions instead — the per-capita optimist, the structural-violence pessimist, whatever you come up with. Every leaf gets a name plus one line on the fact or number that would most embarrass it. Where a combination of branch answers comes out empty, say so and tell me whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody's bothered to explore it. And flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate — if they answer every branch question the same way, don't pretend they're different positions.

Keep it to about ten or twelve branch questions and no more than fifteen leaves. Concrete cases to test the branches on if it helps: a blockade that causes a famine, a police shooting in a rich country, a genocide stopped by an outside power that kills civilians doing it, a drug priced out of reach that kills a million people, and a standoff where nobody dies but everyone is terrified. Also give me a short how-to-use note at the top: what to do when two people land on different leaves, and what to do when they land on the same one and still disagree.
