I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the moral significance of the distinction between doing and allowing. The participants are philosophy graduate students who will have no internet access during the session, so the handout has to be self-contained: it may name positions, but it must not contain citations, reading lists, or references to particular works, and nothing in it may depend on a fact that could only be checked online. It should also avoid attributing positions to individual philosophers, since I will not be able to verify the attributions before printing.

Please produce a decision tree — a flowchart that fits on a single page — that sorts a reader who has never studied this literature into one of the positions on the question of what, if anything, morally distinguishes doing harm from allowing harm. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of familiar positions: identify first the questions on which the candidate positions genuinely disagree, let the answers to those questions generate the positions, and attach labels only afterwards. The requirements are these.

First, five to seven branch nodes, each with two or three answer options and each phrased as a question a reader can answer by appealing to one of the cases below, without already knowing which position the answer leads to. No node may take the form of asking whether the distinction matters; each must isolate one component of the disagreement. The components I want the tree to cover include whether an omission stands in the same causal relation to an outcome as an action does; whether the distinction is a moral ground in its own right or a proxy for something else, such as what the agent knew or how burdensome intervention would have been; whether it is binary or a matter of degree; whether, if it is a ground, it operates as a constraint that cannot be outweighed or as a weight that can; whether the unit of assessment is the individual agent or a practice, institution, or collective; whether what counts as an action rather than an omission is fixed by nature or by convention and institutional rule; and whether the distinction survives once it is recast as the contrast between intending a harm and merely foreseeing it.

Second, eight to eleven leaves. Include at least one leaf for each of: the view that there is no moral difference at all; the view that the difference is a strict, non-scalar constraint; the view that the difference is real but graded, with intermediate cases such as sustaining a harmful process, removing a protection, or enabling a third party; the view that the difference is grounded in causation, with omissions either not causes at all or causes only in a counterfactual sense; the view that the distinction carries no intrinsic weight but is a reliable proxy for something else; the view that the doing/allowing contrast should be replaced by the intending/foreseeing contrast; and the view that the classification of something as an act rather than an omission is fixed by convention, so the distinction is not natural but can still carry weight once the conventions are in place. Add whatever further leaves the space requires. Each leaf should carry a short descriptive name, use the label unnamed where no established one fits, and be followed by one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Third, use the following five cases as the material for the branch questions, and ensure each case does work somewhere in the tree. (a) A maintenance engineer at a water utility receives an automated alert that a pump is failing, does nothing, and the pump fails, flooding a neighbourhood; she performed no earlier action relevant to the flood. (b) A physician stops a drug infusion that has been holding a patient's blood pressure up, and the patient dies; consider also the variant in which the same physician never starts the infusion. (c) Someone has for years maintained a barrier that keeps a landslide from reaching a village, stops maintaining it, and the landslide proceeds; consider also the variant in which she diverts the landslide toward the village by digging a channel. (d) A hospital board fails to update an infection-control policy after the relevant guidance changes, each member assuming another would raise it, and patients are harmed under the old policy, though no individual member did anything. (e) A dispatch system is built to take no action when it cannot classify an emergency, a default chosen by its designers years earlier, and the operator on duty declines to override it, so a caller dies waiting.

Fourth, wherever the tree reaches a dead end — an answer with no coherent position downstream — show the dead end and give a one-line note on why nothing lies beyond it. Where two leaves answer every branch question identically, do not pretend the tree separates them: state which pairs it cannot separate, what further question would be needed, and why that further question belongs to the weight of the distinction rather than its structure.

Fifth, after the tree, list every combination of answers that generates no leaf, and for each say in one line whether the missing position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Sixth, close with a short prose note on which branch question is doing the most work, and which leaf would be hardest for a reader who reasons from the five cases alone to reach.
