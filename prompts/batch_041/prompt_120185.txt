I am a high school junior and a member of my school's student council, and I would like help thinking through a problem that keeps recurring in our meetings. Nearly every dispute we handle is framed by both sides as a zero-sum contest. This year, for instance, one group wants the spring budget surplus spent on a memorial garden for a teacher who died last year, and another wants it spent on laboratory equipment; since there is only one surplus, we assume that every dollar one side wins is a dollar the other side loses, and that the only real question is whose claim is stronger.

A friend of mine who is studying economics suggested that this framing is a mistake, and offered an analogy I have been turning over ever since. I will state it in my own words, because I am not confident I have it exactly right.

Different people hold different values, and they hold them with different intensities: what one student would sacrifice almost anything to secure, another regards as one consideration among many. When two parties differ in these relative intensities, they can trade. I concede ground on the value I hold comparatively weakly in exchange for ground on the value I hold comparatively strongly, and you do the reverse; because our relative priorities differ, both of us can end up closer to what we actually care about than we would have been had we simply fought the question to a decision, even though nothing new has been created. The stranger and more interesting claim is that this holds even when one party is simply better at honoring both values than the other: the party with the advantage still gains by concentrating on the value where its advantage is largest and conceding ground on the other, just as a country that is more productive than its trading partner in every industry still benefits from specializing in the industry where it is most productive.

I am aware that the analogy may not survive the transfer. Values do not obviously come in divisible portions that can be handed across a table; the price of a concession is not something either side can measure; and it seems possible that conceding ground on a value does not merely cost you something but changes what you are, so that a person who trades away half of a conviction is no longer the person who wanted the other half.

Please describe the most important implications of this analogy for how our council should conduct negotiations of this kind, and in particular for what would count as a genuine gain in such a negotiation, given that we cannot measure what either side has given up. I would also like to know which parts of the analogy break down in ways that matter practically, so that I do not lean on them in an actual meeting.
