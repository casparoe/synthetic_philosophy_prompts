I'm in a reading group that's doing a unit on moral luck, and I've somehow ended up on the hook for the closing item of the last session. What I want is a poem — not an essay with line breaks. It should be spoken by the driver who didn't hit anyone. You know the setup: two people drive home the same way on the same night, both of them reaching for the radio at exactly the wrong second, one comes around the bend and there's a kid in the road, the other comes around the same bend and there isn't. I want it in the voice of the second one. He's been following the case, he's heard the sentence everybody's heard, and what's chewing on him is that he can't locate the difference between himself and the man in the dock — and worse, that he no longer knows whether he's a careful driver or just a driver who hasn't been tested, that his own opinion of his own will is now hostage to something that never happened. Don't let the poem forgive him and don't let it convict him, and please don't resolve whether the way we treat the two of them differently is a defect in our moral practice or a real difference in what they did. Leave it hanging.

Formal stuff, because the form has to be doing work here rather than just holding the words up. Exactly five stanzas of four lines, no more and no less. Stanza one and stanza three have to be the same stanza — same road, same reach for the dial, same second — with exactly one word changed, and the word that changes is a fact about the world, not about him (the kid is there or isn't, the brake catches or doesn't). Rhyme or don't, that's your call, but the mirrored stanzas are the constraint that matters. Stanza four is where he starts addressing the other man as "you," and by stanza five the grammar should make the two of them hard to tell apart — I want a reader to lose track for a second of which one is speaking. Setting, if it helps: two-lane state highway, dusk, late fall, wet leaves on the shoulder, a car that smells like cold coffee — move it if you have a better road. Banned words: luck, lucky, guilt, blame, deserve, responsibility, accident, tragedy. If the idea can't survive without them, then I've learned something about the idea. Somebody in the group who knows this literature should be able to tell which strand of the problem the poem keeps running aground on — the one about what you cause, the one about what you are, the one about how it turned out — without me labeling it, and somebody who's never read a page of philosophy should still find it a decent poem about a guy in a car at night. Just the poem, five stanzas, no intro, no notes on your choices, and American spelling please.
