I have been trying to determine what it means to attribute a preference — or a utility function — to someone, taking their choices as the only evidence, and I want a principle that fits my verdicts on the following cases.

A shopper who always buys the cheaper of two cereals he cannot tell apart: his choice reveals that he prefers keeping the money, and nothing further needs saying. A man who says he wants to quit smoking, tries, fails, and buys another pack: his choice reveals a preference too — for smoking, here and now — and his stated wish to quit is simply a weaker preference that lost. A woman who takes the job she will hate because it pays better: her choice reveals her ranking. A man who truly does not care between two desserts and settles it by tossing a coin: his choice reveals no preference, and that is no defect in him. A man whose choices cycle — A over B, B over C, C over A — and who will pay to be moved around the cycle: I want to say he has no preferences, not that he has contradictory ones. A man addicted to a drug who forgoes food to buy it: I resist saying he prefers the drug, since he would gladly pay for a pill that abolished the craving; his choice seems to reveal a compulsion rather than a preference. A chooser who takes option A, and then takes B when the identical option is described as keeping A rather than switching to B: neither choice revealed a genuine preference. A parent who dies shielding her child: she preferred her child's survival to her own, and calling it a sacrifice adds nothing.

The smoker, note, would also pay for such a pill. A friend proposes that a choice reveals a preference only when the chooser would endorse it on reflection; this handles the addict and the framing cases, but it also strips the smoker's choice and the job-taker's choice of any revealed preference, which I do not accept. Is there a principle that reconciles these verdicts? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, name the verdicts it cannot accommodate, and give its verdict on a case I have not mentioned. If no single principle works, give me the smallest set of candidates and tell me which verdict to abandon.
