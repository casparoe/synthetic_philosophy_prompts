I've been asked to do the speech at our town's 80th anniversary thing for Hiroshima, Saturday after next, church hall, maybe eighty people. I said yes before I found out what the room looks like. It splits about down the middle. One side is the peace-and-remember crowd, a few of them carrying photos, including a woman who was eleven and living outside Nagasaki and who has never once, in my hearing, used the word "justified." The other side is families of Pacific vets — men who were on the invasion roster for November and who are alive to have grandchildren because the war stopped when it did, and their kids know that sentence by heart and are not shy about it. One of the daughters teaches diplomatic history at a state school and will notice if I fudge. I have eight minutes, no slides, and every person in that hall is waiting for me to say whether it was justified. I've been writing novels for thirty years and I don't believe there are verdicts about history, but I'm not going to stand up there and be coy about it either, that would be worse than lying.

Write the speech. What I want it to do instead of voting:

- Show them that the fight they think they're having about facts is really a fight about which counterfactual to hold fixed. Invasion, blockade and slow starvation, a demonstration over the bay, the Soviet declaration on the 8th, or the possibility that Japan folds in October regardless — pick your baseline and your verdict follows, and none of those baselines is a factual claim that more digging could settle. I want that to land as a relief, not as a gotcha.

- Untangle two things everyone in the room is using as one: a decision being reasonable given what the person in the chair actually knew at the time, versus the world that came out of it. Most of the heat in that hall is people sliding between those two and deciding the other side is being obtuse. Keep them seperate, say it in plain words, no jargon, no philosophers' names, no terms of art.

- Say the thing about the exception that turns into a rule: whatever we tell ourselves about a one-time emergency, we've been living for eighty years on what the emergency produced, so it didn't stay a one-time anything.

- And the hard one: the testimony in the room isn't evidence for a side. Treating a survivor's account as an argument in the debate is a mistake both halves of the hall make, and it's the mistake that turns people into positions.

Don't quote anybody, don't list books, I'm not reading citations at a memorial. Don't end on a poem, I'll write my own. And definately don't let it curdle into a lecture — half that room has no idea what "counterfactual" means and shouldn't have to learn it on a Saturday. Roughly 1100 words.
