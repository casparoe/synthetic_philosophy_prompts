So in Germany the chancellor can only be thrown out by a no-confidence vote if parliament elects a successor in the very same vote -- you can't just pass a motion saying "we've lost confidence, you're done," you have to simultaneously put someone else in. They built it that way after Weimar, where governments kept collapsing and you'd get revolving-door cabinets. But here's whats bugging me: that rule seems to say that a clear majority of the elected parliament saying "we've had enough of this government" is not, by itself, sufficient to actually remove it. The majority's say-so is necessary but not enough. So what does that presuppose about political legitimacy? Does it commit you to a positive-investiture view -- a government needs an actual majority actively behind something, not merely the absence of a majority against it? And is that even consistent with the same system insisting that sovereignty resides in parliament? Or does the constructive vote quietly smuggle in a rival theory, one where "stable governance" or "the ability to govern at all" is itself a source of legitimacy that can outrank the momentary will of the majority? And notice the same country hedges the other direction too -- dissolving parliament early is wrapped in all these conditions, so the majority can't just call a snap election whenever it likes either. Tell me what theory of democratic legitimacy this whole setup presupposes, whether the practice really needs that theory or could be justified on boring prudential grounds alone, and what would actually have to change if you swapped in the naive view that a majority can fire the government any time it pleases.
