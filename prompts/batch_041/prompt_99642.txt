Alright, longtime lurker in the rationalist blogs and forums here, and the voting paradox is the thing that keeps dragging me back into arguements. Every time it comes up, someone runs the expected-pivotality math, someone else says "but expressive value!" or "but duty!", and we all go home having learned nothing. I don't want another pitch for one of the rescues -- I want the spec first.

So: write me the desiderata for a theory of turnout. What does any account of why people vote (and why they don't) have to deliver to count as adequate? I want the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, and a one-line case or observation behind each one, so I can tell whether it's a real requirement or just a prejudice of one camp.

Stuff I specifically want it to have an answer for, off the top of my head:

- The base fact. The pivotality calculation says staying home is basically free and voting costs you an afternoon, and yet in alot of elections a large fraction of eligible people show up anyway.
- The response pattern. Turnout *does* move with cost and with closeness, but weakly -- rain dents it, a nailbiter helps a bit -- but nobody's behavior looks like a step function on the probability of being pivotal, which is what the naive story predicts.
- The reverse case. In committees, juries, faculty meetings, small town votes, a single vote really is decisive -- and yet participation there isn't obviously higher, and the people who do show are the same habitual joiners. A theory that says "people vote when it counts" has to say something about this.
- The secret ballot. This one nags at me. If voting is mostly expressive or a signal, why is the act deliberately hidden from everyone? Expression to whom? If it's a signal to yourself, why does the sticker matter?
- The ceiling end. Places with compulsory voting or single-party elections push turnout near the top. That suggests a big chunk of turnout is pressure and habit, not belief -- but then the theory still has to explain the voluntary low-turnout end without quietly becoming two theories in a trenchcoat.
- Stickiness. Past turnout is the best predictor of future turnout, and non-voters often report guilt or shame rather than indifference. That smells like an internalized norm, not a calculation anyone actually ran.
- The asymmetry. We treat voting as meaningful and abstention as a void. Almost nobody models not-voting as itself expressive, even though it obviously can be.

One more thing to fold in: a friend has a pet line she calls the "rent theory" -- casting a ballot is paying rent on your membership in the polity, and not voting is squatting. I have no idea if it's any good, but I want the list to say what it would have to get right and what would kill it.

Then the two things I actually care about most:

1. Flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied. If the list is overconstrained, then the list itself is the result, and I'd rather know that than get a tidy scorecard.
2. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp.

If it's quick, finish with how the usual suspects do against the list -- the rational-choice calculus, expressive/consumption accounts, norm-and-habit accounts, group/ethical-voter accounts -- but keep it short, the criteria are the point and I'll do the arguing myself.

Keep it in the weeds but self-contained: assume I know the puzzle and the standard moves and nothing more obscure than that, and I'm not going to go read anything. No reading list.
