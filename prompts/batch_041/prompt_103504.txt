I'm putting together a little anthology of poems about philosophy-of-AI stuff, and I need one more piece. You're going to be my poet here, so put on two hats at once: a poet who can write formal verse in their sleep, and someone who's spent too much time in the rationalist/decision-theory corner of the internet and can actually argue about whether "coherent extrapolated volition" is even well-defined. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming a bit — see my notes at the end about that.

The concept is coherent extrapolated volition (CEV), Yudkowsky's 2004 idea. Roughly: the thing to aim at isn't what we happen to want now, it's what we'd want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together — our volitions extrapolated out and made to cohere into one unified thing rather than a pile of interfering wishes. (That phrasing is his, more or less, from the original paper.) The point of it is to dodge the trap of freezing our current, half-baked values in place, and to keep some human "we" in charge instead of a handful of programmers.

Here's why I want it as a sestina specifically, and this is the part I actually care about: a sestina takes six end-words and brings them back over and over, each time in a new position and a new grammatical role, so the same word keeps meaning something different as it rotates. That's the extrapolation — the same wants coming back, transformed, in a fixed permutation, never escaping the six. And then the envoi has to cram all six into three lines at once. That's the "coherence." I want the poem to be a little bit suspicious of its own envoi: the form manufactures a unity that the raw material might not actually have. So the poem should live in the tension between "this is what we'd converge on if we were better than we are" and "or maybe there's no unique fixed point, just path-dependence and whoever's holding the pen."

Constraints:

- Sestina. Six six-line stanzas, then a three-line envoi, standard end-word rotation.
- Use these six end-words: knew, more, faster, wished, together, never. (If one of them is genuinely hopeless to rhyme-position, you can swap it, but keep six and keep the rotation strict.)
- The envoi should gather all six.
- Someone who knows CEV should nod along. Get in at least a couple of the real objections, not just the glossy version — the indeterminacy / no-unique-fixed-point worry, the whose-volition problem (Bostrom's point that the extrapolation base is a free parameter you could set in different ways and get different answers, and that it quietly decides who counts), and the slightly creepy paternalism of overriding what someone actually wants in the name of what they'd want if they were someone else. But don't just name-drop these — let the rotation and the envoi do the work. The form should be the argument.
- Somebody who's never heard of CEV should still read it as a good poem, not a glossary with line breaks. No jargon that only makes sense if you've read the blog posts.

After you've drafted, do a quick pass where you check that each end-word is actually pulling its weight in every appearance and isn't just sitting there filling a slot — if a stanza only works because the word happens to be there, redo the stanza.

Don't explain the poem to me afterwards and don't give me a preamble about what you're going to do. Just the poem.
