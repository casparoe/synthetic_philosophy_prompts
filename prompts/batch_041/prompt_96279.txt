Ok so my city has this set of rules about monuments and I genuinely cant work out if they hang together, and I want you to tell me what view of commemoration they presuppose.

Here's the practice. Rule one: a monument to a specific person can only be taken down by the same supermajority vote that would've been required to put it up in the first place. Fine, whatever. Rule two, bolted on later: every monument older than 60 years automatically goes on a review list, and if it's a monument to a person, a board adds a small plaque at the base stating what the person did that we'd now object to. The statue stays; the plaque explains it. Rule three, for a completely different category: anything that's basically a banner for a regime we fought -- Nazi stuff, the Confederate flag on a courthouse lawn -- comes down immediately, no vote, no plaque, no review list.

What I want:

- Does the "plaque, dont remove" rule presuppose that putting a statue up is an act of honoring or endorsing, so that the plaque has to cancel the endorsement? Or does it presuppose the opposite -- that a statue is just a record, and you can remember someone without honoring them? Could both readings license the exact same rule? If so, say so plainly; I suspect parts of this are underdetermined and I want to know which parts.
- Does the supermajority-for-removal rule presuppose that the city today is, in some morally relevant sense, the same agent as the city that erected the thing -- that we inherit its commitments and so need a high bar to reverse them? Or does it presuppose the reverse, that removal is a fresh act by a fresh "we" and the supermajority is just a brake on mobs? Which one does the rule actually need, and which one is it merely compatible with?
- The asymmetry with the flags: does treating flags as remove-immediately but statues as contextualize presuppose that a flag asserts something a statue doesn't? Is there a principled line there, or is the real difference just that the flags went up later / are cheaper / read as current speech rather than historical deposit?

Don't hand me the usual "memory vs. honor" sloganeering. Reconstruct the implicit theory the practice commits to, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it just happens to fit, and then give me a clear-eyed call on whether these three rules can share one coherent theory or whether the city is quietly running two incompatible ones. Keep it tight. No reading lists, no citations, I'm not going to go look anything up.

Last line: start it with "Verdict:" and give me one sentence.
