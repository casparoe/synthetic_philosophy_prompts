I'm a philosopher by trade, and through a combination of guilt and bad luck I've ended up on the board of our neighborhood association — about 400 households, a shared well, two aging boilers, and a drainage problem that floods the four lots at the bottom of the hill every spring. There's a proposal on the table from a retired engineer who also sits on the board, and I have to vote on it next week, and I keep flip-flopping, so I'd like help thinking it through.

Here's the scheme. For any decision the board agrees is "technical" — repair vs. replace the boiler, which contractor's bid to take, whether the drainage plan will actually fix the lower lots — a five-person volunteer panel would first make a recomendation, and if the panel is unamimous the board would be bound by it. Anyone can volunteer for any panel. The engineer's pitch is that this is just obvious: people who know about drainage should decide about drainage, and it's absurd that his vote counts the same as someone who's never opened the manual. He also floated, half-jokingly, that we could give every board member a "competence score" at the start of each year, assigned by the other members, and weight votes by it. I asked who scores the scorers and he laughed, which I did not find reassuring. Two things nag at me. First, the people who actually volunteer for these panels are almost all retired, home during the day, and (I say this as one of them) fairly comfortable; the younger families who'd eat most of the special-assessment cost basically never show up. Second, and this is the part that really gets me: I'm perfectly happy for a panel of experts to decide the boiler, and I would be furious if some panel decided whether we're allowed to put up a sign or fly a flag. I can't tell whether that's a principled line or just me liking the outcome on one and not the other.

I teach this area, so I know the usual moves, and knowing them has not helped me one bit. What I want from you is the part I keep failing to see. Put on your political-philosophy hat — imagine someone who's spent a career on the question of who should decide what, and who's good at catching the moment when an argument about "better decisions" quietly turns into an argument about something else. Before you commit to a position, brainstorm out loud: lay out the competing considerations, say which ones actually bear on this proposal and which are window dressing, and tell me whether the boiler/sign distinction can be drawn on principle or only by taste. Then give me a straight recommendation on how to vote, with your confidence level, and name the one consideration that would flip you. Don't worry about citing literature or name-dropping famous positions — I care about the reasoning, not the reading list.
