I sit on my town council's culture committee as the lay member, and I have to vote on Thursday. A charitable trust has offered to build and endow a 250-seat theatre on the old cattle market — professional touring companies most weeks, our own amateur operatic society and youth drama group for the rest — and the only question before us is whether to accept the offer and the modest annual subsidy that comes with it (roughly what we now spend on the summer festival, which we would keep either way). I have no training in this. But I have been reading Rousseau's long letter against d'Alembert's proposal that Geneva should have a theatre, and it has unsettled me more than I expected. As I understand him, he is not saying the plays will be filthy or the actors wicked. He is saying something harder to shrug off: that a theatre cannot make people better, only more pleased with themselves, because applauding virtue from a seat spends the feeling we ought to spend in the street; that it trains us to be spectators of our own lives; that a small town is precisely where this does most harm, having no great city's anonymity to absorb it; and that the market, the festival, the choirs and the amateur operatic society that half the town belongs to are a better school of citizenship than anything that would arrive on a lorry. I can see the other side too: the money, the jobs, the children who would otherwise never see a play, and the plain fact that people here would enjoy it.

Draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before the meeting. Recommend a decision — accept, decline, or accept with conditions — and make it concrete enough that the trust could answer it. I want no reading list and no survey of what philosophers have said; I want the argument made, and I want to be told which of Rousseau's points is actually carrying the weight in your recommendation, and whether the recommendation would change if that point turned out to be false.

Please use these sections, in this order: Recommendation (two or three sentences); What is actually at stake (about 150 words); The strongest case for accepting (about 250 words); Rousseau's objection, put at its strongest (about 250 words); Where the objection bites, where it does not, and which point decides it (about 400 words).
