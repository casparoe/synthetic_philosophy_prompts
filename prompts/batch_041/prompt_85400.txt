I am a novelist, and I am writing a book about a man blind from birth who is given sight in middle age. The scene I cannot get right is his first sight of a sphere he has handled every day of his life. I know this territory is Molyneux's problem, and I know the argument it belongs to is about concepts rather than about eyes, but I have only ever met it in summaries, and I want it put into the only technical vocabulary I actually command. I have no philosophical training beyond a bad undergraduate year; I use the language of craft with precision and nothing else.

So take the positions in the dispute as they are actually held and restate each of them, as faithfully as the restatement allows, in my terms: that the man made to see would not know which object was the sphere; that he would know, because something is common to both senses, whether one calls it extension or calls it the shape itself; the compromise on which flat shapes would come at once while solids would not, unless he reasoned his way to them as a geometer might; the position that the question is really several questions bundled together, since being able to name what one sees is not the same as possessing the concept of what one sees; and, if you know it, the version that appears in a medieval Arabic philosophical novel, in which the man whose sight returns recognises everything and finds only brightness new — a version, as I understand it, that answers the question by fiat, in a colour key rather than a geometric one. My terms are these: scene and summary; showing and telling; what a character perceives against what a character knows; point of view and its restrictions; the reader's knowledge held up against the character's; defamiliarisation; free indirect discourse; and the standing difficulty of rendering one sense through a medium that has no access to it.

Then the part I actually need, the residue report. Tell me what carries over intact, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I want to know specifically whether craft has anything answering to the philosophers' notion of possessing a concept, as distinct from being able to attach the right word to what one sees; I suspect it does not, and I want your view on whether that is a hole in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim is doing less work than it appears to. I also want to know whether the philosophers' word, recognition, means what my word means, because in a novel recognition is mostly the business of one person being seen for who they are, and I have a suspicion that I have been reading a perceptual question as an emotional one. And I want the converse residue: what does craft have that the philosophy does not, and is that a resource the philosophers lack or a confusion I am smuggling in.

Bring in the empirical work, since it is the reason I have stopped trusting my own version: the recent cases of adults whose sight was restored, who failed to identify the shapes at first sight and then succeeded within days. Say what that does to each position above, and whether it settles the question or merely narrows it.

Finally, give me the verdict on my scene, even if it is one I will not enjoy. On the best-supported reading, is the moment of recognition I have been trying to write an honest rendering of any of these positions, or am I obliged to write an unassimilated visual field, an object with no name attached to it yet? Flag which parts of your translation are contested readings of the philosophers, which are stipulations you are making for the sake of the fiction, and where your confidence is low. And if I have posed this badly — if a question about the acquisition of concepts cannot be translated into craft terms without becoming a different question, or if I am simply wrong that the surgical cases are evidence of anything — say so plainly rather than performing the exercise for me.
