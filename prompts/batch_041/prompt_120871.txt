On the blogs I follow, and in a reading group I sit in on, the same argument about the end of slavery keeps recurring and never resolves, and I would like a diagnosis rather than another round of it.

The positions are roughly three. The first holds that the moral case against slavery was made, heard, and acted on — that the arguments did the work. The second, in the tradition of Eric Williams's claim that British abolition arrived once the West Indian sugar economy had already stopped paying, holds that the moral case had been available for a century before anything changed, that what moved was the balance of material interest, and that the argument served at most as a cover for a decision already forced. The third, following Appiah's account of moral revolutions, holds that neither is sufficient — the arguments were old and the interests were mixed — and that what actually killed the practice was a shift in what honor required, so that the slaveholder became contemptible rather than merely mistaken. Every time this is argued out, the same moves circulate (the argument was known earlier; so was the profitability problem; then why here and not there), and nobody budges.

My hypothesis is that this is three disagreements wearing one name, and that the camps are answering different questions: the interest camp about enabling conditions, i.e. when abolition was possible; the argument camp about the proximate trigger, i.e. why then; the honor camp about transmission, i.e. why the change propagated rather than fizzled. If that is right, much of the heat comes from treating an answer to one question as a refutation of an answer to another. Test the hypothesis against what each camp actually claims, and against the awkward cases: Haiti, where slavery ended by insurrection rather than persuasion; Cuba and Brazil, where the institution expanded after the British had turned against it; dueling and footbinding, where the case against the practice long predated its end. Then tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, whether a hidden factual dispute is doing the work, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a decisive case, a specification of what the claim that the argument did the work would even have to establish, or a reason to think the question is underdetermined and the argument will recur indefinitely. No survey of the literature and no reading list: I want the diagnosis and its consequences.
