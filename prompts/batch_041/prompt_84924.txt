My housing co-op is rewriting its bylaws this month — I somehow ended up on the committee — and we've hit a snag that's turned into a running joke. Our current bylaws say they can be amended by a two-thirds vote of the membership. Then someone (Priya, obviously) proposed a clause making the amendment clause itself changeable only by unanimous consent, plus a second clause saying any provision whatsoever is amendable by two-thirds. So now we have two rules that contradict each other about whether the amendment clause can be touched, and at the Thursday meetings everyone just talks past each other about which one wins. Someone brought up Nomic, the game where changing the rules is itself a move, and then Hart's rule of recognition, and the Kelsen basic-norm thing where the top rule can't be validated by anything above it, only accepted — and honestly the whole thing would land better as a poem than as a Robert's Rules argument. I want to read something at the next meeting. Not a joke exactly, but something that makes the paradox felt instead of explained.

Here's what I'm after. A poem in the form of a set of rules that keeps amending itself: five or six stanzas of four lines, under 40 lines total, with a title. Let the rules be real rules about the poem — the fourth line of each stanza states a constraint the next stanza has to obey, like a rhyme it has to pick up, a line it has to repeat, a word it's no longer allowed to use, a thing it has to stop doing. I'd love one rule that bans rhyme and then a later stanza that quietly ignores the ban, and one rule that's declared unamendable in stanza two and then amended in stanza four without ceremony. The last stanza's rule should repeal the rule that gave all the earlier rules their authority, so the poem ends standing on nothing, and that final line should be visibly unable to license itself — I want the reader to feel the exact moment the floor goes. Make the register statutory and a bit deadpan, like a municipal code that's trying to be a poem, but don't let it be only a gag: the self-reference has to be doing the actual work and not just decorating a joke about bureaucracy. Somebody who's thought about the paradox of self-amendment, or Hart on the rule of recognition, or the way a constitution's own amendment clause ties itself in knots, should recognize the moves; somebody who hasn't should still find it a good, slightly wierd poem.

Then a section titled "Notes on the rules", 300 words or so: which rule does what, where exactly the paradox bites, and whether the final repeal is a legitimate move inside the poem's own system or a coup — tell me honestly whether my last stanza is a cheat, and what I'd have to change to make it not one.
