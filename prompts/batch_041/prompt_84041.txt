I should be grateful for your help with what began as a family argument and has since turned into something more serious. My brother-in-law has spent the past couple of years reading a great deal of philosophy, and has arrived at the view that none of us can know anything about a world existing outside our own minds. In itself that would be harmless — he is welcome to his opinions and I am happy to argue about them over dinner — but it has begun to have practical consequences. Three weeks ago he withdrew from the purchase of a flat on the morning of the exchange, on the grounds that he could not be certain that the building was still standing, that the solicitor at the other end of the telephone existed, or that the signature he was about to make would really be his own; my sister had already given notice on their rented house and is beside herself. When I pointed out that the building stood directly opposite us and that we had walked round it together only the day before, he replied that these were precisely the sorts of claim he was doubting, and that I was assuming the very thing I was trying to prove. I then attempted a slightly more careful version of the same thought: there is a building on this side of the road, and there is another building on the other side of it; if that is so, then there are things in existence besides our own minds, and so the world we are arguing about exists. He was unimpressed, and said that a proof which begins from premises he is entitled to doubt proves nothing whatever. He adds that unless I can rule out the possibility that I am dreaming all of this, I cannot claim to know that the building is there — and, to my considerable annoyance, I have to concede that I cannot rule it out. I have a strong sense that something has gone wrong in this conversation and that the fault is not entirely mine, but I cannot put my finger on where it lies.

I should like you to act as a philosopher with a specialist's knowledge of epistemology and of the analytic tradition's long quarrel with the sceptic, and to think slowly and carefully before answering, rather than reaching for the first tidy formulation that comes to hand. If it helps you to be accurate, consider two or three candidate diagnoses of what my brother-in-law is really claiming before you commit yourself to the one you think best. Please begin by setting out what the disagreement between us actually amounts to: what he is asserting, what I am asserting, and what is genuinely at stake beyond the fate of one flat. Then tell me how much of a proof I have in fact given him; whether the charge that I am assuming what I set out to prove is fair; whether there is any principled difference between the sort of certainty he demands and the sort that ordinary claims about buildings, solicitors and signatures can bear; and what I should make of the fact that I cannot rule out that I am dreaming, yet still take myself to know that the building is standing. If the move I stumbled upon has a familiar name in the literature, I should be glad to learn it, and to know how it is usually received and what the standard objections to it are.

Finally, please give me a short reply I could actually use at the dinner table, one that does not presuppose that my sister has read as much as her husband has; and tell me honestly whether you think this disagreement can be settled by argument at all, or whether it is the sort of thing that no proof can ever do. Where your answer turns on a point that philosophers still dispute rather than on something settled, say so plainly, and distinguish your own view from the state of the discussion. Please write in British English.
