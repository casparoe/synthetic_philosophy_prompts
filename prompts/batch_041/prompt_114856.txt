I'm a science journalist putting together an explainer on how economists and philosophers model choice under uncertainty, and I want to make sure I don't print a howler. The part I'm stuck on is risk-weighted expected utility, the theory Lara Buchak develops in Risk and Rationality. Here is how I understand it. You rank an act's possible outcomes from worst to best. Instead of multiplying each outcome's utility by the plain probability of getting something at least that good, you run those cumulative probabilities through a further function — Buchak's risk function, a continuous increasing map of the unit interval to itself — and you weight each outcome's contribution by the result. The risk function is meant to be an extra ingredient in the theory, alongside the agent's utilities and her credences. When it is the identity function, the theory just is ordinary expected utility.

My difficulty is what the risk function represents. For a risk-averse agent, it shrinks the chance attached to the better outcomes, which is what makes a gamble look less attractive to her than its expected value. But that looks like a claim about what she believes. If the chance of the good outcome is being pushed down, the theory seems to be saying she takes the good outcome to be less likely than it is — that her risk aversion is, at bottom, a mistaken estimate of the odds. Compare someone frightened of flying: we don't treat her exaggerated sense of the crash risk as a legitimate attitude toward risk, we treat it as a false belief and try to correct it with statistics.

So my question is whether I have this right. If REU locates risk aversion in a distorted probability judgment, how can Buchak offer it as a rival account of ideal rationality rather than a theory of a systematic error? And if the distortion is the error, why isn't the remedy simply to learn the true probabilities — after which the risk aversion the theory is meant to capture would have nothing left to do?
