Last month I started messing around with prediction markets — nothing serious, a few hundred bucks on forecasting questions and an office betting pool — and my buddy Sam, who has read basically the entire rationalist blogosphere, has been coaching me. He gave me his Dutch book spiel, and I've been repeating it in arguements with my brother, who thinks the whole thing is a trick gamblers use to feel smart. Before I keep using it as a trump card I want to know what actually holds up, so here's Sam's version:

'A Dutch book is a set of bets where you lose no matter how things turn out. Ramsey and de Finetti showed that if your degrees of belief don't follow the probability rules — like if you think it's 70% likely to rain and 50% likely not to rain — then there's a bunch of bets where every single one looks fair to you, but taking them all guarantees you're out of pocket. So rationality means your credences have to be probabilities, which is the whole basis of Bayesianism. It works for learning too: if you don't conditionalize when you get evidence, the same kind of bookie can take your money, so being a Bayesian is the only way to be safe. The argument is pure logic, it doesn't need any assumptions about what's actually likely or about how the world works, which is why it's better than all the frequentist stuff. Basically, if you want to actually make money forecasting you have to do this, and if you don't you're just a mark waiting to be cleaned out.'

My brother's counter is that this only works on someone who takes every bet anyone offers, which nobody does, and that real betting has fees and limits and he doesn't go around quoting odds on everything anyway, so he says the whole thing is a non-starter.

How much of Sam's version is actually right? Go claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on probability and decision theory would nod along with, what they'd wince at, and what's straight up wrong — and whether the conclusion I've been running around repeating (get with the program or get fleeced) actually follows from whatever survives. And does my brother's objection really kill the argument, or does it just narrow what the argument is a claim about? I ask because I've been using this to win fights and I'd rather not be the one who's definately wrong.
