I am a high school student who has spent most of this year working on an essay for my school's philosophy club, and I have arrived at a position that I would like you to argue for on my behalf. The position is this: the wrong kind of reasons problem cannot be solved by restricting the buck-passing account of value to reasons of the right kind, because no feature of a reason's object or content marks it as right-kind, and every alternative criterion either fails on cases or quietly presupposes the very value it was supposed to explain, so the account should be abandoned rather than patched. I should state the problem as I understand it, since I do not want you to assume that I have read my way into it and I would rather you argued from the materials in front of us. Buck-passing holds that for something to be valuable is just for there to be reasons to value it; the objection is that plenty of reasons to value a thing have nothing to do with its value, as when I am paid to admire a painting or threatened until I do. The usual repair is to say that only reasons of the right kind count, and that these are the ones grounded in the object or the content of the attitude rather than in what happens to me if I have it.

Two things should form part of the argument. First, please press the following case, which I invented and have not seen discussed anywhere: a town holds an annual lottery whose winner must spend the anniversary of a founder's death in genuine mourning, on the grounds that the ritual has its point only if the grief is real, and the prize is generous. When the council is asked why the mourning matters, it says that insincere mourning dishonours the founder — a consideration that looks exactly like an object-given reason, even though what actually moves the entrant is the prize. Second, please deal with a repair that a friend of mine proposed, namely that a reason is of the right kind just in case it would still be a reason to have the attitude even if nobody's having that attitude made any difference to anything else. I believe this fails, and I would like you to show how, using at least one case of your own rather than only the ones I supply, and also to explain why holding everything else fixed cannot be made precise without first deciding which reasons are the right ones; I have in mind a promise to love someone, and the more ordinary case of loving a partner partly because the love makes both lives go better.

Please give me the argument as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first four should carry the argument in order: the adequacy condition any repair must meet, the failure of the object-or-content criterion, the failure of my friend's test, and the way the remaining alternative smuggles value back in. The fifth should be the objection that comes closest to rescuing the account, together with your reply to it.
