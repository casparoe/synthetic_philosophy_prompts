I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on death and the meaning of life, and I am working through the final exams. One of the compulsory questions, together with one student's answer to it, appears below. I need a grade for the answer, and the written comments that justify it.

The question, worth twenty points:

The Epicurean argument against the badness of death is standardly presented, in this course, as having two horns. The no-subject horn: death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because there is no time at which the person who is supposedly harmed exists and is dead — while we live, death has not come; when death has come, we are not. The no-experience horn: being dead is not something anyone undergoes, and nothing can be bad for a person unless it is bad for them in their experience. (a) Explain how the two horns differ, and what each assumes about the nature of harm. (b) The deprivation account is usually taken to be the strongest reply to the Epicurean argument. Which horn, if either, does it defeat, and what would a defender of Epicurus say in reply? (c) The symmetry argument is sometimes said to reinforce the Epicurean conclusion and sometimes said to undermine the deprivation account. Explain how one argument can be put to both uses, and say which use you find more persuasive.

The student's answer:

(a) The two horns are usually treated as separate arguments, but I think they come to much the same thing. The no-subject horn says that a harm needs a subject who exists at the time the harm occurs, and that death never supplies one, since before death we are alive and after death we are not. The no-experience horn says that a harm needs to be felt. These two assumptions are not really distinct: only an existing being can feel anything, so the requirement that a harm be experienced already contains the requirement that its subject exist. The argument is therefore best reconstructed with the experience premise as its engine, and the no-subject horn is a corollary of it rather than a separate line of thought.

(b) The deprivation account holds that death is bad for the person who dies because of what it deprives them of: the goods that the rest of their life would have contained. The badness is comparative rather than experiential — it is the difference between the life the person actually had and the life they would have had — and it does not require the person to be around to register the loss. Harm of this kind is familiar from cases that have nothing to do with death: someone can be harmed by a betrayal they never discover, or by the failure of a project they never learn about.

So the deprivation account clearly defeats the no-experience horn. The premise that nothing can be bad for us unless it is felt is simply false; plenty of things are bad for us without our knowing it, and death is one of them if it takes away goods we would otherwise have had.

The account also has consequences worth accepting rather than dodging. If the badness of death is measured by the goods it removes, then, other things equal, death is worse for the young than for the old, and worse for someone who would have had a long and happy life than for someone whose remaining years would have been short and painful. Many people find the first of these results counter-intuitive; I think the account should bite the bullet here, because the alternative is to say that the amount of good a person loses makes no difference to how badly off they are, which is worse.

The no-subject horn is defeated too. The harm of death does have a time: it occurs at the moment of death, which is exactly when the person's life is cut off and the remaining goods are lost. The person exists right up to that moment, and the loss is a loss to them, so there is a subject of the harm after all. Once the two horns are seen to be the same argument, the reply to the experience premise settles the whole thing.

I would add that if Epicurus were right, it would follow not only that being dead is nothing to us but that dying is nothing to us either, which is an additional reason to think the argument has gone wrong somewhere.

(c) The symmetry argument invites us to compare the time after our death with the time before our birth. We do not regard the time before our birth as a misfortune, and the two periods are alike in that we do not exist during either of them; so, if we are consistent, we should not regard the time after our death as a misfortune either. Read this way the argument supports Epicurus: it tells us to be as indifferent to our future nonexistence as we are to our past nonexistence. But it can also be turned against the deprivation account, and this is the more interesting use. The deprivation account says that we are harmed by the goods we do not get. If that is the standard, then the goods we missed before we were born should count as harms as well, and no one believes that.

The most promising reply is that the comparison the deprivation account needs is not between two periods of nonexistence but between two versions of one person's life, and that death, unlike birth, deprives someone of a life that has already begun. Before I was born there was no life of mine that was interrupted; when I die, there is. That is a real asymmetry, though it needs more defence than I have given it here.

Overall I think the deprivation account is the right account of why death is bad, and that the Epicurean argument fails, although the symmetry argument shows that the account still has to say more about why the prenatal case is different.
