I teach a seminar on science and religion, and every year I have to pry my students off the Wikipedia summary of Galileo and get them to actually think with him. So here's what I take him to be running in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, which he wrote in 1615 and which only got into print in 1636, in Strasbourg. First, nature and scripture both come from God, so they can't genuinely contradict; when they seem to, the fault is in the interpreter, not in either book. Second, scripture is accommodated — it speaks the way ordinary people speak, and its aim is our salvation rather than an account of the heavens (he borrows a line from Cardinal Baronius to that effect, and he makes the same point with the way the text talks about the sun rising and setting, about God's hands and God's anger, and about the sun standing still over Gibeon when Joshua asks it to). Third, and this is the bit my students always skip: he only licenses re-reading a scriptural passage against a demonstrated conclusion in natural philosophy. Where the science is still a hypothesis and the traditional sense is settled, he says keep the literal sense and don't upset the faithful. Fourth, division of labour: theologians shouldn't be pronouncing on physics, mathematicians shouldn't be pronouncing on faith, and the whole letter is really a move about who has the authority to interpret in the first place.

Now run him forward into three fights he never got to have. (a) The modern young-earth crowd, who read Genesis 1-11 as straightforward history and who love to invoke Galileo as the man the church was wrong about, i.e. they laughed at him too. Note that they also quote his concession about the literal sense standing where nothing is demonstrated — and that the people they're arguing with, the theistic-evolution sorts, claim him just as hard. (b) Adam and Eve. A lot of Christian doctrine has insisted the human race descends from a single pair, and the population-genetics consensus is that our ancestral population was never anywhere near two. (c) Historical-critical reading of Genesis, on which the cosmology in the text is just the ordinary ancient Near Eastern three-decker universe — which makes the accommodation look like the accommodation of an ancient human author to his audience rather than of the Holy Spirit to our capacities.

For each one: what does he say, which of those four principles is doing the work, and where do the principles pull against each other. Then tell me which of the three he'd find genuinely hard rather than easy, which one is the closest analogue to the Joshua problem he actually tackled, and give me one further case that would seperate two readings of him that both fit everything above. And be honest about where you're inventing a Galileo rather than extrapolating one — I'd rather have that flagged than smoothed over.
