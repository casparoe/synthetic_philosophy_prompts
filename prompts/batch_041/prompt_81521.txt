ok, so I've read alot of how-to-solve-the-St-Petersburg-paradox posts and I want the actual spec first. Every solution I run into either only handles the coin-flip version or quietly assumes the casino runs out of money, and I'd like to know what a resolution is even supposed to do before I grade any of them.

So: write me the desiderata for a resolution of the St Petersburg paradox -- the list of things any adequate answer has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each item, and a flag on which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the commitments of one camp. Then tell me which pair of them I can't have at the same time, and which single requirement does the most work in killing off candidates.

Stuff I'd want the list to cover (add whatever I'm missing):

- The base case: 2^n dollars with probability 2^-n, n = 1, 2, 3... Should the resolution assign the game a value? If it says there's no well-defined value, does it still owe me an account of what's wrong with the person who pays $20 and the person who refuses to pay $1000, or is 'no fact of the matter' the end of the story?
- Does it have to agree with the boring finite answer? Cap the payoffs at a bank with capital C and the expected value is log2(C) + 1 (when C is a power of 2). That goes to infinity, slowly. Whatever the theory says about the uncapped game, it can't just ignore this limit, right?
- The physical-limits dodge -- no casino can pay it, you won't live to see n = 60, real coins aren't fair. Resolution or change of subject? If it's a resolution, what does it say about the idealized agent with an unbounded bankroll, and is that idealization even coherent?
- Family, not just the one game. Same coin flips with doubled payoffs: that game stochastically dominates the original and also has infinite EV, so the resolution owes me an answer about paying a small fee to swap. Then change the n-th payoff to (-1)^n * 2^n / n, so the naive EV is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - ..., which converges but can be rearranged to any number you like. Is the resolution even defined there? If the answer is 'different problem', say why.
- Repeat plays. If the theory says play once, what does it say about a hundred independent copies? Does the verdict have to be stable when the agent is offered the same game again and again?
- If bounded utility is the fix, it should own the cost: some wealth level past which more money literally doesn't help. Is there a requirement on the list that rules that out, or is 'more money is always at least a little better' something we're allowed to give up? Same question for continuity and the Archimedean axiom -- which axiom is getting sacrificed, and who gets to sacrifice it.
- Pascal's mugging is the same shape as this (astronomical payoff, astronomical improbability), so does a resolution of one have to be a resolution of the other, or can they come apart?
- And the general one: the verdict can't depend on how the lottery was written down. The same distribution described as coin flips vs. as a limit of finite games vs. as a density shouldn't get three different answers.

At the end, one line each on how the usual suspects do against the list -- bounded utility, ditching expected-value maximization altogether, the deflationary 'it's infinite, deal with it', and the threshold-on-tiny-probabilities move.
