I am a PhD student working on naturalistic accounts of religion, and I keep getting stuck on the legal category of religion. Accommodation regimes have to sort claims into religious and non-religious ones, because religious commitments typically trigger exemptions that otherwise identical commitments do not: in many jurisdictions the claimant must pass a sincerity inquiry and show that the commitment occupies in their life the place a paradigm religious commitment would, while someone whose commitment is ethical or philosophical receives less protection or none. The cognitive and evolutionary literature, meanwhile, treats religion as a loose cluster of ritual, coalitional, and byproduct phenomena with no defining essence, and some of it denies that religion is a natural kind at all. The practice also seems to assume that religious commitments are non-negotiable in a way ordinary preferences are not, whereas naturalistic accounts might explain that non-negotiability as a feature of coalitional commitment rather than as the mark of a special domain. What conception of religion does the accommodation practice presuppose? Does it require religion to be a distinct domain with a determinate boundary and a characteristic psychological signature, or can a deflationary, practice-centered account underwrite the same practice just as well? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out, are its parts consistent with one another, and what would have to change in a regime built on a naturalistic account? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined.
