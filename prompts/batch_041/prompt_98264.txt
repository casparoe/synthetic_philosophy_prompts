I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a logician of an unusually stubborn kind, and I have been trying to write the scene in which she is forced to defend the practice of deduction itself rather than any particular theorem; the scene keeps collapsing into Carroll's tortoise, where the premises are laid out, the tortoise asks by what right she moves to the conclusion, she adds the conditional, the tortoise asks again, and the regress begins to look less like a proof than like a demand for a practical commitment that no further proposition can supply. I do not want an exposition of that regress. I want the specification a theory would have to meet to count as an answer to it, and I want the specification to be demanding enough that it does not simply redescribe the problem in technical vocabulary. Please write the desiderata for an adequate account of the justification of deductive inference -- not validity as a semantic property, but the act of inferring, of taking a rule as one's own. I have been calling the leftover part of the tortoise's challenge the adoption residue, and I have found myself distinguishing a thin regress, in which one keeps adding premises, from a thick regress, in which one keeps adding commitments; use those distinctions if they help, or tell me they are confused.

The list should include the cases the account must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it must be consistent with: the tortoise's repeated demand; the difference between a rule of inference and a conditional premise; the way a proof can transmit justification without creating it; the possibility of revising a logical rule without merely changing the subject; the fact that we can reason correctly without being able to state the rule we are following; validity versus inferential entitlement; rule-circularity versus premise-circularity; descriptive reliability versus normative guidance; being compelled by a rule versus being guided by it; and the fact that any finite set of further premises will not close the gap. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- that is the part I most want, because the scene only becomes dramatic if the demands are genuinely in tension. Then, briefly, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the familiar candidates, without turning the answer into a literature review. I am not asking for readings, citations, or names of articles; I am asking for the shape of the standard itself. Begin your reply with the bottom line in one sentence -- the requirement you think is most likely to be non-negotiable -- then give the reasoning and the list, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. State how confident you are in the overall specification and single out the point you are least sure of.
