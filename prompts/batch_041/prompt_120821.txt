I'm TAing a seminar on natural kinds and its turned into a mess, because every author we read quietly assumes their own answer to what a theory of kinds is even supposed to do, and then declares the other side wrong for not doing it. So before we read anyone else I want the spec on the table. Give me the desiderata for an account of natural kinds — what it has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The ones I'm fairly sure belong on the list: it has to get the theoretical-identity cases right (what's in the beaker is H2O, and in some sense 'water' was always about that, so the old speakers didn't fully know what they were talking about); it has to handle terms that split or turn out heterogeneous (jade being two different minerals, 'reptile' turning out paraphyletic once you take birds seriously, maybe 'vitamin'); it has to explain why kinds are what induction and explanation run on, and why 'grue' or 'things on my desk' don't earn the name even when you can project from them; it has to say something workable about the special sciences, where species, money, race, sex and psychiatric categories are the kinds we actually use, none of them with a chemical-style essence, and some of them change because we classify them; and it has to preserve some real difference between a kind and a mere classification, or the whole picture of nature having joints and kinds being where we cut is just empty. Tell me what I've left off.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these can't all be satisfied at once, and what has to give. My suspicion is that the mind-independence requirement fights the induction/explanation requirement, and that both fight the special-science cases — especially the looping ones, where being sorted into a category makes the members start behaving differently. Is there a single package that keeps water and jade and species and mental illness under one account, or does the honest answer end up a pluralism about what 'kind' means in each science? Also tell me which one requirement does the most excluding work, ie which single item on the list by itself throws out the most popular theories. If its cheap to add, a line or two each on how the micro-essence/chemical picture, the causal-cluster picture, and a deflationary or pluralist picture do against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria, the scorecards are what my students will argue about anyway. And please dont hand me a reading list, weve got plenty.
