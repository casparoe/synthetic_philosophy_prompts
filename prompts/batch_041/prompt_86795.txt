I convene a final-year seminar in reproductive ethics, taken mostly by philosophy students and a handful from law, and I have to set the final paper within the month. I am writing to ask for one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, and I should explain why I am asking rather than drafting it myself.

Until this spring, surrogacy was a tidily organized teaching topic for me. My younger sister has since spent eighteen months arranging a gestational surrogacy in California, using a donor's oocytes, after exhausting what our own health system would offer her; we live in a jurisdiction that prohibits surrogacy outright and that, until recently, refused to register any parent-child relationship created by such an arrangement abroad. Sitting in on her consultations has taken my lecture apart. I had been teaching the objections to surrogacy as one list (commodification, exploitation, defective consent, the interests of the child, the gift-relationship argument) and the regulatory responses as another (criminal prohibition, toleration, a regulated market), and my students could reproduce both lists without noticing that the items do not line up. Her file makes the misalignment concrete: an objection to the transaction does not tell you what to do about a child who already exists, and a state can denounce an arrangement while conceding that its results must be recognized.

The question should be built around a hypothetical statute, so that nothing turns on whether a student has read any particular author and no one can answer from memory of a textbook case. I have roughed out the provisions and would like you to build the question on them, redrafting as you see fit:

1. It is an offence to enter into, arrange, or facilitate a surrogacy arrangement, whether performed in this jurisdiction or abroad.
2. A parent-child relationship resulting from such an arrangement is not to be registered, notwithstanding that it was validly established in the state where the arrangement was performed.
3. A court may nonetheless register the relationship where it is satisfied that the child would otherwise be left with no legally recognized parent, or that registration is required by the child's best interests.

The crux I want the question to reach is this: for each provision, which of the standard objections to surrogacy does it presuppose, and can the three provisions rest on a single view of what is wrong with surrogacy at all? Students should have to say whether provision 2 does any work that provision 1 does not already do, whether provision 3 concedes a principle that collapses the prohibition into a denunciation, and whether the ban's rationale survives the comparative fact that several European states prohibit surrogacy while permitting compensated, and in some cases anonymous, oocyte donation. The question must require a defended verdict, and the marking scheme should reward the argument rather than the verdict reached. It must not be answerable by reciting the standard objections in sequence; the difficulty should lie entirely in the articulation of the provisions against the objections.

Assume the students have the following from the course and nothing else: the commodification objection and its critics; the exploitation and undue-inducement objections; the contrast between altruistic and commercial models; the distinction between genetic, gestational, and intentional parenthood; the distinction between prohibiting an act and refusing to give it legal effect; the expressive function of criminal law; the best-interests standard and the child's interest in knowing its origins; and the argument that a domestic ban relocates rather than reduces the practice.

Please provide:

- the question as students will see it, with the scenario, for a ninety-minute closed-book paper, worth 25 of the 100 marks;
- a model answer of about 800 words, written as a strong candidate would write it, arguing rather than citing — the exam is closed book and I do not want a reading list, authorities, or references anywhere in it;
- a marking scheme of six must-hit points summing to 25 marks, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a closing sentence naming the specific move that separates an A answer from a B one.
