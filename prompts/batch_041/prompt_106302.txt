I've been volunteered to lead a session of a monthly philosophy reading group, and I could use your help designing it. The members are sharp and quarrelsome but not philosophers — a couple of doctors, a solicitor, a retired structural engineer — comfortable with a little symbolic notation and with the standard first-course material on arguments and validity, though none of them has touched modal logic, and I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard. My topic is the modal ontological argument, the one associated with Gödel: it treats 'positive property' as an undefined primitive, defines God as whatever instantiates every positive property, and then tries to carry us from the bare possibility of such a being to its existence in every possible world, by way of essences and a property of necessary existence. What I want from you is not the argument itself but a plan for teaching it — above all the order in which the components must be introduced, and why each has to be in place before the next will land; I take it that something has to be done about possibility and necessity before any of the 'positive property' machinery will mean anything to them, but I'd like you to tell me precisely what, and what can wait. I want a single carrying example to run through the whole session, one that can do duty in turn for 'positive property', for essence, and for necessary existence, and I'd like you to say what that example must have and must conspicuously lack; I have been tempted to build the session around a parody — a maximally excellent island, or a necessarily existing perfect pizza — and I cannot decide whether that would clarify the machinery or merely teach them that the whole business is a joke, so advise me there too.

I expect them to arrive carrying a cluster of misconceptions, and I'd like each one mapped to the exact point in the sequence where it ought to be headed off: that 'positive' is a term of moral praise; that possibility here is the everyday 'I can picture it' sense rather than a technical notion; that the argument is meant to establish God's existence outright rather than to exhibit what follows from its premises; and that any valid argument with a startling conclusion must be concealing a trick. There is also a worry I keep circling and would like your judgement on — that once the axioms are granted, everything seems to come out necessary, so that no room is left for contingency anywhere — and I want your advice on whether to raise it with this audience, and if so at which point, or whether at this level it would only muddy things.

Finally, give me one closing question that would tell me whether they have actually understood what the argument claims, as distinct from whether they have been persuaded or merely amused; tell me plainly what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving it out, since I would sooner name the omissions than quietly fudge them; and, if there is a step where the honest thing is to concede that the argument is doing something we have not fully earned, say so rather than papering over it. Please write your answer in American English.
