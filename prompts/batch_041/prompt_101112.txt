For some time I have been following the long-running disputes between archaeologists and proponents of a lost advanced civilisation — the Atlantis and ancient-astronaut literature and its relatives — and I keep encountering a style of argument for which I have no name. Its structure is not the naive one of citing a single decisive discovery. Rather, the proponent assembles a heterogeneous collection of small incongruities: an artefact that someone says resembles a modern device, a drawing that someone says depicts an aircraft, a map that someone says reproduces a coastline that could not have been known, a word that someone says is a loanword from a distant culture. Each item, taken alone, is weak, and the orthodox reply to each is different and locally plausible — the object is a ritual item, the drawing is a bird, the map is simply inaccurate, the resemblance is coincidence. The proponent's response is not to defend any one item but to appeal to their number, often with an explicit probabilistic flourish: what are the odds, they ask, that all of these should be coincidences? And from this they conclude that the best explanation is the existence of the lost civilisation.

Two things about this trouble me, and I want to know whether either has been named. The first is that the items are not independent in the way the probabilistic flourish requires: several are the same disputed object redescribed, or one author's assertion repeated by another, so the "many" is smaller than it looks and the multiplication of improbabilities is not licensed. The second is harder for me to state. Even granting genuine multiplicity, there seems to be something illicit about moving from "the orthodox account has no tidy explanation for each of these" to "therefore the heterodox account is probably true," when the heterodox account does not itself predict the items in any specific way. But I suspect this second worry is not quite right either, because there are cases — in the history of science and in forensic reasoning — where a heap of otherwise-unexplained observations really does justify a new hypothesis, and I cannot say what separates those cases from this one.

So: is there an established name or treatment for this pattern — the aggregation of individually inconclusive anomalies, sold by their number and by a spurious multiplication of probabilities, into support for a hypothesis that predicts none of them individually? If the phenomenon has no settled name, please coin one and define it, and in either case tell me how it stands to the nearest things I do know: argument from ignorance, the Gish gallop, the conjunction fallacy, and whatever the philosophy of science has to say about the evidential weight of anomalies. What I am after is not a label but the carving — what exactly the move is, what it is not, where the line falls between an illegitimate pile of anomalies and a legitimate one, and whether the legitimate version can be specified without begging the question against the heterodox side.
