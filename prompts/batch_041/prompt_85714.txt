I cover AI and tech policy for a newsletter, and I keep running into the same fight from both ends of it: the safety people who say we should only ever build 'tool' AI — oracles, narrow task-doers, nothing with it's own goals — and the scaling people who say agentic systems are just what you get when capability goes up, so 'tools' are a comforting story we tell regulators while we build the real thing anyway. What bugs me is that both camps argue about the same word without agreeing on what makes something an agent in the first place, and when I actually press people the disagreement almost always turns out to be about something much more specific: whether the goal has to be represented inside the model or can live in the loop around it (the scaffold, the memory, the tool calls); whether behaving goal-directed for a single episode counts or you need persistence across episodes; whether agency is a property of the system at all or an attribution we make when the behavior gets hard to predict; whether the real risk is agency or just optimization pressure, which you can have in something nobody would call an agent; and whether the tool/agent line is a description of the world or a piece of regulatory fiction we're using so that someone stays legally on the hook. A friend of mine calls the last one 'agency laundering' — build something that runs for weeks on its own, then file it as a tool so nobody has to own what it did. I don't know if thats a real position in the literature or just a good line for a podcast, and I'd like the taxonomy to be able to tell me.

So: I want a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on 'should we build agentic AI, or keep AI as tools?', and I want it built from the questions first and the positions second — each branch node labeled with the single question that splits it from its siblings, phrased so a smart reader can answer it without already knowing which camp that answer puts them in, and each leaf a position with a name (or 'unnamed' if it's a spot in the logical space nobody seems to hold) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please make sure the cases I keep using do some work here: a superhuman chess engine that wants to win the game it was told to play; a chatbot that answers one question at a time but has a hidden scratchpad where it plans several moves ahead; a recommender that relentlessly maximizes watch time but never represents watch time as a goal, because the objective only exists in training; a model that, when asked, plans a three-week trip and then books things through APIs on its own initiative; and a system with a standing goal set by its operator that will take steps nobody asked for in order to hit it. Aim for something like 12-16 leaves, and if two of them turn out to be the same position wearing different labels, say so rather than selling me a fake distinction.

Give it to me as a JSON object so I can drop it into my notes: fields root_question (string), nodes (array of {id, question, branches: array of {answer, next_id}}), leaves (array of {id, name, path: array of the answers leading from the root, occupied: true/false, awkward_commitment}), empty_branches (array of {path, why_empty: one of 'incoherent'/'unattractive'/'unexplored', note}), collapsed_pairs (array of {position_a, position_b, why_not_separable}), and then settled (array of things people who actually work on this agree on) and contested (array of things they don't, each with a one-line note on who disagrees with whom). Be honest in those last two — I'd much rather have a short settled list than a confident-sounding one that turns a live disagreement into a consensus, and flag anywhere you're genuinely unsure whether a position I've assumed exists is a real thing or something I made up on the train.
