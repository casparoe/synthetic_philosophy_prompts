Write an essay on whether consent can be valid when it is delegated to an AI proxy that gives or withholds consent on a person’s behalf.

Consider the following case. A person, P, authorizes an AI system to consent or refuse on her behalf in a specified domain—for instance, participation in medical research. The AI has access to P’s history of choices, values, emotional responses, and reflective endorsements. It updates continuously and predicts, with high accuracy, what P would consent to if she were fully informed, competent, and free from coercion. In practice, the AI sometimes consents to something that P, if asked at the moment, would refuse; yet when later informed, P often endorses the AI’s decision as more consistent with her deeper values than her momentary inclination. The AI does not deceive P, and P retains the formal power to revoke the authorization.

The controversy is whether such delegated consent is valid. Some argue that it is valid because it tracks P’s autonomous will more reliably than her occurrent judgments, and because P authorized the delegation. Others argue that consent is not a prediction but an act—a performative commitment made by the person herself—and that an AI cannot consent on P’s behalf. Still others hold that validity requires the person’s occurrent endorsement at the time of the act, so delegated consent is at best a proxy that must be ratified.

Weigh these and other arguments and reach a definite conclusion about whether AI-delegated consent can be valid, and if so, under what conditions. Address at least the following: the standard conditions of valid consent (information, voluntariness, competence); the distinction between consent as an act and consent as a state of will; whether a proxy can be more faithful to a person’s autonomy than the person’s own momentary choices; and the problem of irrevocability or irreversible consequences.

Structure your essay as follows, using these section titles in this order:
1. “The problem” (150–250 words): state the controversy, the case, and your thesis.
2. “The case for validity” (500–700 words): develop the strongest arguments that AI-delegated consent can be valid.
3. “The case against validity” (500–700 words): develop the strongest arguments that it cannot be valid, or only in a diminished sense.
4. “My verdict” (400–600 words): state and justify your conclusion, including the conditions, if any, under which delegated consent is valid. A conditional verdict is acceptable, but a non-committal survey is not.
5. “The cost of my verdict” (200–300 words): explain what your conclusion forces you to give up or accept, and identify one case where your verdict yields an answer you find uncomfortable.

Do not cite specific authors or works; argue from the concepts themselves. The essay should be self-contained and should not presuppose any particular philosophical theory of consent.
