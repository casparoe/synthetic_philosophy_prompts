Write a joke that turns on Simone Weil's critique of political parties. Her objection is not the familiar one that parties are corrupt, hypocritical, or insufficiently democratic; it is that the party form has an inner logic of its own, one that subordinates truth, individual judgment, and the capacity for attention to the demands of collective passion and organizational survival, so that a party intending to serve justice ends up reproducing inside itself the structure of the very thing it set out to oppose. The joke must be genuinely funny to someone who knows this position, and its punchline must turn on that inner logic rather than on the failings of particular politicians or on the bad faith of party members.

Build it around the following situation. A small group of sincere and intelligent people — none of them careerists, none of them stupid — found an association whose single stated purpose is the abolition of political parties. The joke is set at their founding meeting, and is written as the minutes of that meeting (or as an exchange occurring within it). The humor has to arise from the way the association's own procedures generate the structure it exists to abolish: for instance, the need to present one position to the outside world, the pressure to bring dissenting members into line, the conversion of a shared conviction into an object of collective loyalty, or the drift from attending to a problem to adhering to a position. No one in the scene may be a hypocrite, a fool, or a secret seeker of office; everything that goes wrong must go wrong because the participants are reasoning well and acting in good faith.

Constraints: keep the joke itself to roughly two hundred words; do not quote Weil, and do not invent quotations and attribute them to her; do not lean on biographical anecdotes or on the titles of her works; and make the last line the punchline, one that lands only for a reader who has followed the structure of the critique. After the joke, in no more than three sentences, identify which element of Weil's account is doing the work and say what the joke would lose if that element were replaced by the generic observation that power tends to corrupt.
