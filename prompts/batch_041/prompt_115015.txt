My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether it can ever be right to believe something on insufficient evidence, and we broke up without settling it. She takes the hard line: to believe without evidence is always a kind of dishonesty, and whatever comfort it buys is bought at the price of becoming the sort of person who believes what suits them. I found myself defending something messier and could not state it. So I would like you to work out what principle, or small set of principles, my verdicts below actually add up to.

A climber on a hard pitch who is sure she will make it, and whose confidence steadies her hands: fine. A surgeon facing a long-odds operation who believes her patient will pull through, and whose confidence steadies her hands: fine. A student who has not studied and believes she will pass, on the grounds that confidence improves performance: not fine. A man who, on no particular evidence, believes his late father died without pain, and finds the thought a comfort: fine. The same man believing that his father forgave him for something he never apologised for, and finding that a comfort: not fine, though I cannot immediately say what has changed. A widow who believes her husband is alive somewhere and will come back: not fine. A juror who privately thinks the defendant did it but acquits, because the evidence was insufficient: fine, and I do not think he is being dishonest. A defence lawyer who privately thinks her client is guilty and argues zealously for acquittal: fine. A doctor who privately thinks a treatment is futile but tells the patient it will work: not fine. A man who, on the strength of a horoscope, comes to believe he should turn down a job, and turns out to have been right to: still not fine, even though nothing goes wrong. And the case I keep returning to: a woman who believes her estranged brother will eventually come home, with nothing to go on, and who keeps a room ready for him and turns down a job in another city on that basis — I admire her and I also think she is ruining her life, and I cannot get my verdict to hold still.

My guess at a principle is that it is about whether the belief steers anything. A belief that guides a choice needs evidence; a belief that is idle — that nothing is staked on, that no decision turns on — may be held for comfort. That would make the father-without-pain belief fine and the widow's belief not fine, since hers directs how she lives; it would make the horoscope case not fine, since the man acted on it; it would leave the juror and the lawyer alone, since their private convictions steer nothing, only their official acts. But my friend says the horoscope case is no worse than the father-without-pain case, and I cannot refute her; and I am unsure whether the student's confidence is really different in kind from the climber's and the surgeon's, since all three are unsupported and all three do something. Perhaps the difference is that the student's confidence stands in for the work that would have justified it, while the climber's and surgeon's accompany the work — but the student might have studied as well, so I am not confident the line survives.

What I want back: the principle, stated precisely; for each clause, the case that forces it; your prediction on three or four cases I have not mentioned, at least one of which is a case where an unsupported belief happens to be true; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts you have to overturn, because I would rather give up a verdict than keep a principle that needs an exception for every case. I am also unsure whether any of this is about belief at all, rather than about what we may say and do — several of my verdicts seem to turn on the courtroom and the consulting room rather than on anything going on inside anyone's head — so please say which of the two you think I am really tracking.
