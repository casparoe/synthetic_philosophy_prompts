I have been arguing about diet and animals with a group of friends for years now, and there is a particular move in those arguments that keeps coming up and that I do not have a name for. Someone proposes something modest and concrete — cutting out chicken, say, on the grounds that the birds are small and the deaths per calorie are enormous, or shifting toward plants and away from beef. And someone replies: "But think of all the field mice and insects killed by crop cultivation — if you eat plants you are still causing deaths, and at harvest there may be more of them per calorie than in a steer, so your scruple is arbitrary." Or, in the other direction: "You want to reduce the suffering of farmed animals? Then your real concern should be wild animals, who vastly outnumber them and suffer disease, starvation, and predation by the billion; farmed-animal advocacy is a rounding error." What strikes me is that the reply is not simply "there are worse things elsewhere." It is that the larger harm is either manufactured by the very remedy being proposed, or is so much larger that it swallows every remedy at once, so that the effect is to make any concrete action look pointless or hypocritical rather than to redirect it.

I have looked at the usual suspects and none of them quite fits. The fallacy of relative privation is close, but that is normally about dismissing a complaint because something worse exists, whereas here the point is often that the solution to the smaller problem is self-undermining because it produces its own, larger crop of victims. The nirvana fallacy is also close, but that is about rejecting a workable solution for falling short of perfection, whereas here the objection is not that the solution is imperfect but that its imperfections are larger, in the same currency, than the problem it was meant to address. And I do not think it is merely the demandingness objection, since nothing in it turns on how much morality asks of a person. Is there an established name for this specific move — where an argument defeats action by pointing to a larger instance of the same harm, especially one that the proposed action itself brings about — and if there is not, can you name and define it in a way that separates it from relative privation, the nirvana fallacy, the doing/allowing distinction, and the intention/foresight distinction? I care much less about the label than about the boundary: what exactly has to be true for this move to be a fallacy, and when is it instead the sober observation that the remedy would be worse than the disease?
