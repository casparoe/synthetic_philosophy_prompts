I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am at work on an essay about the Condorcet jury theorem — the result, first set out by the Marquis de Condorcet in 1785, that if each member of a group is even slightly more likely than not to answer a binary question correctly, and if their errors are independent, then majority rule drives the group's probability of being right toward certainty as the group grows. My angle is that the theorem has been pressed into service well beyond Condorcet's judicial-jury setting — for prediction markets, crowdsourced forecasting, ensemble and many-model machine-learning pipelines, and the general rhetoric of the wisdom of crowds — and that the guarantee these applications borrow is bought almost entirely with the independence premise, which they quietly violate. Before I file, I would like your comments on the draft's argument, and I would rather you be blunt than kind.

The spine of the piece is a pair of terms I am trying out. I call the benefit the independence dividend: the extra accuracy a group enjoys solely because its members' errors are uncorrelated, so that idiosyncratic mistakes cancel in the aggregate. I call the cost the correlation tax: the accuracy forfeited when errors move together, so that adding members adds votes without adding information. My claim is that most popular appeals to the theorem are really appeals to the dividend, and that the live empirical question for any actual group is whether the tax is small enough to leave a dividend at all.

To make this concrete I use a toy case, and this is where I most want your scrutiny. Suppose each member is right with probability 0.6. Under independence, majority rule over three members is right about 0.648 of the time, and over five members about 0.683 of the time — modest gains, but gains, and they compound as the group grows. Now suppose the same 0.4 error rate is not idiosyncratic but shared: the members are wrong together 40 percent of the time and right together 60 percent of the time, with no independent noise layered on top. Then the majority is right exactly 0.6 of the time no matter how many members are seated; the curve that was rising is flat, and sheer size is worth nothing. I take this to show that the theorem's engine is not aggregation as such but the decorrelation of errors, and that a group can be as large as you like and still be no wiser than a single one of its members.

I then carry the argument into machine learning, where ensembles and multi-agent pipelines are frequently justified by exactly this reasoning, and into the deliberation literature, where I claim that discussion can raise individual competence while simultaneously raising correlation, leaving the net effect on group accuracy theoretically ambiguous and empirically undetermined.

What I need from you is this. First, is the toy model's arithmetic correct, and is the flat-line case fair, or does it stack the deck by stipulating that the shared error is the only error? Second, is the independence dividend / correlation tax distinction genuinely useful, or is it a rebranding of a point that is wholly standard in the literature? If it is standard, say so plainly, because I would rather not present a commonplace as a discovery. Third, did Condorcet himself recognize the independence requirement, or does my framing credit him with a sophistication he did not have? Fourth, does the leap from juries to AI ensembles overreach? And finally, tell me which paragraph to cut and which to expand, and flag anything I have asserted that a statistician would wince at.
