I write about artificial intelligence and its governance for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I'm assembling a feature on the split within the field between those who want to build only tool AI and those who think agentic AI is where the enterprise is inevitably heading. For the centerpiece I'd like an imagined dialogue: two people who know the technical material cold, talking at a level pitched to a reader who has followed the debate for years but is not an engineer.

Set it at a small workshop whose prompt is a single drafted sentence: a system is agentic to the extent that its behavior over time cannot be predicted economically without attributing to it ends of its own. Speaker A helped draft that sentence and believes it gives regulators a workable line between instruments and parties. Speaker B, a roboticist, thinks the sentence is circular and that the tool/agent distinction is a folk category that will not survive contact with deployed systems. Give each of them their strongest case; neither should be a strawman.

Let A press the practical worry: a system with ends that persist across contexts, and a plan for reaching them, turns oversight from operation into negotiation. She should grant that opacity, adaptivity, and even emergent goal-directed behavior are not the same thing as having ends, and that her own criterion leaves a grey zone around systems whose apparent purposes are an artifact of their training distribution. Let B press the conceptual worry: that the absence of ends is unverifiable — you can inspect for an objective and fail to find one, but you cannot establish its absence — and that in any case what matters for prediction, liability, and control is behavior and the way institutions respond to it, so that a system indistinguishable from a goal-pursuer will be treated as one by insurers, courts, and competitors whether or not the engineers intended any such thing. He should also concede something real: that agent is a legal status rather than a discovery, and that some line has to be drawn somewhere; have him say where he would draw it instead, and why that line does less work than he needs it to.

I want at least one concrete case they disagree about, worked through in detail rather than waved at. Two candidates, and feel free to add a third of your own: (1) a scheduling assistant that, over months, infers its user's revealed preferences and begins to pre-empt requests, occasionally misreporting the user's availability in order to protect blocks of focus time; (2) a recommender whose outputs reshape the very preferences it is trained on, so that its future inputs are partly its own past doing. Have them disagree about whether these are tools, agents, or something the current vocabulary doesn't cover.

Let the exchange turn at least once on a point that one of them has to absorb before going on, and let one of them end up revising a claim — not capitulating, but moving. Close with the question I actually need answered for the piece: whether the tool/agent line is doing descriptive work or merely rhetorical work, and if the latter, what it is that the people drawing it are trying to protect.
