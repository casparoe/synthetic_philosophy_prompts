I'm an undergraduate in my third year, taking a seminar on the metaphysics of time alongside a course in the philosophy of physics, and the reading that has stuck with me is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the one that runs from the relativity of simultaneity to the conclusion that the future is as real as the present. What I keep circling back to is the small-scale version of it: two people passing each other on a street, one walking toward Andromeda and one away, whose present moments on Andromeda are days apart, so that for one of them a fleet has already set out and for the other the decision to send it has not yet been taken. I have to hand in a creative piece for the seminar and I want it to be a poem, because the thing I am trying to get across — that the same event is past, present, or future depending on where you are standing, and that this is not a fact about the event — is a matter of perspective in the literal sense, and I think a poem can enact that in a way an essay cannot. I do not want the poem to be a physics lecture, but I also do not want it to be a slogan: anyone in the seminar should be able to check that I have the argument right, and not merely that I have named it.

So here is the shape I am working with, and I would like you to write the poem. Roughly twenty lines, rhymed or unrhymed as seems best, but built around a refrain that returns once per stanza: the same sentence about the fleet, except that its temporal marker shifts each time it comes back — already, then now, then not yet — so that by the fourth or fifth return the reader sees that what has changed is not the fleet but the vantage point. The poem should be spoken from the street, by the two walkers in turn or by one who keeps changing his mind about which of them he is; I want the last two lines to be a single line that both of them could say, and I want the poem to arrive — without ever stating it flatly — at the thought that "now" behaves like "here": not a property the events have, but a relation to someone. It should be clear that the fleet is neither visible nor knowable to anyone on the street, and that nothing here turns on what can be seen.

One more thing, and it matters more to me than the rest. I do not want a poem that simply delivers the block universe as a verdict. Give one stanza to the dissenting voice — the reader who thinks the argument cheats, because it takes for granted that an event counts as real only if it is simultaneous with somebody's now, and who insists that the present is a local matter and not a slice drawn across the whole of the universe. And let the ending leave that objection standing rather than resolving it in either direction, since that is honestly where our seminar ended up: two people who agree about the physics and disagree about what it leaves us with. A philosopher of physics should find nothing to wince at, and someone who has never heard of the argument should still find it a poem worth rereading.
