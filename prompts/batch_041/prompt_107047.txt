I've been reading about the replication crisis for a while now and theres something about how everyone talks about it that I can't square, and I can't tell if it's just sloppy language or an actual position people are taking.

So the basic setup, in the fields I follow: you run a study, you get p under .05, you get to say you found an effect. p over .05 and you say you found nothing and it never gets published. Then when someone tries to repeat the study and also gets p over .05, everyone declares the original a false positive, meaning the effect was never real and the first guys just got unlucky.

But the same textbook that says you can never accept the null hypothesis, you can only fail to reject it, is the same crowd that then treats a failed replication as proof the effect is zero. And when a repeat gets a smaller effect than the original but the interval still doesn't touch zero, people say the effect shrank or wasn't as big as claimed, as if there's a true size out there that we keep revising. So is an effect a yes or no fact, or a probability, or a number that wobbles? I feel like the field switches between all three depending on what's convenient.

The other thing that bugs me: everybody agrees .05 is arbitrary, but journals and drug regulators and my old stats teacher all act like .049 and .051 are different in kind, and the regulators basically want the same result twice before they'll believe anything. And now there's talk of journals banning p-values entirely and demanding effect sizes and confidence intervals instead, but a confidence interval is the same arithmetic as the test, just reported differently, so I don't see how that changes the underlying problem.

Oh and here's the one that really gets me: it's cheating if you keep collecting data and stop when it finally looks significant, but if you take that exact same data and work out how much your beliefs should shift, suddenly it's fine and the peeking doesn't matter. Why would the same numbers be tainted one way and clean the other?

So what I want to know is: what does this whole machine actually presuppose about evidence and about probability? Does it secretly assume every study is a yes/no test of whether an effect exists at some fixed size? Is it consistent with itself, given that nobody thinks the null is ever exactly true? And if I really committed to the view that a study should just tell me how much to change my mind given what I already thought, what would have to change in how studies are designed, reported, and whether they get published at all? Or does that view just dress up the same problems in new math?
