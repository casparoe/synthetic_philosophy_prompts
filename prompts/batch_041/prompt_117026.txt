I've been in a fortnightly reading group on rule-following for about a year now — we started with the later Wittgenstein and Kripke's reading of him, and we still haven't gotten out of the woods — and I've come to think the whole thing is stalled for reasons that have nothing to do with who's right. The lineup is: (a) the skeptic, who says there is no fact about me meaning addition rather than the deviant function that agrees with addition on every sum I've ever actually performed but comes apart on some bigger one, and that all that's left is a non-factualist story about how we correct each other; (b) the dispositionalists, who think meaning is grounded in what I'm disposed to do, patched up so it's my idealised or counterfactually robust dispositions rather than my actual shaky ones; (c) the people who say all of that is beside the point because meaning is a primitive normative fact — correctness isn't underwritten by anything about me or my community, it's just what the rule says — and (d) the communitarian/assertibility crowd, who think the skeptic's demand for a fact was illegitimate from the start. Every argument on every side has been on the table since about month three. (b) and (c) in particular never move an inch, and I've noticed that when someone presses them they don't so much defend their view as restate it in a louder voice.

My hypothesis — tell me if it's the right one — is that they aren't disagreeing about the skeptical argument at all, but about what the thought experiment is a thought experiment about. (a) and (b) treat it as a question about which facts or states underwrite correctness; (c) and (d) treat it as a question about what it takes to be entitled to go on, or about whether the request for a justifying fact was ever coherent. If that's the diagnosis, then the standoff is partly verbal and partly a disagreement about what a satisfying answer to a philosophical question is even supposed to look like. But there's a second thing I want you to weigh, because it's the one place where I suspect the setup of the case is doing hidden work. The skeptic's argument seems to need my past to be finite — finitely many applications, so that some rival function can fit all of them and diverge only later. One of us keeps pushing a variant where the person is stipulated to have an infinite past, or where the community includes a being with unlimited computational power and full knowledge of everyone's actual usage. The dispositionalists say that changes everything; the primitive-normativity people say it changes nothing because the question was never about my dispositions; the assertibility people say it's a distraction, since the original worry was about what justifies me now, not about what some ideal being could know.

So: what kind of disagreement is this really, and is my hypothesis right, or is there a hidden formal question — something about finitude, or about what counts as a fact — doing the real work underneath? I don't want a survey or a who-said-what; I want a diagnosis, and then an honest answer about what if anything would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that nothing will ever move it because there's nothing there to move. And tell me which side, if either, is asking the better question.
