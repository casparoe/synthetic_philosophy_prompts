My father-in-law, Ray, spent thirty-one years in the state corrections system, most of it as a warden at a maximum-security prison, and I have to give his eulogy on Saturday. My mother-in-law asked me to do it. I am a software engineer with no philosophy training, so I have been trying to read my way into what Ray actually believed, and here is the closest I can get. He was, in the vocabulary I have picked up, a retributivist: he thought the only thing that justifies punishing someone is that they did something that deserves it, and that whether the punishment scares anyone else or rehabilitates anyone is beside the point, a happy side effect at best and never the reason. He was also one of the gentlest people I have ever known. He knew every man's name on his tiers, he ran a literacy program for two decades, and he would have been genuinely angry if anyone praised those things as crime-reduction measures. A friend of mine who teaches philosophy told me the distinction I was groping for is the one between what justifies a punishment and what constrains how a punished person may be treated, and that a person can hold a desert-based justification while insisting that the constraints on treatment are absolute. Ray lived in exactly that space. He also opposed the death penalty, which annoyed several of his own colleagues, and I do not want the eulogy to make that look like a contradiction in him.

The room will be roughly three parts. Family, including a devout branch that will want the usual comforts about where he is now. Uniformed colleagues from the department, who will want to hear that the work mattered and that he kept people safe. And a group of men he taught to read, several of whom are still incarcerated and are being allowed to attend. Please write the eulogy, about a thousand words, plain-spoken and warm, pitched at people who are not philosophers. The hard part is that it has to comfort a grieving room while saying nothing Ray would have considered false, and while resisting the three justifications he rejected: I do not want deterrence smuggled in as praise, I do not want the literacy program recast as something that worked, and I do not want his life summarized as a good man who did good by fixing bad men. If a life like his can be honored at all, and I assume it can or I would not be asking, I would like the eulogy to show me how, and to be honest about the parts of him that do not resolve neatly.
