I am a professor of philosophy — ethics and political philosophy, with a long-standing side interest in decision theory and the philosophy of technology — and I am assembling an application for a twelve-month fellowship at a centre for ethics and technology. The deliverable, if I get it, would be a working paper of roughly fifteen thousand words plus a short brief pitched at people who actually sit in deployment and procurement meetings. I have a half-formed idea that I would like you to turn into a two-page research proposal. Please do not spend any of the two pages on citations or a literature survey; I will supply the references myself, and I would rather have the conceptual architecture than a reading list.

The idea is this. In current discussion of advanced AI development, two claims get stated as though they were straightforward empirical findings, and I suspect both are misdescribed in a way that quietly does argumentative work. The first is the alignment tax: the claim that making a system safe, interpretable, or honest costs capability. But a cost is only a cost relative to a metric and a risk attitude, and the metric usually in play — a point estimate of benchmark performance — is precisely the one under which safety looks most expensive. If one treats capability instead as a portfolio whose value includes variance reduction and option value (the worth of not having spent an irreplaceable resource, such as public trust, on a bad deployment), then some safety work looks like a hedge rather than a tax, and conceivably a negative tax. I want to know whether there is a principled, non-question-begging way to settle which metric is the right one, or whether that choice bottoms out in substantive moral claims about what we are trying to preserve — in which case the alignment tax is not an empirical quantity at all. The second claim is the competitiveness constraint: the assertion that, whatever one's own preferences, one cannot unilaterally slow down or spend heavily on safety because others will not. I think this is ambiguous between a hard constraint — a feature of the world that binds regardless of anyone's beliefs — and a prediction about others' conduct that is partly constituted by the beliefs of those who take it to be binding. If the latter, the constraint is an equilibrium phenomenon, and the live question becomes what kind of game it is: an assurance game, a prisoner's dilemma, or a coordination problem with several equilibria, since these differ sharply over whether unilateral restraint is futile, cheap talk, or exactly the move that selects the good equilibrium. I also suspect that much of the felt force of the constraint is precautionary rather than competitive — it concerns a small probability of a catastrophic outcome, not the loss of a race — and that recognising this shifts who bears the burden of proof and what would count as adequate evidence.

Turn this into a proposal for the fellowship. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; if it has to be split into two or three sub-questions, say so and say which one is load-bearing and which are subordinate. I need an account of why the existing positions on each side leave the question open rather than settled, without that account degenerating into a survey. I need a method: what I would actually do with twelve months, month by month — what distinctions I would try to draw, what cases and counterexamples I would test them against, what would show that a distinction is doing no work, and whether any of it can be formalised. I am willing to include a small game-theoretic or decision-theoretic model if it earns its place, but not one that merely restates the intuition in symbols. I need to know what a good outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the honest conclusion is that both framings are perfectly serviceable and I have simply been confused by a metaphor. And I need a candid assessment of scope: where I am likely to overreach, and what I should cut first when the year runs short. Write it as first-person prose I can edit, and assume the readers are philosophers and computer scientists who will be irritated by hand-waving in either direction.
