I'm slowly writing a long thing about modal epistemology and I keep coming back to the non-Euclidean geometry business as the real test case, so let me just spell out what I want from you.

The thesis I care about is the ordinary one: if a scenario is conceivable, that's defeasible evidence that it's possible. I want the history of non-Euclidean geometry -- and one twentieth-century cousin -- run through that thesis, to see whether the history supports it, undercuts it, or turns out to be a case the thesis was never built to handle.

To keep us honest about what "conceivable" is supposed to mean, use this three-way distinction. I made it up, just take it as given:
- grade 1: I can't help but picture it the other way. Pure failure of imagination -- the negation won't stay in my head.
- grade 2: I can hold the negation before my mind, describe it at some length, and nothing in the description strikes me as incoherent, though I can't build it either.
- grade 3: I can actually construct a structure that satisfies the constraints in play and in which the negation holds.

Now the stages I want mapped. (a) The Kantian stage, where Euclidean geometry is supposed to be a priori true of space, so the negation isn't even a candidate. (b) Bolyai and Lobachevsky working out the alternative geometry and treating it as something like an "imaginary" or formal exercise. (c) The model-building that came later -- Beltrami, Poincare, Klein -- which I take to be where the possibility claim actually gets earned. (d) Riemann's manifolds and then general relativity, where the alternative geometry stops being merely possible and gets to be actual. (e) G"odel's rotating universes -- the ones with closed timelike curves -- where he took the mere existence of a solution to the field equations to show relativity doesn't rule out time travel.

For each stage: which grade of conceivability was actually available to the people there, which grade the thesis needs in order to deliver a possibility verdict worth having, and what did the real work in getting to that verdict -- conceivability, felt coherence, a constructed model, or observation. The thing I most want to know is whether any stage ever moved from a grade-2 verdict to a real possibility claim without a grade-3 upgrade, and if not, what that does to the thesis. Because my suspicion is that the only reliable grade delivers consistency and not much else, which would make the thesis true but idle -- and if that's where we land, say what it does to the zombie argument, since that's the case everyone actually cares about. Where a stage just doesn't bear on the thesis, say so rather than forcing it.

Don't bother naming names or citing anything, I know the literature, I want the reasoning. If you're shaky on any of the history, flag it instead of fudging it.

Then give me a table at the end, one row per stage, with these columns: stage | possibility claim at stake | grade of conceivability in play (1/2/3) | what actually earned the possibility verdict (conceivability / coherence / model / observation) | verdict for the thesis here (supports / undercuts / silent). Add a row for any stage you think I've missed. Under the table, a short paragraph with the bottom line: the weakest link in the thesis, and whether the history is an argument for it, against it, or neither.
