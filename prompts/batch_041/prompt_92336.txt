I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on probability and metaphysics, 14 students, one 90-minute session, and I want to run an activity on the self-sampling vs self-indication split -- the thing where one side says you should reason as if you're a random observer drawn from the actual observers there are, and the other says the sheer fact that you exist is itself evidence that there are lots of observer-slots you could have occupied. I want them to feel the force of both readings before anyone names them, and to end up staring at the presumptuous philosopher (the armchair argument that the universe must be enormous). No lecturing up front.

Heres my rough plan, cut it up as much as you like. I have a bag of 1000 identical blank poker chips. Round one: I number them, 1-10 in one pile and 1-1000 in the other, flip a coin to pick a pile, draw one chip and show it, and they bet on which pile. Seeing 847 is obviously the big pile, seeing 6 is a slight nudge to the small one -- this is just to get them fluent in reasoning as a random chip. Round two: same two piles, but now every chip is blank. Coin picks a pile, I draw a blank chip and hold it up. Bet small pile or big pile? Now there's nothing to go on, and I want that disagreement to actually happen in the room -- half of them will say it's a coin flip and nothing changed, and some will say wait, there were 1000 chips in one pile and 10 in the other, so I'm way more likely to have been pulled from the big one. Round three: I write 10^10 and 10^20 on the board as the two possible numbers of people in the universe, same blank evidence, and ask what they'd now stake on it. If you buy the round-two reasoning you should be near-certain the universe is the big one, which is the presumptuous philosopher staring back at them.
