I am drafting a specification for a theory of individual rights within collective decision-making: an account of which choices must remain with the individual regardless of how the rest of us feel about them, and of how such protected spheres are to be traded off against aggregate welfare and against one another. Before I examine any particular candidate, I want the desiderata — the full list of what any adequate theory of this kind has to deliver. Fix the purpose for me as follows: the theory is meant to guide the design of a bill of rights that must survive real preference profiles, not merely the tidy ones.

Set out the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. I expect the list to cover at least three groups. First, the cases the theory must get right — including the two-person case in which one person's reading habits meet another person's meddlesome preference, and the case of the worker who would accept a hazardous job for a wage the rest of us find unconscionable, where his consent looks like a Pareto improvement over his unemployment yet most third parties would forbid the bargain. Second, the distinctions it must not collapse — between having a right and having that right be decisive; between a right that protects its holder and one that protects third parties; and between rights that can be waived or exchanged and rights that cannot. Third, the formal results it must not contradict — that social preference be an ordering, that weak Pareto hold, and that the domain of admissible preferences be unrestricted.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of these requirements that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and state the conflict as a consequence of the list rather than as an objection to any single theory — if the list is itself an impossibility result, say so plainly. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and which of the requirements is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one camp. Finish with a short scorecard: how minimal-liberalism, rights-as-side-constraints, and straightforward welfare aggregation fare against the list, and precisely where each fails.
