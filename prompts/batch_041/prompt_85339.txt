I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop my foundation is hosting on religion, secularization, and trust, and I'd like you to write them for me.

The room is about forty people in a hotel conference room. A third of them study religion for a living — sociologists, a couple of anthropologists — a third are donors and program officers from secular philanthropies like mine, and the rest are clergy, journalists, and two people who run religiously affiliated charities. The workshop's title, which my co-chair and I chose before we had thought hard about it, is Who Keeps the Promise Now? Religion, Secularism, and the Bonds of Trust. My remarks run about ten minutes and open the first morning; everything after them is other people's sessions, and I would rather set them up with a question they can actually work on than with a tour of a debate they already know.

Which is the problem. The obvious speech is a recitation of the usual positions: the religious give more money and more time; the effect may be in-group only and may come bundled with suspicion of outsiders; the correlation may be selection rather than causation; Scandinavia exists. I don't want that speech. I want the remarks to do something narrower and harder — to make the audience see that the question in our title is at least three questions wearing one coat, and that each one asks for a different kind of evidence and a different sense of the word better.

Here is the reframing I have been carrying around. You should feel free to sharpen it, split it differently, or replace it if you think it's wrong, but keep the job it does. First question: do people who practice a religion behave, on the whole, in ways outsiders would call better? Second: does the practice make them behave that way, or does it attract and retain people who already would? Third, and the one I think we have been avoiding: if it does make them behave that way, what is doing the work — the teaching, the ritual, the community, the fear of being watched, or simply having a weekly appointment with people who would notice your absence? Call the last answer the scaffolding hypothesis if you need a name for it. I suspect it is the most interesting one, partly because it implies that the moral content of a tradition may be the least of what the tradition contributes, and partly because it suggests a secular society could rebuild the scaffold out of different materials — at which point someone has to say what those materials are.

Then there is the word better, which I want handled honestly rather than resolved. A religious community's own yardstick is partly internal: it measures fidelity to a code the tradition supplies, and it will not be impressed by a study showing that its members are no kinder to strangers than anyone else. Our yardstick is external and mostly about behavior toward people who cannot reciprocate. Those are not the same instrument, and I want the audience to feel that discomfort — including the discomfort that we secular funders smuggle our own conception of the good into what we present as neutral measurement, which is our own version of the mistake we spend our time accusing other people of.

The second half should turn on trust, because that is what the workshop is really about. I want a distinction the room can carry into the next two days: between trusting someone because you can predict them — because the incentives, the contract, the reputation, or the law will hold them in line — and trusting someone because you are counting on their goodwill, on the fact that they would have to be talked into letting you down. The first kind scales; markets and states manufacture it in enormous quantities, and I suspect it is what many people mean when they say trust has collapsed, when what they mean is that only the first kind is left. The second kind is what small communities, religious or not, seem to produce, and I have not been shown a secular institution that produces it at scale. If you think that is wrong, the speech is a good place to say so and to name the counterexample.

Constraints. You have no citations and should not reach for any — no studies, no scholars' names, no statistics, because I cannot defend a number from a lectern and half this room knows the literature better than I do. Argue from the concepts and from what everyone in the room has already lived through. Do not settle the empirical question; if you must stake something, stake it in a single sentence and say plainly what would change your mind. Do not preach to the secular third or wink at the religious ones — a speech only half the room can accept has failed, and both halves will notice which half you are playing to.

End by costing us something. My foundation has been funding secular substitutes for the things congregations do — community, ritual, mutual aid, the weekly appointment — and I want the last minute to make the audience, and me, feel the size of the assumption underneath that work: that the scaffold can be rebuilt without the doctrine, and that if it cannot, we will have spent a decade discovering it. I would rather the room leave uneasy and specific than reassured and vague.

Give me the speech itself and nothing else — no framing note, no options, no list of what you changed. Write it in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables. It is a script I will read aloud, and anything that looks like a slide will make me sound like I am reading a slide.
