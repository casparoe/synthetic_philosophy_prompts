I am a graduate student in a metaphysics seminar, and we have just finished a unit on the problem of universals. I can rehearse the standard positions — realism about universals, resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, trope theory — and I can recite the usual formula about "one over many," but I have come to suspect that I understand the shape of the debate without understanding the specific conceptual pressure that keeps it alive. What I would like from you is a careful, self-contained explanation of one particular knot in the dialectic, which I take to be where the real action is.

Take the resemblance nominalist's proposal: a general term such as "red" applies to a collection of things just in case those things all resemble one another, and there is nothing further — no shared universal — that needs to be postulated to explain why the term applies. The trouble I keep running into is that resemblance is always resemblance in some respect: two objects may resemble each other in color, or in shape, or in size, and the nominalist cannot simply say "they resemble each other" without quietly helping himself to a respect. But a respect appears to be precisely the kind of shared, repeatable entity — the universal — that the nominalist set out to eliminate. When I raised this with a classmate, she suggested that the respects themselves can simply be treated as further classes of resembling objects, so that nothing is smuggled in; I could not tell whether that is a genuine escape or merely postpones the problem by one step, and I would like you to adjudicate it. Please explain, as carefully as you can, exactly where the circularity worry bites and what the most promising replies available to the nominalist are.

There is a second, sharper puzzle I would like folded into the same explanation. Consider whether there could be a collection of objects such that every member resembles every other member, and yet there is no single feature that they all share. If such a collection is possible, then the resemblance nominalist's proposed definition of a property — the F-things being exactly those that all resemble one another — would count a genuine property where intuitively there is none, since the pairwise resemblance would be "borrowed" from different respects in different pairs. I would like you to work through whether such a collection is possible, construct the clearest concrete example you can, and say precisely what it shows about the resemblance nominalist's definition. Throughout, keep the discussion self-contained and grounded in the concepts themselves rather than in any particular author's formulation, and end with a single sentence, on its own line, beginning "Bottom line:", stating in plain terms what the deepest conceptual problem for resemblance nominalism turns out to be.
