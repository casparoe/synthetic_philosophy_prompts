I'm in the middle of a seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of mind, and I have staked out a claim in my paper proposal that I would like you to test. The claim is that L. A. Paul's account of transformative experience -- the argument of her 2014 book Transformative Experience, and of the paper whose title plays on the phrase about what you can't expect when you're expecting, namely that some choices are ones in which you cannot know what the experience will be like until you have had it and cannot know in advance what you will value once it has changed you, so that the ordinary comparison of outcomes by expected value is unavailable -- has a case it was never built to handle. A classmate of mine thinks the whole framework is just a grand name for uncertainty, and I would like to be able to say something more precise than that. The case I want to use is the long-running public dispute over cochlear implantation in deaf children: the argument that has been running for decades between clinicians and most hearing parents on one side and much of the Deaf community on the other, about whether to implant a deaf child early, about whether deafness is a condition to be corrected or a culture to be preserved, and about who has the standing to decide on behalf of a child who cannot decide for herself. I do not want a verdict on implantation, and I do not want the audiological literature summarized; I want the mapping between theory and case to be explicit, and I want it to run in both directions.

Begin with the theory applied to the case. Who, exactly, is the chooser here? Paul's apparatus is built for a first-person decision, in which the person who bears the ignorance is the person who will undergo the change; in the standard case of implantation the chooser is a hearing parent while the person transformed is the child, and I want you to say precisely what work the framework can still do once the chooser and the transformed party come apart, and what it cannot. Separate the kinds of ignorance in play -- the parent's ignorance of what it is like to be deaf, the parent's ignorance of what it will be like for this particular child to hear, the child's own ignorance, and the awkward fact that the child's later preferences will be partly manufactured by the choice itself -- and tell me which of these the argument actually turns on and which are merely adjacent to it. Then say what the theory implies about the alternatives, especially the option of waiting until the child is old enough to decide for herself, given that delay is itself costly and at least partly irreversible, and whether the framework's characteristic advice about how to choose under transformative ignorance survives transplantation to a decision made on someone else's behalf and contested by a third party who claims to speak for that someone else's good.

Then reverse the direction and tell me what the case does to the theory. Does it confirm Paul's account, by supplying an unusually clean instance of the phenomenon she describes? Does it complicate the account, on the ground that her central examples, in which the chooser and the changed person coincide, conceal a structural feature of surrogate transformative decisions? Or does it deflate the account, if the case can be handled perfectly well by ordinary reasoning about a child's interests, in which case the theory was doing less work than it advertised? Finally, be candid about the parts of the dispute that the framework cannot reach at all -- the cultural and political claim that a community rather than a family owns the question of what kind of life a deaf child should have, and the suspicion that the very description of the options, as a life of hearing or a life of deafness, has already been shaped by the parties to the dispute. I would much rather have a short and exact account of where the theory runs out than a long one in which it is stretched to cover everything.
