i'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, trying to pin down what i probly mean when i say a system has "the frame problem." here are my gut calls, and i want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

- A thermostat: no frame problem. It only tracks temperature, and nothing else is even in its world.
- A chess engine: no frame problem, even though it searches a huge tree. The board is fully described, a move changes exactly the squares it says it changes, and nothing else is representable.
- A robot vacuum with a map and a planner: yes, big frame problem. When it moves forward, it has to know the couch didn't move, the battery didn't teleport, etc., and nobody wrote all that down.
- A SQL transaction: i want to say no, because ACID and the transaction log do the frame work for it. But that feels like saying it doesn't have the problem because it has a solution to it, which is different.
- A bureaucracy: yes, and this is where i think the frame problem is really about more than AI. A form says "report any change in address"; the clerk also has to know that changing your address doesn't change your name, marital status, etc., and the rulebook never says that.
- A large language model writing a long story: no, because it predicts tokens and doesn't maintain a world state. But if you ask it to keep a character's eye color straight across 10,000 words, it fails in a way that feels frame-problem-ish. I keep going back and forth.

My tentative principle: a system has the frame problem when it must explicitly represent what does not change in order to update what does, and that representation isn't given by the problem itself. That makes the chess engine a no and the SQL transaction a yes (the log is explicit), which i like, but it also makes the vacuum a no if its map is just a grid and the planner only updates the cells it touches -- and i think the vacuum still has it. So maybe it's about open vs closed worlds? But the bureaucracy and the human are open-world, the vacuum is closed-world, and i don't think that's the right cut either.

What principle, or small set of principles, actually captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? What does it say about a self-driving car that has to know a plastic bag on the road isn't a rock, or that the truck ahead hasn't turned into a tunnel? And please say how confident you are in the principle you land on and which single claim in your answer you're least sure about.
