Ok here's a view I've basically talked myself into and I want you to try to knock it over: the agricultural revolution was a bad deal for the people who lived through it and for a long stretch after, and the hunter-gatherer affluence picture is roughly right -- foragers worked less, ate better, were healthier, and farming was less a discovery than a trap. Not that some cabal imposed it, just that once you're sedentary and there are more of you, you can't go back, and each generation inherits the worse version of the deal.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this. And I want three claims kept apart, because I suspect they have very different exposure:

1. Per-head welfare: the average individual in an early farming village had a worse life than the average individual in a foraging band in the same region.
2. The aggregate: even if (1) is true, farming is what made the enormous later population possible, so if you sum welfare over all the people who exist, farming may win. Tell me whether that comparison is even coherent given that the extra people only exist because of the farming -- nobody is worse off, there's just more of us -- and whether I'm quietly switching between two different questions when I move between (1) and (2).
3. The inequality claim: farming didn't create hierarchy so much as let it get big and hereditary. The roots are in sedentism -- storage, a defensible surplus, property in land -- not in domestication as such. I'd like to know what the archeaological record would have to look like for that to be false, and whether it's a real empirical claim or just me relocating the thesis from 'agriculture' to 'sedentism' so it survives whatever the bones say.

For each of the three, give me what counts against it, ranked by how much it should actually move me, and separate the ones a defender would themselves concede from the ones they'd just deny. I'm not interested in defeaters I can wave off by adding 'on average, controlling for X'. Also tell me what my view predicts about the record that a rival -- say, 'early farming was grim but that's just what the bones of the poor look like, and foragers were no picnic either' -- doesn't predict, and whether any of my three claims has quietly become unfalsifiable as I've stated it. Claim 2 feels like the suspect.

Two things to take seriously rather than bracket. First, the evidence: I know the health findings are real (the teeth, the anaemia, the growth-arrest lines in the bones), but I've also heard the forager leisure numbers are extrapolated from a handful of groups and possibly romanticised. If the affluence picture is shaky, does the 'mistake' claim survive on the inequality and coercion grounds alone, or does it shrink to just 'early farming was grim'? Second, the possibility that the honest answer is 'the question is bad' -- that per-head welfare comparisons across ten thousand years are so theory-laden (what counts as a life going well, how you weigh infant mortality against chronic hunger, whether you count the children who never made it) that there's no fact of the matter and my view is a mood with footnotes. If that's where this lands, say so, and say whether that's a defect in the question or in my answer to it.
