Write me a fake auction-catalogue entry for a middling regional auction house — deadpan, full house style, right down to the coy "attributed to" hedging and the paragraph thanking people by name — for a painting the catalogue freely admits is a forgery. Around 700 words: the lot description, the provenance paragraph, the condition report, and the specialist's note explaining how the estimate was arrived at. Make up the artist and make up the forger; I dont want it to be about a real case, and I dont want the catalogue to wink even once. It should be indistinguishable from a real one until you notice what it's actually saying.

The jokes have to come out of the arguments, so here's the material I want it quietly running on. A forgery can be a perfect painting — nothing you can see, with your eyes or under a microscope, tells you who made it, because being authentic isn't a visible property of the surface, it's a fact about who did what and when. Which means the whole apparatus of connoisseurship and pigment analysis in the catalogue is really a machine for inferring something unobservable. And the gap between the copy's price and the original's can't be justified by anything visible, so either it's justified by the history or it's a superstition with an invoice. Now the flip side, which I want treated just as seriously: what's wrong with a forgery isn't the object, it's the lie about a person's action, a false claim about who made it and when — which makes an honest forgery a genuinely strange thing, a lie that's been retracted and is now just a painting sitting there. And the market is a device for turning testimony into money: the provenance document, the expert's letter, the certificate. What's being sold is a story, the story is the asset, and stories can be reissued. One more thread: if the thing was believed genuine for eighty years, it's been cleaned, reframed, catalogued, hung and written about as an original, so the false belief is now genuinely part of its history — the forgery has a history of its own, and the catalogue can say so with a straight face.

Two things I want the reader to feel by the end without either being stated: that the estimate is absurd, and that something would really be lost if the hand weren't the hand. If it turns into sneering at collectors it's failed; the strong version makes you briefly sympathise with the person who still wants the original. At least one figure in the piece should raise an objection that sounds devastating and then get smoothly absorbed into the price. It should be funny to someone who's read the philosophical literature on fakes — the autographic/allographic distinction (scores get copied, canvases don't), the question of whether authenticity has value beyond what it tells you, the fact that no test could settle it — and still readable by someone who hasn't.
