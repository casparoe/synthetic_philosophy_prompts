I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, mostly moral psychology, and I've been stuck on a thing that started as a dumb argument with my roommate and has now infected my disseration chapter on manipulation. I was at a bar with my roommate and two friends from cognitive science, and we started listing cases where someone influences someone else, and my verdicts are all over the place. A salesperson who truthfully says this is the last one in stock and it'll be gone tomorrow: fine, that's persuasion, even if it's designed to make me panic-buy. A salesperson who invents the scarcity: not fine. A canvasser who tells a true story about a family losing insurance, knowing it'll make me cry and donate: I want to say fine, but less fine than ordinary argument. A canvasser who tells a true story about a family losing insurance but edits it so the family looks more sympathetic than they are, without saying anything false: not fine, maybe worse than the invented-scarcity case? A partner who says they'll be really hurt if I skip their work event, and they genuinely will be: fine? A partner who says they'll be really hurt, and they know they won't be, just to get me to come: not fine. A therapist who uses reflective listening to get me to hear how I sound and change my mind: fine. A friend who repeats my own words back to me in a mocking tone until I agree: not fine. A university that puts the salad bar at eye level in the cafeteria: fine. A casino that puts the ATM next to the slot machines: not fine. A union organizer who says the boss is taking everything and we're getting crumbs, which is basically true but omits that the company is barely solvent: I feel okay about it, though I wouldn't if a CEO said the mirror image. And then the one I keep flip-flopping on: a friend who says, sincerely, that they're worried about me because I've seemed off lately, when really they want me to stop dating someone they dislike. I think that's manipulation, but I also think it might be just friendship with an agenda?

My tentative principle is that manipulation is influence that bypasses or sabotages the target's rational agency, while persuasion addresses it. But that makes the crying canvasser manipulation, and I don't want it to be. Another friend says it's about whether the influencer would be willing to have their method made transparent, but the union organizer fails that and I still want to acquit. A third friend says it's about whether you're being used as a means to the influencer's end, but the therapist is using me as a means to my own end, so that doesn't carve it either. Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that gets most of these? I'm especially interested in what it says about the flip-flopping friend case and about cases I haven't listed, like a parent who tells a kid that the car won't start unless everyone is buckled, or a journalist who frames a true story to outrage readers. I'd like you to be upfront about how confident you are in the principle you land on and tell me the one verdict or move you're least sure about. And please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or tables -- just an email-style answer.
