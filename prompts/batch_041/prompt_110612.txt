I spent my working life in nonlinear dynamics and have only recently begun reading philosophy of history, where I keep meeting an analogy I find dubious: that history is chaotic, that a butterfly's wing becomes a dynasty's fall, that a kingdom is lost for want of a nail — and that this licenses the conclusion that the large-scale course of events is radically contingent and that structural or law-like accounts of history are therefore futile. Audit the analogy for me. Set out the correspondence table — what is meant to play the role of the state space, the initial condition, the perturbation, the divergence rate, the attractor — then say where the mapping breaks, sort the disanalogies into those that bear on the conclusion actually being drawn and those that do not, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table. Two things interest me in particular: whether "small" has any well-defined sense when applied to a perturbation of a historical state, and whether the unpredictability of a single trajectory, even granting it, tells at all against the predictability of aggregate or statistical regularities.
