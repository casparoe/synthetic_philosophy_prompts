I sit on the planetary-protection advisory panel of a national space agency, and we must settle how to classify what our returning probe will bring back next year. The instruments describe a microscopic system that maintains a membrane and an ion gradient, runs a self-sustaining metabolic cycle, and copies a template in order to reproduce — but with such fidelity that no heritable variation arises, so the population never evolves. If we declare it "life," it falls under the agency's strictest containment rule: no distribution to laboratories, sterilization of the canister on entry, and a hold on any further missions to the source body until the rule is revisited. If we declare it "non-life," ordinary sample handling applies and the science proceeds quickly. The panel has split. One faction holds that the question is settled by stipulation: "life" is a term of art, we should define it by whatever serves the containment rule's purpose, and since this thing is inert with respect to evolution it poses no biohazard and should be classed as non-life. The other insists that "life" names something real, that a definition must track the phenomenon rather than our convenience, and that a membrane-bounded, metabolizing, self-reproducing system is alive whether or not it happens to evolve — noting that we already count sterile organisms and mules as alive, so "capable of evolution" cannot be doing the work the first faction wants. Draft the written recommendation I will circulate before the vote: a concrete classification, the reasoning behind it, any conditions or review timeline attached (including what should trigger reconsideration if later analysis shows the system can vary and be selected), and a fair statement of the strongest case for the classification I am rejecting.
