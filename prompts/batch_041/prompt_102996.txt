People keep treating Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism as the same argument in different costumes — dont rip out inherited things whose purpose you can't currently see. I'm not so sure. Compare the two properly, and I want the comparison to turn on something I've been calling the recoverability assumption. Chesterton's fence reads to me like it assumes the reason for the fence is in principle findable if you actually bother to look, so the rule is just a caution about hasty reform. Burke seems to assume the reason is spread across dead generations and may never be recoverable by any living inquirer at all, which makes it less a caution and more of a standing presumption against a whole kind of reasoning. Is that a real distinction or am I forcing it? Work through (1) whether each view thinks the rationale for an institution is recoverable, (2) who carries the burden of proof, the reformer or the defender, and (3) whether either view can be pulled away from conservatism and used by a radical — say, to shield a radical institution from a moderate reformer. Finish by saying which of the two, if either, gives the better rule for a society that has genuinely forgotten why some of its institutions exist.
