ok so i need a poem for the last session of a seminar i'm running, and the topic for that week is the sphex wasp. you probably know the one: she paralyzes a cricket, drags it back to her burrow by the antennae, leaves it at the mouth, goes inside to check the nest, and if you nudge the cricket a couple of inches away while shes in there, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the mouth — and then has to go check the nest again, and you can do this to her all afternoon. cut the antennae off and she won't think to grab a leg instead. the usual reading is that she's a machine and so, probably, are you. i've also seen it argued that the anecdote is more useful as a rhetorical device than as a fact — that she does adapt after enough repeats, and that re-inspecting a nest that keeps getting disturbed might just be the sensible thing to do. i don't want the poem to settle that either way, i want it to sit in it.

what i actually want the poem to do is the harder thing: the moment where you notice you've done the same small action in the same order for years — checked the lock, checked it again, and the second check is the same check — and you can't tell from the inside whether noticing that is you stepping out of the routine or just the next step in it.

form: a pantoum, five quatrains. lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the last line of the poem is the first line of the poem, so the whole thing closes the loop on itself. rhyme if it helps, skip it if it starts sounding like a greeting card.

the rules that matter more than the form:

- the repeated lines are the routine. each one should be true of the wasp the first time it shows up and true of the person by the end, or the other way round. the words themselves don't change. only who they're true of changes.
- one stanza where a single line could be about either of them and i honestly can't tell which.
- the person needs one concrete, boring, repeated action with a check step built into it — locking up at night, a closing checklist, rereading the same paragraph to see if it's sunk in. pick one and stay with it, and don't turn it into a metaphor for itself.
- get the wasp right: the prey at the mouth of the burrow, the inspection, the nudge, the reset, the antennae. it should read like something that actually happens, not a nature documentary.
- no jargon. free will, determinism, sphexish, instinct, program, hardwired, algorithm, agency, autonomy — all banned. so is any line that explains what the poem is about.
- no moral and no last-stanza uplift. i'd rather it end unresolved.

and here's the actual point of the exercise: when the opening line comes back at the very end, change one word. just one, and it should be a word you'd have to be paying attention to catch. leave it genuinely open whether that's the loop opening or just a bigger loop closing. don't tell me which.

if you want, one line after the poem saying which word you changed. thats it — no explanation of the idea.

accurate enough that someone who knows the wasp, and knows the higher-order version of the worry, doesn't wince. and good enough as a poem that someone who's heard of neither reads it twice.
