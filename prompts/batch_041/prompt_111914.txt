I'm a third-year PhD student working on reproductive ethics, and I've managed to talk myself into something pretty close to Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence -- roughly, the view that when you're choosing between possible children (embryo selection after PGD being the obvious case, but the principle is meant to be more general than that), you have a genuine reason, maybe even an obligation, to choose the one who's expected to have the best life, and not merely the one who clears some threshold of "healthy enough." I've defended this in a seminar and in a chapter draft, and now I want to do the opposite of defending it: I want to know what counts against it. I don't mean the objections I can recite in my sleep (designer babies, Gattaca, playing God, the wealthy get better genes). I want the ones that should actually move someone who's already on board -- the ones that, if they held up, would make me give the principle up or substantially weaken the version I hold.

So, a few places I keep half-worrying and would like you to sort out. There's the standard line that not selecting the "better" embryo wrongs nobody, since the unchosen child never exists to be wronged, and the standard reply that procreative beneficence isn't a harm-based principle at all, so that objection is aimed at the wrong target -- I'd like to know whether that reply really works or whether it just relocates the problem. There's the disability-rights and expressivist family of objections, including the idea that a parent who selects against some trait is thereby committed to loving their child conditionally, and I genuinely can't tell how much of that is an argument and how much is rhetoric. There's a cluster of empirical presuppositions I'm not sure the principle can survive losing: it assumes we can know what makes a life go well, that the traits we can actually screen for are good proxies for that, and that selecting on them will reliably deliver the better life. The literature on people with serious impairments reporting life satisfaction at or near population averages seems like it should make me nervous, and so does the fact that as far as I can tell the heritability of something like life satisfaction is middling at best and the polygenic scores we have predict pretty little about how a life actually goes -- though I might be overreading that, so tell me if I'm. And then there's the structural worry, which is maybe the one that actually keeps me up: does the principle stay put, or does it either collapse into a mere permission (a reason that never obligates anyone to do anything) or slide into straight impersonal consequentialism about how much total good is in the world, at which point the repugnant conclusion is waiting for me and the "liberal" part of liberal eugenics was doing no work at all? Relatedly, I've noticed that the defenders, when pressed, tend to say things like "procreative beneficence is one reason among many" or "it's a reason, not necessarily an obligation," and I can't tell whether that's a principled refinement or whether the view has been qualified into something that can't be wrong about anything.

What I'd like back is a map of the exposure, not a survey of the literature. Rank the defeaters by how decisive they'd be if they went through -- fatal, serious, merely awkward -- and for each one say what the world or the argument would have to look like for it to actually bite, rather than just being logically possible. Tell me which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to which ones they'd wave away, and be honest about where the waving away is legitimate and where it's just evasion. I'd also like you to say plainly whether the principle, in the version that's actually defended these days, risks anything at all, and if it turns out to be pretty well insulated, whether that's a bug or just what you'd expect from a normative principle. Finally, if you had to bet on which single consideration would eventually do the view in, which would it be -- I'm partly asking because I suspect it's the empirical one, and I'd like to be told I'm wrong. Write it as continuous prose, like you're explaining it to me in an email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, please, I find that style makes me skim and I actually want to think about this one.
