Ok so I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and over the last year I've pretty much talked myself into futarchy — the idea that instead of voting directly on policies, we vote on a measurable national goal (GDP, life expectancy, whatever) and then let prediction markets pick the policy: a market opens on whether the goal will be higher in N years if we adopt policy P than if we don't, and whichever policy the market favors gets implemented. To me it's just information aggregation with skin in the game, and I trust it way more than I trust congress. But I know this is the kind of idea that sounds clean until you look closely, and I dont want to be the guy who's just pattern-matching to a LessWrong post.

So what would actually count against it? Not "markets can be wrong sometimes." I mean: what has to be true for futarchy to be a good idea, and which of those assumptions are shaky. The stuff I'm already worried about is structural, not fixable-with-a-better-UI — who picks the indicator and what happens when the indicator is gameable; whether the people with the best information are also the people who'd profit from the policy passing, so they can make money by making the prediction come true rather than predicting it; and whether a conditional market can even deliver the counterfactual it promises when you only ever get to observe one branch. Also be honest about whether this thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every failure gets waved away as "the market was too thin" or "wrong metric," is that a real defense or a dodge?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones the smartest futarchy defenders would themselves admit, and tell me what specifically would have to happen for me to give it up. Format it as: "What futarchy is actually committed to" (short, about four sentences), "The strong case against" (the bulk of it, ~600 words), "Fatal vs. awkward" (a ranked list), "What defenders concede," and "What would change my mind" (a short list).
