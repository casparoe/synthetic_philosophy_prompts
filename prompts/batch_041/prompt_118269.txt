I am trying to pin down what I actually believe about dispositions — about things being such that they would behave in a certain way under certain conditions, whether or not those conditions ever arise — and my verdicts on a run of cases have stopped fitting together. What I want from you is the principle, or the short list of rival principles, that best captures the pattern; the case that forces each part of it; what it implies for cases I have not put to myself; and a candid account of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A drinking glass on my desk. Were I to knock it to the tile floor, it would shatter. Fragile, plainly.
2. The same glass packed in foam. Were I to knock it to the floor, it would survive. I still call the glass fragile: the foam is doing the saving, not the glass growing tough.
3. A glass under an enchantment that would catch it in mid-air should it ever fall. Knocked off the shelf, it would not shatter. I still call it fragile, and I take the enchantment to be precisely why the fragility never gets to show itself.
4. A lump of lead under an enchantment that would shatter it were it struck. Struck, it would shatter. I do not call the lead fragile: the spell would do the breaking, not the lead.
5. A match soaked through with water. Struck, it would not light. I do not call it disposed to light. I think the water has taken the disposition away, not merely hidden it.
6. A match kept in a sealed case and never to be struck. I call it disposed to light all the same, though it never will.
7. A radium atom. Wait a half-life and it may or may not have decayed. I say it is genuinely disposed to decay, and that the bare possibility of its not decaying is no argument against the disposition.
8. A sugar lump that would dissolve in water only because a sorcerer standing by dissolves it himself the moment it is submerged. Placed in water, it would dissolve. I do not call it soluble: the dissolving would not be the sugar's own doing.
9. A steep stretch of trail. I say it is disposed to trip a novice but not disposed to trip an expert, and I do not think that is a contradiction — the disposition seems to me relative to the one exercising it.

The simple principle I keep reaching for is this: a thing is disposed to M when S exactly if, were S to occur, it would M. Case 2 and case 3 break it in one direction, case 4 breaks it in the other, and case 7 seems to want something other than a flat would.

So give me the repaired principle, or the competing repairs. For each clause, name the case that forces it. Say what each candidate implies for at least two cases of your own construction that I have not listed, and construct one of those deliberately to separate the leading candidates, telling me what each says about it. Then tell me squarely which of my nine verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and whether you would abandon the verdict or add a clause to save it. I am most interested in whether a single distinction accounts for case 2 against case 5, and for case 3 against case 4, or whether I have been quietly applying two different standards.
