You are a scholar of eighteenth-century French letters and moral philosophy, at home both in Molière's dramaturgy and in Rousseau's quarrel with the stage, and I would like you to think carefully before committing to a verdict. I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the Enlightenment and the theatre, and I want to pair Molière's Le Misanthrope, first performed in 1666, with the attack on it that Rousseau mounts in the Lettre à M. d'Alembert sur les spectacles of 1758, written against d'Alembert's proposal in the Encyclopédie that Geneva should have a theatre. Rousseau's case against the play is more interesting than the usual complaint about satire: he grants that Le Misanthrope is a work of genius and then argues that it is morally backwards, that Molière places an honest, upright man at the centre of the comedy and makes him the butt of the laughter, so that the audience is trained to find sincerity ridiculous and to admire the accommodating good manners of Philinte, who has learned to live with people as they are. Comedy, on this account, cannot help teaching the audience to laugh at virtue and to flatter itself; the play's wit is spent on the one character who refuses to be charming.

I want you to read Le Misanthrope through that critique, and then read the critique through the play, and I want the reading to be faithful in both directions — faithful to the play, so that it does not collapse into an illustration of a thesis Rousseau held before he opened the book, and faithful to Rousseau, so that his objection is not softened into a taste for plain manners. Begin with a short sketch of how you intend to proceed, then give me the reading, attending in particular to Alceste's verdict on Oronte's sonnet and the simple old song he praises in its place; to the scene in which Célimène and her suitors dissect their absent acquaintances; to the love letter Arsinoé uses to expose Célimène; to the lawsuit Alceste loses for having told the truth; to the guard of the Marshals of France who comes to make him answer for his criticism of Oronte's verses; and to the ending, where Alceste offers Célimène marriage on the condition that she abandon society for his solitude, she refuses, and Philinte and Éliante run off to bring him back. Two further things deserve weight. First, Molière himself created the role of Alceste at the premiere, so the man Rousseau condemns for making a living out of passions he does not feel was the man playing the honest man who cannot bear falsehood; say whether that fact is a gift to Rousseau's argument or an embarrassment to it. Second, ask whether the play dramatizes something the essay cannot capture — that Alceste's sincerity is itself staged and addressed to an audience, that Célimène's drawing room is a theatre and the court a spectacle, and that this comedy withholds the reconciliation it seems to promise.

Then turn to where the reading strains. Alceste's honesty is tangled up with vanity, with a wish to dominate, and with a love he cannot justify to himself, and Rousseau's claim that the play ridicules virtue is a stronger and stranger claim than the observation that it declines to flatter virtue; tell me which of the two the play actually supports. Is Rousseau's demand, that a comedy leave its audience admiring the honest man, a demand that comedy stop being comedy? And is his proposed alternative — the civic festival at which the spectators are themselves the spectacle — an escape from theatricality or only a relocation of it? Close by saying what Le Misanthrope contributes to this debate that argument alone does not: what it knows about the moral psychology of laughter, or about the possibility of sincerity in a society that runs on display. No citations or reading list are needed; I want your reasoning.
