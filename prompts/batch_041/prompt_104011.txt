I have spent fifteen years designing controllers for industrial processes and I hold a doctorate in control engineering. I am fluent in the language of plants and controllers, references and setpoints, open and closed loops, state estimation, model-based and model-free design, cost functionals and constraints, disturbance rejection, and stability. I have no training in philosophy and none in biology, and I would like to understand a position in the philosophy of biology that I have seen called the free-floating rationale view.

As I understand it, the view holds that a system can have a reason for behaving as it does even when nothing in the system, and nothing upstream of it, represents that reason. The reason is not held by anyone; it is lodged in the design, the organization, and the history of the system. Such reasons are supposed to be assessable — a structure or a behavior can be a better or worse response to them — and the assessment is supposed to be more than a convenient way of talking. The motivation, as I understand it, is that one can explain how a system that genuinely represents reasons could ever have come into existence only if one already allows reasons that no one represents: reasons first, reasoners afterwards.

I would like this position restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as it can be, and then an honest account of what does not survive the restatement. Take the following claims one at a time:

1. A system can act for a reason that nothing in it represents, the reason being located in its history and organization rather than in any state or model it contains.
2. Free-floating rationales are normative: they can be good or bad, and a rationale's being good is not the same as the behavior's occurring, or as its being stable or robust.
3. A free-floating rationale explains the behavior, answering why the system does this rather than that; it is not merely a redescription of what the system does.
4. Free-floating rationales are prior to represented reasons, in the sense that the emergence of reason-representing systems is explicable only against a background of reasons that no one represents.
5. A system cannot be mistaken about its own free-floating rationale, since it takes no attitude toward it at all; correctness and incorrectness have no purchase there.
6. To attribute a free-floating rationale is to describe something about the system, not to adopt an instrumental stance that could be dropped without explanatory loss.

For each claim, say whether it carries over cleanly, carries over only with distortion (and what the distortion is), or has no counterpart at all in control-theoretic terms. For the last category, say whether the missing element is a resource my vocabulary lacks — name the resource — or whether the original claim was doing no work and nothing is lost.

Then give me the residue report and a verdict: is the position one I could state, and defend, in the terms I already use, or does it depend on a notion of a reason that my vocabulary cannot reconstruct? Two things I want flagged specifically. First, any point at which the translation succeeds only because you imported an assumption the position itself rejects — in particular, a designer, or an objective that someone chose. Second, which of my ordinary habits — treating the reference as given, treating the cost functional as stipulated, treating the plant's behavior as what needs explaining and the objective as what explains it — are doing illicit work in the translation. Where my vocabulary forces a choice that the position does not force, say so rather than choosing silently.
