Having spent the better part of a career in the natural sciences, I have only lately begun to read philosophy in earnest, and I find myself preoccupied with the question of AI takeoff speeds. It strikes me as a question that is more conceptual than its practitioners often acknowledge, and I would like you to treat it as such. Please give me a single credence, followed by the decomposition that generates it.

State your probability that the following proposition is true: conditional on a system reaching rough parity with human beings across the cognitive domains relevant to science and engineering, and doing so within this century by an approach continuous with the large-model paradigm of today, the transition to a state that dominates human performance across essentially every economically and scientifically relevant domain is completed within one year, without a prolonged plateau or a perceptible deceleration along the way.

Now decompose that number. I would like a rough probability for each of the following, with one line of justification apiece, and then an account of how they combine:

1. That reinvestment of improved cognition into the design of further cognition yields increasing rather than diminishing returns in the regime that matters, rather than obeying the logistic or saturating curves I am used to from population and resource dynamics.
2. That at the threshold the binding constraints on further progress are cognitive rather than physical, logistical, or industrial — that is, that chip fabrication, energy supply, experimental apparatus, and supply chains are not the rate-limiting steps.
3. That a stock of latent, unelicited capability exists at the threshold, such that the transition consists partly in drawing out what is already present rather than in training new capability from scratch.
4. That the transition is genuinely abrupt — a discontinuity or near-discontinuity in the relevant observable quantities — rather than a fast but smooth and foreseeable process. I care about this distinction and suspect it is often elided.
5. That a process bottlenecked by the need for real-world experimentation, replication, and physical measurement can nonetheless complete within a year.
6. That the target state — dominance across essentially every economically and scientifically relevant domain — is a coherently specifiable threshold at all, rather than an artifact of our vocabulary for describing capability.

I would put my own credence near 0.15. A former colleague, now working on the safety side of these questions, puts hers near 0.7. Having given your number and your terms, tell me where our disagreement most plausibly lives — which term or terms carry it — and which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what observation or argument, available to someone reasoning without any special access to the future, would move it most.
