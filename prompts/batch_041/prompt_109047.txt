I am taking a seminar on the history of utilitarianism alongside one on population ethics, and the two have begun to bleed into one another. A position I keep encountering in the contemporary literature — though I have not seen anyone put it quite this way, so treat the label as mine — is what I have been calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. On this view, pleasure and pain remain commensurable and additive across most of their range, so that a sufficiently large quantity of pleasure can outweigh any finite quantity of mild discomfort; but there is a threshold of severe suffering such that, once an experience crosses it, no amount of pleasure elsewhere can compensate for it, because the severe pain is lexically prior. Its advocates are fond of the claim that a world containing a great deal of contentment plus a single instance of prolonged torture is worse than a world containing only the contentment, however the totals are run, and some of my classmates think a threshold of this kind is the only way to block the repugnant conclusion without adopting a wholly lexical view of the good.

I would like you to write an essay predicting what Jeremy Bentham would have made of this position. He obviously never encountered it, so the interest lies in what his actual commitments would force him to say. Reason from his hedonism and from the way his felicific calculus treats pleasures as differing along dimensions such as intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, and purity rather than in kind; from his inclusion of animals in the moral community on the ground that they can suffer; from his lifelong hostility to cruelty and to unnecessary punishment; and from his theory of fictions and his suspicion of purportedly natural entities that cannot be cashed out in experiential terms. Ask yourself where he would dig in and where he might surprise us: whether he would insist that a threshold of the sort described smuggles a non-hedonic good back into the theory, or whether his revulsion at certain kinds of suffering might lead him to accept a lexical priority he never formulated. Would he treat the torture intuition as a datum to be explained away, or as a genuine constraint on any hedonistic calculus?

Please write continuous prose throughout — no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold or italicized labels, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague. Argue rather than merely report, and where you are unsure what he would say, say so and explain what turns on it.
