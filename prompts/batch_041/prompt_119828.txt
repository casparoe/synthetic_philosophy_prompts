You are a philosopher specializing in normative ethics, especially the theory of supererogation and the boundary between moral requirement and praiseworthy but optional action. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could underlie the verdicts below.

A colleague of mine, R, has worked on these questions for years. She classifies each act as either required or supererogatory (where performing a supererogatory act is morally good but not required, and omitting it is not blameworthy). Her verdicts on earlier cases are:

- A lifeguard on duty can rescue a swimmer in moderate surf at moderate risk to herself. R says: required.
- A bystander who is an equally strong swimmer can rescue the same swimmer in rough surf at high risk to herself. R says: supererogatory.
- A parent can donate bone marrow to their child; the procedure carries moderate risk and discomfort. R says: required.
- A stranger can donate bone marrow to a child; in this case the procedure carries high risk and discomfort, and other potential donors are available. R says: supererogatory.
- A person who negligently started a kitchen fire can run back in to save a stranger at moderate risk to themselves. R says: required.
- A person can give a small fraction of their income to save a stranger's life. R says: required.
- A person can give half their income to save a stranger's life. R says: supererogatory.
- A sibling can donate a kidney to their brother; the surgery carries the usual moderate risks. R says: required.
- A stranger can donate a kidney to another stranger; in this case the surgery carries unusually high risks, and other potential donors are available. R says: supererogatory.

Now consider a new case: A stranger needs a kidney. Without a transplant, the stranger will die within a year. R is a compatible donor. The surgery would carry the usual moderate risks—the same risks as in the sibling case. R has no prior relationship with the stranger and did not cause the stranger's condition. Other compatible donors are listed in a registry, but none has yet been identified or approached.

What would R say about the new case? Identify the competing principles that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, state what each predicts for the new case, give your best guess about her verdict and the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would most cleanly tell the principles apart.
