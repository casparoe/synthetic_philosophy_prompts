I have been reading about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, and I have convinced myself that the whole idea contains a fatal dilemma. As I understand it, a Ulysses contract works by making the later self literally unable to do what it will then want to do: that is what the ropes do for Ulysses, and it is what an entrenched constitutional clause is supposed to do for a people. The analogy, however, seems to break down precisely where it matters. Constitutional clauses are almost always accompanied by an amendment procedure, and an amendment procedure is a rope with a knife taped to it — the later people can always untie itself, so nothing has really been precommitted. Remove the amendment procedure, on the other hand, and the clause truly cannot be undone, which installs the dead over the living and so defeats the very purpose of a legitimate precommitment. Either constitutional precommitment is toothless or it is tyrannical, and I can see no third possibility. Is there one? And if there is, what does it imply about a concrete case: a municipal charter that entrenches the formula for its water tariff, providing that any change to the formula — including repeal of the entrenching clause itself — must pass two successive council votes separated by an election and then a referendum? Is that a genuine precommitment or the toothless kind, and by what criterion should I tell?
