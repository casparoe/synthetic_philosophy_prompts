The question was:

Q3. Peirce and James both call themselves pragmatists about truth, and their accounts are usually said to differ.
(a) State what each thinks truth is, and identify the main point of disagreement between them.
(b) James's critics (Russell and Moore, among others) charged that his account makes truth depend on what is comforting or useful to believe. Give the strongest version of that objection, and the best reply available to James.
(c) Suppose a community of inquirers settles on a belief that pays off across their experience and would keep paying off, but the community is wiped out by a plague before any further inquiry could test the belief. Is that belief true on Peirce's account? On James's? Explain.

And the student wrote:

(a) For Peirce, truth is what the community of inquirers is fated to converge on in the long run. He calls this the final opinion, and it corresponds to the way reality actually is. For James, truth isn't a property that floats free of practice: a belief is true insofar as it works, i.e. insofar as it proves expedient across the whole of our experience. The disagreement is mainly about the role of the future community. Peirce ties truth to an idealized endpoint of inquiry; James ties it to the present and future payoff of holding the belief.

(b) The objection is that if truth just is what works, then any comfortable falsehood becomes true, which is absurd, because then the belief that makes me feel better would be a truth-maker. James's reply is that he never meant individual comfort. "Works" means coheres with the rest of our established beliefs and the evidence, over the long haul, so the objection trades on a caricature. This is the point of his Will to Believe essay, where he argues that we're entitled to believe what is true for us when the evidence can't settle the matter.

(c) On James's account the belief is true, since it pays off and there's nothing left to dislodge it. On Peirce's account it's neither true nor false, because truth is the opinion that would be agreed at the limit of inquiry, and this inquiry got cut off before the limit, so there's no final opinion to appeal to. This shows Peirce's account is more realist than James's, since it keeps truth independent of what any actual community happens to find useful.
