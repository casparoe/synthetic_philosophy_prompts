I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, but I somehow ended up on the grants committee of a small AI-safety funder (we give out about $2M a year, mostly to academic labs and two nonprofits). We vote next Wednesday on this year's batch, and I'm the one who has to circulate the written opinion that basically decides it, because the chair and I are the only two who reliably show up to the pre-meetings.

Here's the fight. We got 34 proposals and they clump into two piles. Pile A is what people around here call intent alignment: make the model actually do what the operator asked — instruction following, not getting hijacked by text it reads, not gaming the spec, flagging its own uncertainty, not quietly wandering off and doing something else. Pile B is what they call value alignment, or sometimes just "the values question": who gets to decide what the system should want, what happens when the operator is a jerk or a state, moral uncertainty, the fact that a perfectly obedient system in the hands of a bad actor is arguably the worst outcome we could build, and that "aligned to the user" is not the same thing as good.

The chair's line is that only Pile A is our business. He says value alignment is either politics or philosophy, it never terminates in an artifact, and if we fund it we're funding essays. He also says intent alignment is the only definition of the problem where you can actually write down what success looks like. My line, which I can't really defend, is that if you define the problem as "does the system do what its operator intends," you've defined the hard part out of existence — the operator might intend something monstrous, and now you've built them a very reliable weapon and called it a success. But I also can't tell him what a Pile B proposal is supposed to deliver, and honestly half of them read like they were written specifically to avoid having to say.

There's a third thing making this worse: two proposals about making a model's stated reasons match its actual reasons — some honesty/transparency angle — and I genuinely can't tell which pile they belong in, which I suspect is the whole problem.

What I need from you is the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete allocation of the $2M across the piles, or a rule for deciding if you think allocating by pile is the wrong frame entirely. Say what conditions you'd attach to Pile B grants so they aren't just essays, and say where those two honesty proposals go. Then deal honestly with the chair's strongest point — that a definition of the problem which can't be operationalized isn't a definition of a research problem — instead of just asserting he's wrong about the ethics.

Don't hedge on me. Pick a number and a rule, tell me what to say when he pushes back, and if my "defined the hard part out" move is confused, say so plainly and tell me where it goes wrong. Assume I have to defend this out loud in front of people who know the literature way better than I do, so flag anything you're asserting that's genuinely contested.
