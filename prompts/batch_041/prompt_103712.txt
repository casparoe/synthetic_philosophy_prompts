The thing that's been bugging me: alot of people quote Anscombe's line from the Truman pamphlet — "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder" — as if it settles everything. But all the work is being done by "as a means," and as far as I can tell nobody has really asked what it takes for a killing to count as a means rather than a side effect, once you stop quietly borrowing the machinery of the standard double-effect formula (the intended/foreseen split plus the proportionality clause). And I don't want to just assume she needed that formula — part of the job is working out whether her verdict on Truman can be got without it.

My hunch, which I want to make precise and then stress-test: "as a means" should be read plan-relatively, not as a fact about the agent's mental state. Roughly, the death of the civilians counts as a means for you when it's a step in the chain you're actually executing — when you couldn't drop it from your plan without the plan no longer being a plan for what you're doing — rather than something that merely runs alongside it. I think that's definately a cleaner criterion than "did you intend it," and closer to what her own account of intentional action would license.

Then I run it on the hard cases: strategic bombing of a city to wreck its war industry (deaths foreseen, industry is the target); terror bombing to break morale (deaths are the lever); and then Hiroshima specifically, where I think there are two live descriptions — the city as a target versus the shock as the point — and the verdict flips depending on which description is the right one. The interesting failure mode is if the plan-relative criterion starts condemning things Anscombe wouldn't want condemned, like the tactical bomber, in which case it's too strong and I have to say so.

So the proposal needs to: (1) state the question so it has a determinate answer — something like, does the "as a means" clause, read plan-relatively, both (a) deliver the Truman verdict and (b) stay stable across the standard bombing cases without collapsing back into the intended/foreseen distinction it's meant to replace? (2) explain why the existing positions leave this open, but as positions and arguments rather than a literature review — I'll slot the citations in myself. (3) give the method concretely, month by month: build the criterion, construct the test cases, check extensional adequacy, and figure out whether the criterion or the cases are doing the real work. (4) say what a result looks like and what would count as the project failing (if the plan-relative reading just reinstates intended/foreseen, or if it can only separate the two Hiroshima stories by begging the question). And (5) be honest about scope — is this a chapter or a dissertation, and what's the piece I can actually finish in a summer.
