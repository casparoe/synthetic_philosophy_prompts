I write literary fiction in close third person, so most of my working life goes into managing the distance between three things: what a character avows, what she actually acts on, and what the prose lets the reader infer. My tools are the ones you would expect — unreliable narration, free indirect discourse, dramatic irony, subtext — and I know them cold, though I have no philosophical training.

I would like the philosophical debate about whether self-deception is possible restated in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage: the position that the self-deceiver must in some sense be doing the deceiving, the position that the paradox dissolves once the psychology is described properly, and the positions that divide the mind so that one part of it can keep something from another. Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which of their claims survive translation into things a narrator can do on the page; which come through only distorted, and precisely what the distortion is; and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — above all, can narrative represent a character as both believing and not believing the same proposition, or does prose always have to resolve the contradiction into conflict, ambivalence, or an unreliable report? Close by saying whether the residue looks like a limitation of what narrative can express or like evidence that the philosophers are disagreeing about something thinner than it appears.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to another novelist — no headings, no lists, no bold, no tables.
