I'm a junior in high school, and for the past few months I've been going to a small philosophy club that meets in our advisor's classroom on Thursday afternoons. Last week we read about the question that's usually called Molyneux's problem: suppose someone has been blind from birth and has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch alone, and then, as an adult, gains sight. At the very first moment of seeing the two shapes, before any fumbling or learning, could they say which is the cube and which is the sphere? Our advisor had us argue it out and then refused to tell us who was right, which has been quietly bothering me ever since. What I keep circling back to is that the interesting part isn't really the yes or no — it's whether a word like "cube," learned through the fingers, even means the same thing when it arrives through the eyes, and whether there's such a thing as the same shape over and above being the same thing touched and seen. There's also a distinction I can't stop worrying at: telling the two shapes apart by sight might be a different achievement from recognizing the shapes as the very ones you had held before. For the club's end-of-year reading I'd like a poem that takes this seriously instead of picking a side.

I've settled on a form and I'd like you to hold to it. Write a two-part poem, eight lines in each part, where the two parts are forced to share the same set of end-rhymes or end-words, so that each rhyme word has to do double duty — describing the shapes in the vocabulary of touch in the first part and in the vocabulary of sight in the second. The turn between the parts is the moment the bandages come off; the rhyme scheme should make that turn land the way a volta does, and the shared end-words should be the place where the whole question lives, because if the same word can carry both ways of knowing the shape, that's one answer, and if it twists or breaks between the halves, that's the other. I want the ending to leave it genuinely open rather than resolving into a neat verdict.

It should be accurate enough that someone who has thought hard about the problem recognizes the real crux — modality-specific meaning, and the gap between telling apart and re-identifying — and not merely a poem that name-drops. Please don't name any philosopher or cite anything; it should stand on its own as a poem, readable aloud in under two minutes.
