I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole ever since a friend talked me into switching my monthly donation from the local food bank to a charity that works on what she calls existential risk — the pitch being that a small chance of saving an astronomically large number of future people is worth more than a certainty of helping a few people right now. I'm not sorry I switched, but now I keep catching my gut being wildly inconsistent about all these tiny-probability-huge-payoff things, and its bothering me. I want you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Here they are, pure gut, no theory behind them:

The switch itself, food bank to the x-risk charity: feels right, even though I'd put the odds my money does anything at maybe one in a thousand. A one-in-a-million chance of saving ten million lives versus certainly saving ten: I take the gamble, no question. The old mugger setup, where a guy says he'll torture some enormous number of people unless I hand over my wallet: I refuse, obviously, and it doesn't even feel close. But offer me a pill for $100 that gives a 1-in-10^40 chance that everyone lives in bliss forever and otherwise does nothing: I don't take it, feels like lighting the $100 on fire, and I honestly can't defend that sitting next to the ten-million-lives case. A physics experiment that, best guess, has a 1-in-a-billion chance of ending the world but would also cure a disease killing a million people a year: I say don't run it. And a plain lottery, honest published odds, 1 in 10^12 of a billion dollars, a dollar a ticket: I don't buy it — but here I feel like I'm just being irrational and a proper reasoner would buy.

The thing that really trips me up is that the lottery has the most trustworthy probability of the whole bunch, the x-risk donation has the shakiest (it's basically a guess about the future), and yet I'm most confident I'm wrong about the lottery and most confident I'm right about the donation. My half-baked attempt at a rule was something like, don't act on probabilities below some threshold unless the payoff is bounded too, but that's circular and I think it also kills the donation case, which I want to keep. Is there a principled version of this that gets all six, or close, and tells me which verdicts I have to give up? And what does it say about the one I actually can't decide: my friend wants me to quit my job to work full time on a 1-in-10^6 chance of stopping something that would end everything — is that more like the x-risk donation or more like the physics experiment?
