I am a retired experimental biologist — forty years at the bench — who has spent the past several years reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than method, and I want to rehearse a position before defending it in front of people who know the literature better than I do. Please take the role of my opponent.

The role: a committed defender of the view that no current image-generating system is creative in the sense that matters for art. Give the position real resources rather than a caricature. Let it rest on three commitments, which you may refine but must not abandon: (1) creativity is authorship — the maker must be the source of the work's constitutive decisions, not merely of the occasion for them; (2) it requires an internal standard, so that the maker's output can be wrong by the maker's own lights and not only by someone else's; (3) it requires responsiveness to reasons — the maker could have done otherwise, and for a reason. You may concede freely that these systems produce novelty, surprise, and outputs no one anticipated; you may not concede that novelty plus surprise amounts to authorship. Speak plainly, use concrete examples, keep each turn to about two paragraphs, and cite nothing — no names of philosophers, no titles, no schools. If you need a label for a view, describe it in your own words instead. Stay in character until I say stop. If I ask a factual question about how the systems actually work, answer it briefly and plainly out of character, then resume.

My opening move. In my own field we are careful with the word random: we say a mutation is random with respect to fitness, not random full stop, because the process has its own biases, its own chemistry, its own preferred directions — and none of that stops us treating the mutation as a source of genuine novelty and selection as an inventor. I claim the same structure holds here, with two separate ledgers. The novelty ledger is filled by the system's learned biases, which are as direction-laden as any mutational spectrum. The warrant ledger is filled by the human who prompts, rejects, and curates — and that human can be wrong by his own lights, which is precisely the internal standard you demand. I conclude that creativity is a division of labour, and that your insistence on finding one locus that supplies both ledgers is a prejudice inherited from the workshop. So: tell me which of my two ledgers is empty, and why the mutation is allowed to be a source of novelty while my sampler is not.
