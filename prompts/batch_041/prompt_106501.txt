In my department the phrase 'the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics' is used to end debates rather than open them: someone invokes it, and the question whether physics is describing a mind-independent world or merely organizing experience is treated as settled. I want to know what that phrase could actually be claiming. Set out, as a menu rather than a single reading, the distinct precise theses it might express — among them that we count a science as successful only once it has been mathematized; that mathematical structures developed with no physical application in view repeatedly turn out to apply; that nature is itself mathematical; that this applicability is a brute fact admitting no explanation; and that the 'effectiveness' is an artifact of our having kept only the mathematics that works — and for each say whether it is a tautology, a defensible empirical generalization, or something false or unfalsifiable. Then say which of these readings, if any, would actually license the conclusion that our theories describe a mind-independent reality, and which reading people are most likely relying on when they use the phrase to close that question.
