A colleague of mine, Dana, got into an argument with another engineer, Sam, at our team retro, and they've asked me to settle it since I stayed out of it — I don't have any philosophy background, so I'd rather hand it to someone who does. The background: we started tracking the share of pull requests merged within a day, to cut review latency. A quarter later the number is excellent, but reviewers say quality has quietly dropped, because people split work into tiny trivial PRs and rubber-stamp them. Dana says this is Goodhart's law and shows you can't manage by metrics at all: any number you target becomes something people optimize directly, so it stops measuring what you cared about. Sam says that's too strong — the metric failed because we picked a bad proxy and handed people both an incentive and an easy way to game it, and that where the person measured and the person deciding are the same, or where gaming is impossible, metrics work fine. Dana answers that any metric worth targeting creates an incentive, and anything you can compute is gameable, so "bad proxy" just means "proxy" and Sam's safe cases are empty. Sam then says some measures are the thing itself rather than a proxy, and adds that even with no gaming at all, ranking people by a proxy and promoting the top ones selects precisely for the cases where the proxy is most misleading, so the correlation can collapse on its own. Dana replies that nothing countable is ever the thing itself, and that once a measure starts driving decisions it stops being a description and becomes the goal, so asking whether it "still tracks" the real thing assumes there's a real thing left over. Please referee this point by point: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what each of them is right about, and note anything both of them are missing.
