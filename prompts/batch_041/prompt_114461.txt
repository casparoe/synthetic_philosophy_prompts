A borough council's waste-collection rules charge residents per item, which has forced the council to take a stand on the special composition question: when do some things compose something? Write the council's official guidance note on the matter, deadpan and entirely straight-faced. The council has settled on the view that things compose an object when they are held together, whether by fastening or by mere contact, and the note must work that criterion through consistently, so that the fee schedule, the treatment of a sofa whose cushions and legs are replaced one at a time, and the case of the sofa that touches the floor that touches the earth all follow from the criterion rather than from arbitrary silliness — including where the criterion leads somewhere the council would rather not go. One of the provisions should record the rival answers the council considered and the administrative grounds on which it rejected them: that nothing composes anything, that any plurality of things composes something, and that only living things compose. A metaphysician should recognise the positions being satirised, and a reader who has never met them should still follow the joke. Do not name or quote real philosophers. Give exactly five numbered provisions, each a sentence or two, with no preamble or closing remarks, in British English.
