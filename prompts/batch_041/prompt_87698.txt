ok, so I'm a fourth-year PhD student — officially in phil of science, but I basically live in an applied modeling lab that does contract work for a state natural-resources agency (coupled econ/ecology stuff, fish-stock models, harvest quotas, that kind of thing). And I keep getting the same question from the agency people every time we hand over a model: you assumed X, X obviously isn't true, so can we still use this thing to actually set a quota? Right now I answer case by case and I'm pretty sure I contradict myself between meetings, so I want a procedure I can actually run.

Background so you know where I'm coming from: this is basically the Friedman thing — unrealistic assumptions are fine as long as the predictions come out right. I definately get the appeal, but it can't be the whole story, because sometimes the assumption is doing the real explanatory work and the prediction is right for the wrong reason, and sometimes two models with contradictory assumptions predict the same number, so predictive success can't pick between them. I don't need you to relitigate the F-twist, I need it turned into something a non-philosopher can execute.

I've got a half-baked taxonomy I want you to either use or replace with something better. I think every assumption that's actually load-bearing is one of three kinds: a limit case, where the assumption is what the model reduces to as some parameter goes to zero or infinity and it's roughly true in the regime we use it in; a tractability fiction, where we know it's false and we'd fix it if we could solve the fixed model, but we can't; or a stand-in, where the thing in the model doesn't correspond to anything real at all (a representative agent, a 'population' that's just the mean of a distribution), so asking whether it's 'true' might be a category error.

What I want from you: a decision procedure I can run in maybe an afternoon on a single model, before I sign off on handing it to the agency. The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with don't use this, where it stops with fine to use, and where it stops and hands the call to a human because it's really a value judgment about what we're willing to get wrong. Two steps I care about especially: (1) for any assumption, say which direction the answer will be biased if the assumption fails — I care way more about bias direction than about whether the assumption is realistic in the abstract; and (2) something that catches the case where the model predicts well for the wrong reason, or where predictive success can't distinguish two seperate assumptions.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a fish-stock model that assumes the population is well mixed and that the fish don't evolve, used to set a harvest quota; (b) an economic model that assumes firms are price-takers and fully rational, used to score a carbon tax for revenue; and (c) your choice of a third where I'm not going to like the answer. Be honest about where the procedure is just judgment dressed up as a checklist, and tell me what it can't do at all.
