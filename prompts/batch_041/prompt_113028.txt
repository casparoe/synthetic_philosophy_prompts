I'm a novelist, and I have put an instrument in a book that I need to keep honest. Given an observer and any event on that observer's worldline, it prints the set of events it calls same-dated with that event for that observer, and its internal rule is the ordinary one: an event counts as same-dated when a light signal's round trip between the observer and the event divides the observer's elapsed time exactly in half. My characters treat its printouts as verdicts about which events are already written into the world, as real as the observer's own present.

The scene I am trying to write. Two observers, A and B, are in relative motion and far apart. A is now at event a. Her printout lists, as same-dated with a, an event b at a distance, and B's worldline passes through b. When B consults her own printout at b, it lists as same-dated with b for her an event c that lies on A's worldline, at a point A places in A's own future. One of my characters then argues as follows. b is as real as A's present, because A's instrument dates the two alike. c is as real as B's present, because B's instrument dates the two alike. And if a thing is real for someone who is himself real for A, then the thing is real for A; reality does not thin out as it passes from hand to hand. So c, though it lies in A's future, is already real, and the future is written.

I want to know where the weight of that inference actually sits, because I will not put an argument in a character's mouth that a competent reader can dismantle in a sentence. How much is carried by the instrument's rule, and is that rule a discovery about the world or a convention that could have been otherwise? How much is carried by the assumption that an observer's present is a whole surface of events rather than the single event she occupies? How much is carried by the assumption that being real for someone is being real, full stop, and that this relation composes in the way the character says it does? Lay the inference out step by step and flag each assumption at the point where it is doing work. Then tell me what becomes of it if the present is taken to be a single event, and again if it is taken to be the set of events from which a signal could already have reached the observer. Finally, say what the argument does not establish even if it succeeds: I suspect one of my characters has been running together the reality of future events with their availability to us, and I would rather learn that now than in a review. No names and no citations, please; I want the reasoning, and I want it exact. End with a single compact paragraph I can hand to a character as the objection.
