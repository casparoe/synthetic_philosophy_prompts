I teach a 75-minute session to roughly 18 undergraduates in a course on concepts and categorization, and I want them to feel, rather than be told, why induction from a handful of examples is underdetermined — why Bongard-style puzzles feel easy to us even though no finite set of labeled examples logically fixes what comes next, and why our ease depends on a prior about which features are relevant. Design an in-class activity built around a hidden category that I generate live from member and non-member cases. In the first round, present the group a small set of cases, have every participant commit in writing both to the rule they think governs membership and to a prediction for one held-out case, then reveal a second rule that fits every training case exactly as well yet reverses that held-out prediction. In the second round, the hidden rule should turn on a relation between parts of a case rather than on any single feature, so that anyone who succeeds has to notice something the first round taught them to ignore. Give me the specific cases to use and the medium for displaying them, the exact wording of the commit-and-reveal steps, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the underdetermination of theory by evidence and onto the role of prior "relevant features" in making concept learning possible at all. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be candid about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. No lecture up front, and the group should not hear the term for what they have just done until the debrief.
