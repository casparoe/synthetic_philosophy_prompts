Question: Does Aquinas think it is permissible to overthrow a tyrant? Discuss with reference to his treatment of tyranny and sedition.

Student's answer: Aquinas's discussion of tyranny is more radical than people expect. In the Summa Theologiae he asks whether sedition is a mortal sin. He defines sedition as discord within a multitude, opposed to the unity of law and the common good, and he says it is always a mortal sin because it attacks the common good. That sounds like a blanket ban on rebellion. But Aquinas goes on to say that a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed not to the common good but to the private good of the ruler. So disturbing a tyrannical regime is not sedition at all: it is not the kind of discord the prohibition targets. He even treats it as praiseworthy to deliver a people from a tyrannical rule. Of course, he is not naive. He warns that a successful rebel may seize power and become a worse tyrant, and that the upheaval may harm the multitude more than the tyrant did. So the permission is conditional: where a tyrant is genuinely intolerable, the people may depose or kill him, provided the remedy is likely to leave them better off. The only real constraint is that the action must serve the common good rather than private ambition. Aquinas therefore gives us a conditional endorsement of tyrannicide — one that later resistance theorists would develop — and he thinks any citizen who acts for the common good rather than out of private ambition may take part.
