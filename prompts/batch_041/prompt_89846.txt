Reading group got into vagueness last night and a friend of mine — the guy who always has the argument that ends in "so language is broken" — left me with this one, and I've been chewing on it alot since. He claims vagueness isnt a fact about words, it's a proof that most of our predicates don't pick out anything, and here's the argument:

(1) Line up collections of rice: one grain, two grains, ..., ten thousand grains. The ten-thousand-grain one is a heap. (2) There's no sharp boundary to "heap" — there's no number n such that n grains is a heap and n−1 grains isn't. So if ten thousand grains is a heap, nine thousand nine hundred and ninety nine grains is; and if that one's a heap, the next one down is; ... down to one grain. (3) So one grain is a heap. (4) One grain obviously isn't a heap. (5) So (2) is false after all — there is a sharp boundary, some particular n where heaps stop and non-heaps begin. (6) Nobody could ever know what n is, and the same argument runs on "definitely a heap", so there's a boundary between definitely-heap and not-definitely-heap that we also can't locate, and then a boundary between definitely-definitely and definitely, and so on forever. (7) So there's a real line in the world that nothing could ever detect — "heap" isn't a feature of the world, it's a feature of us. And nothing in the argument cares about rice: run it on "red", "bald", "child", "awake", "same object as", "knows". (8) So basically none of our ordinary predicates cut the world at its joints, and ordinary reasoning about medium-sized dry goods is a convenient fiction.

I'm pretty sure (8) is too much. I have a standing prior that any argument concluding that most of my everyday concepts are empty has a bug in it, and I don't think "heap" and "knows" are in the same boat as phlogiston. But I can't find the bug, and when I say "then just deny the tolerance premise" he says "fine, give me the number," which I can't. Half of me wants to keep tolerance and throw out classical logic; the other half thinks an unknowable cutoff is less crazy than it sounds, since the blogs trained me to distrust arguments from "I can't imagine where the line is." So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does each of the standard rescues actually cost — the epistemicist line that there's a cutoff we're constitutionally barred from knowing (Williamson-style), the supervaluationist line that the boundary sentences are neither true nor false while the predicate still works, the degree-theoretic line that they're true to a degree, the contextualist line that there's no boundary at all and the sorites is sliding the context? And a second thing: does the higher-order move in (6) hurt the first two equally, or does one of them get hurt way worse? I don't need reading, I know the cast of characters — I want the diagnosis and the price tags.
