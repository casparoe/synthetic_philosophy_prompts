Hey, I'm putting together a little teachng thing on the philosophy of computation and I need a dialogue I can hand to students. You're a philosopher who actually knows complexity theory — not just the pop-science version — so take a moment to think it through and maybe sketch a quick plan before you start writing. Don't rush the setup.

Two characters, one room. Keep narration to the absolute bare minimum, basically just "Ines said" / "Ravi said" and the occasional gesture. No essay framing, no stage directions beyond that.

Ines thinks complexity results have real philosophical teeth. Her line is that when we prove something like "problem X is NP-hard" or "no single learning algorithm outperforms all others across every possible distribution", we've learned something about the limits of any bounded reasoner, not merely a fact about worst-case running times on some abstract machine. She's a realist about "feasible": she thinks there's a fact of the matter about which inductive strategies are computationally available to finite agents, and that this genuinely constrains what we can be justified in believing.

Ravi is just as sharp and thinks this is mostly a category error. For him, complexity classes are artifacts of a machine model plus an asymptotic worst-case convention. "NP-hard" is a claim about the hardest instances in a family and tells you almost nothing about the instances anyone actually meets. He'll keep hammering Ines on equivocations between "in principle", "in polynomial time", and "justified for us finite folks". His strongest move: a bounded reasoner is itself a physical system embedded in the world, so for tons of problems the world just runs the computation for free and you read the answer off the outcome — the universe doesn't bill you for simulating itself.

I want them to actually lock horns over one concrete case rather than swap speeches. Have them work through this: suppose someone proves that predicting whether a certain messy physical system (a big protein folding a particular way, a market crashing next quarter — pick one and stick with it) is NP-hard. Ines says that's a deep epistemic limit; we can never have justified belief about such outcomes except by luck. Ravi says: worst case, irrelevant, and anyway you can build the thing and watch it happen, so the "intractability" evaporates the moment you stop pretending you have to solve it in your head.

Let them go back and forth for at least five or six exchanges, each responding to the other's actual last point rather than the point they'd prefer to attack. Neither side gets to win by being dumbed down. When one concedes something small, the other should press the advantage; when one lands a good hit, the other should try to absorb it without collapsing. Ines isn't allowed to just name-drop famous people to settle things — she has to argue.

Aim for 700–900 words, casual prose, no jargon dumps.
