I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized regional health system, and at the end of the month we vote on whether to let an outside company run a one-year pilot of something they call a "conscience coverage exchange." Background: our region legalized a procedure that a fair number of our staff — I'd guess a fifth of the relevant clinicians — won't take part in, on moral or religious grounds. Nobody's forced to do it, but somebody has to, and right now the whole burden lands on the same small group of willing people, who are burning out and starting to resent their colleagues. The company's pitch: objectors pay into a pool (monthly fee, or the hospital can pay it for them as a benefit), and the clinicians who actually do the work draw a per-shift premium out of it, higher on nights and short-staffed weeks. There's a clause letting an objector pay "in kind" instead — take an equal number of the shifts nobody wants for other reasons. And the rep keeps mentioning that objectors could pre-buy coverage a year ahead at a locked rate.

Half the committee is already calling it the sale of indulgences; the other half says we let people sell plasma and pay surrogates, so what's the difference.

Write the opinion I'll circulate before the vote. I want a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — the conditions spelled out specifically enough that legal could drop them straight into the pilot contract, and a date when we review it. Argue it, and don't duck the strongest point on the losing side: that the status quo is already a hidden transfer from the willing to the objecting, and this just makes it visible and paid.

The thing I most want you to actually think through: what is someone buying when they buy their way out of doing what they think is wrong? If paying a colleague to do it makes you complicit, the objector who pays is doing wrong and the scheme falls apart. If it doesn't, then maybe the objection was never about the act, only about their own hands. And say plainly who ends up on the selling side, and whether a conscience clause you can only use if you can afford the fee is still a conscience clause.
