So my friend and I just spent like three hours going back and forth about this and I need to get it out of my system by turning it into a class session instead of texting her again at 1am.

Her line is the familiar one: look at Singapore, look at China. Order, growth, infrastructure that works, people who feel safe walking home at night. If a government actually delivers that, what precisely is the complaint? She says "legitimacy" is just the word academics reach for when they don't like a government that happens to work. And by the end of it I honestly couldnt tell whether I was defending democracy or just defending my own priors.

My objection — and this is the part I want students to feel rather than hear me say — is that the comparison is fixed. When she says "better than the alternative," the alternative she has in mind is chaos, or a failed state, or some corrupt mess. But that alternative is manufactured by the same regime. You suppress the opposition for thirty years and then observe, correctly, that the opposition is incompetent. You never get to see the counterfactual, and the reason you never get to see it is precisely the thing you're supposed to be evaluating. So the benchmark ends up being endogenous to the thing it's meant to judge. She thinks that's a debating trick. I think it's the whole ballgame.

I teach a political theory seminar — 16 undergrads, one 90-minute session. Design an activity for it. Not a debate, please. I've run debates on this and they just re-run whatever priors walked in the door. I want them to actually play out a compressed version of the thing: I want them to make real (small) decisions under a setup where order and prosperity are genuinely on the table and genuinely good, so that they find themselves endorsing the trade, and only then discover what they've agreed to.

The important bit: build the trap into the rules, not the framing. Whatever setup you use, participants should end up in a position where the only alternative they can actually evaluate is one the rules have been quietly degrading the whole time — so their "compared to what?" is rigged, and they only clock it afterwards. If you can work in a periodic renewal vote or a sunset clause, please do, because that's the test I keep wanting to run: if the legitimacy really is performance-based, it ought to survive being re-earned every few years, and I want to watch the room find out whether it does.

Give me the setting and roles, the rules, materials (printable, no tech, no real money), the payoff or incentive structure, a minute-by-minute-ish timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the pieces of the concept — performance as a source of legitimacy versus the others, the counterfactual problem, the endogeneity point. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight with me about which part of this the activity can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud.

And dont let it drift into "is authoritarianism bad" — we all know where we stand on that. I want them to feel the mechanism.
