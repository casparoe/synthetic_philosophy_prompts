ok so I'm putting together a small crossover thing — poets who like game theory, that kind of crowd — and I need a poem about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution. Not a poem that name-checks it. A poem that *is* it, structurally, so that someone who knows the solution can point at the parts.

Let me spell the idea out here so you don't have to rely on half-remembering a textbook version. Two sides bargaining. There's a fallback: what each gets if the talks collapse. There's an ideal point: what each would get if it could have everything it wants — and the two of them can't both have that. Draw a straight line from the fallback to the ideal point and take the farthest point on that line that's actually available. Thats the deal. Which means the two sides' gains over the fallback always sit in the same ratio as their two maximum gains: if her best case is three and his is two, its three-for-two, whatever the wall turns out to be. Its selling point is that it answers to the whole shape of the wall — push the wall out on your side and your share can't shrink, though the other one's might. Nash's rule is the rival: it only answers to the bit of the table it actually picks, so if a smaller table still contains the answer, the answer doesn't move.

Now. Put on the hat of someone who is genuinely both — a poet who knows bargaining theory cold, or a game theorist who writes real verse — and please think it through before writing a line. Start by brainstorming in plain words: name the two sides, say where the fallback is, where the ideal point is, how big the wall is, and check on paper that the poem's own arithmetic comes out. Then write the poem.

What I want:

- Two voices. Two sisters and their mothers house, a divorce, two countries on one river — concrete and physical, not players. Let them name what they want, and let the poem say the two claims out loud early so the counting has something to attach to.
- Start at the fallback. First stanza, two lines each: what each of them gets if they walk away. Thats the floor.
- From there the poem climbs the line and never leaves it. Every step outward, both ask for more, and the ratio between their claims never budges — no detours, no trading one side's loss for the other's gain. Let the form carry it: split the lines between the voices in the ratio of the two claims, in every stanza after the first, so the reader can count. If its three to two, she gets three lines to his two, all the way up.
- The ideal point gets named and never reached. It's a direction, not a destination — but say that in a way that doesn't sound like a fridge magnet.
- The poem ends at the wall, and exactly there: the last stanza's asks should be the largest step that still fits inside what the two of them can actually get, and the next step would overshoot. The final line should be the wall itself rather than either voice, or a line that breaks off at that point. A reader with a pencil should be able to check that you stopped neither early nor late.
- One stanza in the middle where one of them notices the thing that separates this rule from Nash's: an option that nobody ends up taking can still move the deal here, because the whole wall counts. The other one should push back with Nash's instinct — if we're not taking it, why should it move us? Let them argue in their own words, no jargon.
- No theory words anywhere in the poem. No names of solutions, no axioms, no players. If someone teaches this stuff they should be able to check the poem and find nothing fudged; someone who's never heard of it should just read a poem about two sisters and a house.

Free verse is fine, rhyming is fine, whatever serves it. Roughly 20 to 30 lines. The count is doing real work here, so keep it disciplined and don't fudge a line to make a rhyme land.

After the poem, give me two or three sentences of plain prose telling me which lines are the fallback, where the line runs, and where the wall is, so I can see at a glance that the arithmetic holds.
