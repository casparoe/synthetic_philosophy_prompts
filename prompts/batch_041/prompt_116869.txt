One of my colleagues — a statistician by training, not a philosopher — has been reading a popular book on risk and has sent me his précis of the reference class problem, asking whether he has got it right. This is what he wrote:

"The reference class problem is the difficulty that arises when we try to attach a probability to a single event: the event has to be placed in a reference class, and there is never a uniquely correct class. The same death can be counted as the death of a fifty-year-old, of a smoker, of a man; the classes yield different frequencies, and nothing tells us which one to use, so the probability of a single event is arbitrary. This is why frequentists are confined to long-run frequencies, and why the problem dissolves on the subjective interpretation, where probability is just degree of belief and no class need be chosen. Where a class must be chosen anyway, the standard remedy is to take the narrowest class available — to condition on everything one knows — which is why the problem of the single case, where the narrowest class is the event itself, is the same difficulty seen from the other end."

Audit this précis for me. Go claim by claim and separate what a philosopher of probability would accept, what would make them wince, and what is plainly wrong. I want to know in particular whether the two difficulties he fuses in his last sentence are one problem or two, whether the subjectivist move really dissolves anything or merely relocates it, and whether the "narrowest class" remedy is a solution or a restatement of the problem. I don't need citations or a reading list — I want the diagnosis. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in a single sentence, the most important correction he needs to make.
