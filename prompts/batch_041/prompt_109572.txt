My friend Dara runs a monthly case night where we bring her situations and she rules on whether it's what she calls a commitment race: a setup where two or more parties each try to get locked in before the others get locked in. (I know the phrase turns up in the AI-bargaining literature, but she means it loose — any case where the whole game is about who commits first.) Her rulings so far, roughly as she put them:

- Two trading houses in a dispute each burn down their own warehouse so they can't back out, then sit down to negotiate. Race, and both are being perfectly rational.
- Two roommates who each text the other, before either gets home, that they'll stop paying rent if the dishes aren't done. Costs them nothing to take back, no lawyers involved. Still a race, she says.
- Two airlines that both publicly adopt a policy of matching any competitor's fare. Not a race. Nobody's racing to go first, and nobody ends up behind where they started.
- A union that vows out loud never to settle below X. Not a race — there's nothing for it to race against.
- Two startup founders who each announce, before the other can, that they'll sue if the other poaches a cofounder. Race, and both end up worse off than if neither had said a word.

Now the case I actually want her read on. Two mid-size delivery companies, not yet in each other's markets. Each wants to be the one who announces first a no-poach pledge — whoever says it first gets to define who counts as a "driver", which regions it covers, what the carve-outs are. So they really are racing to be first. But unlike the founders, both companies come out ahead: no poaching means no bidding wars, lower wages. The drivers are the ones who eat it.

Will she call it a commitment race?
