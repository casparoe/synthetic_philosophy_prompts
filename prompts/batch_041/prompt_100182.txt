I've been arguing about the problem of old evidence with two friends — one a working Bayesian who does statistics for a living, the other a historian of science who thinks Bayesianism is a rational reconstruction of what scientists actually do — and after three months we have gotten precisely nowhere. The case that started it is the familiar one: general relativity entails a specific value for the perihelion advance of Mercury, that value had been measured and accepted decades before the theory existed, and yet the agreement between prediction and datum is universally treated as powerful evidence for the theory. The formal trouble is easy to state. The datum is already certain for the agent, conditionalization on a proposition whose probability is 1 changes nothing, so an agent who has already absorbed the observation cannot raise her credence in the theory on account of it — even though almost everyone, Bayesian or not, feels that the agreement does raise it. We keep cycling through the same three moves. The first repairs the formalism by relativizing confirmation to a counterfactual credence function, the one the agent would have had before she knew the datum (Garber's fix, if I have the name right). The second insists that there is no problem at all, because once you are scrupulous about which agent learned what, and when, the troubling cases evaporate. The third grants the problem and treats it as a symptom of something larger: that Bayesianism is a theory of reasoning for a logically omniscient agent, and that learning a mathematical fact — that the theory entails this particular perihelion value — is not something conditionalization can represent in the first place. My two friends divide neatly along these lines, and each thinks the others are quietly changing the subject.

Here is the hypothesis I would like you to attack. I suspect the impasse is not a mathematical one that more cleverness would dissolve. I think it is a disagreement about what the object of the theory is supposed to be — an idealized agent's credence function, on the one hand, or a reconstruction of the actual inferential practice of scientists, on the other — compounded by a disagreement about whether confirmation is a relation relativized to somebody's actual credences or an objective evidential relation that those credences are supposed to track. If that is right, then the very same formal move, plain conditionalization, will strike one side as obviously correct and the other as obviously question-begging, and no theorem will settle it. I also have a suspicion, which I hold more loosely, that the counterfactual repair is not independent of the problem of new theories: at the earlier time the agent had no credence at all in a hypothesis that had not yet been invented, so the counterfactual probability of the old datum given the new theory seems undefined until you supply a principled rule for extending an old probability space to propositions it never contained — and that rule is exactly what the problem of new theories asks for, which would mean the old-evidence fix quietly presupposes its own sibling. What I want to know is whether the disagreement is really as I describe it, or whether it is a largely verbal quarrel about the word confirmation, or whether there is a live formal question underneath that I am mistaking for a philosophical one.
