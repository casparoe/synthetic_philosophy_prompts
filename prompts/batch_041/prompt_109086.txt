I write about science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I've spent the last few months reporting on the longtermist-adjacent crowd, the people who argue we should be spending real money on tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs. Somewhere in all those interviews I started keeping a list of my own gut verdicts on their bets, and the list doesn't hang together. I want you to find the principle behind it, or tell me there isn't one. No reading list, please -- I've got alot of those already. I want the actual argument.

Here's where I actually land.

The mugging. A stranger says he'll torture a huge number of people for an absurdly long time unless I hand over $20, and says he'll do it with probability one in 10^1000. I don't pay, and I don't feel even a flicker of temptation. It's a con, full stop.

A friend asks me to give $100 to a group working on reducing the risk of human extinction. I think the chance my donation makes any difference is about one in a trillion. I give it, and it feels like the obviously right thing to do. My editor says this is the mugging with the sign flipped, and I can't tell her why it isn't.

My country spends half a percent of GDP shaving a small fraction off the chance of some far-future catastrophe. Fine. Obviously fine.

A lottery ticket costs $1 and pays $400 million at odds of one in three hundred million. The expected value is positive and I don't buy the ticket. It feels like setting a dollar on fire.

A stranger offers a different bet: $1 for a one-in-a-billion shot at $10 billion. I take it. As far as I can tell this is the lottery again with bigger numbers, and yet.

I won't take a one-in-ten-thousand chance of dying in exchange for a guaranteed $1 million. Not close, no hesitation. But I'll give $1,000 to something with a one-in-a-trillion chance of averting a disaster that kills ten million people, and I feel good about it. I'm brave with other people's futures and a coward about my own skin, is the pattern I keep noticing.

The case that really bothers me: in the real ones, I can't even sign the probability. My $100 might nudge the far future up, might nudge it down, nobody knows which, and I give anyway.

And someone asked me whether I'd pay $1 for a one-in-10^100 chance of everyone being happy forever. I want to say yes. The question feels broken. I suspect that saying it feels broken is a dodge.

So: what principle, or small set of competing principles, gets all of this? State it precisely, and tie each clause to the case that motivates it -- I don't want something vague like risk aversion, since the lottery pair alone shows I'm not simply risk-averse and the donation case shows I'm not simply an expected-value maximizer. Then do two more things. First, invent two cases I haven't listed and tell me what your principle says about them; make one of them a case where the probability is so small that no payoff you could actually produce in this universe would make the expected value worth noticing, since I suspect that's the real fault line and not the size of the payoff. Second, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one I should give up -- and if the honest answer is that there's no principle here, that I just have inconsistent intuitions and should stop pretending otherwise, say that instead and seperate out which of my verdicts are the ones doing the damage.

Last thing: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about the principle you land on. I'd rather see the weak joint than a tidy answer.
