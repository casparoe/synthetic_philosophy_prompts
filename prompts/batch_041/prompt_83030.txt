I am applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in formal epistemology and need a proposal of roughly three pages. The project I want to propose is on Dutch book arguments, but approached from an angle I think has been underexplored, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into something a review panel will treat as a genuine research plan rather than a clever remark.

The standard argument runs, in outline, as follows: if your credences violate the axioms of probability, there is a collection of bets such that each bet is one you will accept by your own lights and the collection guarantees you a net loss. The familiar objections attack the bridge premise — the inference from your being willing to accept a bet to that bet's measuring your credence — and I do not want to re-litigate that dispute. What interests me is an assumption that is almost never stated: the bookmaker. In every version of the argument the adversary is idealised. It knows your credences exactly, it can offer any bet whatever, it has unlimited capital, and it can compute the required combination. Remove any of those and the conclusion — that incoherence is a defect you can be made to pay for — stops being obvious. An adversary with finite resources and its own credences cannot drive you to a sure loss merely because your credence in a disjunction exceeds the sum of your credences in its disjuncts by an epsilon.

My half-formed idea is to stop treating that as an objection and start treating it as a research programme. Rather than asking whether incoherent credences are exploitable, ask how exploitable they are: define, for a given credence function, the minimum cost — informational, computational or financial — at which a resource-bounded adversary could mount a sure-loss book against it. That yields a graded notion of incoherence, an ordering on credence functions, in place of the coarse coherent/incoherent distinction. The conjecture worth testing is that this ordering does not simply track the size of the axiomatic violation, and that where the two diverge the ordering tends to agree with our pre-theoretic sense of which epistemic failings matter — that the cheaply exploitable failures are the ones we are right to care about, and the exotic violations only a demon of unlimited computational power could punish are ones we should stop losing sleep over. I want the proposal to cover both the synchronic cases (violations of the probability axioms, including the countable-additivity cases where the standard argument is already delicate, and keeping in view that the bets on offer define what counts as a book in the first place) and the diachronic ones (failure of reflection, self-undermining or package-deal credences, and the money-pump arguments), since the diachronic arguments are the ones people actually rely on and also the ones where the bookmaker's resources look most load-bearing.

Please write the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a slogan; explain why the existing positions leave it open — in particular why the standard defences of the Dutch book argument do not already settle it, and why the standard objections do not exhaust the options; set out the method, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as simply formalising bounded rationality or attacking the betting interpretation again; give a realistic month-by-month plan for twelve months; say what a result would look like — what would count as showing the conjecture false, and what would count as the project succeeding but being uninteresting; and be honest about whether the formal core is within reach for someone whose mathematics is competent but not exceptional. If a substantial part of the project needs machinery I would have to learn from scratch, say so and say how the scope should be cut. No citations or bibliography — I shall add those myself; refer to positions and arguments by description.
