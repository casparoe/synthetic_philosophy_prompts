I'm a final-year student in a decision theory module, and I've volunteered to write one long-answer question for a mock paper my study group is assembling, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The topic is the Allais paradox and the independence axiom, and I want a question that cannot be passed by recounting the paradox or by reciting a non-expected-utility model.

Please build the question around the following material.

Standard pair. Problem 1: £1,000,000 for certain. Problem 2: an 89% chance of £1,000,000, a 10% chance of £5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. Problem 3: an 11% chance of £1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing. Problem 4: a 10% chance of £5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing.

Variant pair. Problem 1*: an 89% chance of a lottery that pays £1,000,000 or nothing with equal probability, together with an 11% chance of £1,000,000. Problem 2*: the same 89% lottery branch, together with an 11% chance of a lottery that pays £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing otherwise. Problems 3 and 4 are as above.

The question should require the candidate to do three things. First, state precisely what independence forbids, and show that the choice in the first problem (1 versus 2, or 1* versus 2*) and the choice in the second (3 versus 4) must agree: in both versions the two options in the first problem differ only in their 11% residual — a certain £1,000,000 against a 10/11 chance of £5,000,000 — which is exactly the residual Problems 3 and 4 present, so no expected-utility maximiser can prefer the first option in the first problem and the second option in the second. Second, assess whether the usual diagnosis, that the pattern turns on the certainty of the first option, survives the variant, in which no option is certain and the branch common to the two options is itself a lottery; say what the variant therefore isolates. Third, adjudicate whether an agent who takes the safe-then-risky combination in the variant can be given a coherent representation that makes her choice rational, or whether any such representation merely relabels the violation.

Then give me the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks that names the specific moves a first-class answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks — for example treating the pattern as mere risk aversion, or invoking a non-expected-utility model without saying which axiom it denies or what that model predicts for the variant.
