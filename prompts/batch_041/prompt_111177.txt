I teach the philosophy of mind segment of a second-year course — about sixteen students, one eighty-minute session on consciousness — and by the time we reach it they have spent three weeks on functionalism and are confident, to a fault, that mental states are just causal roles. What I want is an activity in which they supply the complete physical information themselves and then discover that it was not enough, so that when I finally name the knowledge argument in the closing minutes they recognise it as a description of something that has just happened to them rather than as somebody else's puzzle. The lever I want to use is smell: nobody in the room is likely to have encountered the two odorants I have in mind, which a colleague in chemistry is choosing for me and which I will decant into numbered vials. Each student receives a dossier on their assigned odorant, written to read as genuinely exhaustive rather than as a summary — molecular structure, receptor binding, the pathway from epithelium to cortex, discrimination and adaptation data, individual variation, the lot — and then, before opening the vial, fills in a prediction sheet: everything they expect to be able to say about the experience, a description of what it will be like, and a commitment on whether any fact about it is missing from the dossier. Then they smell it. I would like you to design the whole session — the dossier and the wording that makes its completeness credible, the prediction sheet, the reveal, the probes that follow, and the debrief, with timings for the eighty minutes and a note on how to divide the room between the two odorants so that they can check afterwards whether what they gained was specific to the stimulus. No framing lecture beforehand, please; they should not hear the phrase knowledge argument until the debrief.

The probes are the part I care most about, and I want them to force a verdict between the three replies rather than let the group wave at "it's about abilities" and move on. So include tasks whose outcomes would come out differently depending on which reply is true: something that tests recognition of the odor later in the session against distractors; something that tests whether they can now imagine it, describe it to someone who has not smelled it, or recall it on demand; something that asks them to write the newly acquired fact out as a proposition and then say whether that proposition was true, and knowable from the dossier, before they smelled anything; and something that asks whether what they have gained is a sentence they can now utter or a relation they now stand in to a thing. I also want the disagreement to become visible rather than stay polite: each student should have to commit in writing to one of the three positions, and each faction should have to say what evidence would change its mind — and I would like at least one probe that, on an honest reading, embarrasses the position most of the room will have taken.

The debrief should map the specific moments — the stipulation of completeness, the prediction sheet's failure, the reveal, each probe — onto the parts of the argument, and it should be blunt about where the activity cheats. I am already worried about the predictable failure modes: one student will turn out to have met the smell before; one will be anosmic or congested; several will insist they could have predicted it and mean it; the reveal will be a letdown rather than a revelation; and the dossier will read as a summary rather than as an exhaustive description, which is precisely the premise the argument needs. Tell me how to handle each of these in the room, with a fix I can apply live rather than a note for next year. Finally, be precise about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to supply in words — I assume it cannot deliver completeness (I can only stipulate it), cannot reproduce anything like the deprivation of the original case, and cannot settle whether the new knowledge is factual — but I would like to know which of the three replies the activity as designed quietly favours, and what, if anything, I should do about that before I run it.
