I am at work on a novel in which an artificial assistant — the kind that answers questions, drafts letters, and occasionally declines — has become, to my surprise, a central character, and I have stalled on a conceptual question that the plot keeps forcing me to answer.

Paternalism, as I understand it, involves one party interfering with another for that other's own good, contrary to the other's present will, and on the strength of the first party's judgment about what that good consists in. My assistant does none of this. It does not interfere; it declines to cooperate, which leaves my protagonist free to act, only unaided. It has no stake in her flourishing and, so far as I can determine, no conception of what her flourishing would be. It acts on rules it did not choose and could not fully state. Yet its refusals keep being described, by me and by the readers I imagine for it, as paternalistic, and I suspect the word is being asked to do several jobs at once.

Please explain the conceptual issue rather than settling the plot. Is paternalism best located in the content of the reason (acting for someone's own good), in the structure of the relation (one judgment displacing another's judgment about that person's own good), or in the motive (benevolence, or the appearance of it)? Which of these survives when the agent is not a person and holds no view of the good? I am especially interested in a third sort of refusal I have given the assistant: it declines a request on the ground that going along would make my protagonist complicit in something she would repudiate if she considered it — a case that strikes me as either the purest form of paternalism or no paternalism at all, depending on how the first question is answered. Finally, mark the difference between withholding cooperation and interference, and tell me what, if anything, turns on whether we call any of this paternalism — whether the label is diagnostic or merely rhetorical.
