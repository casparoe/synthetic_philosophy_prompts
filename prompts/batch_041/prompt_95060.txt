I am a graduate student working at the intersection of the history of economic thought and welfare economics, and I have been arguing with a colleague about a claim of his that I suspect is too quick. His claim is that John Stuart Mill's treatment of higher pleasures — specifically his criterion that the quality of a pleasure is settled by the preference of those who have experienced both, and his insistence that utilitarianism can and should recognize that some kinds of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others — is the direct ancestor of the modern practice, in behavioral welfare economics, of laundering or purifying preferences: treating an agent's actual choices as merely provisional evidence about her welfare and substituting for them the preferences she would have had if fully informed, free of present bias, and unswayed by framing and anchoring effects. His further claim is that Mill would therefore have looked kindly on soft paternalism, on arranging choice architecture so that people are steered toward their own laundered preferences rather than the ones their choices actually reveal. I am skeptical. It seems to me that Mill's competent-judge criterion ranks kinds of pleasure by their intrinsic quality; it is not a license to override an agent's own ranking of her options, and I doubt the harm principle leaves room for an expert or a bureaucrat to decide what an agent really prefers.

Please write an essay-length reconstruction of what Mill's view would most plausibly have been on three questions: (a) whether an agent's choices are a reliable measure of her welfare, or whether the meaning of utility for him was fixed by something other than choice; (b) whether there is a coherent notion of an agent's true or laundered preference that is distinct from what she actually chooses, and whether Mill's qualitative hedonism commits him to such a notion; and (c) whether the state, or a paternalistic designer acting on the agent's behalf, may legitimately act on that notion against the agent's expressed choices. Argue for a particular verdict rather than surveying the space of possible readings, and be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction are extrapolation from what Mill actually wrote rather than positions he addressed. I would also like you to resist the temptation to give me a generic survey of Mill's ethics or of behavioral economics; the interest here is precisely in the collision between a nineteenth-century qualitative hedonism and a twenty-first-century welfare economics that has lost confidence in choice. Then tell me what would change your mind: which piece of textual evidence, or which conceptual argument about the relationship between Mill's qualitative hedonism and his liberalism, would most plausibly lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be going wrong.
