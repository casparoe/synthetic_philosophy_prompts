Ok, I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me, but I've noticed I have these rules of thumb that I didn't know I had, about when a character's beliefs actually change what they percieve versus when it's just them misjudging, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

I write in close third, so I'm always inside somebody's head reporting what they see and hear, and here's what I've caught myself doing:

A character who's been told the house is haunted walks in at night and every creak is a footstep. But I'd say she doesn't *hear* footsteps. She hears a creak and reads it as a footstep. The sound itself is untouched.

A woman who's just found out her husband has been lying to her looks at him across the kitchen and his face is different — colder, narrower, the mouth not the mouth she knew. I'd say she genuinely *sees* a different face. Not that she's just interpreting the same old face differently.

She's also a wine person, this character, and when somebody pours her the cheap stuff and tells her it's the good bottle, she says it's smoother. But I don't believe her. That's not perception, that's her wanting it to be smooth, or performing, or both.

I have a radiologist in the book too, and he glances at a film and the fracture jumps out at him. That feels like perception, real seeing, but not the same thing as the haunted house — it came from years of looking, not from wanting.

A father who's been told his kid is gifted looks at the kid's stick figure drawing and it looks, to him, more advanced than the other kids'. I'd say that one actually changes his perception. He sees more there.

But here's where I get tangled. A woman who rolled her car on a certain bend of a certain road drives it now and the bend looks sharper to her than it is — she's sure she's seeing it as sharper, and she drives accordingly. That's just a plain geometric fact about a curve, same family as the creaks, and I still want to say her perception changed.

My working theory, which I don't trust, is something like: belief reaches down into perception only when the property being seen is a property with *meaning* — menace, warmth, intent, value — and not when it's plain shape or sound or distance. But then the bend is just geometry and I've already let it through, so either the theory's wrong or one of my verdicts has to go.

What I want from you: a principle, or a couple of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could argue with it, with the case that forces each clause. Test it on cases I haven't listed — I'll take whatever you invent. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save, because I suspect at least one of them is just what I need for the scene rather than what I actually think. The one I keep going back and forth on is the bend.

Also, please no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just write it out as prose, like a letter. I read enough outlines at work.
