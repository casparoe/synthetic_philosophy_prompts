For about a year now I have been running a small monthly discussion group out of my local library — eight or nine of us, none with a law degree, a few who read more than they let on — and we keep circling the same argument without ever landing anywhere. The question is whether the criminal law may forbid conduct that harms no one: the man who feeds live rabbits to a snake in his front garden, the consenting adults who pay to be branded on camera, the market in kidneys, that sort of thing. We all start from the same place — none of us wants the police in anyone's bedroom, and none of us is comfortable with the state enforcing a majority's taste — and yet we split over the cases in ways that have nothing to do with how bad we think the conduct is, and I have come to suspect the real fault line is whether disgust or indignation at a practice is ever itself a reason for anything. I have since learned that this is roughly the old quarrel between Lord Devlin and H.L.A. Hart, and that most of us, when pressed, turn out to be running some hybrid that neither of them would own. What I want is not an essay and not a reading list — I would far rather you describe a position in a plain sentence than attribute it to a book I cannot check, and if a name genuinely fits a leaf you may use it, but only once the branches have done their work. What I want is a handout I can print for the group: a decision tree that carries a member of it from a short series of yes-or-no questions about concrete cases to a named position on what may be criminalised, with the position named only at the end, once the answers are in.

Build the tree so that every question can be answered by someone who has no idea which position it leads to. I want the first fork to be over whether the immorality of a harmless act can ever count as a reason for the criminal law at all, and, among those who say that it can, a second fork between the view that the reason is the wrongness of the act itself and the view that the reason is some bad consequence of permitting or tolerating the act. The consequence branch should then be split by which consequence is doing the work — the offence the conduct gives to others, the erosion of a shared morality that supposedly holds a community together, the coarsening of the moral climate in which children are raised, or the damage the conduct does to the actor himself. Somewhere on the tree I want a node asking whether the morality being protected is the one the community actually has or the one that is actually correct, and another asking whether the claim that a shared morality keeps a society from coming apart is meant as an empirical prediction about what will happen or as a claim about what a shared morality is; and I want a node that separates punishing a practice from merely refusing to recognise or facilitate it — declining to marry the couple, to license the venue, to let the business trade — because I suspect half of us are in that second camp without knowing it. Aim for somewhere between eight and twelve leaves, and make sure the following appear: a strict harm-principle position; a harm principle with a paternalistic exception; the view that offence is a distinct ground of prohibition; the view that the law may protect a person from his own degradation; the view that the intrinsic wrongness of an act is itself a reason to punish it, both with and without a retributivist account of why; the view that what is really being protected is the community's shared morality, and the view that what is really being protected is the moral climate rather than the morality; the conventionalist position that whatever the community sincerely condemns may be prohibited, and the position that only what is genuinely wrong may be; and one leaf for the view that the question is not about reasons at all but about the state's standing to enforce any of it. For every leaf give me one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence naming which of the cases below embarrasses it most. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, say so and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to defend it; and where two leaves cannot be told apart by any of my cases, flag them and tell me what further case would separate them.

Please run the whole tree against one fixed set of cases, and invent nothing beyond them: (i) a retired man in a village who keeps a large constrictor and feeds it live rabbits in his front garden, in view of the road — the rabbits are legally his, no person is harmed, and the neighbours are disgusted; (ii) a private club of consenting adults who pay to be branded with hot irons, on camera, for paying subscribers, and who are left with real and permanent scars; (iii) a licensed market in kidneys from living adult sellers, informed and paid, which appears to reduce the harms of the black market, and which some residents call a degradation of the human body; (iv) a club whose consenting adult members fight with blunt weapons under waivers and medical supervision, sometimes breaking bones and occasionally killing someone; (v) a small religious minority that kills a goat quickly and painlessly in a private courtyard as part of a rite, which the town's majority finds barbaric; and (vi) a business that pays adults to undergo irreversible body modification — tongue splitting, implants, ear removal — on camera for subscribers, with the participants insisting afterwards that they are happier. Finally, on the last page, tell me where the instrument puts someone who answers the way I would — against the state policing private life, uneasy about the branding club on his own street, and unwilling to call his disgust a reason for anything — and what that person would have to give up in order to stay on that leaf.
