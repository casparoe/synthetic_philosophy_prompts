My reading group keeps circling an argument I can neither accept nor dismantle. It runs:

(1) The more gender-equal and affluent a country is, on average, the larger the measured sex difference in broad preferences such as working with people versus working with things, and the more sex-typed the resulting occupational sorting.
(2) When a difference between two groups narrows as conformity pressure rises and widens as that pressure is lifted, the difference is not an effect of the pressure; it is what remains once the pressure is gone.
(3) In egalitarian societies the operative pressure runs against sorting by sex, so the widening there is not a social artifact.
(4) Hence the difference expresses a disposition that such societies have stopped suppressing.
(5) So egalitarian policy is not answerable for failing to close these gaps, and justice need not aim at equal representation along these lines.

I suspect something is off around (3) and (4), but every repair I attempt feels like special pleading, and I would rather keep (1) — I take the cross-national pattern seriously — than abandon the whole line of thought. Which step should go, and what does keeping (1) actually cost me? Concretely: if the principle in (2)–(3) is sound, why does it not equally license the move from "the pressure is lifted" to "the difference is natural" for differences we are confident are cultural — say, the ones that track whatever occupations a given society happens to read as masculine or feminine? And if the parallel is disanalogous, I want to see exactly where the analogy breaks, not just that it does.
