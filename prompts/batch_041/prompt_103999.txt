I'm a novelist, not a philosopher by training, and I'm writing a chapter whose spine is a decision my protagonist cannot get right however carefully she reasons. I've been reading about the structure usually called the absent-minded driver problem — an agent who must make the same kind of choice at several points she cannot tell apart, where what she ought to do depends on which point she is actually at — and I'd like you to build me a fresh instance of that structure, then show me that it holds together.

The scenario: realistic and contemporary, something a person might plausibly face in ordinary life, one paragraph. No driving, and no convenient lapse of memory — I want the obstacle to be genuine uncertainty about which stage she is in, not forgetting. No lotteries, dice, coins, or other randomizing devices; no self-addressed notes or other commitment tricks available to her; nothing science-fictional. She must make a single kind of decision at two or more points she cannot distinguish, with the payoff depending on which point it is.

Then, in this order:

1. State the situation precisely: what she knows, what she cannot know, and what each outcome is worth to her.

2. Work out two verdicts. First, the plan made from the outset — 'if this is how it is, here is what I will do at each stage.' Second, the reasoning that must choose the same thing at every stage, precisely because she cannot tell the stages apart. Then the part I care about most: whether any policy she could actually execute reconciles them. If the advance plan is one she cannot follow, say so, and explain exactly what blocks it.

3. Name the single feature of the case that does the work, and show what happens when you take it away — in particular, if the stages became distinguishable, or if she could leave herself a marker, does the puzzle dissolve, and into what?

4. Finish with how someone who rejects your preferred verdict would attack the case.

I would rather you tell me plainly if I have the puzzle wrong or if what I am asking for cannot be done well — if the divergence I am after is not really available in a case this realistic, or if my ban on randomizing devices makes the interesting verdict unreachable — than hand me something polished that quietly does not hold together. If the honest answer is that the structure I have described cannot do what I want, or that a different version of it would, say that instead of going along with me.
