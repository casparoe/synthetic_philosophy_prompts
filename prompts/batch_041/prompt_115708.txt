Question: Explain Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. Your answer should cover the hard core, the negative and positive heuristics, the protective belt, the difference between progressive and degenerating programmes, whether it is rational to continue working on a degenerating programme, and how Lakatos's view of scientific revolutions differs from Kuhn's. Conclude by assessing whether Lakatos succeeds in giving a rational reconstruction of scientific change, or whether his account is only retrospective.

Student answer: Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes is a response to Popper and Kuhn. A research programme is a sequence of theories united by a hard core. The hard core is the central set of assumptions that the programme treats as irrefutable. It is irrefutable because it has survived many tests and is therefore well corroborated; if it were refuted, the programme would collapse. The negative heuristic tells scientists not to direct modus tollens at the hard core, while the positive heuristic tells them which auxiliary hypotheses to modify when a prediction fails. The protective belt is the set of auxiliary hypotheses, observational assumptions, and initial conditions that can be adjusted. When a prediction fails, scientists blame the protective belt rather than the hard core.

A programme is progressive when its modifications lead to novel predictions that are corroborated. It is degenerating when it only makes ad hoc adjustments to accommodate known anomalies, without predicting anything new. Lakatos thinks a degenerating programme should be abandoned once the anomalies pile up; continuing to work on it is irrational because the methodology has already shown it is failing. However, he also says that we can only judge progress or degeneration with hindsight, after a programme has been superseded. This is why he denies that there are crucial experiments in the instant: a single experiment cannot refute a programme, because the hard core is protected by the negative heuristic and the protective belt can always be adjusted.

Lakatos agrees with Kuhn that scientific revolutions involve a kind of Gestalt switch or conversion. When a research programme is replaced, scientists do not simply weigh evidence; they change their fundamental assumptions, and this change cannot be fully captured by rational rules. Lakatos's main disagreement with Kuhn is that Lakatos thinks the new programme is usually more progressive, whereas Kuhn thinks the change is irrational. But Lakatos still admits that the decision to abandon a hard core is a matter of faith or conversion, not rational appraisal. Thus MSRP is partly rational and partly sociological.
