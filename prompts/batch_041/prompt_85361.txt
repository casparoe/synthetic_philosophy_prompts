After a career in reactor physics I have retired and taken up moral and political philosophy, and I find myself returning to a puzzle that my old field raises without resolving. The German phase-out was enacted as a schedule of fixed shutdown dates, one per plant, rather than as a conditional rule that would have lapsed had the safety case or the carbon arithmetic changed; the public rationale, as I read it, was that a severe accident is a residual risk to be excluded rather than minimized, so that its low probability was never permitted to be weighed against the benefits of the electricity. French practice, as I understand it, runs the other way: a fleet licensed against quantified safety objectives, with lifetimes extended where the probabilistic analysis supports it. Both countries then defend the outcome in consequentialist terms, as emissions avoided and cost per tonne abated.

What I want is an audit of what each practice presupposes, not an argument about which policy is correct. Does the German stance commit its holders to a maximin or lexicographic decision rule, to the incommensurability of catastrophic harms with ordinary benefits, or to a deontological constraint on the technology as such — and are those three even distinguishable in this case? Which rival views does the practice rule out, and which would it equally license, so that the law is doing less philosophical work than its defenders suppose? Is the German position internally consistent, or does the climate justification smuggle back the probability-weighting that the safety argument rejected? I have some resistance to that charge, since the radiation-protection tradition I worked in combines individual dose limits that may not be traded against collective benefit with an optimizing requirement that exposures be reduced so far as is reasonably practicable; tell me whether that is a genuine precedent for a mixed rule or a false comfort. Finally, I keep meeting two objections and would like to know whether either engages the practice: that the same polity tolerates far larger expected mortality from road traffic and from coal, and that a rule keyed to dates rather than to conditions cannot be rational. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say so; and say what would have to change in each country if its energy policy were rebuilt on a single explicit criterion.
