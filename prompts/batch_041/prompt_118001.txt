A colleague of mine argues as follows. When we say that the two-bitser — the circuit wired to the vending machine, the one that registers a can dropping into the chute — believes there is a Coke waiting, we are talking the way we talk when we say a thermometer knows it is warm outside: we are projecting, and nothing inside the device answers to the description. He takes this to license something stronger, namely that no artifact, however elaborate, can possess aboutness of its own, since whatever a machine's internal states mean is conferred from outside by designers and users, whereas a brain's states mean what they mean on their own. The distinction between derived and original intentionality is, on his view, a distinction in kind.

I would like the analogy audited rather than the conclusion debated on its merits. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the thermometer, the temperature, the reading, the reader, and the belief being ascribed — and then say where the mapping breaks once the target is not a thermostat but a system that learns: a warehouse robot, say, that has taught itself to predict which pallets will topple, and whose internal states have no single outside assigner. Sort the disanalogies into those that bear on the conclusion he wants and those that are idle, and tell me whether that conclusion — a difference in kind, and the impossibility of the artifact case — survives them. Finally, if the intentional stance is the only stance we ever occupy, what analogy would replace the thermometer, and would the replacement do better or merely relocate the difficulty?
