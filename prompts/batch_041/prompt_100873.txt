Ok so I write a column on science and ideas and I've been working up a piece on how a bunch of philosophy-of-language stuff has quietly migrated into the way we all talk about brains and AI now. The thing I keep bumping into is semantic externalism, the Twin Earth business, where water means whatever it was that your community was actually tracking, so a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you on a planet whose oceans are XYZ doesn't mean water by water even though he feels exactly like you do from the inside. I've basically come around to it. It seems right, and it explains why I can sling around arthritis and elm and molybdenum without knowing much about any of them.

But I dont want to write a puff piece, so here's what I need. Play a professional philosopher who knows this literature cold, and give me the exposure map for the view: what would actually count against it. Not the textbook hits — I already know about the cases where a word's reference gets switched out from under a whole community by explorers, I know someone always brings up the swampman with no causal history, and I know there's a long fight about whether content can be narrow and still do causal work. I want to know which of these genuinely threaten the position, which are merely awkward, and which ones its own defenders already concede are costs they pay on purpose. And I want to test it on a case I made up: imagine a community whose word for a common local bird was introduced by a guy who'd only ever seen photographs, and the photographs were actually of a different species than the one these people live among and point at every single day. What does externalism say their word means, what does it say their beliefs about that bird are about, and does your answer force a revision, or is there a principled move I'm missing?

Take your time with it — maybe start by spelling out exactly what commitments externalism is on the hook for before you start grading objections, then rank the defeaters from fatal down to merely awkward. And be genuinely self-critical at the end: tell me the one consideration that, if it came out one way rather than the other, would actually make you abandon the view — or if there's nothing that could, just say so, because a view that can't lose is its own column. Keep it conversational, I'm a journalist not a grad student, but please dont dumb it down.
