Ok so I'm prepping for a seminar on Descartes and animal minds and I keep hitting a wall with the Newcastle letter and the More letter (mid-to-late 1640s) — I'm working from a library translation so I'll just give you the gist rather than pretend I can quote it exactly.

The gist: Descartes tells Newcastle that if you built a machine that looked and moved exactly like a monkey, or even like a man, you couldn't tell it from the real thing by behaviour alone, except by two things. First, no machine could ever use words, or arrange signs, the way we do to declare its thoughts to others — it could be rigged to utter sounds, or even to produce a phrase when something pokes it, but not to respond appropriately across the endless variety of situations. Second, even if a machine did lots of things as well or better than us, it would fail at other things, which shows it acts not from knowledge but from the arrangement of its organs, like a clock. And somewhere — the Discourse, I think — he says magpies and parrots can utter words like us but can't show they're thinking, whereas humans born deaf and dumb invent their own signs.

Then in the More letter he seems to go the other way: he says he doesn't deny life or sensation to animals, only thought. And he grants animals have passions, memory, can be trained, and that dogs naturally hunt partridges.

My question is about how these fit together, not just whether they do. Specifically: (a) what work is sensation doing in the More letter if animals are pure machines — is he using it in a bodily sense that implies no experience at all, or does he think there's some dim awareness that just isn't thought? (b) The two tests look like tests for reason or language use, but he presents them as if they settle whether there's a soul — does that follow? (c) How much of this is really an argument vs. a rhetorical move against people who thought animals had immortal souls? And which of these moves is the most load-bearing for his whole system, because it feels like if you gave animals a little experience you wouldn't obviously break the physics.

Can you walk me through the passage and untangle it? Also flag anything that's actually a mistranslation or that hinges on a Latin or French term doing something slippery (sentire? âme? I have a suspicion but no real grip on it) — I don't need citations, just help seeing the shape of the thing.
