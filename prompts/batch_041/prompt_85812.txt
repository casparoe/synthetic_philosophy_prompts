I spent a career running randomised trials and came to philosophy late, from the natural sciences. For the past decade I have argued about sortition in a monthly discussion group, and the argument has stalled in a way I cannot account for. We agree on the empirical record: allotted bodies of citizens deliberate seriously and produce recommendations no worse than those of elected bodies. We agree that political equality matters. Yet one camp holds that an allotted chamber would be the most democratic institution available, since election rewards a particular temperament and the organised, while the other holds that it would not be democratic at all, since an allotted body has no mandate and cannot be held to account. Nobody has moved.

My own suspicion, offered as no more than that, comes from the trials. There randomisation is valued for removing bias in the assignment of treatments; nobody supposes it confers authority on the trial. Perhaps the two camps are not disagreeing about democracy but about whether fairness of selection can supply legitimacy of decision — one side treating office as something owed to citizens, the other as something entrusted to them. It also strikes me that nearly every real allotted body has been advisory, so the two camps may be imagining different institutions under a single name.

Diagnose rather than adjudicate. Is this a verbal quarrel about the word 'democratic', a substantive disagreement about what legitimacy requires, two questions under one name, or a hidden empirical dispute about capture and accountability? Say whether my suspicion holds, and what is really doing the work. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a change of question — or why none should be expected.
