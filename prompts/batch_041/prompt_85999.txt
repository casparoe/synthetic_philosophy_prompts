Draft, working title: Cost is doing two different jobs in "costly signaling" and we keep mashing them together

Every time somebody in these circles says "that's a costly signal," they mean something like: it's expensive, therefore it's honest. I want to push on that, because I think expense is doing two different jobs in these conversations and nobody seperates them.

Job one: the handicap job. Here the cost is what makes the signal credible, and it only works if the cost is *differential*. What matters is the marginal cost to a low-quality sender vs a high-quality sender. If a signal costs every sender the same amount, it transmits nothing about quality no matter how many resources it burns. Take the peacock's tail. People say: look how wasteful, therefore honest. But if a sick peacock could grow the same tail at the same price as a healthy one, the tail would be noise. It's informative because the low-quality bird pays more at the margin — or maybe because it's just an index of health, in which case the waste isn't doing the work at all and "handicap" is the wrong label. (Zahavi's handicap principle, I think — and then somebody formalized it later, Grafen maybe — the point being that honesty comes from the slope of the cost function, not the level. Correct me if I've got the attribution backwards.)

Job two: the commitment job. Burning your boats is costly, but nobody learns anything about your *type* from the boats being on fire. What changed is your own future incentives — you deleted the retreat option, so now you'll actually fight, and the other side can predict that. That's a real equilibrium effect and it isn't a handicap: the cost can be identical for everyone and it still works, because it doesn't have to separate types. It has to bind you. And notice the cost here is paid in options, not in resources. The supplies you torch are gone, sure, but that's not what does the work — what does the work is the missing boat.

So my claim is these are two mechanisms, not one. Handicap signaling needs the good type to pay less at the margin. Commitment needs the option to be gone, and is indifferent about who paid for the matches. The usual reply is "but real signals are both!" Fine — a gift, a degree, a public pledge can be a signal and a commitment at once. My claim is weaker: they're distinct mechanisms, they come apart in cases where the joint story isn't available, and the conflation makes us misdiagnose.

Here's the test I'd propose. Subsidize the cost. If a third party pays the expense for everybody — free peacock tails, free boats to burn — then a handicap signal should collapse into noise, because differential cost was the whole engine. A commitment should survive, because the option is still gone even if somebody else bought the matches. So if a case we all call a "costly signal" survives full subsidizing, then cost wasn't what made it credible and we've been telling ourselves a story.

Meta-worry: "that's a costly signal" is a claim about why a behavior is stable in a population, not about what any individual is thinking. "He's signaling" is a different claim. I think we slide between them constantly, and it makes alot of our explanations unfalsifiable. I dont know how to fix that, but I suspect it's where most of the confusion actually lives.

Ok, tear it up. I'm especially interested in whether the subsidy test cuts where I think it does, and whether the differential-cost condition needs the sign I gave it.
