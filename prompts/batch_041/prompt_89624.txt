I've been going down a rabbit hole on al-Ghazali's attack on causation and I want you to write me an essay that actually takes a side. Don't bother with references or citations, I'm not going to check them and I'd rather you spend teh words on the argument.

Here's the setup as I understand it, tell me if I've got it wrong. The philosophers said fire burns cotton because burning is in fire's nature, so the connection is necessary. Ghazali says no: all you ever observe is fire, then burning, because God has been arranging the two in that order, and the connection isn't necessary, God could simply decline to do the second half. Standard so far.

What I can't get past is what grounds the regularity on this view. Not the natures of things. Not a law, unless you're willing to count God's decision as one. What you're left with is a habit, an extremely reliable habit but a habit, and that's uncomfortably close to Hume: constant conjunction and nothing else. The theist will say the difference is that Hume has nothing behind the regularity while Ghazali has a divine intention behind it. I want to know if that's a real difference or just a nicer story told about the same structure.

I think it all turns on counterfactuals. "Fire burns cotton" means it would burn it if you threw cotton in, and that's the bit that separates a law from a coincidence. Under occasionalism, what makes that counterfactual true? If it's God's intention to keep doing it that way, then a law of nature starts to look like a prediction about someone's future behaviour, and the necessity we were after has quietly evaporated. If instead God's intention is eternal and fixes the counterfactuals all at once, then necessity looks like it's been smuggled back in under another name, which was supposed to be the very thing being denied.

Also take seriously the reading where Ghazali isn't denying causation at all, only necessity, i.e. fire really is a cause, God just isn't obliged to let it work. If that reading is right, does occasionalism collapse into a fairly boring compatibilism between divine and natural causation, with nothing interesting left over?

So reach a verdict on whether occasionalism is a genuine alternative to Humean regularity or a redescription of it with theology attached. If you think it's genuine, say precisely what work the theology is doing that the Humean account can't do. If you think it isn't, say what, if anything, is lost when you strip it out. Don't quote the text, paraphrase is fine.

And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where in your reasoning you'd expect it to give way first.
