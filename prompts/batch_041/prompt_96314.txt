I have no training in economics or philosophy. What follows comes from reading on my own, and from a puzzle I have not been able to put down. I would rather have a definite answer than a survey of the options, so please do not end by leaving the choice to me.

Here is the argument as I understand it. Take two people who are equally good at reasoning, who begin with the same prior beliefs about how the world works, and who know each other's beliefs and trust each other's rationality. Suppose they look at the same evidence, or suppose they simply tell each other everything they know. Then whenever one of them finds that the other disagrees, that fact is itself evidence: the first should treat the second's conclusion as information, adjust, and the two should converge. On this argument, a disagreement that survives after everything has been laid on the table cannot be a disagreement between two fully rational people who started from the same prior — something else must be going on. I have seen this attributed to a result called Aumann's agreement theorem. It seems to me the same reasoning extends past opinions to deals: if two people begin from the same beliefs and each knows everything the other knows, they cannot both think a bet or a trade between them is a good idea, so they should not trade at all on the strength of what each has separately found out.

Now here is the practice that puzzles me. The financial industry spends enormous sums on research — analysts, data, models — so that someone can buy a stock from a seller who has read the same filings and news and reached the opposite conclusion, with both sides believing they are getting the better of the exchange. At the same time, the same industry's most successful product is the index fund, which is built on the view that nobody reliably beats the market because prices already contain what everyone knows. And banks that both research companies and advise them are required to build walls inside the firm so that the two sides do not share what they know; the point of the wall is that information, if it moved, would give one side an advantage over people who lack it.

I would like you to treat this cluster of practices as something to be explained, and to tell me what view of belief and disagreement it presupposes. Does research-driven trading require that the two sides of a trade hold different starting beliefs, or different private information, or different readings of the same information — or does it only require that they be overconfident or mistaken? Which of those does the practice genuinely need, and which does it merely happen to fit? Is the cluster internally consistent: can one coherent picture of rational belief support active research, index funds, and information barriers at once, or do those three commitments pull in different directions? Where is the practice simply indifferent, in the sense that several rival views of rationality would license it equally well? And if the view you identify were replaced by the one in the argument I opened with, what in the practice would have to change, if anything?

Please take a definite position about what the practice presupposes, give your reasons, and defend that position against the alternatives rather than presenting a menu.
