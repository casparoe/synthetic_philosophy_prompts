I am putting together a seminar session for an undergraduate course on the philosophy of mind, and I want to use Christopher Nolan's Inception as the focal text, but I am worried that every treatment of that film in a classroom defaults to Descartes and the dreaming argument, which is not really what I want to discuss. What interests me is a narrower and stranger question that the film seems to take for granted: whether dreams are experiences at all. There is an older debate here that I find genuinely unsettling. Norman Malcolm, in his book Dreaming, argued that the phrase "I dreamed" is not a report of anything one underwent while asleep; on his view the concept of dreaming is anchored to the waking recollection and to the way we tell and assess such reports, so that the notion of a dream as a lived, real-time episode is close to a confusion, something we retroactively project onto the night. Daniel Dennett reached a superficially similar conclusion by a very different route, suggesting that the "dream" may be assembled at or after the moment of waking rather than played out in the sleeping brain, so that there is no privileged inner cinema running during the night to be remembered. Both, in effect, deny that a dream is an experience in the way a perception or a waking thought is. I would like you to read Inception against both of them. The film's entire machinery -- shared dreaming, the differential time of nested levels, the kick, limbo, the totem that tells you whether you are awake -- presupposes that dreams are experiences occurring in real time, in a shared space, with real causal and emotional consequences for the dreamer, which is exactly the presupposition Malcolm and Dennett resist.

Please give me a reading that is faithful in both directions. Where does the film's premise put pressure on Malcolm and Dennett -- does the fact that we so naturally narrate dreams, and that Cobb's grief over Mal operates as though the dream-life were lived, tell in favor of the commonsense view, or is that precisely the illusion they are warning about? Where does their skepticism illuminate a specific scene or choice -- for instance the moment a character must perform a test to determine whether they are dreaming, or the film's final ambiguity about the spinning top, which arguably only makes sense if a dream can be introspectively identified as such while it is happening? I am especially interested in whether the film, by making dreams into public and engineerable spaces, quietly concedes Dennett's point that the dream is a constructed artifact rather than a raw experience, even as it stages that construction in real time. And I would like you to be honest about where the reading breaks down, since Inception may simply be a film about skepticism wearing the costume of a film about dreams, and Malcolm's analysis may not even get a grip on a fiction that never pretended to be a report of an actual night's sleep. Close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the two philosophers' arguments alone do not -- whether it dramatizes a cost of their view, or exposes a gap in it. No need for citations; I want the argument and the close reading, not a bibliography.
