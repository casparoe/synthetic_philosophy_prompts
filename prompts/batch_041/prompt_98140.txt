Health systems that ration care by cost per QALY put treatments on a single scale, fund whatever falls below a threshold, and treat a year of life in full health gained by one patient as interchangeable with a year gained by any other. Regulatory cost-benefit analysis does something similar when it converts reductions in mortality risk into money at a uniform value of a statistical life. Yet the same disciplines that generate these numbers officially hold that utility is ordinal and that comparing one person's welfare with another's is meaningless. Reconstruct the theory of interpersonal utility comparison these practices presuppose: what they must assume about the cardinality of welfare, about a common unit or numeraire in which gains to different people can be summed, and about the equal weighting of persons. Note that where the numbers are built from willingness to pay, the practice presupposes something stronger still — that a dollar marks the same, or at least a knowable, increment of welfare for rich and poor alike. Then distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely happen to assume, say which rival views (prioritarianism, a Rawlsian lexical priority for the worst off, a resolutely ordinal approach) would leave the arithmetic of cost per QALY intact and which would break it, and identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of welfare would license the same spreadsheet.
