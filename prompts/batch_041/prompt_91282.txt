For the last three years my firm's ethics week has ended with a satirical skit, and this year I have been handed the part of the job nobody wants: writing the handout that goes with it. The audience is a couple of hundred communications consultants who have all sat through the same compliance training and none of whom believe a word of it, and the joke we are trying to sustain is a document — a deadpan internal training manual, numbered clauses, house style, no narrator — for a firm that has made a settled corporate commitment never to lie. I would like you to write it, at roughly 800 words, with a two-sentence preamble, a run of numbered clauses, an appendix on historical precedent, and a three-question FAQ at the end. Set it in an invented jurisdiction whose statute (invented for the skit) prohibits only false statements of material fact and says nothing at all about statements that are true and yet will be understood otherwise, so that the firm's compliance department has formally concluded that honesty is a property of sentences rather than of audiences; the firm, Ashgrove & Bell, and the regulator, the Office of Statement Accuracy, are both fictional and should be treated as ordinary institutions. Please coin two or three house terms — a name for the manoeuvre of answering a question other than the one asked, a name for the practice of putting the exculpatory fact where nobody will look for it — and then use them, without fanfare, as if everyone in the building had used them for years.

The clauses should be funny because they are competent rather than because they are absurd. I want a worked example in which the sentence is true and the thing the listener concludes is false, with the manual explaining, levelly, that the second is the listener's business; a clause on omissions, drawing the act/omission line and telling the reader that a misunderstanding the firm did not create is not the firm's to correct, with a single qualifying clause underneath it that gives a little ground and then takes it back; a clause that acknowledges, with professional regret, that telling true things in order to mislead works better than denial and costs less than denial when it is discovered (my account team calls this the paltering clause, and it should get the psychology and the deniability right); and at least one clause where the framework visibly fails — a statement that is true only because the firm buried the document that would have made it false, or true in a place where nobody can check it — which the manual should handle not by resolving but by routing to a committee. The appendix should be deadpan-admiring about precedent, describing the casuists' old distinction between a wide mental reservation, where the words themselves could in principle bear the true sense given the circumstances, and a strict one, where they could not, and describing the American perjury ruling in which literally true but unresponsive answers could not be prosecuted and the remedy was said to be a better question. Cite nothing anywhere: no sources, no reading list, no names of living people, and describe precedents rather than quoting them.

Two last things. First, no winking: no narrator, no aside, no moment where the document admits it is a joke, and no clause that tips into self-parody by being obviously monstrous — the horror, such as it is, should come from how reasonable each individual clause sounds. Second, a colleague on the account team insists the whole manual is just applied Grice, and I would like the thing to be good enough that she cannot say it is applied Grice badly, so the said/implicated distinction has to be handled accurately and the position that a true sentence cannot be a lie has to be stated at its strongest rather than as a straw man. A reader who works in public affairs and a reader who has read the philosophy of language should both find it fair; by the end, without the manual ever conceding the point, the reader should be left with the actual question of whether there is a moral difference between lying and misleading, or only a legal one.
