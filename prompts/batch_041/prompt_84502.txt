I chair the mortality review committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital. When a patient dies on the wards in circumstances that raise a question, our committee reviews the record and issues a written determination — "preventable," "not preventable," or "indeterminate" — together with any system-level recommendations. The determination goes to the quality office and, where the family asks for it, to the family.

A case is before us now, and I have to circulate my draft determination before Thursday's meeting. Briefly: an elderly woman with several chronic conditions was discharged from our emergency department after a workup that omitted a test our own pathway recommends for her presentation; she returned two days later in extremis and died. The chart shows that the resident considered the test and was told by the attending that it was unnecessary. On the merits, my reading is that the death was preventable — not because anyone was careless, but because the pathway exists precisely to catch this and it was not followed.

What makes the case hard is what the quality office handed the committee last week. It had audited three years of our determinations against independent blinded panels that read the same records with the outcome removed. Where our committee had said "preventable," the blinded panel agreed roughly half the time. The disagreements were not scattered: they clustered on cases like this one, in elderly patients with several conditions, where the counterfactual is genuinely murky. The audit also found that our members' stated confidence bore almost no relation to whether the blinded panel agreed. I should add that I am not sure how much the audit itself ought to move me, since its "independent" panels are drawn from the same small pool of clinicians we are.

I find that I still believe the death was preventable, and I also believe, on the strength of the audit, that my judgment about preventability in cases of this kind is close to a coin flip. I cannot tell whether those two things can both be true of me at once, or, if they can, whether I am entitled to write "preventable" in the determination — or whether honesty requires the higher-order point to govern and the finding to read "indeterminate." Our charter defines "indeterminate" narrowly, as "the record does not permit a determination either way," which is not obviously what I would mean by it.

Two further wrinkles. Last month the committee adopted a rule I had proposed: a "preventable" finding now requires both a majority vote on the merits and a separate recorded vote on the committee's confidence in its own process, and if that second vote falls below two-thirds the finding is flagged and the case is referred for external audit. Tell me whether that rule actually resolves the problem or merely relabels it. And one of my most experienced colleagues, who has read the same audit, thinks the answer is obvious: where a process is known to be unreliable, the committee should stop issuing confident findings in this class of case at all. I am not persuaded, but I want his position stated at its strongest.

Please draft the determination memo. It should (1) recommend a concrete determination and any recommendations, with a timeframe for referral; (2) state and answer the strongest case against that recommendation, my colleague's included; (3) address directly whether I can rationally hold the first-order belief while accepting the higher-order evidence about my own reliability, and what that implies for what I may write; and (4) close with a short section on what would change the recommendation and where the reasoning is most likely to fail. I don't need a literature survey or citations — I need the argument applied to this case.
