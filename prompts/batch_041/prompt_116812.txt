I'm a philosophy professor who moonlights on our university's research-integrity and dual-use review panel -- it's mostly synthetic biology with a little ML mixed in, we get maybe three or four items a month that need a yes/no from us inside a week or two, and honestly no one on the panel really knows the wet-lab side. I keep getting stuck on the same shape of problem and I'd like to stop reinventing the wheel every time. The shape is this: the reason we're tempted to say yes to something risky is almost always "well, someone's going to do it anyway, and if it's us at least it's done carefully," and the reason we're tempted to say no is "but if we do it, we make it that much more likely to happen." Which is basically the unilateralist's curse as I understand it -- when a bunch of people can each independently do a thing and each one goes ahead whenever their own private expected-value sum comes out just barely positive, the collective result is skewed toward the thing happening even though most of them, on reflection, would've preferred it didn't, because it only takes the single most optimistic actor to tip it. Fine, I get the idea. What I don't have is any way to actually use it on a Tuesday afternoon.

So write me a decision procedure my panel can run on a submission. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure bottoms out in a clean yes or no, and -- importantly -- where it should hand the thing back to a person because we've stopped doing analysis and started doing values. It has to survive being run by someone who's read the paragraph above and not much else, in about twenty minutes, and it has to keep working when we can't get a number for anything. Two things I especially want it to get right. First, it must not collapse into "is this already out there?" -- the inevitable-discovery point and the curse point are genuinely different and I've watched people mash them together until the analysis was mush. Second, it should distinguish the case where our panel is itself the marginal actor (our doing it is what tips the scale) from the case where we're one of fifty and our contribution is basically a rounding error, because I suspect those two want opposite answers and I've never seen anyone spell out why. My partner says I'm overthinking this, but she doesn't have to sit on the panel.

Then run the procedure, start to finish, on two cases: (a) a methods paper from a group we fund that would make a nasty pathogen more transmissible, in a way that's probably a two-year project for a competent lab, and (b) our own group proposing to post a cleaned-up dataset and code that would let someone with the same training reproduce a risky result in an afternoon. I want to see it return an answer I won't like on at least one of them, and I want the sign-off line spelled out -- the chair signs, and the VP for research rubber-stamps anything that goes forward, so tell me what each of them is actually certifying. And at the end be straight with me about what the procedure can't do: which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where it'll misfire because the curse's own assumptions -- independent actors, each roughly rational, each roughly informed about what the others are doing -- don't hold in our little corner of the world.
