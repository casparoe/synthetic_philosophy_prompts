I am trying to determine whether the following pattern has an established name. If it does, I want to know which established thing it is and how it differs from its nearest neighbours; if it does not, I want a name and a definition. Either way the label matters less to me than the boundaries: what the pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

The pattern is this. An organisation adopts an instrument — a piece of software, a procedure, a specialist unit — in order to pursue a goal it already had. The instrument works. Because it works, the surrounding arrangements are gradually rebuilt around it. The people who used to do the task by hand are reassigned; the older methods are retired; the formats, schedules, and reporting lines of everything else are adjusted to suit the instrument. Each step is defensible in isolation, and each is taken by someone who has the authority to take it. A decade later the instrument is no longer one option among several; it is the condition under which the organisation functions at all. Now suppose that someone proposes to modify it, to replace it, or merely to audit it. The proposal has to be assessed using the instrument's own outputs, or by the staff whose positions exist because of it, or in terms of a disruption cost that is enormous precisely because the earlier steps were taken. The prudent conclusion is always to leave it alone — and leaving it alone is also what the instrument would want, if it wanted anything.

Two instances I keep returning to. The first is a parcel firm's routing system. It begins as an aid to human dispatchers, who are then removed; depots are relocated to suit the hub structure the algorithm prefers; and a proposal to change the routing objective is now evaluated by the resulting fall in on-time deliveries, a quantity that the current system both defines and optimises. The second is a national statistical office's survey instrument: a question set introduced decades ago has since become the basis of funding formulae, so any proposal to alter the questions is met with the objection that it would break the historical series on which the allocations depend.

Two features of this strike me as important, and I do not know how to weigh them. First, there is no moment of decision. Nothing was seized; at no point did anyone act outside their remit or against the organisation's stated aims; there is no villain and no single act one could point to. Second, the instrument does not merely become expensive to remove — it becomes the thing in terms of which removal is judged. The first feature I take to be a matter of degree and of bookkeeping. The second seems to me to be something else, and it is the part for which I cannot find a word.

I have met several nearby ideas and each seems to miss part of what I am describing: mission creep, regulatory capture, path dependence and lock-in, the self-preservation of bureaucracies, and the suggestion one hears from people who worry about machine intelligence — that a sufficiently capable machine, whatever it was trying to do, would end up defending itself and acquiring whatever it needed to keep going. I would like to know which of these, if any, is the right ancestor, what exactly each gets wrong, and whether there is already a single term covering the whole pattern, including the part about the instrument setting the standards by which it is judged. If no such term exists, please coin one, define it, and tell me what it excludes: whether it applies to a single person inside an organisation, to a whole market, and to a machine that no longer has any designer who understands it.
