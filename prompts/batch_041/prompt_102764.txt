You are a philosopher and historian of nationalism and of collective memory. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the candidate concepts that might fit the case below before you commit to any of them.

I am on the committee for my town's 400th-anniversary celebrations, and the research has unsettled me. Our signature midsummer bonfire, which everyone I know treats as immemorial, turns out to have been cooked up by a newspaper competition in the 1950s. A family recipe that my in-laws call ancient is unrecognized in the region it supposedly came from. A dialect word we all cite as medieval first shows up in a twentieth-century poem. I have read enough to know there is a concept called invented traditions, and I have some vague memory of nations being described as imagined communities, but neither seems to fit what actually puzzles me.

My puzzle is not the original fabrication. It is what happens afterward. Nobody in these cases was deceived at the start and nobody is lying now, yet within a generation or two the thing has become genuinely old in the only sense that matters to the people who keep it: they will defend its antiquity sincerely, transmit it faithfully, and retro-date it further with each retelling, all without any authority or hoaxer pushing it. Is there an established name for this second-order process, the way a young and manufactured custom quietly ages itself through ordinary transmission and comes to be experienced as authentic? If there is a term, tell me which nearest concepts it must be distinguished from and where the boundaries lie; if there is not, coin one and define it, including cases at the edge. I want the carving, not a label.
