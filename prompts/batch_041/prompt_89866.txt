At a friend's birthday dinner a fortnight ago I was offered a swap of two sealed envelopes, one containing twice as much as the other. I have taught the two-envelope paradox for six years, and I gave an answer at the table that I have been unhappy with ever since — which is why I am being unusually careful about marking this term's problem set. The question, worth 20 marks in a second-year undergraduate module on paradoxes and decision theory, with the two parts weighted equally, was this:

"(a) Reconstruct the exchange argument — that whatever amount x your envelope holds, the other envelope holds 1.25x in expectation, so you should always swap — and identify precisely where the reasoning fails. (b) Suppose instead that the pair of amounts is fixed in advance, that you open your envelope and find £20, and that you know the smaller of the two amounts was drawn from some distribution you regard as reasonable. Does the paradox return? Say what determines whether swapping is now the better option, and what that shows about the argument in (a)."

One answer, from a student who is normally among the strongest in the year, ran as follows:

"Let x be the amount in my envelope. The other envelope contains either 2x or x/2, and the two cases are equally likely, so the expected value of the other envelope is ½(2x) + ½(x/2) = 1.25x, which is greater than x. Nothing in the argument depended on the particular value of x, so it tells me to swap whatever I hold; running it again tells me to swap back, which is absurd. So the argument is invalid, and we need to find the false step.

The false step is the claim that the two cases are equally likely. That cannot hold for every x. A probability of one half at every point on the scale would require a distribution over the smaller amount that never tapers off, and no proper distribution does that: for large x the other envelope is almost certainly the smaller one, and for small x it is almost certainly the larger one. The two deviations from one half cancel out when averaged over everything I might hold, which is why the process as a whole offers no expected gain. The argument applies the same probability to every value of x, when the correct probability depends on x.

(b) Once I have opened my envelope and found £20, and I know that the smaller amount was drawn from a proper distribution, the argument cannot be revived: the probability p that £20 is the smaller amount is fixed by that distribution and will not in general be one half. The other envelope has expected value 40p + 10(1 − p), so I should swap only if I think it more likely than not that £20 is the smaller of the two. What this shows is that the paradox depends on the amounts being unbounded: once we stipulate a maximum, the premise that the two cases are equally likely fails at the top of the range and the paradox disappears."

Please grade the answer, writing the comments as feedback the student could act on. Use these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: "Verdict" (the mark out of 20 and one sentence on where the answer stands, about 40 words); "Credit due" (about 120 words); "Where the reasoning slips" (about 200 words — for each slip, quote the phrase that gives it away, explain why it is wrong, and supply the corrected reasoning); "What a first-class answer would have added" (about 120 words); "Mark breakdown" (how the 20 marks fall across the two parts and the deduction for each slip, about 80 words).
