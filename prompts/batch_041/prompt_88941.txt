I'm TAing an upper-level decision theory course this term (phil grad students plus a couple of econ MA's) and I have to write the final. I want one essay question on risk-weighted expected utility that can't be answered by reciting the standard setup — beliefs, desires and risk attitudes are three seperate things, REU represents all three, so an REU agent can be genuinely risk averse without being a closet expected-utility maximizer with a concave utility function. Most of them can do that spiel in their sleep, so that's exactly what I don't want to see.

Here's what I want the question to bite on. We worked out in class that for a two-outcome bet paying x with chance p and y with chance 1 − p (x better than y), REU reduces to u(y) + r(p)[u(x) − u(y)]. Which means that over two-outcome bets, an REU agent is choice-for-choice identical to an expected utility maximizer who takes the chance of the good outcome to be r(p) instead of p — i.e. someone whose *beliefs* are systematically off in a particular direction (and a risk-averse r, convex with r(p) ≤ p, makes her a pessimist). The two models only come apart once their are three or more outcomes, because then r gets applied to the probability of doing at least that well, worst-to-best, rather than to each outcome's own probability.

So the question should make them (a) say what work the rank-dependence does at three-plus outcomes that a plain belief-distorting EU maximizer can't do, and (b) confront whether that rescues the claim that r is a risk attitude rather than a relabelled distortion of belief — in particular, whether any pattern of choices could ever tell the two readings apart, or whether the separation of beliefs from risk attitudes is something the formalism asserts rather than reveals. If they can also say what happens to the picture if you let r vary with the act's structure (a different risk function for bigger-stake gambles) instead of being one fixed function, great.

Please give me the question itself (a few sentences of setup is fine, and it's fine to hand them the two-outcome formula), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must make — and for each point, the usual way students blow it. They've read the chapter and done a problem set on Allais-style cases, so they know the machinery; the question shouldn't be answerable by handing the machinery back to me. 45 minutes, closed book.
