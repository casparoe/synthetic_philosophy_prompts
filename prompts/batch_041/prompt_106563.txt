I've been reading Dewey on and off for about a year now, mostly because a friend and I got into a long arguement about whether voting is even the interesting part of democracy, and I keep getting stuck on the bit where he says a problem is basically a situation that's gone wobbly — you're not just sitting there wondering, something in the world has actually come unstuck — and thinking is what you do to get it back on its feet, and whatever you land on is only ever provisional, because the real test is what happens when you act on it and see. Fine, I buy that for one person. What I can't work out is how you're supposed to do it with a whole country, where there's no single mind doing the inquiring and no clean way to run the experiment and then check the result.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take the Irish Citizens' Assembly — the one set up in 2016, where they got about a hundred randomly picked ordinary people in a room, gave them time, experts, alot of small-group discussion and weekends of it, and it ended up recommending a big change to the abortion law, which then went to a referendum in 2018 and passed about two to one. Read that whole episode through Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, and do it in both directions. Tell me who the inquirer is supposed to be here (the assembly? the parliament that set it up? the electorate?), what the actual unsettled situation was, what counts as the hypothesis and what counts as the test, and then where his picture of inquiry stops fitting: the question was handed to them by the Oireachtas instead of growing out of the situation itself; a referendum is a one-shot deal with no do-over, which sits badly with him insisting that every conclusion stays open to revision; and I don't know what to do with the fact that this looks like it created a public rather than being an inquiry carried out by one that already existed. Then flip it — what does the Irish case show about his theory? Does it confirm him, embarrass him, or is it a case he never anticipated?

Last thing, and it's the part I actually care about. If the whole point is that inquiry never really closes, why is this a one-off? I keep daydreaming about making it permanent — a standing body of randomly picked people who get to choose their own questions instead of being handed them, who publish their conclusions as provisional, and who have to revisit those conclusions after five years whether they like it or not. Someone told me the German-speaking bit of Belgium has set up something permanent along these lines, though I haven't checked and you shouldn't lean on it. Is that genuinely what Dewey's view implies, or am I just bolting a gadget onto his philosophy? Please write it all in British English, and please don't just give me the standard summary of his views — I want the mapping done properly, and I want you to say plainly where the theory goes quiet instead of pretending it covers everything.
