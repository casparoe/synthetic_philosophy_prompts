Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about heritability, and we parted with him convinced that I was refusing to face the numbers and me convinced that he was reading a variance statistic as though it were a claim about causes. Since we were both arguing from memory about a paper neither of us had in front of us, I have decided to settle the matter by constructing a toy study in which I control every number, writing down the conclusions he defended, and asking for a verdict. I would like a tier list.

The toy study. Four thousand children, followed from age four to twelve, with reading fluency measured every year. The sample includes monozygotic twins raised together (correlation between the twins' scores, about 0.85), monozygotic twins separated at birth and raised in different families (about 0.60), dizygotic twins raised together (about 0.50), and adopted children, whose scores correlate about 0.30 with their biological parents and about 0.05 with the parents who raised them. When the sample is split by household income, the estimated heritability of reading fluency is roughly 0.70 in the upper half and roughly 0.30 in the lower half; the estimate for the sample as a whole is about 0.60, with shared environment accounting for roughly 0.10. Nothing in the study is an experiment, and the adoption placements were not random.

The ten conclusions.

1. A heritability of 0.60 means that, for a given child in the sample, roughly 60 per cent of the reason she reads as well as she does is genetic and 40 per cent is environmental.

2. The figure is a fact about this sample, in this range of environments, at this moment, and not about reading in general; widen the range of environments and the figure could move.

3. Since heritability is 0.70 in the upper half of the sample and 0.30 in the lower half, roughly 70 per cent of the gap in average reading scores between the two halves is genetic.

4. Since the estimate is high, the scope for raising reading levels through better instruction is limited: genes account for most of the variation, so teaching can move the average only slightly.

5. Since the adopted children resemble their biological parents far more than the parents who raised them, the family environment barely matters for reading.

6. In a population where every child received the same instruction and grew up in the same kind of household, heritability would be near 1, even though it would be that uniform environment doing the work of making the children literate at all.

7. High heritability means that with enough DNA samples we could predict individual children's reading scores reasonably well.

8. A trait that everyone in a population has, such as two hands, has a heritability of zero, which shows how little heritability tells us about how genetically controlled a trait is.

9. A trait can be highly heritable even when something that every member of the population shares, such as a nutrient in the diet, is causally required for the trait to appear at all.

10. Because the heritability estimate differs between the two halves of the sample, genes and environments cannot be separated even in principle, and heritability estimates are therefore of little use.

Sort these ten into four tiers: those that would survive a careful methods seminar without qualification; those that are defensible only with a caveat that is itself the whole point; those that are misleading enough that I would strike them from a policy brief; and those that are simply false. Give a one-sentence justification for each placement, and where an item genuinely straddles two tiers, say which two and what turns on the choice. Then tell me which items fail for the same underlying reason, name the single subtlest item — the one a well-read non-specialist is most likely to nod along to — and close with a short statement of what the study does entitle us to conclude, particularly about whether and how reading could be improved. Work from the numbers given; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
