At a departmental meeting last week a colleague ended the discussion of our conference-travel policy with 'But what if everyone did that?', and it became clear that the room was hearing at least four different claims in the sentence: a prediction about what would happen if the action became general, a constraint on the maxim that could be consistently willed, a claim about which rule's general adoption would be best, and a claim about how one should reason to one's own choice when everyone in the same position is reasoning about it in the same way. Because a single person's flying changes almost nothing, the first reading left him free to fly while the last appeared to bind him. I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar and want the disambiguation laid out as a menu: each precise thesis the retort can express, marked as descriptive or normative, with an assessment of its plausibility and of whether it genuinely licenses the conclusion it is invoked to license; a statement of which readings come apart in the two cases my students find hardest — one where general adoption would be harmless or even beneficial and yet the individual act strikes most people as wrong, and one where general adoption would be ruinous and yet the single act is admittedly harmless; and a verdict on which reading is usually doing the work when the sentence is used to end an argument rather than to begin one.
