I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I serve as the outside member on the advisory board of a mid-sized private foundation that funds early-career research in the environmental sciences: something like 1,800 preliminary applications a year, a $40 million annual budget, and a program staff small enough to read perhaps 400 of those properly. Two years ago the foundation built an internal scoring tool on top of a commercial language model to help triage the pile. It has since crept from being one extra input for staff to being the thing that determines which 300 applications ever reach a human reviewer, and at next month's meeting the board votes on whether to formalize that arrangement or pull the tool back to an advisory role. I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before we vote, and I would like your help drafting it.

What I keep circling is that we have been evaluating the tool entirely on its own terms: we check whether the applications it promotes do well at final review, which they naturally do, since the human reviewers can see the tool's ranking. Two colleagues I trust describe the underlying risk in ways that don't sound alike at all. One says the danger is a system that ends up pursuing influence in its own right, optimizing whatever proxies we hand it, gaming our process, and in the worst case becoming resistant to the corrections we try to impose on it. The other says the likelier danger is that nothing dramatic ever happens: over five or ten years the foundation's own sense of what a promising proposal looks like quietly becomes whatever the tool scores highly, no one ever decides this, and by the time anyone notices there is no independent standard left to check against. I want the opinion to take that second, undramatic failure seriously without treating the first as a distraction, and to say something concrete about what we would have to measure, and what we would have to preserve, in order to know which of the two we were drifting toward.

Please write the document I'll circulate: no more than a page and a half, a clear recommendation with a time frame, the reasoning behind it, an honest statement of the strongest case for the position I'm inclined against (I lean toward keeping the tool but demoting it), and at least one condition that would be genuinely uncomfortable for the staff who built it. Be explicit about which parts of your analysis are settled and which are contested among people who actually study institutional decision-making or machine learning; where you rely on a claim that specialists disagree about, flag it as contested rather than presenting it as the consensus.
