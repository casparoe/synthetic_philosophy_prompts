ok so I've been trying to sort out how I feel about taking other people's word for aesthetic things, and I've got a pile of gut verdicts that I suspect don't all hang together. hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

- A friend whose taste I trust says a new restaurant is great. I've never eaten there. I think it's completely fine for me to believe her, and to say, if someone asks, that it's a good restaurant.
- Same friend says a film is great. Also fine. I believe it, I put it on the list, I tell other people it's supposed to be great.
- A sommelier tells me the Barolo is better than the Chianti and I've tasted neither. I just believe the Barolo is the better wine. No problem.
- But: someone tells me a painting is beautiful, and I've never laid eyes on it and probably never will. I think I can believe it's famous, or that people find it beautiful, but I don't think I can believe it's beautiful, and I sure can't say it's beautiful myself. That feels different from the wine, and I can't articulate why.
- Someone tells me a novel's last chapter reveals the narrator was lying the whole time. I believe that instantly, and it changes how I'd judge the book once I read it. Fine.
- A friend says a certain piece of music is devastating, makes her cry every time. I believe the music is devastating — devastating to her, devastating to most people who sit with it. But I don't feel like I've learned that the music is sad the way I'd learn it by actually hearing it. Though now I wonder if that's just the painting case again.
- A dance critic whose technical eye I trust says a performance was superb in its phrasing and balance, though I sat there bored stiff. I take the technical verdict on board. I don't take on board the unspoken "so you should have loved it."
- Here's the one I keep flip-flopping on. An old friend and I have nearly identical taste in poetry. She texts me a poem and says I'll love it. Do I know the poem is good before I read it? My gut says I know I'll love it — that's a prediction — but I don't know it's good, that's a judgement, and judgements need me and the poem in the same room. But then the restaurant case comes right back at me: I was perfectly happy to call the restaurant good on her say-so. Why is a poem different from a restaurant?
- And the convergence case: if every serious critic for two hundred years has called some composer a genius and I've never heard a note, is my saying "he's a genius" just shorthand for "everybody says so," or is it a real belief I'm entitled to hold?

I think I might have half a principle already: you can take testimony for any judgement that's really about how a thing will strike people, me included, but not for a judgement about the thing itself — its beauty, whatever that is. But I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being precious about paintings while happily outsourcing my wine opinions.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close to all? Which of my verdicts does it make me give up, and what does it say about a case I didn't list — say, a friend tells me a joke is funny and I haven't heard it. Also, if the answer is "it depends what kind of aesthetic judgement it is," please say what the kinds are, precisely, and which clause of the principle each of my cases is doing the work of motivating.
