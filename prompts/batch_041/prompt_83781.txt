I write a small philosophy blog, and I want to build a self-scoring quiz — 'where do you actually stand on calling something a genocide?' — for readers who have just come out of a heated discussion about atrocity and naming. Please write exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no introduction, no conclusion, no separate scoring key, no closing summary. Each item is one concrete, invented case with two or three short answer options, plus, inside the same item, the position each option points to, so that the key is inline and a reader can tally their own answers by the time the fifth item ends. Inventing the cases is deliberate: I do not want the quiz to become a referendum on real, contested historical events, or to turn on disputed facts about particular atrocities. I also want readers sorted by what they say about cases rather than by the label they happen to like, so no item may simply ask whether the word is legal, moral, or political in character.

Five positions must be separable, and each must be named the first time an option points to it. (a) The word is a legal term of art whose narrowness is a feature: it requires intent to destroy a protected group as such, in whole or in part, working with the list of groups as adopted — national, ethnic, racial, religious — with political groups left out. (b) The word is a moral verdict whose legal narrowness was a historical compromise: destroying a group's language, religion, and institutions counts even where few are killed, and the list of protected groups is an artifact of negotiation. (c) The word is an accusation rather than a description, its application settled by recognition and power, so the only tractable question is who gains by applying it. (d) The word names a real social kind: there is a fact of the matter, the term is vague at the edges (how much killing is 'in part'; must the group be the target or only the foreseeable casualty), but vagueness is not the same as arbitrariness. (e) The word should be retired in favour of a graded scale of atrocity, because a binary forces exactly the line-drawing that (b) and (d) disagree about.

The cases must include a campaign that destroys a group's language and places of worship and sterilises its members while killing only those who resist; a mass killing aimed at a political faction as an organised force; and a famine produced by indifference, where the perpetrators foresaw but did not intend the group's destruction. Where two of the five positions would answer one of my items identically, say so inside that item rather than papering over it, and use one item to record which of the five the quiz as a whole cannot separate. Do not quote any statute or convention, do not attribute any of the positions to a named theorist, and keep every item to a sentence or two.
