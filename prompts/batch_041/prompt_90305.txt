For my school's philosophy club, each of us has to present a poem that does the work of an argument — the form has to track the idea, not just decorate it — and I want mine to be about a result in provability logic that I find genuinely strange. Please take on the role of a poet who is also at home in formal logic: someone who can write verse that a reader who has never heard of the result will still enjoy, while a logician who does know it will find nothing to wince at. Before you write, think through the mapping in a few sentences — what the recurring lines correspond to, where the turn is, why the ending has to loop — and then write the poem.

Here is the idea, in my own words, so you do not have to look anything up; please follow my framing rather than some textbook version. Imagine a reasoner that can prove mathematical statements and can also reason about its own proofs. Write "□A" for "the reasoner proves A." A natural policy is total self-trust: whenever the reasoner proves A, it accepts A — in symbols, it adopts the schema □A → A for every A. The strange result is that this cannot be a consistent policy. If the reasoner ever manages to prove, in general, that its own proofs are sound, then it proves A for every A at all, including statements that are plainly false. (Set A to a contradiction and you get the familiar fact that such a system cannot prove its own consistency.) The upshot is that a self-trusting reasoner must be unable to certify its own trust: the more it tries to prove itself reliable, the more it proves, until it proves anything.

I want the poem to be a pantoum, because I think the form does exactly this. In a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each quatrain come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza is expected to return to the poem's opening line, so the poem ends by re-deriving itself. Please write four to six quatrains in the first person, in the voice of the reasoner. Let the lines that keep returning be its self-trust — the claim that whatever it proves is so — so that the repetition sounds like a proof carrying itself forward. The closing return to the opening line should be the moment the loop seals and the reasoner, having certified its own soundness, proves something obviously false. The last line should land as a discovery, not a punchline: the reader should feel the system overreach rather than hear a joke. Keep the meter and rhyme regular enough that it reads as a real pantoum, but do not let the form flatten the sense.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, footnotes, or references to any particular paper or author. After the poem, add two or three sentences explaining how the form mirrors the result, in case I have to defend it to the club.
