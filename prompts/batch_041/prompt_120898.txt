I am a philosopher of social ontology by training — my work concerns shared intention, joint commitment, and the ontology of the first-person plural — but I hold a joint appointment with a group that builds multi-agent systems, and for three years I have co-taught a cross-listed graduate seminar with the distributed-systems faculty. I mention this only to establish precisely how fluent I am in the target vocabulary and how fluent I am not. I can read a protocol paper and follow the argument; I know what local states, message-passing, unreliable channels, consensus, two-phase commit, idempotent operations, at-least-once versus exactly-once delivery, and Byzantine faults are; and I understand the technical notion of common knowledge — the sense in which a fact is not merely known by each party but known to be known, to all orders — along with the coordinated-attack result that over a channel which can lose messages, two parties can never attain common knowledge that a message arrived, however many acknowledgments they exchange. What I cannot do is write the protocol paper myself, and I have no wish to learn. What I want from you is a translation, and then a careful inventory of the translation's failures.

Restate, as faithfully as the vocabulary above permits, the three canonical positions on shared intention in my own field. The first holds that collective intentionality is primitive: a we-intention is a basic form of intentionality rather than a compound of individual intentions plus mutual belief, and a single agent can hold one alone — a player standing by himself on the field can intend that we are executing this play. The second treats a shared intention as a web of interlocking individual intentions, each with its own content, together with meshing sub-plans and mutual responsiveness, with any obligations among the parties falling out of the coordinating structure rather than constituting it. The third holds that joint commitment creates a plural subject: the parties are jointly committed, the commitment cannot be rescinded by either of them alone, and it carries entitlements and obligations that no participant can unilaterally dissolve. For each position, give me the protocol rendering as literally as you can — say where the we-intention would live in the system's state, what plays the role of meshing sub-plans, what the nearest analogue of joint commitment is (two-phase commit is the obvious candidate, and I want to know how far the resemblance really runs), and what plays the role of common knowledge. Then tell me where the rendering has to cheat: which term I will be tempted to redefine so that it does double duty across the two frameworks, and which distinctions in the original the rendering quietly erases.

The residue report is what I care about most, and I would like you to test my hypothesis rather than confirm it. My own guess is that what fails to survive is normativity, and with it the plural subject. A protocol can guarantee that a process either commits or aborts; it cannot make an abort a wrong, confer standing on the other party to complain, or generate an obligation that outlives the coordination problem it was introduced to solve — fault tolerance treats deviation as something to be tolerated, not as something to be answered. If that is right, then the third position is largely inexpressible in this vocabulary, and the second is expressible only if we read its talk of obligations as decorative. I want you to weigh the opposite diagnosis as well: that the vocabulary's silence is not a defect of the vocabulary but evidence that the philosophical views were over-claiming, that we intend is doing no work beyond mutual belief plus a coordination equilibrium, and that the coordinated-attack result marks something real — namely that if shared intention required common knowledge, joint action over any lossy channel would be impossible, which is either a reductio of that requirement or a discovery about how modest our shared intentions actually are. Say which diagnosis you favor and how confident you are, and be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to the other verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail — in particular, whether at any point you are importing an assumption from one framework into the other and calling the result a translation. No citations or reading list, please; argue from the content, and write in American English.
