I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am midway through a long feature on the way psychiatric categories shape the people they are applied to. My editor has drafted the explainer box that will run alongside the piece, and I want a second opinion before it goes to the fact-checker, because something about it troubles me in a way I cannot quite pin down. Here is the draft.

Psychiatry sorts people into categories: depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, personality disorder. Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects explains why these categories are made rather than found. When someone is labelled, she becomes aware of the label and her behaviour shifts to fit it; clinicians then revise the category in the light of what they see; the revised category reshapes the next cohort of patients in turn. Hacking calls categories of this sort interactive kinds, as opposed to the indifferent kinds studied by the natural sciences, since quarks, on his account, do not care what physicists say about them. Because the people inside the loop are shaped by the classification, there is no fact of the matter about whether a given patient really has the disorder; what looks like discovery is the settling of a social convention. And since the loop runs in one direction, the label reshaping the patient and the patient then reinforcing the label, diagnosis is self-fulfilling: the more a condition is diagnosed, the more of it there is. That is how multiple personality disorder spread through the 1980s, and how ADHD has spread since.

What I would like from you is an audit, claim by claim: what is accurate as written, what is defensible but needs a caveat, and what would make a specialist wince. Where the draft has quietly merged two claims that the underlying view keeps apart, or has turned a description into a verdict, say so plainly and explain what turns on the difference. I am especially unsure whether the closing claim about diagnosis spreading a condition follows from anything in the earlier sentences, or is an extra step the argument does not license, and whether the draft is right to treat this as a general truth about psychiatric classification rather than something that holds in particular cases. I would also like to know what a defender of the view would say the box has left out. No bibliography or reading list, please; just tell me what to keep, what to soften, what to cut, and why.
