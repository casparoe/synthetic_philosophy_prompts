ok so I'm slowly working through Sor Juana's reply to the bishop of Puebla — the long letter usually called the Reply to Sor Filotea — and I've hit the middle stretch where she shifts from defending her own habit of studying into defending women's education in general, and I genuinely can't tell what the argument is doing there.

I only have it in a Spanish edition and my Spanish is not up to baroque prose, so what follows is my own plain-English reconstruction of the sequence of moves, not the letter's wording — I'm sure I've mangled it:

she says she was born with a strong inclination to letters, that the inclination is God-given, and that smothering it would be like burying the talent; that she never studied in order to teach in public or to publish, only to understand; that she was shut out of the university and had to learn on her own; that women kept from learning are worse off, not safer; that women teaching other women and girls is a better arrangement than men instructing women in private; that the prohibition she keeps getting cited at her is about speaking in church, not about study; that she's been told to cultivate a holy ignorance, but that she doesn't think the ignorance scripture praises is the kind you get from refusing to learn what could be learned; and that she learned a great deal in the kitchen, and that if Aristotle had spent more time cooking he'd have written more philosophy. Then at the end she says she submits the whole thing to the Church and will stop writing if told to.

What I can't get my head around:

- Do the submission and the defense belong to the same argument, or is one the argument and the other the frame? What in the letter tells you which?
- What work does the women-teaching-women move do that the God-given-talent move doesn't? Is it prudence, theology, or both?
- Is the kitchen bit just a charming aside, or is it a claim about where knowledge comes from — a swipe at the book-learning hierarchy?
- What tradition is she actually leaning on with the holy ignorance business, and is she conceding the point or quietly redefining it?
- Does being a nun under obedience help her here or box her in?
- And what would a 17th-century reader have heard in this that I'm missing — what am I importing from modern feminism that she wouldn't recognize?
