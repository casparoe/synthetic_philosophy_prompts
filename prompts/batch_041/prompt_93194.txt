I run the scoring side of a small forecasting outfit — a few hundred resolvable questions a year (elections, court rulings, whether a bill clears committee, how many named storms), submitted by members, all run through a Slack and a spreadsheet. Every quarter we grade everyone with a Brier score and a log score, publish a leaderboard and calibration curves, and pay the top few; every forecast also has to come with a short written rationale that we post and that I do actually read. I was arguing with a friend who does philosophy of science about what all this commits us to, and now I can't stop seeing it. The whole reason we use a proper scoring rule instead of just counting hits is that we want it to be optimal for a forecaster to write down the number she actually believes, and the reason we want *that* is that we think a credence is good exactly to the extent that it's accurate — closer to the truth, whatever else it's doing. Fine. But then the pieces don't obviously line up. We rank people and pay prizes by score, which I'm told is definately not the same thing as scoring each person separately against the truth, since a ranked contest rewards a different kind of betting than an honest number does. We require written rationales, and we give the better ones airtime, even though nothing in the score depends on the reasoning — only the number. We quietly only ask questions that will resolve cleanly by a fixed deadline, so the things people actually care about (whether a treaty holds up in twenty years, whether some technology goes badly) never get scored at all, even though we'd say the point of the exercise is accuracy about things that matter. And once you've been accurate for three years we start treating you as a credible source on subjects you've never touched, which I can't derive from accuracy being the only good in play.

So what does this practice presuppose? I want a real reconstruction of the implicit epistemology, not platitudes: what it has to be committed to (that credences are the thing under evaluation, that their only epistemic virtue is accuracy, that the score is a stand-in for the truth itself, that the forecaster's utility is linear in the score), which rival pictures of epistemic goodness it quietly rules out (that understanding, explanation, knowledge, or coherence is valuable in itself; that accuracy on unscoreable questions counts for anything; that *why* you believe something bears on whether the belief is good), and then where the practice is honestly underdetermined — could a knowledge-first person, or someone who thinks credences are the wrong unit and we should be grading outright beliefs, or a pragmatist who thinks a forecast is only good relative to a use, run the same shop with the same rules and change nothing? Where the pieces genuinely conflict, tell me which ones would have to go, and describe concretely what this outfit would look like if we built it on a different theory of epistemic goodness — what we'd start scoring, what we'd stop scoring, and what we'd start paying for.
