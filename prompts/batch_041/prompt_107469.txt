I'm a second-year PhD student on the decision-theory / formal-epistemology side of things, and I need to put together a short research proposal — two pages, for a summer fellowship, and honestly it can be scrappy. The thing that got me onto this: last spring I had to decide between a stable postdoc-track job and going in with two friends on a company, and I kept noticing I wasn't scared of the *risk* so much as I had no idea what the probabilities even were. Like, I could name a number, but it would be fake. That made me wonder whether a lot of what the risk-weighted expected utility framework treats as a stable feature of the agent — the "risk function," the thing that makes you treat a small chance of a bad outcome as if it were bigger then you actually think it is — might really be second-order uncertainty, i.e. a belief about how much to trust your own probability estimate, wearing a risk-attitude costume. Here's how REU is usually set up, in my own words so we're on the same page: you rank the outcomes of a gamble from worst to best, and the gamble's value is a sum over outcomes, where each outcome's utility is weighted by [risk function of the cumulative probability of doing at least that well] minus [risk function of the cumulative probability of doing strictly better]. The risk function gets applied to probabilities, and it's a property of the person, not of the situation. My half-formed suspicion is that in exactly the cases people care about — one-shot, high-stakes, thin-evidence choices — you can't tell apart "this agent has a concave risk function" from "this agent is unsure of her own probabilities," and that the framework quietly needs the second thing to be off the table in order to count as a genuine alternative to plain expected utility. I want the proposal to turn that suspicion into a real question.
