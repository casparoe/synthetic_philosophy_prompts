I am a fourth-year PhD student in political science, and in a few weeks I have to run a ninety-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on democratic theory and social choice. There are seventeen students, the room has movable chairs and a whiteboard, and I want to spend the session on an activity rather than a lecture. My aim is narrower, and stranger, than the usual demonstration of a majority cycle. I want the students to arrive at the impossibility themselves: to articulate a handful of decision-rule desiderata that they find individually compelling, to try to construct a rule that honors all of them, to fail, and to discover — this is the part I care about most — that the desideratum they will quietly abandon, usually without noticing they have done so, is the one about ignoring alternatives that no one ranks differently. I would also like them to feel that much of the theorem's force comes from our having restricted ourselves to purely ordinal, interpersonally incomparable preferences, so that when the activity later permits them to use information about how strongly people prefer things, the impossibility ought to seem to loosen. Both of these should fall out of what they do, not out of anything I say beforehand; ideally they do not hear the name of the theorem until the debrief.

Design the activity for me. Give me the rules, the materials, the roles or group structure, the sequence with rough timings, and whatever small stakes or payoffs will keep it honest without real money. Arrange it so that the failure is something they run into rather than something I reveal, and so that the moment when a previously unobjectionable condition starts to look expensive is visible in the room. Then give me the debrief: how each stage of what happened maps onto the formal conditions, how I should handle the student who insists that some cleverer rule escapes the result, and how I can tell whether they have understood the theorem rather than merely felt thwarted. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply myself.
