My suspicion is that the debate has been kept alive less by a genuine disagreement than by a quiet equivocation, and that the equivocation can be made precise in a way that dissolves part of the dispute and relocates the rest. The Williams-style internalist holds that any true statement of the form 'A has a reason to φ' must be answerable to A's subjective motivational set, whereas the externalist holds that some such statements are true regardless of what A happens to care about. I want to argue that each side is tracking a different role the word 'reason' plays: one in first-person deliberation, where a consideration must be able to move the agent, and one in third-person appraisal, where a consideration must be able to license criticism of the agent. On this reading the two roles impose different admissibility conditions on what is ostensibly the same relation. The open question I want to pose is whether a single notion of a reason can discharge both roles at once, or whether the term is systematically ambiguous between them; and if it is ambiguous, whether the ambiguity is benign, a mere matter of register, or does real explanatory work, so that internalism and externalism turn out to be answers to two different questions rather than rival answers to one and the same question.
