I have been reading the first chapter of Henri Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* in the English translation of 1905, and there is a run of paragraphs at the opening that I can follow sentence by sentence but cannot assemble into a single argument. Here is the passage:

"The very possibility of mathematical science seems an insoluble contradiction. If this science is only deductive in appearance, from whence is derived that perfect rigour which is challenged by none? If, on the contrary, all the propositions which it enunciates may be derived in order by the rules of formal logic, how is it that mathematics is not reduced to a gigantic tautology? The syllogism can teach us nothing essentially new, and if everything must spring from the principle of identity, then everything should be capable of being reduced to that principle. Are we then to admit that the enunciations of all the theorems with which so many volumes are filled, are only indirect ways of saying that A is A?

No doubt we may refer back to axioms which are at the source of all these reasonings. If it is felt that they cannot be reduced to the principle of contradiction, if we decline to see in them any more than experimental facts which have no part or lot in mathematical necessity, there is still one resource left to us: we may class them among à priori synthetic views. But this is no solution of the difficulty it is merely giving it a name; and even if the nature of the synthetic views had no longer for us any mystery, the contradiction would not have disappeared; it would have only been shirked. Syllogistic reasoning remains incapable of adding anything to the data that are given it; the data are reduced to axioms, and that is all we should find in the conclusions.

No theorem can be new unless a new axiom intervenes in its demonstration; reasoning can only give us immediately evident truths borrowed from direct intuition; it would only be an intermediary parasite. Should we not therefore have reason for asking if the syllogistic apparatus serves only to disguise what we have borrowed?

Even if these consequences are challenged, it must be granted that mathematical reasoning has of itself a kind of creative virtue, and is therefore to be distinguished from the syllogism."

I want a reconstruction of the argument rather than a summary of Poincaré's philosophy of mathematics, and I want it aimed at the following four questions.

1. State the contradiction he says besets mathematical science, and say exactly what is supposed to be incompatible with what. He allows that treating the axioms as "experimental facts" is a way out of one horn of the dilemma, but he treats the appeal to synthetic a priori views as no way out at all. Spell out the difference between the two moves, and say what an appeal to the synthetic a priori would have to accomplish in order to count as a solution rather than a relabelling.

2. Take the sentence about reasoning being "an intermediary parasite" and explain what the metaphor is being made to carry. Does "direct intuition" here name a source of knowledge, a psychological capacity, or a constraint on what may be counted as a demonstration? What work does it do in the argument that the syllogism cannot be creative?

3. This is the part I care about most. A constructivist is often described as holding that a proof of "for every natural number n, P(n)" must be a construction that can be recognised to yield P(n) for any n we might actually reach, and that this is why she refuses to treat the principle of recurrence as a proposition about an independently existing, completed totality of numbers; on that description the principle functions instead as a rule licensing the passage from P(n) to P(n+1) — a licence to build rather than a truth to be known. Does the passage support that reading of the principle, or does Poincaré's appeal to intuition commit him to something the reading excludes? Give me the textual evidence for whichever answer you settle on, and state explicitly what the two readings disagree about — what would be true on one and false on the other — rather than merely labelling each.

4. Finally, consider whether his own complaint that naming the axioms synthetic a priori leaves the contradiction where it was can be turned back against his appeal to direct intuition. If it can, say how, and say what he would have to give up in order to answer it. If it cannot, say what makes the two cases disanalogous.

Two constraints. Do not tell me what later commentators or historians have said about Poincaré; work from the passage and from what it directly presupposes. And where a step of the reconstruction needs something the passage does not supply, say so explicitly rather than filling the gap, so that I can see which parts of the argument are in the text and which are being added.
