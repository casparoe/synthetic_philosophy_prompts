Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like you to treat it as central rather than as an aside. Satisficing says you stop when you hit an aspiration level — you take the option that's good enough instead of the best. But where does the aspiration level come from? The version I was taught (Simon's, I think) is that it drifts: it rises when you've been doing well and falls when things get scarce. That's pleasingly dynamic, but it also looks circular if the drift is itself tuned to do well. And if you say instead that the agent picks whatever aspiration level maximizes expected value net of thinking costs, you've just rebuilt optimization one level up — and now you need a rule for how much to think about how much to think. There's a program I half-remember, coming out of AI and lately picked up by some cognitive-science people, that leans right into this: a bounded agent should choose the inference procedure with the best payoff once you charge it for its own runtime, and that's supposed to be a kind of optimality rather than a retreat from it. Is that a genuine successor to Simon's project or just optimization with the cost term made explicit, and does anyone have a principled way to stop the regress other than by fiat ("compute until the clock runs out" strikes me as giving up rather than answering)? Related things I suspect are upstream but can't tell: whether satisficing is meant as a claim about behavior, a claim about norms, or a recipe for building agents; whether the environment half of Simon's scissors does load-bearing work that the formal models quietly drop; and whether any of this bites on how we should think about machine agents, which is about half the reason the trustees are interested at all.
