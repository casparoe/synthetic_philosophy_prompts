I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a course on rational choice and game theory, and I've volunteered to open next week's seminar by defending a position I've come to hold, so I want to know how exposed it is before I do. The position is that the backward induction solution of the centipede game — the game in which two players alternate, the pot grows with every pass, either player may stop at any node, and the number of nodes is known in advance — gives the uniquely rationally permissible play; that the outcome at which the first player stops immediately is not a reductio of the solution concept but an uncomfortable feature of practical rationality itself; and that the fact that people almost never stop at the first node is a fact about us rather than about the theory, so the correct response is not to revise the theory but to become the sort of agent who defects at once. I extend this to finitely repeated games with a known final round, where the induction seems to me equally compelling. What I want from you is a map of the position's exposure: the things that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, then the ones that are merely awkward, together with an honest verdict on whether the view has been quietly qualified into something that risks nothing at all.

Four points came up when I floated this and I didn't have good answers to any of them. First, the epistemic premise: the argument tells me to stop at the first node on the strength of what would happen at later nodes that the argument itself says will never be reached, and it appears to require that each player is rational and knows the other to be rational, all the way down. A classmate claimed that in games with this structure that condition is not merely unavailable in practice but inconsistent — that a rational player could never be in a position to apply backward induction at all — and I cannot tell whether that is a serious objection or a confusion between reasoning about counterfactual nodes and reasoning about nodes that occur. Second, the standard repairs: adding a small probability that the game continues past its announced end, or that the other player is not a maximiser but a cooperator type, seems to license cooperation in equilibrium; I want to know whether that genuinely refutes my position or merely relocates the puzzle one level up, and whether the finitely repeated case survives it. Third, a version I have been constructing myself: suppose the entire game is played by one person at successive times — the same agent with the same preferences, deciding again and again whether to take the smaller pot now or wait — so that the opponent whose rationality I must assume is simply my future self, there is no separate party about whom I could be mistaken, and no way of committing my later self by intention alone. I expected the paradox to evaporate there; instead the backward induction conclusion strikes me as harder to accept, and I would like to know what that tells me about where the difficulty really lies. Fourth, a friend says the reasoning is just the surprise-examination paradox in game-theoretic dress — the student who proves the exam cannot happen and is then surprised by it — and that the centipede is the same fallacy with payoffs attached; I think that is too quick, but I cannot say exactly why.

Finally, tell me what a committed defender of my position would concede counts against it, as distinct from what only critics say, and be willing to conclude that the position is insulated if that is the honest verdict rather than handing me objections I can wave away; I am not after a reading list, just the substance. The thing that would move me most is a demonstration that, in order to survive the cases above, the position has to give up something I would not want to give up elsewhere — and if nothing of the sort exists, I would rather hear that said plainly than have the view flattered.
