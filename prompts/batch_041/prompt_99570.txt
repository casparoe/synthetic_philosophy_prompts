For a long-form recorded conversation I'm taping next month, my guest is a philosopher of mind who has spent the better part of a decade defending an unfashionable view about mind uploading: that a person can survive the gradual, neuron-by-neuron replacement of her brain with silicon units that reproduce each neuron's input–output profile exactly, provided the causal stream is never interrupted, whereas a read-and-rebuild copy of the same brain — however perfect the resulting state and organization — kills the original and leaves behind only a duplicate who sincerely believes she is the original. The hinge of her view is a notion she calls continuity of process, which she insists is neither the same as nor guaranteed by continuity of pattern, and which she says is what the read-and-rebuild case lacks. To make the point vivid she uses a device she calls the Loom: the subject sits in a chair, awake and conversant, while the swap proceeds one cell at a time, and reports no seam, no flicker, no felt moment of transition. She is a fluent and largely unflappable interviewee, and my sense is that the standard objections — that she is really just appealing to physical continuity of the same matter, that she is smuggling in a soul, that her position is unfalsifiable — have all been deflected so many times that she can now field them in her sleep.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten questions for the interview, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that the sequence keeps tightening rather than allowing her to restate the view. At least one question should press on the halfway point: what, on her account, is the truth about the subject when exactly half the neurons are silicon — one person, two, or neither — given that she wants to say there is no fact of the matter about when the swap "completes," and I want the follow-up to make her choose between embracing that indeterminacy and abandoning the sharp survival claim she needs. At least one should press on the copy problem directly: why the Loom preserves the person while the cold read-and-rebuild does not, when by stipulation both end with a brain in the same state and the same organization, and what work the phrase "the stream" is doing that isn't just a covert appeal to the persistence of the original atoms — with a follow-up for the likely reply that the copy is a different token of the same type, asking whether that difference is anything more than spatiotemporal location. At least one should press on fission: if the Loom is paused at sixty percent and the remaining biological neurons are then used to seed a second replacement running in parallel elsewhere, what does her view say about how many people there are, and does the answer change if the two replacements are later reconnected. At least one should press on the epistemology — how the subject could ever know, from the inside, that she survived rather than being displaced by a duplicate who merely thinks she did, and whether her view can give any first-person traction at all. And I want one that turns her comfortable welcome of vagueness against her: if the boundary between survival and death is vague all the way down, why isn't the cold copy simply somewhere further along the same continuum, and would she accept that a person can be "gradually" killed merely by slowing the swap down far enough or making the replacement units fractionally slower than the tissue they displace.

Keep the register polished and journalistic, the kind of question a well-prepared interviewer asks to force a committed answer rather than to be liked; no soft openers about how she came to the topic or what first drew her to philosophy, and nothing that depends on her citing literature or naming who said what. The questions should be answerable by someone reasoning from the position and the Loom scenario as described here, not from a reading list.
