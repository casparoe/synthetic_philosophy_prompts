I've been having the same argument with a mate for about three years and I need it settled by someone who isn't me. I know the old Ranke picture — the historian just reports how it really was, no theory, no point of view — is supposed to be naive, and that Carr basically said the historian's selection is unavoidable, and that Hayden White went further and said (as I remember it, so don't hold me to the details) that the plot shape of a history gets picked before you've even sorted the evidence. Fine, I'll take all that. But I still have strong gut verdicts about which histories are honest and which are crooked, and I don't think I'm just tracking whether I happen to agree with the author, because some of the ones I think are honest are ones I disagree with politically. So either there's a real distinction here that survives Carr and White, or my gut is just tribal and I should shut up. Here's what I actually think:

- A doorstop military history of the Somme that's all corps-level decisions and never a word about the men who drowned in the mud: honest, just narrow.
- A school textbook that's scrupulous about dates and names, but our side's massacres have vanished and theirs get dwelt on: crooked. Note that this and the Somme book both leave things out, so leaving things out can't be the thing.
- A book on the empire written openly from the point of view of the colonised, and openly of the view that the whole business was a crime: honest. The moralising doesn't bother me at all, and I expected it to.
- A firm's centenary history: every sentence true, zero moralising, and I think it's basically an advertisement. The question it set itself — how did this great firm come to be? — picked the material for it.
- One of those denial tracts: real documents, footnotes, tables, arguing that some atrocity never happened. Not just crooked, not history. What I think is going on is that it can't lose — any document against it just becomes evidence of the cover-up.
- A cliometric book that counts what's in the parish registers and the tax rolls and nothing else, exhaustively: honest, but it isn't the history of the period, it's the history of what got written down.
- A history of the Middle East that starts in 1917. Nothing in it is false. The start date is doing the work.
- An oral history of a strike, collected fifty years later from the strikers' grandchildren: fine, and I don't think it's the same kind of move as the denial tract, even though in both cases you can't go and check.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? The nearest I've got is something like: a history goes crooked when its shaping choices — scope, start date, whose voices, what counts as a cause — are answerable to a verdict rather than to a question, and it stops being history altogether when nothing could count against the verdict. Does that survive my list? Which verdict does it make me give up? And what does it say about the two I keep flip-flopping on: a historian writing about his own family's eviction, as a partisan but honestly; and a historian who says "I'll write the strongest possible case for one side, you write the case for the other, and we'll let them fight it out"?

Please commit. Pick the principle, state it precisely, defend it against those last two cases, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it costs me — I'd rather have a definite answer I can push back on than a tour of the options with the decision left to me. Also give me its verdict on one case I haven't listed, your choice, so I can see the thing working. No reading lists please, I'm not going to chase them down. British English.
