I'm a high school student, and I'm in a philosophy club that has spent the last term on thought experiments — trolley cases, Gettier-style cases, Mary's room, the Chinese room, that sort of thing. Our faculty advisor keeps insisting that these aren't just rhetorical tricks: a thought experiment, he says, is an experiment, only run on the imagination instead of in a lab. His favourite way of putting it is that a thought experiment is to philosophy what a wind-tunnel test is to aeronautical engineering. You can't put a real airliner in a hangar and blow air past it, and you can't run a real trolley over a real person to see what you'd say; so you build a model, put it in a controlled stream, and watch what happens. The scenario is the model; the intuition it provokes is the reading off the instruments; changing one feature of the case — the footbridge instead of the switch, the structure of the causal chain in a Gettier case — is varying a parameter; and getting the same intuition from other people is like running the test again. He draws a moral from this: intuitions about cases are data, a paper that reports them is doing empirical work, and when people disagree it is like two wind tunnels returning different numbers — evidence that one of the tunnels is badly built, not that there is no fact of the matter. He has used this to shut down my objections twice now, and I want to know whether it actually holds up or whether I have been losing arguments to a picture.

So please act as a philosopher of science who works on thought experiments and on experimental methodology, take your time, and begin by brainstorming the structural features that wind-tunnel testing and thought-experimenting genuinely share before you judge anything. Then give me the audit. I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the model, the airflow, the instrumentation, the calibration standard, the observer watching the smoke, and the engineer's purpose in running the test — and then a serious account of where the mapping breaks. The thing I keep circling is that a wind tunnel has something the imagination doesn't: a real aircraft the model is supposed to resemble, and shared physics that make the scaling legitimate, so the model can be checked against an actual flight test. Is there any analogue of that in a thought experiment, or does the analogy quietly smuggle in an independent target that isn't there? The same question arises for calibration: an instrument that cannot be checked against anything already known is not obviously an instrument. And is there anything on the thought-experiment side corresponding to the dimensionless quantities engineers use to decide whether a scaled model still behaves like the full-size thing — any reason to think the toy case scales up to the messy real one? One more difference I notice: the engineer who builds the tunnel usually wants the plane to fly, whereas the philosopher who writes the case usually wants a particular intuition out of it, so the apparatus is chosen with the reading already in view. A friend of mine says the wind tunnel is simply the wrong picture and that a thought experiment is closer to a Rorschach blot, telling you about the reader rather than about the world; I would like to know whether that is an improvement or just the opposite mistake. Separate the disanalogies that matter for my advisor's conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque, and tell me whether his conclusion survives them.

Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey: say plainly whether the wind-tunnel picture can carry the weight he puts on it, and if it cannot, say which conclusion about the evidential status of thought experiments you would actually defend, and what analogy — if any — you would put in its place. I don't need a reading list or references; I want the argument itself, and I would much rather have a definite position I can push back against than a balanced menu of options.
