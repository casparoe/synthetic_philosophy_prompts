I'm a graduate student in a seminar that pairs the history of decision theory with contemporary machine learning ethics, and my final assignment is a piece of counterfactual intellectual history: choose a thinker who died before the relevant problem existed, and reconstruct as rigorously as the evidence allows what he would have said about it. I've chosen Frank Ramsey — the Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who died young, long before anyone trained a model on data about people. I don't want a summary of his published positions; I want a disciplined extrapolation from his actual commitments: his subjectivism about probability, the idea that a person's degrees of belief are revealed by the bets she would accept, his treatment of conditionals in terms of what one would believe upon adding the antecedent, and his proposal for handling theoretical terms by existentially quantifying over them. The question is what those commitments would have led him to say about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness.

The concrete case I'm working with is a lender's default model. It is well calibrated within each of two groups, but its false-positive and false-negative rates differ across the groups, and the two groups have different base rates; the group category is a legacy of a historical injustice, and the outcome label — whether someone defaulted within two years — is itself partly produced by this same institution's earlier lending decisions, so deploying the model also shifts the labels that will later be used to audit it. My assigned reading claims that calibration within groups, equal false-positive rates, and equal false-negative rates cannot all hold at once when the base rates differ, and I'm told this is a theorem about fairness rather than a fact about our particular data. That framing is what I want Ramsey to weigh in on. Would he accept that the impossibility is a substantive discovery about fairness, or would he say it is an artifact of treating several incompatible measurement conventions as if they were competing descriptions of one thing? Does his account of partial belief give any reason to privilege calibration over the error-rate criteria, or does it make the choice of criterion a decision problem with no purely epistemic answer? And if his treatment of conditionals is applied to the counterfactual "this model would be fair if the two groups had the same base rates," does that counterfactual come out meaningful, vacuous, or simply false — and does the answer change once you notice that the base rates are themselves downstream of the institution's past behavior and could be altered upstream?

I'd like the response as exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing summary, since I'm pasting them into a comparison sheet alongside other students' reconstructions. The fifth point should carry the overall verdict: which criterion he would have chosen, or whether he would have refused the choice, and why. Please argue for the reconstruction rather than hedging; if you think the exercise is ill-posed, say so in the fifth point and explain what would make it well-posed.
