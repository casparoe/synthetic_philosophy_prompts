Ok so I run a small philosophy-of-logic reading group and I'm putting together a bank of essay prompts, and I need one on the surprise exam paradox. You know the one — teacher announces there'll be an exam next week, on one of the five days, and it'll be a surpise, the students do the backwards induction (can't be Friday, so can't be Thursday, so... ) and conclude there can be no exam at all, and then there's an exam on Wednesday and it really is a surprise.

I want the essay to actually pick a side. Is the students' reasoning valid, in which case the announcement is just inconsistent or self-undermining? Or is there a specific step where they go wrong — maybe the last one, where they lean on an announcement they've just concluded can't be true? And I don't want a recap of the paradox, I want them down in the weeds arguing about the crux. So here's the wrinkle I want them to build the essay around: keep the standard five-day setup but change one thing. The teacher says she picked the day by a fair random draw over the five days, and the students know this. Now does "it'll be a surprise" even make sense once the odds are common knowledge? Does the backwards induction still go through, or does it break, or does it break the announcement even harder? And what does their answer tell you about whether the original paradox is really about knowledge, or about whether "surprise" should be cashed out as impossibility-of-knowing versus just low credence, or about the students treating an announcement as a premise when they've already ruled it out. They're welcome to say the twist doesn't actually change anything and explain why, but they have to earn that.

Don't let them pull in reading lists or cite who said what — no citations, just the argument. It's for people who've seen the paradox before, so skip the hand-holding.

Lay it out in these sections, with rough lengths: "The setup" (about 100 words, just enough to fix the rules and the twist), "The students' case, at its strongest" (250), "Where it breaks" (300), "What the random-draw wrinkle actually shows" (250), and "Verdict" (200). The verdict has to be a real verdict, not a both-sides shrug.
