In a few weeks I'm presenting a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on long-run trends in violence, and I want to walk into the question period ready rather than improvising. My thesis is that the question 'is the world becoming less violent?' has no fact of the matter as it is normally posed — and the reason is not that the historical data are thin (they are) but that 'less violent' conceals a forced choice among at least three measures that do not reduce to one another: an absolute count of violent events; a per-capita rate; and what I have been calling a harm-weighted incidence, which counts each event not as one unit but in proportion to the expected severity of the harm it imposes on its victims, and normalizes by person-years at risk rather than by persons. My argument is that these three can diverge across the same century without any of them being simply wrong, and that the choice among them is a normative stipulation the decline-of-violence literature generally makes in silence; so the honest verdict is neither 'yes' nor 'no' but a refusal of the question until the measure is specified — and even then the specification is a decision, not a discovery. I should say up front that I don't need references or a reading list worked into the sheet; I'll supply those myself.

Here is who will be in the room, because it shapes everything. The bulk of the audience is quantitative social scientists and economic historians who build and use conflict datasets; they care about coding rules, missingness, and whether my skepticism is a cover for refusing to do the empirical work, and they speak in terms of operationalization and the denominator problem. Sitting among them is an anthropologist who has done fieldwork on violence in small-scale societies and regards the whole civilizing-process narrative as a projection of modern state-centric categories; she will press on whether 'violence' is a cultural universal at all and will not be moved by homicide statistics. There is also a criminologist who thinks violence is whatever the criminal law happens to count, and a legal philosopher who will want to know what my view implies for responsibility and for the legitimacy of state coercion. Draft me the Q&A prep sheet for this room: the eight or so objections I will actually get, phrased in their vocabulary and roughly in the order they will arrive, ordered by how much damage each does to the paper; for each, the two-minute reply I should give and an honest note on where that reply is weakest; what I should concede at the outset to buy credibility; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
