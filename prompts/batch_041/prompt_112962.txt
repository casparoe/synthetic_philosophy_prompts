I run a small reading group on the philosophy of mathematics, and we are closing a term on actual infinity — Hilbert's hotel, the bijections behind it, and the finitist objection that none of it is a real accommodation — with a final session next week. I would like to end the evening by reading a poem aloud, and I want it to be a strict sestina: six six-line stanzas whose end-words permute in the standard way, followed by a three-line envoi that gathers all six, thirty-nine lines in all, rhyme optional. The form is the point rather than the ornament, because a fixed set of words recurring in a fixed permutation is about as close as verse gets to a bijection between a set and a proper part of itself. Keep one set of six end-words fixed for the whole poem; I would start from room, full, guest, night, more, count, though you may trade one of these for a word that does more work, provided the permutation then carries something the poem needs.

The poem should be spoken by the night clerk, and it should pass through roughly this arc: a single guest arrives at a hotel whose sign says there is no vacancy, and is nevertheless given a room; then infinitely many guests arrive, and the clerk accommodates them by an arrangement a reader who knows the material will recognize as different in kind from the first, without either arrangement being spelled out in arithmetic; then one guest — the one who has held the lowest-numbered room longest — refuses to move, on the ground that the clerk has not made a room but only made a new name for one; then the clerk's answer, which should be a real answer and not a shrug. In one stanza a guest should ask whether there is room for everyone on a line, and the clerk should have to refuse, and the poem must make plain that this refusal is a different fact from the earlier hospitality rather than a contradiction of it. Let the end-words do the load-bearing work: the envoi is where the finitist and the clerk have to share a line, and it should leave them sharing it rather than reconciled. I want the finitist in our group, who reads every claim about the infinite as a claim about what can be done in finite time, to find the objection stated fairly, and I want the Platonists not to wince: the two rearrangements must not be run together, and nothing in the poem should suggest that the hotel demonstrates infinity to be incoherent. Give it a title.
