I'm giving the keynote at the annual convention of a lucid-dreaming society, and I'd like you to write it for me. Most of the audience are practitioners who have spent years learning to become aware inside their dreams and to act there deliberately; a small contingent are philosophers of mind who will be listening for whether I quietly assume the very thing that is in dispute. The difficulty is that the practice presupposes there is something it is like to be dreaming, and that one can decide within it, whereas the two strongest philosophical positions deny precisely that — one by arguing, conceptually, that a report of having dreamt is a retrospective verdict rather than a record of anything undergone, the other by arguing, empirically, that the dream is assembled at the moment of waking and is therefore no experience at all. Write a keynote of roughly eight minutes that honours what these practitioners do without granting them the premise they need, and that leaves the question open rather than settling it. Carry one concrete case through the whole speech: the trained dreamer who signals from inside a dream — evidence the enthusiast will read as presence, the sceptic as nothing more than a report — and let that single case do the work of showing why the same datum can support both readings. The tone should be affectionate towards the audience and candid about the gap.
