Last term I took a seminar on fine-tuning and the multiverse, and I have been arguing with a colleague in it ever since. We can each recite the standard moves — the measure problem, anthropic selection, the charge that the multiverse is a prior wearing a lab coat — and neither of us can say what would actually settle anything, so I would like to force the question into a number. Here is the proposition I want your credence in, and I have tried to state it so that it has a truth value rather than dissolving into an observation about what 'confirmation' means: that there exists a defensible confirmation relation C such that an agent permanently confined to one branch of a causal ensemble — able to observe only the local values of parameters that the ensemble hypothesis says vary across branches, and unable to observe the ensemble itself or its measure — can nonetheless raise its credence in the ensemble hypothesis over a serious single-branch rival on the strength of those local observations; where 'defensible' means at minimum that C does not merely redescribe the agent's prior, and that C would not confer the same boost on any hypothesis that postulates a large enough ensemble with a tunable measure. Please give me your number, and then decompose it: the probability that the measure problem admits a principled solution at all, rather than being an artifact of trying to put a probability distribution on an infinite ensemble; the probability that anthropic selection is a legitimate form of inference rather than a rationalization we reach for once we have run out of dynamics; the probability that the fine-tuning data have an ensemble-free explanation — a symmetry, a dynamical attractor, a parameter theory we have not found — that a better physics would prefer; the probability that the step 'I am a typical observer among the ensemble' is well-defined rather than incoherent; and any further terms you think belong. Then tell me how the terms combine, which one carries most of the weight, and which single argument or result would move your number most.

To keep this from floating free of cases, I want you to use two toy models as anchors. In the first, theory T says the world is one of N causally disconnected regions, in a fraction f of which the parameter alpha lies in a narrow range R; T contains one free parameter, the measure, one setting of which yields alpha in R and another of which yields alpha outside R, and we observe alpha in R. Its rival T-prime says there is exactly one region and that alpha lies in R as a brute fact, also with a single free parameter that could have been set the other way; again we observe alpha in R. In the second toy model, the ensemble theory U derives its measure from its own dynamics, leaving no free parameter to tune, and predicts a range for alpha, while its single-branch rival V must posit the value directly by means of a free parameter. My colleague says the observation confirms the ensemble hypothesis in both cases and to the same extent. I want to say it does so in the second case and not the first, but I cannot articulate the difference without smuggling in something like the claim that T is merely T-prime with extra unobservable machinery — and that claim, if it is right, seems to prove too much, since U is also an ensemble theory. So what I want back is: your credence in the proposition as stated; your verdicts on the two toy models and the principle that generates them; and, if you will indulge me, an estimate of where my colleague (who puts the proposition at about 0.8) and I (about 0.35) actually disagree — that is, which of the decomposed terms we most likely differ on, and whether the disagreement is substantive or mostly about what we are willing to count as confirmation in the first place.
