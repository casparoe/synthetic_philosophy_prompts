At the end of next month I am to give the closing speech at the annual dinner of the university debating union, which I have coached for eleven years and am now leaving; the speech therefore does double duty as a farewell and as a toast to my successor, and it comes after the meal, when nobody has much appetite for an argument. The room is mixed in the usual way: current members and alumni who know every ritual and in-joke of the society, three or four colleagues from the philosophy department — one of whom teaches Indian philosophy and will hear immediately if I misuse a term — and a large contingent of parents, partners and spouses who have come for the dinner and have no philosophical background whatever. What I have wanted to say for years, and have never managed to say in a speech, is this: that our house style — attack every premise, take nobody's word for anything, check it yourself — is parasitic on a kind of knowledge that the style itself cannot account for, since almost nothing any of us in that room knows has been checked by any of us; nearly all of it we know because somebody told us. I have been reading about Nyāya epistemology, and I want to build the speech on its four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison (upamāna) and verbal testimony (śabda) — as the four movements of the talk, one for each thing the union has taught me. I am aware that the fourfold list is contested: I understand the Buddhist logicians allowed only two means of knowledge, and the Mīmāṃsakas rather more, including postulation and the apprehension of absence. A clause acknowledging that is welcome if it can be got in without turning the speech into a survey.

There are two traps I want you to help me avoid rather than paper over. The first is upamāna. It is tempting to treat it as a learned word for learning by analogy — I learned how to take a point of information by watching the older debaters, the way one learns what a gavaya is by being told that it resembles a cow — but I suspect that stretches the Nyāya notion, and my colleague will say so; if the stretch is indefensible, the speech should own it rather than hide it. The second, and for me the important one, is testimony. The move I want the speech to refuse is the one that reduces being told something to an inference about the teller — he is reliable, reliable people speak truly, therefore p — because I understand the Nyāya tradition to insist that the knowledge you get from understanding a sentence is not a conclusion drawn from a separate inference about the speaker's trustworthiness, even where trustworthiness has to be in the background. That refusal is exactly what our debating culture denies, and it is what the speech is for. I am told the same fault line runs through contemporary epistemology, where the question is whether trusting what you are told is a special case of inductive inference; if that is right, lean on the parallel in a sentence, not a paragraph.

As for the speech itself: something around a thousand words, written to be heard after a large dinner, warm rather than solemn, with at least one joke at my own expense that the alumni will catch and the parents will still find funny. It should sound like a speech and not an essay — no first, second, third scaffolding, no moment where I stop to gloss a Sanskrit word as though I were reading a footnote, and no quotations of any kind: I once watched a best man attribute a line to Marcus Aurelius that Marcus Aurelius never wrote, and I am not going to fact-check a speech. It should end by raising a glass to my successor, and it should be something I could still defend if my colleague from the philosophy department cornered me afterwards with the transcript.
