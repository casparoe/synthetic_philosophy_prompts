Ok so I've been reading about the "tragedy of the commons" and it kind of bugs me. Take Wikipedia: anyone can read it, nobody has to pay a dime, and they still come around begging for donations every year while refusing to run ads or put up a paywall. Public radio is the same deal. The usual econ story is that public goods get underprovided because everyone free rides, so the "rational" fix is either privatize it or have the government fund it with tax money. But these things keep running on voluntary donations and the honor system, and as far as I can tell a tiny sliver of people do almost all the actual work. So what theory of human motivation are these practices presupposing? Do they just assume people aren't the free-riders the textbooks say we are, or is something else going on — I heard once that the real problem isnt freeloading but that nobody wants to be a sucker when they're not sure everyone else will chip in too, so you hold back not because you want a free ride but because you dont want to pay while everyone else sits it out (I think somebody called that the "assurance" problem). And then the other thing: if a good is genuinely non-rival and non-excludable, does the choice to keep it that way presuppose that its value actually depends on staying open — like, would Wikipedia with ads still be the same thing, or does the practice commit them to open access as part of what the good is? Last thing, I keep seeing people online say the historical English commons weren't really a tragedy at all, that they were managed with rules and the real disaster was the enclosure — if that's right, are these tech and econ people even using the word "commons" correctly, or are they really describing open access with no rules? So: reconstruct the theory these practices seem to assume, tell me which parts they actually need versus which are just vibes, and what would have to change if you swapped in a straight rational-actor model.
