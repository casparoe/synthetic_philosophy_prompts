I run a graduate reading group on the philosophy of punishment, and next term I want to put Kafka's In the Penal Colony at the centre of a session on the contrast between denunciatory and rehabilitative justifications of imprisonment, using Norway's Halden prison and American mass incarceration as the two live alternatives. What I want from you is a reading of the story through that debate, not a summary of either side.

The story gives us an apparatus whose entire purpose is to inscribe on the condemned man's body, over twelve hours, the law he has broken, without ever telling him what the sentence is; an officer who insists that the point of the procedure is the condemned man's enlightenment, which arrives only as he dies; and a colony in which the machine's justification has hardened into faith in its dead designer. Set that against two things. First, the communicative and moral-education theories of punishment, on which hard treatment is supposed to address the offender as a rational agent, convey society's censure in a form he can understand, and aim at his repentance and reconciliation rather than at suffering as such. Second, the Norwegian model: normalisation, the import of health, education and welfare services from outside the prison, unarmed staff who work by relationships rather than by barriers, unbarred cell windows and shared kitchens, and the official view that the loss of liberty is the punishment and that everything else should fit the prisoner to return as a neighbour. Against both, the American arrangement, in which neither censure nor reform organises the institution and risk categories are managed rather than persons addressed.

Does Kafka's machine expose something the humane theorists would rather not see — that the message can be delivered perfectly while the offender understands nothing, so that communication collapses into inscription? Or is it the reductio of expressivism, condemnation as an end in itself, with the officer as its priest and the old crowds as the audience it can no longer find? Which scenes should I put in front of the group: the officer's blueprints, the traveller's refusal to speak publicly and his departure before he can be called to account, the machine's failure when it turns on the officer, the grave of the old commandant and his followers' belief that he will return? Where does the Halden comparison flatter Norway, and where does it flatter Kafka? And what does the story show that argument alone does not — in particular, whether any institution that undertakes to change the offender, however gently, inherits a diluted version of the officer's certainty about what the offender ought to become?

Please say plainly where the reading strains or where you are unsure of the story's details, and write in British English.
