I'm TAing the back half of a seminar on Buddhist philosophy of mind and next week we hit the chariot bit in the Questions of King Milinda — the king asks the monk his name, the monk says the name is only a designation, there's nothing permanent behind it, the king tries to catch him out over it, and the monk turns it around by asking how the king got there that morning and then taking the chariot apart in front of him: is it the pole, the axle, the wheels, the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, the goad? No to each. Is it all of them together? No. Is there something over and above them? No. So the monk says he can't find any chariot anywhere, and the king, instead of being cornered, says well of course it's on account of all those parts together that we use the word chariot, and the monk tells him he's grasped it exactly. And that's where my students and I come apart, because if the argument only gets you that far — the word is a workable label for an arrangement of parts — it seems to prove too much, since it takes the chariot apart just as neatly as the person, and nobody, least of all the monk, thinks the chariot is an illusion. Yet the tradition plainly wants more than a lesson about labels: it wants the grasping at a self to be the thing that hurts. And the answers it gives to the obvious follow-up — then who acts, who is reborn, who reaps what somebody else sowed — are all about continuity without identity, a lamp burning through the night, milk turning to curds and then to ghee, so that nothing is the same and nothing is simply other. Theres also a position I keep drifting back to, where the person is supposed to be neither the same as the aggregates nor something other than them, which other schools wrote off as a back door to the soul, and honestly it's the least crazy of the options to me.

So: write me a pantoum I can hand out at the end of that session. Five quatrains, standard rules — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza closes the loop so that the poem's final line is its opening line. Two speakers, the king and the monk, alternating by line (monk on the odd lines, king on the even ones in the first stanza), no speaker labels anywhere, and don't name them either. What I actually want out of the form is this: because the lines migrate, each one ends up in the other voice's mouth within a stanza or two, so the same words keep changing hands and by the end nothing belongs to anyone — the reader should feel both that the lines have no owner and that they are unmistakably the same lines coming back. Put the turn in the fourth stanza, where the king notices the dismantling fits the chariot he's sitting in at least as well as it fits him, and then don't resolve it: don't let the monk win, don't let the king win, and when the opening line comes back at the end, don't let it settle which of the two was being taken apart all along. It should be accurate enough that someone who's read the dialogue recognises every move — the name, the parts, the king's who-then-does-the-deeds barrage, the lamp and the milk — and legible enough that someone who hasn't isn't lost, so keep the jargon out (no khandha this and skandha that, half the room is there for the poetry credit) and make it sound like two people actually arguing rather than doctrine in costume. If you can recycle the milk or the lamp into the king's mouth, please do, since that's exactly the spot where the tradition's own answer turns back into the objection.
