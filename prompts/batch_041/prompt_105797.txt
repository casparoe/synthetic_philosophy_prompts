I'm a second-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I've been locked in a running argument with a friend from our department's sociology of science group that neither of us seems able to settle. She takes what I'll call the inevitability line: the discoveries that matter are, in her phrase, products of their moment rather than of their authors, and the long parade of "multiples" — the independent formulation of the calculus by Newton and Leibniz, the near-simultaneous accounts of evolution by Darwin and Wallace, the several independent discoveries of oxygen — is the rule rather than the exception. She leans on Robert Merton's contrast between "multiples" and "singletons," and she likes to point out that credit tends to flow not to whoever was first but to whoever was most famous or best positioned; she calls this Stigler's law and quotes its slogan, "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer." From all this she draws the conclusion that priority is a purely sociological artifact and that asking who "really" discovered something is close to a category mistake. I'm the skeptic in the pair. I suspect the inevitability claim trades on a coarse-grained way of individuating discoveries, and that once you ask what Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differentials actually *were* — different foundations, different notations, and neither one identical to the limit-based calculus that eventually won — the assertion that these were "the same discovery made twice" begins to look like an artifact of hindsight rather than a fact about the world.

Please write a dialogue, roughly 1,000–1,200 words, in which the two of us argue this out face to face. Make my friend's case as strong as it can honestly be — I don't want a strawman, I want to see whether my objection survives her best replies — and let the exchange have real back-and-forth, so that each of us is answering the other's actual move rather than talking past it. I'd like the dialogue to work its way toward the several distinct things "does it matter who made them?" could mean: whether the world would have been different had the second discoverer never been born; whether the *content* of the knowledge depends on who formulated it; whether it is just or unjust that credit is allocated as it is; and whether any of this should change how we teach or fund science. Neither of us should get an easy win, and if the dispute turns out to dissolve partly into a disagreement about how to individuate a discovery in the first place, I'd rather see that made explicit than smoothed over.

One request about the ending. Don't hand me a tidy verdict. After the dialogue, add a short note — a few sentences, clearly set apart from the dialogue itself — that (a) flags where the dialogue takes liberties with the historical record or leans on a contested reading, and (b) distinguishes the parts of this question on which historians and philosophers of science broadly agree from the parts that are genuinely contested, without letting any contested position stand as if it were the consensus. Please write in American English.
