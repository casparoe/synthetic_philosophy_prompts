My friend and I burned most of last night arguing about Rashomon — the four accounts of that encounter in the woods, none of them confirmed, then the woodcutter's own version at the end, and the baby. He's convinced it's a Buddhist film, all about how everyone has their own truth, and I'm pretty sure thats close to the opposite of what Nāgārjuna is up to. My instinct is that the film sits nearer the tetralemma — is, isn't, both, neither, each one knocked down — and that what it dramatizes isn't pluralism but four people each trying to keep hold of some fixed essence of themselves, which is precisely what emptiness denies. But I don't want to just conscript the film as an illustration of a doctrine. Read it through Madhyamaka for me — the four corners, svabhava, the two truths — and tell me where it resists the reading as much as where it supports it. The ending bothers me most: the woodcutter takes the baby, and it plays like consolation, and I don't think Nāgārjuna offers consolation. What does the film see here that the arguments don't, and where does it flinch? Prose only, please — no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just write it out the way you'd write a letter.
