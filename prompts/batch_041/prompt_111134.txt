I've been listening to a lecture series on political philosophy by someone with a reputation as a good popularizer, and I'd like to check how much of his account of Rawls I should trust. He explains the veil of ignorance roughly as follows: Rawls asks you to choose the rules for your society without knowing which position you'll occupy in it, which is essentially the golden rule made rigorous — you put yourself in everyone else's shoes, and since you might be anyone, you'll be fair to everyone. Because you can't know whether you'll end up worst off, and because people are cautious about a gamble that large, you'll choose the arrangement that makes the worst position as good as possible; that is the difference principle, on which inequalities are permitted only insofar as they benefit the least advantaged, and otherwise everything should be equal. The practical upshot is a redistributive welfare state, since any other arrangement is a risk you wouldn't accept from behind the veil. He then adds that Harsanyi showed that parties who reasoned by expected value rather than by worst-case caution would choose average utility instead, and since expected value is the standard of rational choice, the difference principle isn't really forced on us — it's a preference for safety dressed up as a theory of justice.

I'd like an audit of this. Which parts would someone who knows Rawls's work accept, which are simplifications they'd wince at but tolerate, and which are actually wrong? I'm especially unsure about the claim that the veil is a version of the golden rule, about why the parties are supposed to choose the worst-case option, and about whether the Harsanyi point really shows what he says it shows. And does his closing conclusion follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction?
