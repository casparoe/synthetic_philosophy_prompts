A friend in my political-economy reading group has become attached to an analogy, and I want to know how much of it survives scrutiny. Her case runs roughly as follows. When the stocking-frame weavers of the Midlands and the croppers of the West Riding broke frames and shearing machines in the 1810s, they were not frightened primitives raging against technology; they were skilled workers defending a trade against employers who used new machinery to cut wages, dilute skill and bypass customary standards. Their violence, on this reading, was less technophobia than an improvised form of collective bargaining, and it was rational. Marx, she adds, saw this: the workers' first instinct to turn on the instruments of labour was right in substance and wrong only in that it took them time to learn to attack the capitalist employment of machinery rather than machinery as such. And in the Grundrisse passage usually called the Fragment on Machines, he already glimpses a future in which social knowledge crystallised in fixed capital becomes the chief productive force — knowledge that capital then tries to hold as private property. From all this she concludes that the present wave of automation, large language models above all, should be met with the modern equivalent of frame-breaking: organised refusal, strikes and legal obstruction rather than adaptation, on pain of repeating the weavers' defeat.

I doubt the analogy will bear that weight, and what I want from you is an audit. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the weavers, the frames and shearing machines, the employers and putting-out merchants, the wage-cutting, the night raids, the state's suppression of the movement — and what plays the role of the model, the firms deploying it, and the workers being displaced. Then say where the mapping breaks. Which features of the Luddite case does the analogy quietly import into the present — a single trade, a shared craft identity, workers geographically concentrated and therefore organisable, machines that were costly capital goods owned by the mill, a legal order that could be contested — that the present simply lacks? Separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. Take seriously the possibility that the analogy, correctly drawn, licenses organised bargaining over wages and conditions rather than resistance to the technology as such. Address her two Marxian moves directly: does Marx's treatment of machine-breaking — in the section of Capital on the strife between workman and machine, and in the Manifesto's account of the early workers' revolts, which it treats as local and inchoate — support her or cut against her? Does the Fragment on Machines help, or does it point somewhere else altogether? And if the Luddite analogy cannot carry her conclusion, say what analogy would do better, and why. No reading list; argue from the concepts themselves, and mark clearly where you are uncertain rather than inventing detail to fill the gap.
