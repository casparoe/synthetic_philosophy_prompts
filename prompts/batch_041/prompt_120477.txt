I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, and the chapter of my dissertation that deals with the semantic paradoxes has just come back from a journal with a referee report whose central complaint is that I talk past the dialetheist: my paraconsistent reader says my very framing of the disagreement begs the question, and every time I try to restate it neutrally I produce something so weak that both sides nod at it while meaning different things. I have to answer the referee this month, and rather than dig in I want to see what the two camps can actually agree on. So please draft the joint statement that a committed paracomplete theorist — someone who holds that the liar sentence is neither true nor false and responds by weakening classical logic, in the spirit of a Kripke-style fixed-point construction — and a committed paraconsistent theorist — someone who holds that the liar sentence is both true and false and responds by adopting a paraconsistent logic — could both sign in good faith after a genuinely cooperative adversarial collaboration. I don't need a bibliography or citations; I know the literature, and I want the claims stated in your own words rather than attributed to anyone.

Please structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both accept about the paradoxes themselves and the validity of the classical derivation of contradiction, about what a theory of truth is for and why the truth predicate is indispensable as an expressive device, about the status of the equivalence between a sentence and the claim that it is true, about what has to be given up and what both refuse to give up, and about what an adequate theory must and need not deliver. Keep these formulations from being either vacuous or slanted — anything a classical theorist would also sign, or anything that quietly stacks the deck, doesn't belong. Second, a disagreements table: one row per precise question on which they still differ, with each side's position in a wording that side would accept as its own. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I would expect these to include at least: glut versus gap; whether negation in the object language can be exclusive, and whether a predicate like "determinately true" or "just true" can be expressed without generating a revenge paradox; whether the truth predicate must come with a conditional that supports modus ponens and full detachment, and what Curry sentences built from the truth predicate do to that; how classical reasoning is to be recovered in ordinary, non-paradoxical contexts; and whether truth is a property at all or primarily a device of generalization. Treat that list as a hypothesis to be revised, not a specification — add cruxes I've missed and drop any that turn out not to be load-bearing. Fourth, for each crux, a case, argument, or formal result that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance; I'd like three specific items treated if they are genuinely live: the strengthened liar (the sentence that says of itself that it is not determinately true), a Curry sentence constructed from the truth predicate, and a Yablo-style infinite sequence of sentences each of which says that all the later ones are untrue — for each, what each theory says and whether that verdict is stable. Finally, a short closing paragraph on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and why, plus, for each side, what it would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate.

Throughout, be explicit about which claims are settled results that anyone working on the paradoxes accepts and which are genuinely contested, and do not let anything contested into the agreed-claims section dressed up as consensus; where the document's own neutrality is itself contestable, flag that in a bracketed aside rather than smoothing it over.
