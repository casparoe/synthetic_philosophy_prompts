I'm about two-thirds through a novel and I've only just noticed that the whole moral spine of it is a philosophical claim I'd actually defend in an argument, so I'd like to find out where it breaks before I write the ending.

Here's the claim. People don't have claims on you just by being people. They have claims on you because of what's already between you. Duty is downstream of relationship: what you owe the woman whose mother you've been bathing for six months isn't some general obligation plus a bonus on top — it *is* the obligation, and there isn't another one underneath it. And the thing is lopsided: the one who cares is the one who's bound. The cared-for doesn't owe anything back merely for having been cared for.

The book is about a daughter and about the aide the family hires to look after her mother, an immigrant whose own kids are back home being raised by her sister. So alot of the caring in this book is bought, and it's also displacing another caring relation somewhere else on the map. I'm stuck on the scene where the daughter realizes the aide hasn't called her kids in three weeks, and I can't tell whether my own view is supposed to find that tragic or whether it's the view working as intended — the aide's whole attention on my character's mother is exactly what the wage is buying. It feels like that should be a problem, but I can't get the problem to take a shape. A friend says this is just the ethics of care with a coat on, which I don't think is quite right, but maybe.

So what I want to know is what would count against it. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a person who holds this view would themselves admit count against them — not just the stuff critics say. In particular:

- whether there are cases where the view gives an answer that's plainly wrong, not just unfashionable, and what those cases look like
- whether "the relation comes first" survives relations you didn't choose, can't leave and never wanted — and relations that are bad for the people in them, where the caring is the thing keeping someone small
- the bought-care thing above: is paid care still care on this view, and does the view have any way to condemn an arrangement it seems to describe approvingly?
- the cared-for's side. If being cared for generates no obligations, what does the view say to someone who doesn't want to be cared for, or who wants to be cared for in a way that eats the carer alive?
- strangers. Not distant ones, the neighbour. Does the view have anything to say, or does it just shrug and call that a feature?
- and the big one: has a view like this actually risked anything? Can it be wrong? Or does every counterexample get absorbed as — well, that's a bad relation, and bad relations aren't what I meant. If it turns out it can't really be argued against, say so, I'd rather know.

And if it has a name and a standard set of problems, tell me what it's called, but don't just hand me the standard list — I want the ones that bite on the bought-care case specifically.

No reading list, please. I'm nowhere near a library and I don't want names, I want the shape of the thing. And if any of these is really an objection to the story rather than to the theory — i.e. it's really "your case is contrived" — flag it, because that's useful to me too.
