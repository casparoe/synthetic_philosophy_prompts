I keep running into a pattern in my own practical reasoning and cannot tell whether it has an established name. Consider a case in which I can affect someone's interests — people who will live long after I am dead, say — while they can do nothing to me: no communication, no reputation, no repeat interaction, no retaliation, ever. Prudence says I should ignore them. Yet the reasoning I would use if we could actually deal with each other keeps reasserting itself, and not because anything comes back to me: my decision is produced by the same reasoning that, presumably, produces theirs, so my taking the generous option is evidence that someone who reasons as I do will take it in their place. The upshot is that it looks rational to act as if we had struck a bargain — with no bargain, no enforcement, and no way even to confirm that the other party exists.

I know this is adjacent to superrationality and to the notion of trading with agents one cannot reach, but it seems to be neither. Superrationality, as I understand it, concerns a reasoner facing a problem alongside near-copies of itself with the same payoffs; here the other party can be arbitrarily unlike me, and what they do to me is nothing. The trading idea seems to presuppose favors exchanged, whereas in my case nothing is returned; the whole point is that my choice and theirs are the same kind of choice.

Is there a name for this, and where exactly are its edges? Which others count as "reasoning like me" — what fixes the reference class, and what keeps it from swelling to include everyone or shrinking to include no one? Is the principle genuinely evidential, or a causal principle in disguise? And how does it differ from universalizing a maxim, or from adopting a rule because of what would happen if everyone followed it? If nothing established fits, I would rather have a carefully bounded coinage than a bare label.
