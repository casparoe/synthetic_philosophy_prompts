I'm in the middle of an ethics-of-medicine module and I've ended up in a long-running argument with a mate about the Seattle dialysis committee — the one from 1962, where the machine was so scarce that they screened patients medically first and then handed the leftovers to a lay panel to sort out. I think there were seven of them: a lawyer, a minister, a banker, a housewife, a union man, someone from the state, and a surgeon who didn't vote. Don't hold me to the exact roll call, its the shape of the thing I care about. What I find interesting is that they weren't maximising anything you could write down — they talked about age and dependents and occupation and whether the person seemed like they'd contribute, which is the bit everyone finds grubby now, and Life magazine apparently dubbed them the life-or-death committee. I want you to use that as the test case for the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. The objection gets traced to Rawls and to Nozick, but please don't be scholarly about who said what — I don't need names, I need the argument actually got straight.

Here's what's really bothering me, and it's why I don't want the standard lecture on this. "Separateness" seems to be doing at least three different jobs in the literature, and I think people slide between them. One is a flat ban on aggregation: you can't add up gains and losses across different people at all. Another is about sacrifice: it's wrong to use some people as the means by which others do better, even if the total goes up. A third is about justification: whatever you do to someone has to be justifiable to *that* person, not merely outweighed in a sum. Which of these does the committee case actually support, and does it support only one of them? Then two things I can't get past. First, why is it fine to trade my twenties against my sixties, but not fine to trade A's life against B's, if the whole answer is just that persons are separate? Second, and this is the one that trips me up: run the committee's problem from behind a veil of ignorance, where you don't know which candidate you'll be, and the aggregating answer comes out looking like the *fair* one — equal chance of being anyone, so pick the rule with the best expected outcome for whoever you turn out to be — and that is precisely the move the objection calls a conflation of persons. So is separateness an ex post claim about outcomes, or an ex ante claim about how decisions are justified to people?

So what I'd like: map the case onto the theory properly — who's deciding, who's affected, what the options actually were, what the "benefit" being summed could even mean when the patients aren't comparable on any single scale — and then run it in the other direction. Which features of the episode does the objection explain well, and which does it misdescribe or quietly need to be patched to handle? If the objection is taken as a flat ban on aggregation, does it condemn every rationing rule there is, including the ones we'd all accept, and if so what does that do to it? Does the case tell us whether separateness is best read as a metaphysical claim about persons or a normative one about justification? And say plainly where the theory goes quiet rather than making it speak. If it helps to widen it out, you can say what changes when you swap identifiable patients for the statistical ones in a pandemic triage guideline, but don't let that take over. No citations needed and no need to hedge about which paper says what. For what it's worth my mate reckons the committee was just utilitarianism with worse PR, and I don't think that's right, but I can't yet say why.
