In mechanistic anomaly detection, a monitor inspects the internal computation a model performs on a new input and flags the input when the output — even if acceptable — is produced by a mechanism other than the trusted reference mechanism we have already vetted, the aim being to catch cases where a model gets the right answer for the wrong reason and would diverge outside the regime we trust. The method presupposes an answer to a question I find badly underspecified: what makes two computations instances of the same mechanism? A purely functional criterion — same input-output behavior over the trusted distribution — appears to make the problem vacuous, since a flagged computation may be functionally equivalent to the reference on everything we can actually check; a purely structural criterion — same circuits, same parameterization, same numerical implementation — appears to make it trivial, since reimplementing one algorithm across two layers, permuting hidden units, or changing precision would each register as a new mechanism; and an interventional criterion, on which two systems share a mechanism just in case they respond alike to the same interventions on their parts, looks like a middle path but inherits the problem of saying which interventions and which parts. Please explain what notion of mechanism identity would let the detector do genuine work: what must be fixed for "same mechanism" to be well defined, why the obvious candidates fail, and whether such a criterion can be stated without covert appeal to the very distribution shifts the detector is meant to anticipate. Ground the discussion in a case where a model computes a given function by one algorithm in the regime we trust and by a different one elsewhere, while agreeing with the reference throughout the trusted regime.
