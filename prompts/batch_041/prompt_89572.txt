Every year I teach the conceivability argument for dualism in an introductory philosophy of mind course, and every year the zombie thought experiment falls flat: my students can recite that a zombie is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an ordinary person, that it behaves exactly as that person behaves and yet has no experience at all, and that if such a creature is conceivable then it is possible, in which case physicalism is false. What they cannot do is feel the force of the first premise, and I have been considering screening Invasion of the Body Snatchers (Don Siegel, 1956) to supply the missing intuition. The premise looks like a literal staging of the zombie: alien pods grow duplicates of the sleeping townspeople of Santa Mira, the copies carry the originals' memories and personalities and are physically indistinguishable from them, and they are described as having no human emotion left at all. I would like you to read the film through the conceivability argument and tell me whether it actually helps me teach it.

The question I keep circling is whether the film dramatizes the zombie or cheats. A genuine zombie is behaviorally indistinguishable from us, so the film ought to be unable to show us that the duplicates are empty; if it gives the audience any tell — a flatness, a deadness, a failure to respond — then it has smuggled in evidence that no real zombie could supply, and the horror is quietly doing the work of an argument. And the film does seem to supply tells: Miles discovers, in a kiss that goes unanswered, that Becky has already been replaced. Worse, the duplicates are not blank at all; they have purposes, they organize, they recruit, they want the rest of us converted. That is not a zombie — a zombie has no inner life, not a different and lesser one — so I want to know whether the film's power depends on our sliding between the absence of experience and a diminished experience, and whether that same slide is what makes the thought experiment so easy to state and so hard to credit. There is also the version of the zombie that talks: the duplicate who says, sincerely and at length, that it is conscious, that it is still itself, that nothing has changed. The film's duplicates insist they are the people they replaced. If I take that insistence at face value, am I imagining a zombie, or only a liar?

So please give me a reading that takes the film and the argument equally seriously: where the film genuinely illuminates the modal claim, that a being physically just like us yet empty is conceivable and hence possible; where it quietly substitutes the epistemic problem of how I could ever tell whether my neighbor is one of them for the modal one, and what is lost in the substitution; and where the film finally resists the reading and the honest critic has to concede that it is a Cold War parable about conformity and the fear of being replaced, with consciousness only along for the ride. Close by telling me what the film adds that the argument alone cannot, and what I should warn my students about before I press play.
