I'm putting together a one-page handout for the second meeting of a graduate seminar on perception, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than the usual list of -isms, because the list always makes students pick a label first and then rationalize. Start the tree from the premise the argument from illusion trades on — that a veridical perception of, say, a tomato and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of one share whatever it is that makes them experiences of the same kind — and let each branch node be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. I want the questions to come apart on at least these axes, though you may add others: whether the good case is fundamentally a relation to something, and if so whether that something is the mind-independent object or a mental particular; whether the bad case is a positive mental occurrence with content or with an object, or instead a mere absence or failure; and whether whatever is shared, if anything, exhausts the phenomenal character or is only a common factor such as content or a proximal cause. Please keep the weaker 'common factor' claim and the stronger 'common kind' claim as distinct nodes rather than one — a colleague in the seminar keeps collapsing them, and I want the tree to make the difference visible.

The leaves should be named where a name exists and 'unnamed' where none does, and each leaf needs one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I need these leaves to appear: sense-datum theory, adverbialism, content-based intentionalism, naive-realist disjunctivism, and at least one version of disjunctivism that locates the disjunction in the subject's epistemic position rather than in the nature of the experience — and, set beside it, the version that treats the hallucinatory case as a positive state of a different kind rather than a mere absence, so the tree has to show what separates those two. Two cases must be usable at some node: the standard indistinguishable-hallucination case, and a mixed or hybrid case in which one and the same experience has both veridical and hallucinatory elements — I suspect the hybrid case is where several of these positions get uncomfortable, and I would rather the tree reveal that than hide it. Finally, mark any combination of answers the tree generates that no one defends, and for each say whether you take it to be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, with a note on what further question would be needed to pull them apart.
