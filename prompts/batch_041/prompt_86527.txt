I want to defend a position in transitional justice and then be told, in earnest, what should move me off it. Please treat the following as mine and attack it properly rather than surveying the literature around it.

My claim is that a conditional amnesty — individual, granted only in exchange for complete disclosure, and issued by a body that is not the outgoing regime granting itself immunity — is not merely a regrettable concession to impunity but the morally correct response to a certain kind of transition. The kind I have in mind is one in which the settlement is fragile, the courts and police that would have to investigate and prosecute are still staffed by the people who did the wrong, and the realistic alternative to a bargain is a resumption of violence. Under those conditions, I hold, it is right to trade the punishment of a class of wrongdoers for a full accounting, public acknowledgement, and the possibility of a shared political life afterwards. I also deny that the state has, in such circumstances, a duty to punish; at most it has a defeasible presumption in favour of punishing, which the costs of pursuing it can defeat.

What would count against this? Not the usual inventory of objections, but the specific considerations that ought to shift me, ranked from those that would be close to fatal to those that would be merely awkward. I would expect the list to include at least these: the possibility that the necessity premise is simply false where prosecutions later proved feasible after all — the Argentine sequence, amnesty first and prosecutions revived much later, is the case that worries me most; the instances where a bargain delivered impunity without the truth, and what they show about whether the conditions can ever be reliably met; the standing question, namely who is authorised to waive punishment on behalf of victims, and whether a state that declines to punish wrongs them a second time whatever they themselves say; the expressive or dignitary function of punishment, which the trade may be quietly declaring dispensable; the contrast with Rwanda's community courts and with hybrid tribunals, which suggest the choice between amnesty and trial is not exhaustive; and the possibility that a norm against impunity for atrocity crimes now forms part of international law, which would constrain the moral question rather than settle it.

I would also like an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it is insulated. If I have drawn the conditions — fragile transition, complete disclosure, not self-granted — so tightly that no real case satisfies them, then the view risks nothing, and I want to know whether that is a defect in the view or simply what a properly hedged moral principle looks like. Distinguish what defenders of the position would themselves concede as counting against them from what only its critics assert, and tell me which of the defeaters I should find most troubling.
