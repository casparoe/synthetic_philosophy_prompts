I never studied philosophy but I've been chewing on Leibniz's best of all possible worlds thing for about two years, mostly on walks, and I've ended up thinking the interesting questions aren't the ones everyone argues about (why is there evil if this is the best one). The stuff I can't get straight is more like: can worlds even be lined up and compared in the first place, or is that a picture we're just assuming? Does there have to be a best one at all, or only one that's good enough — because it seems like if you could always add a bit more good stuff there'd be no top, and yet everyone talks like there's a ranking with a number one. And what does possible even mean here, whether that's the same sense of possible I use when I say it's possible I'll move to Spain. Also I keep wondering whether best is even one thing, or whether it's a bunch of goods that can't be traded off against each other, in which case the whole comparison falls apart.

Say three of us with no training have a year of evenings and want to actually work on this rather than just read about it. Which of these open questions should we put first, and why those? I want the reasoning: which questions the others depend on, which are actually doable by amateurs versus which need machinery we don't have, and which sound deep but wouldn't change anything downstream even if we settled them. And tell me what would make you reorder the list.
