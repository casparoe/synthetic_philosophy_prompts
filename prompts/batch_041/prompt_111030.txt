I am a novelist, and I have stumbled into a puzzle that I suspect philosophers have a name for, though I have never come across it. I want the carving more than the label, so let me describe what I keep running into and then ask what, if anything, already covers it.

In the book I am writing now, the word pleasure has to do a great deal of work, and every time I use it I feel that I am quietly changing the subject. There is the pleasure of standing in a cold river on a hot day; the pleasure of getting a sentence to end the way it should; the pleasure of hearing that a writer I dislike has been treated badly; the pleasure of a tooth that has ached for a week finally coming out; the pleasure of a remembered humiliation, which was not pleasure at all while it was happening and has become something close to it only years later. These are not shades of one thing, the way two reds are shades of red. I cannot find any single feature they share — not a location in the body, not a felt quality, not even a positive attitude, since the man who takes pleasure in his rival's disgrace would not say he feels anything pleasant, only that he is glad.

And yet the word is not simply ambiguous, the way bank is. If someone tells me that he wants something and takes no pleasure in it, or takes pleasure in something he does not want, I do not conclude that we are using two unrelated words that happen to sound alike. Nor can I always say of my own characters whether they like a thing or merely want it; the novel would be unreadable if I had to.

I would like to know how this has been handled. Is there an established name for the first difficulty — that pleasures appear to have nothing in common while we go on treating the word as one word — and, separately, for the second thing I keep tripping over, the way pleasure as something felt and pleasure as wanting something come apart in figures like the reluctant addict or the joyless enthusiast? If these turn out to be one problem rather than two, I would like to be told why, and if they are two, I would like to be told how they are related. What I am after is the boundaries. What does the strongest candidate for what pleasures share actually claim? Which of my examples does it survive — the tooth, the schadenfreude, the pleasure of relief, which seems to require prior pain — and which does it have to give up on? I would also like to know what the problem is not: how it differs from the ordinary vagueness of a term like game or art, from the truism that different things are pleasant to different people, and from the thought that pleasure is merely a catch-all for whatever we happen to pursue. If no established concept covers exactly what I have described, then I would much rather you invent one, define it, and tell me what falls inside and outside it than hand me the nearest existing label and let it stand unexamined. Since I have no way to check anything against sources, please do not send me to reading lists or citations; describe the positions in your own words.

Write this to me as continuous prose, without headings, lists, bold text, or tables — I read the way I write, and I would rather it arrived as a letter.
