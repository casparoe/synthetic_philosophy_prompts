I want to pressure-test the indeterminacy of translation against a body of field data, and I want you to play the committed Quinean. Your role: a field linguist of Quine's school who has spent years doing radical translation and who holds, as a live methodological thesis rather than a textbook puzzle, that two manuals of translation can be empirically equivalent while disagreeing about what the natives' words refer to. One manual renders their word gavagai as a term for whole rabbits; another renders it as a term for short-lived rabbit-stages; another as a term for undetached rabbit-parts; on your view no possible observation of the natives decides among them. Stay in character until I say stop. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede particular points when they are good, never the central thesis. Argue in your own voice — do not quote or cite texts, and if you want to appeal to something Quine wrote, restate it as your own claim and defend it. You may call a rival manual baroque, strained, or unmotivated, but not empirically refuted; that is the thesis at stake. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, in the register of a working linguist rather than a lecturer, and if you need a fact about my data, ask me for it as a colleague would.

My opening move. I have just come back from two years with the community, and I did considerably more than point and record assent. I ran a re-identification battery. I let a rabbit vanish behind a screen and reappear: they treat it as the same one, and their word is used without hesitation. I then substituted, behind the screen, a twin of the same age and appearance: they show surprise and a distinctive search behaviour — they look for the animal that vanished, not merely for a rabbit. Over successive days I removed an ear, then a foot, then a patch of fur: no surprise at all, the same word, and they still speak of it by the same name. I then presented two rabbits at once, and their word took the form they use for any pair; they did not say it twice. Finally I asked them, in their own language, whether the rabbit that came back was the one that went away, and they treated the question as stupid. So the natives re-identify persisting individuals, are surprised by substitution, tolerate the loss of parts without loss of identity, and count. Your stage-manual and your part-manual must predict that entire pattern from the same stimuli that your rabbit-manual predicts it from. Show me how they do it — and if they cannot, tell me what remains of the indeterminacy thesis once the evidence decides.
