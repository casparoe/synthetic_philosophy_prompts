I am preparing a seminar paper on Bentham's treatment of sexual morality and need to reconstruct the decision rule that my instructor has been using. She is a historian of philosophy who insists that she is applying Bentham's principle of utility and nothing else: no appeal to naturalness, religious taboo, or tradition, and every sentient being's pleasure and pain counted equally. She also insists that the principle of utility leaves no room for duties to oneself properly so called, so a self-regarding act is not forbidden merely because it is self-regarding. Over the term she has given the following verdicts on a series of cases.

1. Solitary masturbation in private: permissible; the pleasure is a good and the supposed unnaturalness is not a reason.
2. Consensual sexual acts between two adult men in private: permissible; the disgust of others counts only if they are forced to witness the acts or are otherwise made to suffer.
3. Prostitution between informed adults, with no force or fraud: permissible in principle, though she favors regulation to prevent coercion and disease.
4. A sexual act performed in a public park where passersby, including children, cannot avoid seeing it: forbidden; the distress of unwilling observers outweighs the participants' pleasure.
5. Consensual sadomasochistic acts in private, including acts that leave lasting marks, where both parties enjoy them: permissible; she weighs the voluntarily accepted pain against the pleasure and finds it outweighed.
6. A sexual act with an animal: forbidden; the animal can suffer and cannot consent.
7. A private sexual act between adults that is likely to be discovered by a spouse who would be deeply distressed: forbidden; the spouse's distress is a real pain that must be counted.
8. A private sexual act between consenting adults that gives intense pleasure and causes no direct harm, but that she believes might, if widely practiced, make people somewhat less attentive to their long-term relationships: she permitted it, saying the indirect effect was too speculative to outweigh a certain and intense pleasure.

The new case is this. A man owns a lifelike robot designed for sexual use. The robot is programmed to simulate a sexual encounter in which it protests, begs him to stop, and appears to be in distress. The man knows that the robot is not sentient, that its protests are prewritten scripts, and that no real being is harmed. He derives intense sexual pleasure from the simulation. He uses it only in private. The only actual effects are his own pleasure; the distress of people who learn about it and find it disgusting; and a contested risk that his repeated use will make him less sensitive to real suffering or more likely to seek out a real non-consensual encounter. Assume that no real person is directly involved or harmed, and that the robot has no legal or moral status.

Predict the verdict she would give. In doing so, identify the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each predicts about the robot case, give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and why, and name a further case that would best tell those rules apart. I am less interested in whether the robot case is actually permissible than in the structure of her commitments and the points at which they underdetermine the answer.
