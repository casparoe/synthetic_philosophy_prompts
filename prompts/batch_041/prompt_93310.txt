Compare two attempts to make cooperation rational in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma without repetition, enforcement, or a binding commitment device: Hofstadter's superrationality and the game-theoretic notion of program equilibrium associated with Tennenholtz. I want the comparison to do real conceptual work rather than list features, and I want you to end with a considered verdict on which one, if either, actually solves the problem it claims to solve.

Begin by setting out, precisely, the mechanism each relies on. Superrationality asks a player to reason from the premise that an opponent who is relevantly "the same" will arrive at the same decision, and then to choose the action that is best conditional on both players choosing it. Program equilibrium instead changes the object of choice: players submit programs that are run against one another and that may read each other's source code, and equilibrium is Nash equilibrium of this larger program game; a program that cooperates when it recognizes the opponent as itself, and defects otherwise, can make mutual cooperation an equilibrium even though defection dominates in the underlying one-shot game.

With both mechanisms on the table, address each of the following, giving an argument rather than an assertion:

1. Does program equilibrium formalize superrationality, generalize it, or quietly replace it with a different idea? Identify what, if anything, the informal notion of "the same reasoning" is doing that the formal notion of "the same source code" does not.

2. The commitment worry: the program is chosen and submitted before play, so does program equilibrium earn its result honestly within a one-shot game, or does it smuggle in the very precommitment it was meant to do without? Say whether the same charge can be leveled at superrationality.

3. Symmetry and sameness: construct a variant in which the two players are rational but not identical — say, the same reasoning procedure applied to different payoffs, or one player able to inspect the other's code but not conversely. State what superrationality can and cannot say here, and what program equilibrium delivers, and use the contrast to say which framework is more robust.

4. It is sometimes claimed that program equilibrium can sustain any feasible, individually rational payoff, much as the folk theorem does for repeated games. If so, explain what this tells us about whether the one-shot dilemma has really been dissolved or merely been re-described as a repeated-style problem in disguise, and whether superrationality is vulnerable to an analogous objection.

Close by saying which of the two you regard as the better account of rational cooperation, what each one illuminates that the other obscures, and whether the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is a problem about what to choose or a problem about what to be.
