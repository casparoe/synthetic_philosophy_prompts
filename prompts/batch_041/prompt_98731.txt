Write a sestina on the structure of metaphysical grounding, spoken in the voice of a derivative fact that is trying to establish whether anything underlies it. Let the six end-words be drawn from the vocabulary of the theory -- ground, depend, fundamental, chain, brute, and rest, say -- and let the fixed rotation do the work: each stanza should return the same six notions in a new arrangement, the way a grounded fact hands the question of its own grounding down the chain, so that the reader feels the dependence structure in the form itself rather than only being told about it. The envoi, where all six words converge in three lines, should be the moment the poem either arrives at the ungrounded -- the fact that grounds others and is not itself grounded -- or concedes that the descent has no bottom.

I want the poem to keep both live options in grounding theory in view without settling them: the well-founded picture, on which every grounded fact terminates in the fundamental, and the rival picture, on which grounding proceeds without a terminus, whether by an infinite descent of ever-more-derivative facts or by a circle in which facts ground one another. A reader who knows the literature should be able to tell, from the poem alone, which option each stanza is leaning toward, and should find nothing in it that misstates the formal features the theory assigns to grounding -- that it is irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive, and that the fundamental is defined as the ungrounded rather than as the merely very general or the very small. Let the speaker's difficulty be genuine: a derivative fact cannot, from its own position, tell whether the chain beneath it ends or merely continues, and the poem should not pretend otherwise.

Do not name any philosophers or cite any texts; let the verse carry the view. Blank verse or a looser line is fine if the sestina's repetitions become forced, but if you abandon the form, keep some structural analogue of it -- a returning set of terms whose order shifts from stanza to stanza -- so that the poem still enacts the dependence it describes.
