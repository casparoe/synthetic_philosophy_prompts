I have recently joined my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and at almost the same moment my own research group shifted its work from rodents to cuttlefish. That coincidence has made an abstract question feel personally urgent, and I would like your help with it.

In three weeks the committee votes on whether to extend our humane-handling, housing, and humane-endpoint requirements beyond vertebrates. The proposal on the table would cover cephalopods (an octopus lab in the marine sciences building, my cuttlefish) and decapod crustaceans (a crayfish lab, a teaching collection of crabs), while leaving insects, nematodes, and everything else exactly where it is now. I am the member asked to circulate the framing opinion before the vote.

Please draft that opinion for me: roughly one page, a clear recommendation, a concrete course of action with a time frame, and the reasoning that supports it, in a form the committee can act on rather than merely discuss. Our rules let us adopt interim measures pending later review, if that helps you make the recommendation operational.

Two colleagues have framed the choice in ways I think are too quick, and I would like the memo to engage them rather than talk past them. One argues that since there is no sharp line between a crayfish and a thermostat, any line we draw will be arbitrary; consistency therefore either keeps the vertebrate line exactly where it is or admits the thermostat as a patient too. The other argues the opposite: because the evidence about suffering in invertebrates is genuinely uncertain, extending protections would impose real costs on research and teaching for the sake of a guess, and we should wait for better science before acting.

Be honest about the strongest consideration on whichever side you end up rejecting; I do not want a strawman. Finally, be explicit about the limits of your own position: tell me which piece of evidence or which argument, if it turned out to be sound, would lead you to a different verdict, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
