I'm the collections registrar at a mid-sized museum of technology and everyday life, and questions about identity over time land on my desk roughly twice a month, usually with a curator or a donor standing over me and an exhibition deadline attached. A curator wants to label a heavily restored locomotive as the original 1897 engine; a donor offers a timber-framed house that was dismantled in 1974, moved forty miles, and re-erected with new foundations and about a third new timber; our media art holdings keep needing software pieces re-implemented on hardware the artist never saw. The only policy we have is a rule of thumb someone wrote in the 1980s — if more than half the original material survives, we call it the same object — and it has started to embarrass us. It makes identity depend on a percentage nobody can measure, it says nothing about the case where the surviving original parts have been distributed among three other museums, and it offers no guidance on a piece whose matter is completely intact but whose behavior has changed.

Please write the procedure that replaces it: something I can run myself, explain to the director in ten minutes, and hand to an assistant registrar who has never read any philosophy. Ground it in the metaphysical debate between endurantism (a thing is wholly present at each moment and persists by having different properties at different times) and perdurantism (a thing is a four-dimensional whole extended through time with a distinct temporal part at each moment, so persisting is a matter of having later parts). If my one-line gloss of either view is wrong, correct it before building on it, and use the puzzle about how a single thing can have incompatible properties at different times — temporary intrinsics — as the test that tells the two views apart, since that is exactly where I lose my footing. Give me the tests in the order I should run them, what counts as an answer good enough to stop on, where the procedure returns a flat no, where it has to hand the decision to a person, and which of my questions turn out to have no determinate answer at all and are really just two bookkeeping conventions in a fight, which I suspect is true of at least one of them. Include the counting question, which nobody here has thought through: how many objects do we own when the locomotive is a locomotive plus a spare boiler, and under the four-dimensional picture am I accessioning the whole thing or only its current stage — and if only the stage, is that like owning an undivided interest in something rather than owning the thing?

Then run the procedure on three cases: the locomotive, the transplanted house, and the re-implemented software piece. At least one of them should come out in a way my curators and donors will hate, and I want to see that coming. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire. I don't need a reading list or citations; I need something usable, with the judgment calls marked as judgment calls instead of dressed up as steps. Begin with a single sentence stating the core decision rule — what I should do when material continuity and behavioral continuity come apart — and then give me the procedure itself, with no preamble and no restating of my question. American English, please.
