Ok so I'm a novelist — third book, about a logician who's spent his life on a theory of truth and is now, let's say, not well — and I've spent about six months reading around theories of truth for background and I still don't know how to judge any of them. Every book tells me its account handles the liar, and then the next book explains why it doesn't, because of the sentence you can build out of that account's own fix. And then someone brings up the one that doesn't even use the word not, which I gather is fatal to the people who've given up classical logic. I'm going round in circles. What I actually want, before I read another page, is the spec: what does any theory of truth have to deliver?

So give me the desiderata. The cases it has to get right, with the case behind each; the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse; the results it can't contradict. And here are things I keep seeing demanded and can't tell whether they're genuine requirements or one camp's house style — put them on the list if they belong, throw them out if they don't:

- true has to be usable both to say of a claim that it is true and to move from the claim to the claim that it's true, and the two moves must never come apart;
- it has to cope with the generalising use — someone says that everything a third party said was true, and one of the things that third party said was itself about truth, or about what the first one said;
- no ban on self-reference, and no rule stopping the truth predicate from applying to sentences that contain it;
- no hierarchy of languages that ordinary speakers are forbidden to speak;
- it keeps classical logic, or at least doesn't buy consistency by quietly making the logic strange;
- and don't let it run together the case where a sentence is neither true nor false with the case where it's both. I've seen both moves made and never with a reason attached.

Which pairs on that list can't be had together is the part I most want. If two of them are jointly unsatisfiable, that's the whole answer, and tell me which single requirement thins out the field the most. One line each on the main families if you feel like it, but don't do the scoring for me, I'll do that myself.

And here's my constraint, which comes from the writing rather than from philosophy: the theory has to say something sensible about true as it turns up in fiction, and about the passages in a novel where it isn't clear whether it's the narrator or the character who's committed to the sentence — free indirect style, that sort of thing. Does a theory of truth owe me an account of sentences that nobody ever asserted, or of a sentence whose content depends on which story it's in? If that's a separate job, tell me where the boundary is. What I'm after is a theory adequate for someone who needs truth both inside and outside a fiction and can't have two different predicates for the two.

British English please — my copy-editor is a terror about it.

(and sorry in advance, I've probably got some of the terminology wrong, I'm a novelist, not a philospher)
