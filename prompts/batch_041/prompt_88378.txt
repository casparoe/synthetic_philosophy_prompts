I run the selection process for a small fellowship at a research institute: roughly 200 applications a year, six readers, and at the end we have to hand the director one ranked list. He signs off and that's that. The trouble is that we relitigate the scoring rule every single year. Someone always says the average-score method is unfair to a candidate who's polarizing, someone else says the two-round runoff is rigged by whoever sets the order of the pairwise votes, someone says it's undemocratic that I as chair break ties, and last year a reader who'd been reading about voting systems announced that our whole method was "mathematically impossible" and then couldn't explain what he meant. I've read enough to know Arrow's theorem says you can't have everything, but I've never seen anyone turn it into something I can actually run in a meeting, and I dont want to look like I'm dressing up my own preference as a proof in front of six people with different agendas. Constraints: we meet for 90 minutes, I'd like to run the audit before that meeting, I'm not a mathematician, and half the readers barely skim the files, so anything requiring them to supply full rankings of 200 candidates is dead on arrival.

Could you write me a decision procedure — questions to ask in order about any proposed aggregation rule, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision back to us because it's a value choice and not a fact? I'd like it keyed to the specific failure modes, not just the headline theorem: whether the rule only uses rankings or can use scores, what happens when a candidate is added or withdrawn after people have already scored, whether the winner between two candidates can flip because a third one entered, whether the order we vote on pairs changes the outcome, and whether some individual's ranking can override everyone else's no matter what they say. Then run it on three rules we've actually used or proposed: (a) average score with the chair breaking ties, (b) a two-round runoff where the top two by average go head to head, (c) a proposal from our most senior reader that each reader's ranking be weighted by years of service. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me the rule I personally favor is the problem, and finish with an honest paragraph on what this procedure can't do — where I'll still have to just make a call and tell everyone I made it.
