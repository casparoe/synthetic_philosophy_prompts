I'm not a philosopher, I just run a thing: a monthly argument night in the back room of a pub near me, 15 to 20 people, mixed bag — a couple of teachers, a nurse, a guy who does something with databases, my sister, and a retired bloke who will argue about anything. We get about 90 minutes, there's a whiteboard, and a box of junk I use as props. For ages I've been chewing on the Pascal's mugging thing, and every time I try to explain it out loud it comes out flat. I say "and then the payoff gets enormous and the chance gets tiny and you still feel like you should pay, and that's weird," and everyone nods and we move on, and nothing has actually happened to them. I want them to get got. Not to hear about being mugged — to be sitting there with the chips in their hand and their stomach doing something.

So I want a runnable game for that room, 90 minutes including the debrief, and here's what I've half-worked-out, take it or scrap it. Everyone starts with 10 poker chips and I make the chips worth something real — a jar of scratchcards or a bottle of decent whisky as the prize, chips buy draw entries at the end, so handing one over actually costs. Round one the mugger (me, or someone I've briefed) offers: one chip, I flip a coin, heads you get 50 chips back. Most people take it. Then I ratchet — one chip for a 1-in-100 shot at a thousand chips, then 1-in-a-million for a hundred billion chips — and here I need help, because I can't pay out a hundred billion chips, so I need a payoff scheme that stays semi-credible in the room without me having to honour anything. The last round is the bit I care about: four envelopes to four different people, each with a different story — a thing from the future, a thing from another planet, a simulation operator, a god, pick whatever works — each offering something absurdly large for a chip or two, each clearly made up on the spot but none of them rule-out-able. I want the group to have to choose, first alone then together, which ones to pay, and to notice they can't treat them all the same even though the logic looks identical. I also keep going back and forth on a fifth envelope that threatens something instead of promising something — tell me wether that lands harder or just wrecks it.

What I actually need is the part that only works if it happens in the room and not on the whiteboard: the moment somebody does the maths, sees the expected value sitting there enormous and positive, and still won't hand over the chip, and then has to say out loud why not. So build the rounds so that moment arrives by itself. Then the debrief — where in the game the arithmetic was doing all the work, where the probabilities stopped meaning anything to people, what changes if the payoff is infinite rather than just very big, and whether the fact that nobody can say what the probability of "a being from the future" even is means the whole thing falls over before it starts. Be honest too about what the game can't do. I suspect the one thing that matters — that the mugger might really be able to do it — is exactly the thing I can't fake, since everyone knows I'm the one holding the envelopes and I can't deliver, so tell me what to do about that. And tell me what'll go wrong the first time: my money's on it collapsing into a joke around round three, and on two or three people checking out entirely because they've decided it's a trick. Timeline, what I say and what I don't, what goes on the board, and what to do when someone inevitably points out it's just Pascal's wager with a coat on.
