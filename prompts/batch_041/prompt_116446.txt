I am a novelist—three novels, most recently a historical one that required four years in archives—and for the past two years I have sat as the external literary member on the adjudication committee of the Marden Foundation Prize for Fiction. Our committee has an appeal to decide before Thursday's meeting, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion. The facts are these. This year's prize went to The Lantern Index, a 340-page novel credited to Aurelia 4, a large language model, with a human curator, Ines Marchetti, listed as director of the project. Marchetti says she composed roughly twelve thousand prompts over eighteen months, selected and ordered the model's outputs, cut about forty percent of the generated text, and wrote the connective passages; the publisher's submission describes her as the work's architect. The runner-up, a human novelist, has appealed under the prize's eligibility rule, which says the award honors a natural person whose intentions determine the work's design and meaning. She argues that the rule excludes The Lantern Index because Aurelia 4 is not a person and because any apparent authorial intention is either Marchetti's editorial intention or a statistical artifact of the model's training. The publisher replies that the novel displays a sustained narratorial voice, a coherent set of moral preoccupations, and a pattern of imagery that no prompt-by-prompt explanation captures, and that this is exactly what we mean by an author. The publisher's expert has filed a short affidavit claiming that the novel's narratorial persona is a real pattern because it licenses counterfactual predictions about how the text would respond to editorial changes, while the appellant's expert says those predictions are design-stance predictions about a statistical system, not evidence of an intentional author. Our chair has proposed that we resolve the appeal by running what she calls a three-stance audit: the physical stance (what causal process produced the tokens), the design stance (what the system was built or optimized to do), and the intentional stance (what beliefs, intentions, or authorial agency it is reasonable to attribute). She wants the committee to say whether author is a real pattern in the work, detectable from one or more of these stances, or a useful fiction we project for legal and commercial convenience.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition—affirm the award, rescind it, or create a separate category and leave this award standing—and give a time frame for whatever procedural step follows. It should set out criteria the committee can actually apply to future cases, not just this one, and it should be honest about the strongest point on the losing side: if we deny that Aurelia 4 can be an author, we need to say what we are denying and why the same reasoning does not also strip authorship from heavily scaffolded human writers, collaborative studios, or works assembled from unconscious borrowing; if we affirm it, we need to say what follows for the Foundation's stated purpose of honoring singular human vision and for the responsibility that authorship normally carries. I would like the opinion to engage the three-stance audit seriously, including the possibility that the intentional stance is indispensable for reading but not ontologically decisive, and to end with a bottom line the committee can vote on. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need a document that reasons from the case and from whatever distinctions you can make precise.
