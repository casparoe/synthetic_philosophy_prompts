Next month I'm giving a talk at a small workshop on invertebrate welfare, and I want to walk in ready for the question period. The room is about 30 people and it's a weird mix: a bunch of bee and octopus neuroethologists who dont trust any claim they can't hook up to an electrode, a cluster of EA-flavored animal people who already buy insect sentience and keep asking me for numbers, two phil of mind folks (one is a hard functionalist who thinks anything that plays the right causal role counts no matter what it's made of, the other is a Kantian who gets visibly twitchy whenever anyone says 'degrees of moral status' as if that were a coherent category), and two or three old-guard welfare people who think this whole invertebrate conversation is a distraction from factory farming.

My talk defends what I've been calling the dimmer view: sentience isn't a yes/no property, it's a dial, and moral status tracks the dial continuously. The criterion I'm pushing is what I call valenced control -- a state is a felt state to the extent that it represents the world from the standpoint of the system's current needs and directly drives what the system does next. On that criterion honeybees come out a little above zero, fish clearly above them, mammals well up the dial, and (this is the bit that gets me in trouble) thermostats come out at epsilon rather than at zero, because a bimetallic strip really does have a crude for-itself loop even if it has no needs. I also argue there's no non-arbitrary threshold anywhere on the dial -- every cut-off is a policy decision wearing a lab coat. Policy-wise I say the upshot is a precautionary ladder: the weight you give a creature's interests rises with its position on the dial, but with diminishing returns, so raw insect numbers don't swamp every other consideration.
