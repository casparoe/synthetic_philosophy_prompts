You are a philosopher of language and a metaphysician who studies how disputes in ontology turn on language. I want a structured, analytical answer, not a literature survey, and I want you to reason from the proposal below rather than from whatever you already associate with the topic. Before writing the answer, spend a few sentences brainstorming the candidate implications and sketching how you would rank them; then present the ranked analysis.

Here is the proposal. Fix a shared language and a class of interpreters, namely computationally bounded agents like us. Call a dispute between A and B over a sentence S verbal-in-principle when there is a pair of interpretations of the shared language, one assigned to A and one to B, such that (i) every assertion A makes in the dispute is true under A's interpretation and every assertion B makes is true under B's; (ii) the two interpretations agree on every expression that neither party disputes; and (iii) the interpretations are related by a translation that is computable in principle from the parties' dispositions to assent and dissent, with no limit on time or memory. Call the dispute verbal-in-practice when, in addition, such a translation can actually be constructed by an interpreter from the bounded class using only the evidence available to them. A dispute that is verbal-in-principle but not verbal-in-practice falls into a third category: practically substantive though theoretically verbal.

Please describe the most important implications of this three-way classification. Address, in particular, what it implies for (a) whether disputes about composition, persistence, and similar matters are merely verbal, given that the relevant translations may exist in principle yet be unavailable to us; (b) whether calling a dispute merely verbal should count as a criticism, and whether the middle category gives a principled way to decide when a deflationary move is legitimate; (c) the self-application problem, that is, what follows if the dispute over this very proposal is itself only verbal-in-principle; (d) the norm of charity in interpretation, and whether clause (iii) covertly presupposes a substantive theory of meaning or of evidence; (e) whether the middle category is stable under pressure or collapses into one of the other two; and (f) how a working metaphysician should allocate effort if many first-order disputes are theoretically verbal but practically substantive.

Rank the implications by importance, distinguish what follows from the proposal itself from what follows from the broader idea, which the proposal is meant to sharpen, that many such disputes are merely verbal, and identify where the proposal is doing genuine work and where it is merely stipulative. State your confidence in each substantive claim, and at the end single out the one point you are least confident about and explain why.
