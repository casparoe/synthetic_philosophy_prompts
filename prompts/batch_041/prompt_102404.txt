I've got a reading group slot coming up and I want to actually survive the Q&A for once, so I'm commissioning you to write me a prep sheet. Background on me: I'm a longtime reader of the rationality blogs — LessWrong, Slate Star Codex back in the day, that whole orbit — and I've been nursing this half-baked idea that a lot of what people call "moral compromise" is really just gains from trade, and that you can make that precise without smuggling in a common currency of value. The move I want to make is this: take two people with genuinely different value systems, neither of whom accepts a shared utility metric or even a shared ranking over the options. The standard objection is that you need interpersonal comparison before you can call any trade a Pareto improvement. My claim is you don't, as long as the trades run across issues rather than within them — a kind of logrolling where each person gives up something they rank low for something they rank high, and the issues are seperable enough that the rankings don't interact. I've been calling the resulting structure a "compromise frontier" and claiming it's thinner than the standard contractarian story but thicker than a bare modus vivendi. Concrete toy case I keep using: a vegan and a hunter who share a flat and both care about their thing — the vegan wants no meat in the kitchen, the hunter wants to keep hunting; the alleged trade is that he keeps the hobby but funds her animal-welfare cause, or something like that, and the interesting bit is supposed to be that neither of them has to rank the two outcomes on a common scale for the swap to be an improvement for both.

The audience is the political theory reading group, which in practice means about seven Rawlsians and contractualists of various stripes, plus two people from the econ department who do social choice and a bit of mechanism design. The Rawlsians are allergic to anything that smells like treating values as preferences to be aggregated, and the second you say "trade" they'll reach for public reason and legitimacy and start asking whether I'm describing a modus vivendi or an overlapping consensus. The econ people will go straight for the formal problems — separability, what the numeraire even is, the old Sen-style impossibility results about Pareto and liberalism — and honestly they're the ones who could actually sink it. The two camps mostly don't read each other and I'll be the only person in the room trying to talk to both, which is half the difficulty.
