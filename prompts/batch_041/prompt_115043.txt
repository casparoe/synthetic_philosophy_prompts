Question. Priya, aged 32, signs a legally binding "values directive". In it she states that if she ever develops a severe depressive illness and asks for assisted death, that request is to be refused, because she does not trust requests for death made from inside depression to reflect her settled values. At 55, after two years of severe, treatment-resistant depression, she is assessed as fully competent and asks for assisted death. Does the authority of the earlier Priya settle the matter? Say what the earlier self's authority rests on, and whether it survives the fact that the later Priya is competent.

Answer. The standard justification for an advance directive is respect for autonomy. If we only honour a person's choices while she can still voice them, we let illness dictate the shape of her life and hand her last years over to whoever happens to be making decisions for her; a directive lets her govern her life as a whole rather than only the stretch of it in which she is articulate. So the fact that a choice is made in advance does not make it any less an exercise of self-determination.

The usual objection is that the person who made the directive and the person it now applies to may want different things. But that objection is weaker than it looks, because a directive is not a promise to a third party. Nobody else acquires a right under it, so there is no external claim that could outweigh the patient's own. What we have is one person's settled judgement set against that same person's present preference, and the settled judgement is exactly what the directive was designed to protect.

So in Priya's case the finding of competence is something of a red herring. She is depressed, and a request for death made from inside depression is precisely what she told us in advance not to trust. Her competent request is therefore the very thing the directive was written to refuse, and the clinicians should refuse it.
