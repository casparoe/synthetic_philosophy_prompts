I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book whose protagonist is a cognitive scientist who has spent her whole career trying to explain, in plain information-processing terms, why human beings are so convinced their experiences have this strange private, ineffable, what-it's-like quality that no third-person story ever seems to capture. In the world of the book she basically pulls it off: she's got an account of why creatures like us end up reporting exactly that, and it turns out to be a story about self-models and the way a brain represents its own representations, nothing spooky anywhere in it. My editor keeps asking me what the point of the whole thing is, and I've realized I don't have a clean answer, so before I write the last act I want to actually think through what follows from a solution like that. Could you lay out the most important implications of a fully worked-out deflationary answer to the meta-problem — I mean the meta-problem of consciousness, the puzzle of why we're disposed to think there's a hard problem in the first place — and be concrete about what such an answer would and wouldn't touch? I'm especially curious whether it would dissolve the hard problem itself or leave it standing, what it would do to the trust we place in first-person reports (in psychiatry, in our own introspection, in the courtroom), how it bears on which entities we owe moral or legal consideration, and whether it takes the legs out from under phenomenology as a method or merely relocates it. The part that interests me most is the cases where the deflationary story comes out true and the hard problem survives anyway, so please don't just hand me the tidy version where it's all one package.

For each implication, I'd like you to say how tight the connection actually is — is this something the solution strictly entails, or something it merely makes easier to believe, or just a rhetorical move people would make once they heard it? Rank them by how much they'd really change what someone does, not by how dramatic they sound. And if the whole setup is confused — if solving the meta-problem isn't the sort of thing that could have implications of this shape, or if I'm smuggling in an assumption about what a solution would have to look like that nobody actually has to accept — then please say so straight instead of humoring me; I'd rather find out now than build 200 pages around a mistake. My protagonist is stubborn and would want the strongest case, but I need the honest one.
