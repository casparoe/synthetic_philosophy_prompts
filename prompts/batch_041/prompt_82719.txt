I'm building a unit for a graduate seminar on explanation in history, and I want a graded verdict rather than a survey. The unit turns on a single question: when a historian explains a particular development, how much of the work is subsumption under general laws — the covering-law picture, on which an event is explained once it can be deduced from laws together with initial conditions — and how much is narrative, where the intelligibility comes from the contingent sequence itself rather than from any law?

For each of the developments below, give a score between 0 and 1 for how much of it is captured by covering-law explanation, where 1 means that stating the relevant laws and initial conditions would essentially complete the explanation, and 0 means that laws do almost no explanatory work and only a narrative delivers the explanation. Then rank the developments from most to least amenable to covering-law treatment. For each, add a sentence or two on what earns it its place, and where two developments come out close, say what separates them. Treat the scores as your own considered judgment rather than as a summary of what anyone has said.

1. The extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs at the end of the Cretaceous.
2. The long decline in homicide rates across early modern Western Europe.
3. The radicalization of the French Revolution between 1789 and 1794.
4. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991.
5. Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo in 1815.
6. The spread of agriculture out of the Fertile Crescent.
7. The global financial crisis of 2008.
8. The success of the Protestant Reformation in northern Europe and its failure across much of the south.

Two further things. First, the following generalization is one I have written for this exercise rather than drawn from any literature, and I would like you to treat it as a serious candidate: "Whenever a state comes to depend for its revenue on a single volatile source while a peripheral region acquires an independent military capacity, the state's monopoly on legitimate violence erodes within two generations." Say which of the developments above this would genuinely cover, which it would merely postdict or fail to reach at all, and what that reveals about how much covering-law explanation these developments really admit. Second, close with a short verdict on whether the exercise as a whole supports or undercuts the covering-law picture as an account of what historians are actually doing when they explain.
