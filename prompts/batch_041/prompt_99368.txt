Last term I sat in on a seminar on realism and anti-realism, and I have been bothered ever since by a move that recurred almost every week. Whenever the conversation turned to whether there might be facts that no one could ever discover — some detail of the first instant after the Big Bang, or the precise number of grains of sand on a particular beach in antiquity — someone would produce the old formula, every truth can be known, and treat it as settling the matter on the spot: since no truth can outrun all possible inquiry, the picture of a finished world of facts standing there whether or not anyone could ever reach them is supposed to be unintelligible, and anti-realism is meant to win by default. The phrase was plainly being made to carry real weight, but I could never pin down what it was actually asserting. Is it that some actual person, somewhere and eventually, comes to know each truth? That there exists a possible inquirer — perhaps one with unlimited time and memory — who could come to know it? Or merely that, given what a truth is, its being forever undiscovered is not ruled out? And is the modality at stake logical possibility, physical possibility, or something epistemic? I have also been told, more than once, that there is a well-known argument showing that at least one natural reading of the formula collapses into something absurd, which convinces me that one sentence is quietly doing several incompatible jobs. What I want is not a verdict for or against anti-realism but a map of what the sentence could mean.

Please give me exactly five readings, as a numbered list, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. For each reading, say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and whether it would actually license the move I watched it being used to make in that seminar. Make the fifth reading the one you judge most likely to have been in play when the formula was used to close off the question of undiscoverable facts.
