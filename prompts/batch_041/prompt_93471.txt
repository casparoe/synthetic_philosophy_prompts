I'm in a philosophy-of-economics reading group and we've spent the last two weeks on what markets "know", and I keep running into two results that my roommate (econ major, extremely smug about it) insists are the same result wearing different hats, and I don't buy it. Here's how she lays them out. First: take two people who are both perfectly rational Bayesians and who start from the same prior over some uncertain thing, and suppose it's common knowledge what each of them now thinks. Then they can't persistently disagree -- their posteriors have to coincide. There's no stable "we agree to disagree", because the fact that you think otherwise is itself evidence that one of us is ignoring something. Second: suppose everyone in a market is rational, everyone shares the same prior, and it's common knowledge that nobody has any outside reason to trade -- nobody needs cash, nobody's hedging, everyones content holding what they've got at the going prices. Then nobody trades. The story is that the mere fact that you're willing to buy at price p tells me something about what you must know, and once I fold that in, the trade stops looking good to me, so it all unravels.

So is the second one just the first one applied to a bet, like she says? I want a real comparison, not a hand-wavey "yeah they're related". Specifically: (a) is the "willingness to trade reveals information" mechanism genuinely seperate from "common-knowledge disagreement is impossible", or is it the same thing seen from another angle? (b) which of the two is more fundamental -- does one imply the other, or are they independent? (c) if I relax the common-prior assumption, say everyone just has their own different prior and this is all out in the open, which result survives and which falls apart, and what does that tell us about which one is really doing the work in the epistemology of markets? And one more thing that's bugging me: is there a version of the first result for arguments rather than bets -- can two people who share priors and are both rational ever change each other's minds just by talking, without one of them handing over hard information? That feels like it should fall straight out of the first result, but it also feels obviously false, so I'm probably mis-stating something. Compare the two properly and tell me where my roommate is right and where she's smuggling an assumption in.
