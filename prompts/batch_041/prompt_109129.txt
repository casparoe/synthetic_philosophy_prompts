A long thread on a rationalist forum I follow has been trying to explain why one of its most productive contributors was quietly eased out of the group's inner circle over the past year — a well-received sequence of posts, an invitation to speak, a tool that other people started copying — and the top reply was a one-liner: 'the nail that sticks up gets hammered down.' Several people treated it as self-evidently the whole explanation. One called it the tall poppy syndrome, another said it was just Janteloven, and a third — correctly, as far as I can tell — pointed out that the Jante rules are a fictional code of conduct from Aksel Sandemose's 1933 satirical novel, whose eleventh rule, the one the novel itself calls the penal code, is 'Perhaps you don't think we know a few things about you?' That detail makes the slogan sound less like a description and more like a threat, and I honestly can't tell which of the two the thread meant by it.

I want a menu rather than your own verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the tendentious, and give me a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, unfalsifiable, or false. In particular:

- What does 'sticks out' have to mean for each reading — being better than the others, being visible, or merely being different? Which readings collapse into the tautology that groups apply friction to members who deviate, and which ones say something specific about success?
- What does 'hammered down' have to mean — gossip, exclusion, withholding credit or resources, institutional penalty, or the target's own preemptive self-suppression? And does 'gets' assert a statistical tendency, an inevitability, or a claim of desert, i.e. that they had it coming?
- Which readings would actually explain the contributor's ouster, which would merely justify it, and which would be needed for the reply to count as an explanation at all rather than an epithet?

The thread also contains two rival diagnoses I'd like priced against the slogan: that he was pushed out for correcting a senior member in public, i.e. for a status violation rather than for excellence, and that the group's reaction was rational because letting one member's reputation compound captures a collective resource. For each, say which readings of the proverb it is compatible with, and what would have to be true to distinguish envy from norm-enforcement. The other faction in the thread kept repeating that anyone good enough eventually gets recognized; tell me which readings of the proverb survive alongside that claim, and which reading I am implicitly committed to if I'm going to stay as angry about what happened as I currently am. I can't get at the literature from here, so keep it conceptual and give me a numbered list I can paste back into the thread.
