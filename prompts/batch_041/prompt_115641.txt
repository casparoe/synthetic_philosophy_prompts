Ok, Dutch book question, and I feel like Im missing something that everyone else finds obvious. The way I understand it: if my credences break the probability axioms, a bookie can stitch together a bunch of bets that are each fair by my own lights, and I lose no matter how things turn out. Got it. But thats a claim about a set of credences at a moment, right? Which means if my credences are coherent — and theyre coherent at every single moment, every snapshot passes — then theres no such set of bets and Im safe. I dont see where anything else could get smuggled in.

And staying coherent isnt hard. My policy is: when I learn something I set that thing to 1, obviously, but I dont do the conditionalization thing. I just leave my other numbers roughly where they were, or nudge them around however I feel like, as long as everything still adds to 1. Check me at any time and Im a perfectly good probability function.

Concretely: Im 45% sure my car fails its inspection tomorrow. Im 80% sure it fails if the mechanic frowns when he pops the hood, and 10% sure if he doesnt. And Im 50/50 on wether he frowns. (Those all fit together, I checked.) He frowns. Now I say: ok, 45%. I dont move it to 80%. New snapshot: frown = 1, failure = 45%, rest renormalized. Coherent. So where is the money actually going?

Someone told me the trick is that the bookie sells me a bet on the failure *conditional* on the frown before I ever meet the mechanic, at my own 80%, and then buys it back off me at 45% once he frowns. But a bet that just gets called off if the condition fails looks like it can only help me or do nothing, and anyway I could refuse the second trade. And if the argument needs that conditional bet, isnt it just assuming the conclusion?

So whats the actual move? Every credence Ive ever held is coherent, so the theorem has nothing to grab onto. Either the sure loss is somewhere I cant see, or its not really a Dutch book argument at all and Im being sold something else wearing its coat.
