I am a novelist, and I am finishing a book whose plot turns on a device that reads a person and reconstitutes her elsewhere; its final chapter depends on whether a particular grief is warranted. Next month I am to interview, on stage at a literary festival, a philosopher who defends the view associated with Parfit's teletransporter cases: that personal identity is not what matters in survival, and that what matters instead is psychological continuity and connectedness, whatever its cause. I would like eight to ten interview questions that do not merely invite him to restate that view, and that he can answer from the armchair — neither he nor I will have texts or dates to hand, so please do not ask him to name works or trace a literature.

Three threads of my novel should supply the material, and I want the questions built on them rather than on the stock examples.

In the first, the device is never a single clean copy. Long-haul travellers are relayed through six stations, each of which decodes and re-encodes the pattern, so the person who walks out at the far end has in effect been copied six times over, and no one can say at which station the original stopped.

In the second, a woman records her pattern at thirty as insurance against illness, lives another forty years, and activates the archive as she is dying at seventy. The replica wakes believing herself thirty, is told she has a husband and grandchildren she does not remember, and is treated by the courts as the same person for purposes of inheritance.

In the third, the destruction of the original is instantaneous and complete, and my narrator is the wife left behind, who insists she has been widowed while everyone around her insists that nothing has happened.

I want at least one question built on each of the following: (i) whether what matters comes in degrees, and whether a traveller re-encoded forty times, who remembers his first crossing only as a rumour, still stands in the relation that matters to the man who first stepped into the booth; (ii) whether the view leaves a person any reason to care specially about her own future rather than about her replica's, or a stranger's; (iii) whether the wife's grief can be anything but a mistake about the facts, and the related charge that the whole account is written from nowhere — that it describes the case in the third person while what we are trying to understand, the anticipation of one's own end, is essentially first-personal; and (iv) the case in which a man is transcribed twice before either copy is destroyed, so that two claimants exist — whether there is a fact of the matter as to which is he, and whether the answer to that question is what should settle who owes his debts and whom his wife should mourn.

He is courteous but slippery: when a case embarrasses the view he tends to reply that the question presupposes a further fact about identity, and that there is no such further fact. I would like the sequence to make that reply cost him something, and to end with a question he cannot answer that way.

Please give me the questions in the order I should ask them, and for each one a single line saying what answer it is designed to force, together with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Nothing about how he came to philosophy, and nothing about what he is reading.
