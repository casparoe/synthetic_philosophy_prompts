I spent my career in experimental geophysics and have only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, so I am still learning to distinguish a substantive objection from a rhetorical one. A former colleague, an ecologist, has published a popular book on resource overshoot. In the chapter on Malthus he writes:

"Malthus predicted that population would always outrun food supply, so that mass famine and poverty were inevitable. He was wrong. The Industrial Revolution and the Green Revolution raised agricultural output far beyond what he imagined, and his theory was falsified. The only reason anyone still cites him is that his defenders retreat to the claim that he was right but early: if we ever stop inventing new technology, his prediction will come true. But that is an unfalsifiable rescue. A theory that cannot be falsified by any possible event is not empirical; it is a prophecy. So Malthus's law is not a scientific theory at all, and the fact that some economists still take it seriously shows how unfalsifiable claims survive in the social sciences. The correct lesson is Popper's: a genuine scientific prediction must specify in advance what would refute it, and Malthus failed that test."

I would like an accuracy audit rather than a general impression. Please proceed claim by claim. For each, tell me what a careful philosopher of science or a defender of Malthus would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. I am especially interested in whether the author is conflating a conditional model of population and subsistence with an unconditional prophecy of inevitable famine; whether the distinction between preventive and positive checks matters to the fairness of the summary; and whether the falsification of a long-run prediction is as straightforward as the passage suggests once auxiliary assumptions and ceteris paribus clauses are on the table.

A distinction from a seminar I attended has stuck with me, though I do not know whether it is standard: between a temporal scope condition, which says that a model's antecedent conditions have not yet obtained, and a mechanism-preserving rescue, which says that the mechanism is operating but has been masked by an exogenous countervailing factor. I would like the audit to say which of the author's targets, if any, respect that distinction, and whether the right-but-early move can ever be more than an ad hoc rescue. If it can, what would distinguish a progressive revision of scope from a degenerating one in this case?

Finally, does the author's conclusion—that Malthus's principle is not a scientific theory at all—follow from the accurate parts of his summary, or is it an overreach? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want the conceptual audit, with the strongest version of the opposing view stated fairly.
