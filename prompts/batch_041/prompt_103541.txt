I run a weekly session for a mixed-background reading group — a few computer scientists, a few philosophers, and a couple of people who simply like to argue — and next month the slot is mine and the topic is Pascal's mugging. I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard, and the group's formal background stops at expected value; several of them will have met Pascal's wager in a religious-studies context and will arrive assuming this is the same argument in different clothing. I do not want you to explain the topic to me. I want you to plan how I explain it: the order the pieces have to go in so that the conclusion feels like something the group is forced into rather than something I have announced, and which piece depends on which. My own instinct is that the mugging cannot even be stated until they have a working notion of expected value under uncertainty and some sense that decision theory is meant to be indifferent to the absolute size of a probability provided the product stays fixed — but tell me whether that is the right opening move, and what the real opening move is if it is not.

The part I most want your judgement on is the carrying example. The obvious candidate is the mugger in the alley, and I suspect it is wrong, because it invites the room to dispose of the whole thing by observing that the man is plainly lying, which kills the anecdote without ever touching the structure; the second candidate, Pascal's wager, drags in theological commitments and will make half the room dig in. So propose the single example that should carry the session, say what it must have and must not have for the structure to stay visible, and give me a defensible reason for rejecting the alley mugger if you agree it should go — or for keeping it, if you think the room's first reaction is itself useful material. One constraint on your choice: a colleague has been pushing a variant she calls the slow mugging, in which nothing in the story is infinite and the promised reward is an ordinary finite sum, but the sum is offered as a function of how small a probability I am asked to accept, growing fast enough that the expected value can be driven above any finite price. I am inclined to think this is a better instrument than the alley, because it makes plain that the engine of the argument is not the word infinite but the absence of any principled ceiling on the product. Adopt it, adapt it, or tell me why it fails as the spine of the session.

Finally, map the misconceptions for me and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off: that the mugging is just Pascal's wager; that the difficulty lies in the smallness of the probability, so that a sufficiently generous probability would dissolve it; that it is an argument for some particular religious conclusion; that exposing this particular mugger as a liar is a reply to the argument rather than to the anecdote; and that the only options are to comply or to abandon decision theory altogether. Then give me the one question I should put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the structure of the problem rather than merely been made to squirm, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to cover in fifty minutes and announce as out of scope.
