I am a novelist, and for the book I am writing I need a document rather than a scene.

Two of my characters have argued for thirty years about radical interpretation, and I have reached the point where I must know precisely what they would put their names to. What I want from you is the joint statement they would produce in good faith after a long collaboration: not a synthesis, not a truce, not a survey of a literature, but the text in which they set down what they now hold in common and state exactly where they still part company.

The two camps are these. One holds that interpretation presupposes a shared language — a public, learned, conventional structure that speaker and interpreter both command — and that in its absence there is nothing determinate for the interpreter to recover. The other holds that there is no such thing, in the sense that matters: what a speaker means is fixed by the passing theory a particular interpreter constructs on a particular occasion, so that knowing a language names only a disposition which usually, though not always, produces convergence. Both accept the framework of radical interpretation as Davidson set it out: that meaning is recovered from behaviour and from the pattern of sentences a speaker is prepared to assent to; that belief and meaning are interpreted together, so that no workable interpretation can attribute wholesale error or wholesale nonsense; that the reference of a term cannot be read off the evidence, and rival manuals of translation can be built that fit the same behaviour equally well; and that there is no pre-theoretic fact of the matter about meaning to be consulted, only what the interpreter's theory delivers. Take these as given and build from them.

The statement must do four things. It must set out the claims both camps can sign, and those claims must not be vacuous; I do not want holism restated as agreement. It must isolate the part of the quarrel that is really about words — where the two are using one term, or one criterion, in two ways — and say which part that is. It must state the remaining disagreement in wording each camp would accept for its own position. And it must name the crux, record the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and set down each side's prediction in advance.

Commit to your divisions. Do not lay the options before me and leave me to decide which disagreements are real; tell me which they are and give your reasons. If you judge the dispute to be verbal through and through, say so, and defend that judgment in the document's own terms. No citations and no reading list; I want the argument itself.

Format: exactly five numbered articles and nothing else — no title, no preamble, no closing sentence. Each article is one or two sentences, and the fifth carries the crux, the case both sides accept as bearing on it, and both predictions.
