I am a novelist at work on a book, and I need a case for a scene in which a minister's Rawlsian defence of a policy is demolished in argument. So construct one.

Construct a case in which the parties behind the veil of ignorance — ignorant of their social position, their natural talents and their conception of the good — would reject a policy that does in fact maximise the primary goods of the least advantaged. The rejection must fall out of the device itself: not from smuggling in envy, an unusual degree of risk-aversion or a particular conception of the good, and not from the policy violating the first principle or fair equality of opportunity, since either of those makes the case useless for my purposes. The policy must be one a modern government could plausibly adopt — no science fiction, no new technology. Give me the scenario in a single paragraph; if your case requires the device to be extended in some way, say explicitly what extension you are making and defend it.

Then set out the reasoning on both sides: the difference principle's verdict on the policy and the parties' verdict, with the reasoning that produces each. Say which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Close with the strongest reply available to a Rawlsian who thinks the difference principle already covers this ground, and say whether the reply succeeds.

Much of this ground is contested among people who work on it. Keep what is settled separate from what is disputed, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.

Write in British English.
