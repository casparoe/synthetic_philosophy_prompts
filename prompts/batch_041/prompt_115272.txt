I am writing the take-home essay section of the final examination for a senior undergraduate seminar in medieval philosophy of mind, cross-listed with metaphysics. We have spent three weeks on Avicenna's flying man and the medieval controversy over whether the soul is a substance in its own right, and I want this section to separate students who genuinely understand the argument from students who can do no more than recite the standard gloss — that the flying man proves the soul is immaterial and really distinct from the body.

Please write me a single essay question, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme. The question must not require citations, a reading list, or knowledge of any particular edition, translation, or scholar's name; students have only their notes and the argument itself, and I do not want the exercise to become a memory test for who said what.

The question should do the following work.

First, it should make the student reconstruct the thought experiment precisely enough to say what Avicenna actually asks us to imagine — a human being brought into existence fully formed, suspended in a void, deprived of every sensory contact, including contact with his own limbs — and to say what, on Avicenna's account, such a person would still be able to affirm (his own existence) and what he would not affirm (the existence of his body). I do not want the setup dispatched in a sentence; I want the student to see which features of the scenario are load-bearing and which are decoration.

Second, and this is the crux, the question should force the student to say exactly how strong a conclusion that premise can carry. There is a short list of candidates they should be made to sort through and adjudicate: (a) that the self exists; (b) that the self is not presented to itself as bodily, an epistemic point about self-awareness; (c) that the self is really distinct from the body; (d) that the self is immaterial and could subsist apart from the body. The student should be pressed to say which of these the flying man as stated actually establishes, and to identify the extra premise that would be required to get from the weaker to the stronger — the move from being affirmable without the body to being able to exist without it — and to explain why that move is not free.

Third, I want them to confront a variant I have added to the syllabus this year, precisely to stop them from making that leap too quickly. Call it the grounded flying man. Stipulate that the suspended man's capacity to affirm his own existence is, as a matter of fact, causally sustained by a hidden organ — an undetected brain, say — that he can neither perceive nor infer from anything available within his situation. From the inside he is exactly as Avicenna describes: he affirms himself and nothing of his body. The student must say whether the flying man argument can rule this scenario out from the first-person standpoint, and, if it cannot, what that shows about the argument's reach. My own view, which I want them to argue with rather than swallow, is that it cannot: the causal dependence is by construction invisible from where the flying man stands, so the experiment delivers an epistemic result rather than the metaphysical one it is usually advertised as delivering.

Fourth, they should then say what else Avicenna must appeal to if he wants the stronger conclusion — for instance the claim that rational intellection, the grasp of universals, cannot be the operation of a bodily organ — and whether that additional argument genuinely closes the gap or merely relocates it.

Fifth, I want a contrast with Descartes' cogito somewhere in the answer: the student should say what the flying man and the cogito are each trying to establish, and why treating the flying man as an inference from thinking to existence misdescribes it.

A few constraints. The question must be answerable in about fifty minutes of writing. Everything the student needs should be reconstructible from the setup and from the argument itself, with no dependence on external sources. The model answer should read like a genuinely strong student's essay — it should stake out a position and defend it, not summarize a lecture. The marking scheme should total 25 points, name the specific must-hit moves and give each its weight, and for each must-hit move supply a one-line description of the mistake that a merely competent answer typically makes in its place. Finally, the question itself must state the grounded-flying-man variant in enough detail that a student who has never encountered it can still take a considered position on it.
