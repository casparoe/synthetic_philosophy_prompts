I have spent a decade reporting on the history of moral change, and I keep returning to a dispute that never resolves. One camp holds that the great moral revolutions — the abolition of chattel slavery, the collapse of the duel of honor, the abandonment of footbinding — were won by argument: someone made the moral case, and the case convinced. The other holds that argument was largely epiphenomenal, and that what actually moved societies was honor, interest, or the shifting cost of maintaining the practice. Each new book restates one side without answering the other, and no one's mind changes. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about the evidence but about two different questions wearing a single label: one side asks what justified the change, the other what caused it. Test that against a case the literature usually passes over — the rapid reversal in Western attitudes toward same-sex marriage — and tell me whether the hypothesis survives. If it does not, supply the better diagnosis: a substantive disagreement about the historical record, a conceptual one about what counts as an argument or as a revolution, or a hidden empirical question doing the work. And on any diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a new case, a method — or whether the question as posed admits none.
