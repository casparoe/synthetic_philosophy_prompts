I sit on the pretrial services oversight panel for my county — I'm the lay member, appointed because I used to run a neighborhood mediation program, not because I know anything about statistics — and on Thursday we vote on whether to keep, modify, or cancel our contract with the vendor whose risk-assessment tool the judges consult when they set bail. I want to circulate a short written opinion before that vote, and I'd like your help drafting it.

Here is what we have. The tool turns a defendant's record into a score from one to ten, which the judge sees alongside the charging documents. It never looks at race, and the vendor's validation, which a local university team has now partly replicated, shows that at any given score the two-year re-arrest rate is about the same for Black and for white defendants — in that narrow sense the score appears to mean the same thing whoever receives it. But the same study found that Black defendants are flagged as high risk at roughly twice the rate, and that among defendants who were in fact not re-arrested, the Black ones were considerably more often labeled high risk. The public defender's office calls the tool discriminatory and wants it gone; the vendor answers that the tool is accurate and colorblind and that the disparity merely reflects differences in the underlying arrest data, which is a policing problem rather than a software problem. Two of my colleagues have floated a compromise: keep the tool, but require the vendor to show it is fair not only on average but within every subgroup we can name — young men, older women, people with a prior violent charge, and so on — and to keep showing it for any new subgroup anyone proposes later. A statistician who advised us says that demand sounds reasonable and is in fact impossible to satisfy, or at least impossible to satisfy alongside the calibration the vendor is already boasting about, though I confess I did not fully follow why, and I would like the memo to explain it in terms I can defend at the table.

Please write the opinion I'll read out and then file. I want a concrete recommendation — keep, cancel, or keep under conditions I can actually specify — with a time frame for whatever we decide, and I want the strongest argument on the other side stated fairly rather than as a straw man, since at least two panel members hold it sincerely. If the honest answer is that we are choosing which kind of error to live with rather than eliminating error, say so plainly, and tell me what that choice would mean for the people who appear before us. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter to colleagues who will read it once and then argue with it.
