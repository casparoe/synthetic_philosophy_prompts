I am writing this up for a reading group on information and markets, and I have been trying to reconstruct an argument that trade between rational agents is impossible. I first met the argument as a slogan — that a good opportunity would already have been taken — and I have since tried to build the serious version of it myself. I can no longer tell whether it is a genuine result with a narrow scope or a fallacy with a wide one. Here it is, in the order in which the steps came to me.

(1) A voluntary exchange happens only because the buyer expects the thing to be worth more to him than the price and the seller expects it to be worth less to him than the price. If the two of them valued it identically, there would be nothing for them to trade over.
(2) So every trade rests on a disagreement between the parties about the value of what changes hands.
(3) Rational agents who share the same evidence cannot disagree. If you had my evidence and reasoned as carefully as I do, you would arrive at my belief; and if I know that you have my evidence and reason carefully, I should already hold your belief. Disagreement therefore requires either a difference in evidence or a failure of reasoning.
(4) It follows that in every trade at least one party is working from information the other lacks, or reasoning badly.
(5) The willingness of the other party is itself evidence. That someone offers to sell me an asset at a given price tells me that he does not expect it to be worth much more than that price, and that someone offers to buy tells me the reverse. Even before I inspect his reasons, his readiness should move my estimate.
(6) Taken seriously, this becomes a prohibition rather than a caution: whatever deal is offered to me at a price that is common knowledge, the very fact that it is offered is news against taking it, so a rational agent declines.
(7) But trade is everywhere, and much of it plainly leaves both sides better off; societies that suppress it are poor, and no one believes that every purchase is a swindle.
(8) So either the reasoning is sound and rational trade is impossible, or one of these steps is doing work it cannot support.

I am attached to (3): I do not want to give up the thought that rational agents who share their evidence converge, or that disagreement between them stands in need of explanation. Everything else I am willing to sacrifice. Tell me which step or steps to reject, why each of them looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If the trouble is in (2), I want to know whether the caution in (5) can survive it even if the prohibition in (6) cannot. If the trouble is in (3), I want to know what I am conceding about rational disagreement, and whether the concession is large enough to make the argument collapse on its own. And if the argument is simply invalid as written — if a quantifier, a scope, or a modality has slid somewhere — I want to know exactly where it slides, and which of the surviving steps still has force once the slide is blocked.
