Write a villanelle in the voice of the coin detector Dennett uses to stage the dispute between his account of mental content and Searle's — the two-bitser, calibrated for one currency, that goes on accepting a lookalike coin from another. It will open a seminar meeting on mental representation, so it must stand on its own as verse and still be exact about the philosophy. Its two refrains should carry the two positions and nothing else. One refrain should be the intentional-stance reading: the machine wants the coin, takes it for a real quarter, is satisfied when it drops through and disappointed when it is returned. The other should be the deflationary reading: whatever the machine takes the coin for was lent to it by the people who designed, built, and installed it, and the machine owns none of it. The refrains should alternate as the form requires and meet in the closing quatrain, the second falling across the first so that it qualifies and cancels it; the poem should end there, without adjudicating, leaving open whether the chain of lending terminates in a lender, and whether our own case differs in kind from the machine's or only in the size of the loan. Something should hang on whether the machine can be wrong or only broken, since that is where the question of whose standard of correctness is in play becomes concrete.

Keep the poem about a thing rather than a thesis: brass, slug, rim, weight, calibration, a screwdriver, the crate, the warehouse, the humidity of the place it was moved to. Do not use the words intentional, intentionality, derived, original, aboutness, or as if; let the machine's behavior and the vocabulary of borrowing and lending do that work instead. The form should be a strict villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain, rhyme and meter consistent though slant if you prefer — with the refrains returning verbatim until the last stanza, where a single altered word is permitted if the alteration earns itself. A reader who knows the dispute should be able to say which refrain is Dennett's and which is Searle's, and why the final two lines make it impossible to keep them apart; a reader who does not should still see a small machine making the same mistake twice.
