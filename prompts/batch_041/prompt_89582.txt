I'm taking a course on early modern philosophy, and we've just reached George Berkeley's economic writings — a part of the corpus I had never heard of before this term. A popular history-of-ideas book I've been reading summarizes his position as follows (the author is a historian rather than a philosopher, and I suspect he is tidying things up for a general readership):

"Berkeley's economics is really just his metaphysics applied to money. In The Querist (1735) he asks whether money's true idea is not that of a 'ticket or counter,' and his answer is that it is: gold and silver are conventional signs rather than stores of value, so a nation's wealth has nothing to do with its stock of coin. On this view money is a fiction, and the only real wealth is the labour and industry of the people. Berkeley therefore proposed that Ireland scrap its metal currency and replace it with paper issued by a national bank, which would create industry by putting purchasing power into circulation. The scheme was rejected and never tried, which is unsurprising: the claim that money is a mere ticket cannot be right, since gold has real uses and a real value independent of any convention, and in any case a country that prints its own money does not thereby become richer."

How much of this would a Berkeley specialist accept? Please go claim by claim and tell me what is accurate, what is a defensible popularization, and what Berkeley would have denied outright — bearing in mind that he wrote The Querist as a numbered series of queries rather than as a treatise, which I gather complicates the attribution of flat theses. I would also like to know whether the author's closing objections — that gold has uses independent of convention, and that the bank scheme was rejected and never tried — actually tell against anything Berkeley held, or whether they land only against the summary itself.
