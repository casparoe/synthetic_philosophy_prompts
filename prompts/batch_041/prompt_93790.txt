Here's the position I've landed on, and I want the case against it. I don't think there's any such thing as being fundamental *in the world*. When we say some entity is fundamental, we aren't reporting a feature of reality; we're marking the spot where a chain of metaphysical explanation legitimately stops, relative to a question someone is asking. Call it the stopping-point view. Three bits:

1. "Grounds" is a hyperintensional explanatory notion. Nothing in the world is hyperintensional in the way that notion needs; only our explanatory practices are. So the hyperintensionality of grounding is actually evidence *for* my view, not against it.
2. Structural fundamentality — "x has no proper grounds, full stop" — is unintelligible, because it would require a fact about what grounds what that isn't indexed to any explanatory interest.
3. So the regress terminates where our interests let it. Two inquirers with different questions can both stop at different places, and there's no further fact about which one hit bedrock. The case that pushed me here: whether a statue is grounded in its atoms or in the lump of matter seems to depend on which question you're asking — and I don't think there's a leftover fact about which is *really* the ground.
