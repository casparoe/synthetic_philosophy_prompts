I'm on a panel at our hospital's critical care allocation meeting next week -- it's the group that wrote the surge protocol -- and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion after my 20 minutes. The room: five ICU consultants and two nurses who actually ran the protocol during the bad months, a health economist who built the scoring sheet we now use (she'll defend it), a disability-rights advocate who sits on the committee and has allready told me she thinks the whole exercise is eugenics with extra steps, the hospital's lawyer, and one Kantian from the philosophy department who shows up to these.

My line is that numbers don't count as *claims* but they do count as *reasons*, and that both camps in the one-versus-five debate keep running those two things through the same scale. Claims are what people can demand of me; reasons are what I go by when I decide. Keep the books separate and you get: the five have no stronger claim than the one, the coin-flip answer isn't required, saving the five is permitted, and the numbers get to matter after all -- just on the reasons ledger rather than the claims ledger, which is why the coin flip is optional instead of obligatory.

I want the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones I'd get at a philosophy seminar, ordered by how much damage they do to me. For each one: who in the room says it, how they'd put it in their own vocabulary, my two-minute reply, and where that reply is soft. I'd rather find out now than in the room.

Format it as: (1) "My pitch in five lines" -- the whole thing compressed. (2) "Objections, worst first" -- eight of them, roughly 150 words each, in that who / how they'd phrase it / my reply / where it's weak shape. (3) "What I concede up front" -- three bullets. (4) "The one that has to land" -- one paragraph, and say why. (5) "The question I'm hoping nobody asks" -- and my answer to it. (6) "Lines I should not use."
