Write a short story in which the suspense is arithmetical. A character has to act on her confidence in something consequential — that a slope is about to let go, that a patient is going septic, that a supplier has been lying — and every piece of evidence that reaches her is uncertain: it moves her confidence without ever settling the question it bears on. The story should turn on exactly how such evidence gets absorbed.

Here is the machinery the story must put to work. The character treats the question as a small set of mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive alternatives — call them cells; for instance, the strain-gauge array is well calibrated, it reads high by a constant offset, or it has frozen at its last reading. When experience settles which cell is true, her beliefs carry over intact: whatever she thought about the world conditional on that cell survives, and all comparisons inside the cell are frozen — that is what ordinary conditionalizing on certain evidence does. Her evidence on this night is weaker. It does not tell her which cell is true; it only shifts her confidence in the cells themselves, so that she ends the night, say, three-quarters convinced the array is well calibrated and one-eighth convinced it has frozen, without having learned either for certain. The natural response, and the one your story should be built around, is to hold her conditional judgements given each cell exactly where they were and merely re-weight the cells, so that her new confidence in any hypothesis is the weighted average of her old conditional confidences in it, using her new confidence in the cells as weights. The rule is usually called Jeffrey conditionalization, after the philosopher who introduced it. Three features of it must do structural work.

First, rigidity. Stage a moment in which she refuses to let the new soft news touch a conditional judgement she already held — something like: if the array has frozen, the town has six hours and no more — and make it clear that refusing is arithmetic rather than stubbornness. Let someone press her to soften that judgement under the pressure of the news, and let her say, in concrete terms, why she cannot do so without abandoning the procedure altogether.

Second, order. Two soft reports bearing on different questions can be absorbed in either order, and the two orders can leave her at different final numbers, even though the same two reports have arrived and nothing has been learned for certain either way. Exhibit this concretely: give the figures, or let a second character who logged the same two reports in the reverse order arrive at a different figure, and let the two of them disagree about whether the discrepancy is a scandal or an artifact of what each is taking for granted.

Third, certainty and what is not certain. Include a character who proposes the obvious shortcut — to treat the soft report as if it were hard: the maintenance log says the array is drifting, so let us simply take it that the array is drifting and conditionalize on that. Your story should show that this is a different calculation with a different answer, and should make visible what the shortcut quietly assumes, namely that the report's content rather than the fact of the report is now beyond doubt. Do not settle the dispute; let each character be right about something.

Further requirements. A threshold must be explicit: some number that triggers action, stated as a number, and the story's ending must depend on which side of it the character lands. Nobody in the story may use the vocabulary of formal epistemology — no one says partition, credence, posterior, conditionalize; the characters speak as the people they are, and numbers, bets, and consequences are all fine. The mechanics must be carried by events and dialogue rather than by exposition; if the reader would need a paragraph of explanation to see the point, change the events instead of adding the paragraph. Set the story wherever you can make the arithmetic feel physical — a two-person avalanche-warning office above a valley town would serve, and so would a night ward or a commodities desk. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words, and then add a short, clearly separated coda of about 200 words, outside the fiction, in plain prose, that names the rule and states plainly the two features the story exhibited — the frozen conditionals and the order effect — and says in one sentence what the shortcut in the third beat assumes.
