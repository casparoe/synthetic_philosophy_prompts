Hi — I do the ideas column at a mid-sized paper (I'm the one who got the trolley problem wrong in print last March, so you may know me), and I'm after some help my usual sources can't give me.

Last month I ran a piece on the circle-and-chord puzzle: you draw a chord 'at random' in a circle and ask whether it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 depending on whether you randomise the endpoints, the distance from the centre, or the midpoint. It was meant to be a light thing about how much work 'at random' is quietly doing. It got more mail than anything I've written, including from two mathematicians who each explained, politely, that the other one was doing philosophy rather than maths. So now I owe readers a follow-up on the actual question: when am I allowed to say 'no reason to prefer either, so it's 50/50'? And I've realised I don't have a rule, I have a heap of gut verdicts.

Here they are, honestly:

- The chord. My gut says 1/2 is the real answer and that 1/3 and 1/4 are artefacts of odd procedures. My head says that's just because I happen to think in distances from the centre.
- A factory makes cubes. I'm told only that a cube's side is between 1 and 3 cm, and asked the chance the side is under 2 cm: 1/2, easily. Then I'm told only that the cube's volume is between 1 and 27 cm³, and asked the chance the volume is under 8 cm³ — same cube, same ignorance, different description — and my gut still says 1/2, which I can see is me cheating by silently converting back to sides. A colleague says the honest answer there is 7/26. Neither of us can say which description is the legitimate one.
- Three cards: one red on both faces, one blue on both, one red on one face and blue on the other. I draw one and see a red face. Chance the other face is red: 2/3, and I'd defend that in court.
- I meet a stranger and am asked whether they're over six foot. I'm told nothing about them. Not 50/50 — I know how heights are distributed. So 'no information' apparently never means no information, which looks like it should sink the whole idea.
- A friend says the universe will either last more than another 10^100 years or it won't, no reason to prefer either, so 1/2. I say that's garbage, and I can't tell you what makes it different from a fair coin except that one of them feels silly.

(And yes, I know the Sleeping Beauty coin-and-wakings puzzle is in the neighbourhood. Different column, not touching it.)

My working rule, which I'd like you to fix or kill: indifference is only allowed when the things I'm indifferent between are symmetric in the process that settles the question — so it's fine for a shuffled deck, fine for the cards above, not fine for the cube. But I can't say how to tell which features of the world the process is symmetric in without already knowing the process, which is exactly what I'm ignorant about. That looks circular, and I dont know whether it's a fatal circle or the ordinary kind.

No reading list, please — my editor cuts them anyway. What I need is:

- **The rule, in one breath**: one sentence I can put in the standfirst, then a paragraph actually stating it. (~100 words)
- **My cases, marked**: go through the five above, keep or overturn, with reasons. This is the piece as far as I'm concerned. (~500 words)
- **What I have to give up**: the verdicts your rule kills, said plainly, no cushioning. (~150 words)
- **Three cases I didn't list**: new ones, with verdicts, at least one of them straining your own rule. (~250 words)
- **Bottom line for the column**: a paragraph in newspaper English I can quote, plus one line each to give the two mathematicians. (~100 words)

Plain English where you can; I'll be lifting sentences straight out of this.
