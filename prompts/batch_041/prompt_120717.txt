I run a practical ethics seminar — 16 third-years, one 75-minute session, small seminar room, nothing available but paper and a whiteboard. I want a session where they actually do a moral trade in real time and feel why it's appealing and why it's queasy, without me explaining either thing first.

The thing I want them to run into: suppose I care enormously about cause X and only a little about cause Y, and you care enormously about Y and only a little about X, and it costs each of us about the same to do a bit of either. Then we can both end up with more of what we each care about if I do a bit of Y and you do a bit of X. Neither of us has to concede that the other's values are right, and neither of us has to do anything we think is wrong. That's the attractive half. The other half is what comes with putting a price on it: whoever cares least ends up with all the leverage, some of my commitments start to look like things that aren't mine to hand over, and I may catch myself treating my own moral priorities as chips to bargain with.

So I want this as a trading game with points, not a discussion. Design it so they hit the problem in the playing, before anyone names it. The core mechanic to build on: each player has a private priority profile — secret weights over a handful of causes — and a small hand of action cards, each tagged with the cause it helps. Each player commits to a fixed number of actions (say two). A player's score is the sum over every action taken by anyone in the room, weighted by that player's own priorities. So your action for my cause is worth a lot to me, and mine for yours is worth a lot to you. That's the engine of the whole thing; everything else is your design.

Things it has to do:

- No real money, points only, and the class has to be able to tot up the scores in a few minutes at the end.
- Two rounds: a first round where nobody may trade (baseline), then a round where they may make deals. The scoreboard should make it obvious that trading pays.
- Build in something that makes the commitment problem bite — can anyone actually hold anyone to a deal? — and something that makes the integrity cost bite. My suggestion: every player has one card in their hand they regard as non-negotiable, so their tradable budget is shorter than their hand suggests, and at least one player's dearest commitment is a prohibition (something they need to not do) rather than a project, so they have less to sell.
- Give the room a spread of intensities: someone who would give up alot for their cause, someone who cares only mildly. The leverage asymmetry should fall out of the numbers rather than be announced.
- Every action in the deck has to be something any reasonable person would call permissible. I don't want this to turn into "would you murder someone for a point."

Give me the response in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

"The setup" — 200 words. Who plays what, what's on each card, what I need to print.
"The rules" — 350 words. The scoring, the hand, what's allowed in round two, how deals get recorded and whether they're enforceable.
"The 75 minutes" — 200 words. A minute-by-minute timeline, including when I stop talking.
"The debrief" — 350 words. The questions to ask, in the order I should ask them, and for each one the specific moment in the game it's meant to point back to. Name the concept for the first time here.
"What goes wrong the first time" — 150 words. Predictable failure modes and what I do about each.
"What the game can't do" — 150 words. Which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

No citations or reading lists, I already know the literature. Assume the students have never heard any of this. And definately don't let the debrief turn into me lecturing — it should mostly be them talking about what just happened to them.
