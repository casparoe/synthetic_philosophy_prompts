So I've been reading LessWrong and the rat-adjacent blogs for like a decade, I work in ML (infra side, but I read enough theory to follow a PAC bound without being able to derive one), and I have never gotten a proper grip on the poverty of the stimulus argument. Every version I read is either obviously true — learners need some inductive bias, duh — or it's claiming something much stronger that I can never quite locate. So I want to do the translation exercise on it, and I want you to be ruthless.

Take the argument as a package: the child's evidence is finite and messy; the child gets no systematic negative evidence (nobody tells a three year old that a given word order is ungrammatical); the grammar the kid ends up with is underdetermined by that evidence; therefore something domain-specific has to be built in. Stipulate the empirical premises. I don't want to litigate whether kids actually get corrected, I want the argument translated.

Restate all of it, as faithfully and as hard as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in: hypothesis class, inductive bias, prior over hypotheses, likelihood, sample complexity, generalization bounds, no-free-lunch, overfitting and regularization, data generating process, distribution shift, architecture vs weights, the inner loop vs the outer loop of a meta-learning setup, and whatever else from that family you need. Assume I'm fluent in all of it — don't explain what a prior is, don't dumb it down, and don't spend words on who said what or when. No citations, no reading list, no dates or paper titles; neither of us can look anything up, so if you need to point at a specific piece of work, just describe it in your own words.

Two things I want you to be honest about rather than smooth over. First, don't smuggle: if you render the claim that the child knows some principle as the claim that the model's prior favors that principle, check whether you've actually translated anything or just swapped a psychological claim for a formal one and called it translation. Second, test a hunch of mine rather than agreeing with it. I think the strong version of the argument has to be a sample-complexity claim — no learner whose hypothesis class is that big could get there from that much data, so the restriction has to be domain-specific and built in — whereas the weak version is just that the prior is informative, which is true of every learner and therefore says nothing about innateness. Tell me if that split is carving at the joint or if the real distinction is somewhere else entirely.

Then the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about most. What came through clean? What came through only with distortion, and what exactly got squeezed out? What has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? And for that last pile, your actual read: is the gap a hole in the ML/Bayes way of talking, or a sign that the original claim was definately vaguer than it sounds and my vocabulary just refuses to paper over it?

Format: do the translation first as prose, move by move, one short numbered block per move (aim for six to eight moves, keep each one tight, no filler). Then the residue in prose, short. Then finish with a JSON object — valid JSON, no code fences, nothing after it — with exactly these fields:

- clean: array of strings, the moves that survived with nothing lost, each named in a few words.
- lossy: array of strings, one line per move that only partly survived, saying what got squeezed.
- untranslatable: array of strings, the moves with no counterpart in my vocabulary.
- residue: array of objects with fields item (string, the thing left over), diagnosis (one of "target_vocab_gap", "source_was_vaguer", "both"), and why (one or two sentences).
- smuggled: array of strings, places where you imported an ML assumption and counted it as translation.
- bottom_line: one or two sentences — is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, or a tell that the original was doing less work than it sounded like?
