My oldest friend Priya is retiring next month after thirty-odd years working on why some countries are rich and others aren't -- institutions, geography, culture, the whole fight. I'm giving the toast at her department dinner and I could use alot of help.

Half the room are development economists who've read everything she's written and will notice if I say something sloppy. The other half are her sisters and her nieces, who love her and describe her job as "she does something with numbers."

Here's my problem. Priya's whole career was basically one long argument that we can't know the answer. She had this habit: if someone said a paper had "shown" something, she'd draw a line down a napkin, put what we can actually show on the left, what we'd need to know on the right, and she'd refuse to let anything cross over. She'd say history hands you one draw. One draw, no control group, no do-over, and then everybody wants to tell you a story about why the draw came out how it did. She also thought it was a mug's game to hold one of these things still while you wiggle another -- institutions, geography and culture are all moving at once, so "controlling for" any of them is mostly theater. And she'd say a number isn't a reason: if you can't say what the mechanism is, you haven't explained anything.

So here's what I want, and I think it's genuinely hard. A four-minute toast that honors her without making a single causal claim about her. No "she changed the field." No "without her, this department wouldn't be what it is." She'd be the first to say nobody can run that counterfactual, and she'd say it at her own dinner. So the warmth has to come from somewhere I can actually point to. Keep the napkin line in it if you can, and keep the family half of the room with us.

Then, in a short note after the toast: tell me where it's cheating on her behalf -- where I'm smuggling in a causal claim, or leaning on a reading of her that her own colleagues would contest -- and say what would change your mind about whether staying inside the left column was the right way to honor her at all.
