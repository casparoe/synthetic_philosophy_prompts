I'm a postdoc in political theory, and a week from Thursday I'm giving a twenty-minute work-in-progress talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop whose regulars I know rather too well, followed by forty minutes of questions; I'd like to walk in already knowing where the ambushes are. My thesis is that a large and philosophically interesting class of moral disagreements is better modeled on trade than on adjudication. When two parties who weight different values have to settle a shared decision, there is often a package of mutual concessions — each party yielding on a dimension the other weights heavily and cares about only lightly — that both parties prefer, by their own lights, to the status quo. If that's right, the paradigm case of compromise shouldn't be the midpoint, where both sides give up something they wanted, but the trade, where each side ends up ahead; the exchange rate between the two value systems isn't declared in advance but is revealed by the pattern of concessions each is willing to make. The sting is supposed to be that the standard worry about compromise — that it costs you something you can't afford to lose — applies to midpoints but not to trades, so the cases people agonize over aren't sacrifices at all.

The room is unusually mixed, which is exactly the trouble. Two of them are Kantians who will treat the trade metaphor as a category error: for them a duty isn't a bargaining chip, and to trade away something you take to be required isn't to profit but to corrupt yourself; they'll press the integrity worry — the thought, associated with Bernard Williams, that a person always willing to hand over a conviction for a good enough bundle has already lost the thing that made the conviction hers — and they'll insist that some goods simply aren't the kind of thing that admits of exchange. One is an economist who is broadly sympathetic but will object that better off by its own lights smuggles in an interpersonal comparison and a common currency I haven't earned, and will demand that I produce the exchange rate; he'll be unimpressed if I say the rate is just whatever the two parties agree to, since that makes the whole apparatus circular. Two others work in the Berlin/Raz tradition on value pluralism and incommensurability and will say the opposite of the economist: the whole point of incommensurability is that no rate exists, so my frontier of mutually preferable packages is a fiction dressed up in geometry. And a historian of political thought will ask, gently, whether I've done anything more than relabel logrolling as moral philosophy.

Draft me the Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones a decision theorist or an abstract ethicist would raise — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, each phrased the way the person who'll raise it would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Tell me explicitly what I should concede at the outset, before anyone has to drag it out of me, and what I should refuse to concede even under pressure; and include the one objection I'm hoping nobody in the room has thought of. Where you're characterizing what these particular audiences tend to believe, say how confident you are in that characterization, and flag the single point in the whole prep sheet you're least sure of.
