I'm a software engineer at a small company that builds monitoring and control software for aquaculture farms. Our platform logs water quality, feed, growth, and mortality across a farm's ponds and cages, and on some sites it also drives two actuators on the harvest line: a rapid-chilling bath and an electrical stun. Over the past year our customers, and the certification scheme that audits them, have started pressing us to add a welfare layer — first alarms, and now logic that decides which animals must be routed through stunning before they are killed and what counts as an acceptable stun. I have no philosophy background and I'm wary of hard-coding my own intuitions into the product, so I want you to take the principle behind all of this and turn it into a decision procedure I can implement and then hand to our support staff, who are the ones who will have to explain the verdicts to farmers. The principle, as best I can reconstruct it from the material the certification body sent us, is that an animal's moral standing rests on its capacity for sentience — roughly, on there being something it is like to be that animal — combined with a precautionary rule for acting when the evidence about that capacity is genuinely unsettled rather than merely absent. I need that translated into something mechanical without quietly discarding the distinctions that do the work.

The procedure will run per batch and per species, hundreds of thousands of animals a week, and the stakes are lopsided: set the bar too low and we cause avoidable suffering at industrial scale, set it too high and we impose costs the farm will quietly route around, which is arguably worse because then nothing is actually enforced. It has to be something a farm manager can complete in a few minutes from a tablet without reading a journal article, and it has to be defensible when the certification body audits our rationale. Our staff can consult an aquatic-veterinary consultant, but only after the procedure has flagged a case — not for every batch — and any change to the default policy needs sign-off from our head of product and that consultant. Please give me the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a definite verdict (route through stunning / no special handling / do not support this species on our platform), where it stops and hands the decision to a person, and — importantly — which steps are value judgments dressed up as engineering. Then run it on three cases: a farmed salmon, where I expect the answer to be easy; our main crop, a penaeid shrimp, where I expect the answer to be contested; and a third that you should choose to be maximally awkward for us, perhaps because our highest-revenue customer wants us to support a species we have almost no data on and our hardware cannot stun. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — I would rather have a short honest list of failure modes, including the cases where it will simply misfire, than false confidence that a checklist has settled something it hasn't.
