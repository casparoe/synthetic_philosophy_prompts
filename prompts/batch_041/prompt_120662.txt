I run standards and compliance at a small company that makes modular phones -- screen, battery, camera, radios all user-swappable, that's the whole pitch. We keep having the same fight and I want to kill it with paperwork. The fight is: when is a pile of parts one device? Support says a phone that's come back with a third-party screen and battery is still the same phone, honor the warranty. Finance says once the original parts are gone it's a different object and we owe nothing. Our e-waste filing counts units, and nobody can tell me whether a bare chassis with nothing screwed into it is a unit. Parts-pairing makes it alot worse, because there we'd like the answer to be no.

Underneath all this is the special composition question, or whatever the metaphysicians call it -- when do some things make up a further thing? Always, never, or only sometimes -- and I want a procedure that actually commits to one of those instead of hiding behind it depends. Fair warning on what I've been doing informally: my rule of thumb has been that if you can pick it up in one piece and it does something, it's a thing. I assume thats garbage. Tell me what's wrong with it.

What I need is a one-page procedure my support staff can run in about five minutes with no philosophy background. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and says no, where it has to go to legal, and where we're making a choice rather than discovering a fact. Also tell me whether counts-as-one-device is even the same question for warranty, for the e-waste filing, and for parts-pairing, or whether I'm asking three different things and pretending it's one.

Then run it on these: a phone returned with a third-party screen and battery and everything else stock; a depot bench holding a chassis and a tray of loose modules; two dead phones rebuilt into one working phone; a chassis that's the only original part left. Say which ones it settles and which it can't. And handle the obvious trap that you can add parts one at a time, so if a heap is never a device, when exactly did it become one? Don't spare me the case where the procedure hands you an answer I won't like -- I'd rather find out from you than from a customer.
