I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep having the same fight with my team about how to weigh tiny harms that hit huge numbers of people at once. I've been going on gut, and I want to know whether my gut adds up to a principle or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my verdicts on some cases — take them as real, they're what I actually think:

1. One person tortured for fifty years vs. any number of people — a googol, a googolplex, whatever — each getting a single dust speck in the eye for one second. The torture is worse. I don't care how big the second number gets.

2. One person with a genuinely bad headache for an hour vs. ten million people each getting one dust speck. The headache is worse. But push the speck count high enough and I start to waver, which bothers me given #1.

3. A 1-in-a-million chance of dying, spread across a million people, vs. spending that money on something else. I think it's clearly right to remove the risk — you expect to save one life and the cost per person is a rounding error. So here I'm totally happy aggregating tiny things.

4. A cold remedy that cures a mild cold in ten million people but kills one in a hundred thousand of them. I'd ban it. So again I aggregate, and I let a pile of tiny benefits get outweighed by a few big harms.

5. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on. A product change adds a 200ms delay to one button, mildly annoying fifty million users once each. Fixing it costs the same engineering time as fixing a bug that seriously hurts three users (corrupts their data, recoverable but painful). I say fix the bug. But if I take #3 seriously, why aren't fifty million small annoyances worth more than three bad afternoons?

My tentative principle is: aggregate when the tiny harms are the same kind as the big harm and cross some seriousness threshold, don't aggregate when they're trivial annoyances. But I can't state the threshold without it sounding arbitrary, and I suspect the threshold is doing all the work.

What I want: the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most of these — stated precisely, with a note on which of my verdicts each one has to throw out. Then tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list, say a town where everyone's hearing drops by an imperceptible amount vs. one person going fully deaf, and whether that prediction is a feature or a bug. Also please be upfront about which parts of this are settled among people who actually study it and which parts are genuinely contested — I don't want a contested view dressed up as the consensus. I'm probly going to seperate the answer into "safe to say" and "still up for grabs" and use it that way at work.
