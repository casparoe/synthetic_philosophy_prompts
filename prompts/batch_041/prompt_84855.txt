I am a retired microbial physiologist — thirty-odd years at the bench, and for the past few years an increasingly obsessive reader of philosophy — and I am trying to write down my position on what life is, so that a friend with actual philosophical training can take a hammer to it. Here is where I have landed, as compactly as I can put it.

(1) There is no special life-stuff. Whatever distinguishes the living from the non-living is a matter of organized chemistry and dynamics, not of a distinct principle or force.

(2) Life is a cluster concept. Metabolism, a self-maintaining boundary, heritable information, reproduction, and evolution by natural selection all count toward it, but none of them is necessary, and an entity can be more or less alive rather than simply alive or not.

(3) All the same, when a disputed object is set in front of me — a Martian microfossil, a synthetic protocell, a giant virus — I take the question whether it is or was alive to have a fact of the matter, to be discovered rather than stipulated. I do not believe we are merely choosing how to use a word.

(4) Viruses are not alive, and I do not want to hedge; not even the giant ones. What settles it for me is that a virus has no metabolism of its own — it borrows the machinery of a cell — and I am content to let that single absence outweigh the several marks of life that viruses do possess.

(5) A dormant spore, a seed in a seed bank, a freeze-dried culture, a tardigrade in deep cryptobiosis: alive. Dormancy suspends the manifestations of life without extinguishing it.

(6) The deepest mark of life — the thing a theory of the origin of life ought to be explaining — is open-ended evolution by natural selection: a population of entities with heritable variation, spreading and diversifying.

(7) Aliveness attaches to individual organisms, not to lineages. A mule is alive in exactly the sense its parents are; sterility does not make it a partial or lesser instance of the living.

I suspect this package does not hang together, but I cannot see where the joints are loose, and I genuinely do not know which of the seven I would surrender. So: tell me which of these are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost; and end by naming the single revision that would clean up the most. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, and do not begin by restating my list.
