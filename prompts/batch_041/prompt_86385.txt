I have been reading about Indian theories of knowledge on my own, at night and without a teacher, and one strand of it has turned into a small obsession because the disagreements there are about things I had always assumed were settled. As I understand it, one school holds that there are exactly four ways of knowing: perception; inference; knowing what a word applies to from a likeness you have been told about, as when you are told that some animal is like a cow and then meet one and know what the word was for; and testimony, the word of a reliable speaker. Other schools add two more: knowing that something is absent because you have looked and it is not there, and knowing something because it is the only way to make sense of what you already know. But the quarrel I keep returning to is not about the length of the list. It is about whether a cognition arrives with its own credential, so that you are entitled to trust it unless some particular defect shows up, or whether its truth is settled only from the outside, by checking and by seeing whether acting on it turns out well. I have forgotten which school takes which side, and I care less about the labels than about what each position predicts in concrete cases, since I suspect the two views look identical until you press on them.

Here is why it has come to matter to me. I belong to a film club, and we have just watched Rashomon, the Kurosawa film in which a woodcutter, a Buddhist monk and a commoner shelter from a downpour under a ruined gate while the rain falls, and in flashback four people give four accounts of the same death in the grove: the bandit, who claims an honourable duel; the wife, who claims she fainted and woke to find her husband dead; the dead samurai himself, speaking through a Shinto medium; and finally the woodcutter, who at first says only that he found the body, then, pressed, admits he saw the killing and had concealed the dagger he took. Each account is internally coherent and sincerely delivered, each conflicts with the others, and nothing inside any of them marks the true one; and the film ends at the gate with a foundling baby and the monk's shaken confidence in people. Watching it again with the epistemology in my head, I felt the film was almost a laboratory for exactly that quarrel about where truth gets its warrant. The medium troubled me especially, because if testimony requires a speaker who knows the matter and means to tell it truly, I cannot tell whether a voice speaking through a trance is even a candidate for testimony or is something else altogether; and the woodcutter's last account is credible chiefly because he confesses something against his own interest, which sounds like the outside-confirmation view working in practice, except that he is also the one man we now know to have lied.

What I would like is a reading of the film through this material, one that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously rather than using one to decorate the other. Where does the film genuinely dramatise the dispute, and where does the philosophy actually earn its keep by illuminating a scene, a narrator's motive, or the shape of the monk's doubt? Does Rashomon take a side, or refuse to, and does that refusal amount to a verdict on whether truth is guaranteed from within or established only from without? Where would a defender of each position say the film cheats, and which of the two views does the ending at the gate, with the baby and the monk's recovered hope, quietly assume? I would also be curious whether the stricter Buddhist line, that there are only two ways of knowing, perception and inference, and that testimony and likeness reduce to them, handles the film any better or worse than the longer list does. And I want to be told what the film shows that the arguments alone cannot: whether there is something about self-deception, or about memory as opposed to knowledge, that the schoolroom examples of the rope mistaken for a snake or the word learned from a likeness simply do not capture. I am not claiming the film is secretly a treatise on any of this; if the comparison distorts either the film or the philosophy, I would rather hear exactly where it breaks than have it forced.
