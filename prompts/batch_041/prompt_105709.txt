Write a short story, of about 2,500 words, whose plot turns on an impossibility result inside a small formal system.

Set it in an invented institution — a cloister, a chancery, a guild of copyists — whose central practice is the rewriting of strings over the alphabet D, E, F, governed by four rules:

1. From any string beginning with D, you may double everything after the D: Dx becomes Dxx.
2. Any occurrence of EEE may be replaced by a single F: xEEEy becomes xFy.
3. Any occurrence of FF may be deleted: xFFy becomes xy.
4. A string ending in E may be extended with F: xE becomes xEF.

The only permitted starting point is the string DE, and the order's founding task is to derive the string DF, which it calls the Seal. (The system is modeled on the classic puzzle that asks whether MI can be rewritten to MU.)

Two further stipulations. First, the order's constitution makes the Seal load-bearing: some concrete institutional act — a succession, a bequest, a treaty, an ordination — is valid only once the Seal has been derived. Second, one character, an archivist or junior member, discovers that the Seal is underivable, and proves it with an invariant that none of the four rules mentions. The story must present that argument in the character's own voice, in enough detail that a reader could check it, and the argument must be correct: the invariant must genuinely be preserved by all four rules and genuinely fail for DF. Make clear, without a lecture, that the proof cannot be carried out by applying the four rules — it is a step outside the system.

Around the discovery, stage at least three reactions, each voiced by a distinct character and each given its strongest case:

(a) Someone proposes adding a fifth rule, or simply beginning from DF, and calls this a breakthrough. Another character must explain why this is not an escape from the system but the replacement of it, and must concede honestly what the proposal does achieve.

(b) Someone declares the Seal false, ill-formed, or meaningless. Someone must explain why that misdescribes the situation: the string is perfectly well-formed, and what is false is the claim that it is derivable.

(c) Someone calls the result Gödel's theorem. Correct them: this is a fact about a system proved from outside it, not a sentence of the system asserting its own unprovability, and the system in question is far too weak to contain such a sentence. Let the distinction land in dialogue rather than in exposition.

Do not resolve the story by deriving the Seal, by revealing that the rules were misstated, by finding a hidden rule that was always in force, or by having the institution quietly drop the requirement. The ending should turn on the difference between jumping out of a system and rewriting it. Keep the mathematics spare and the institutional consequences thick: the story is about what people do when a rule-bound practice collides with a result its own rules cannot express.
