I have no academic training, but I have spent a long time thinking about self-binding — the arrangements a person makes to stop their future self doing something, which I have seen called Ulysses contracts — and about why a constitution is meant to be hard to change, and I have arrived at a set of commitments that I would like audited before I lean on them any further. They are these.

(1) Binding my future self is legitimate, and it is self-government rather than subjection, because the person who does the binding and the person who ends up bound are one and the same.

(2) It is wrong to override a competent adult's decision for that adult's own good, and no assurance that they will thank me later makes it right.

(3) What makes such a binding do any work is that it takes the option away at the moment it matters, not that my will has somehow been strengthened. An arrangement I could still get out of when the moment came, however painfully, would not really be binding at all.

(4) The clauses that make a constitution hard to change are the same device at the scale of a people — a people binding itself against its own future passions — and are legitimate for exactly the reason I gave in (1).

(5) Even so, I would not accept a constitution that a later generation could never alter. There has to be a way of changing it, however costly the way is made.

To illustrate (3): I have given my brother the only key to a box I do not want to open for a year, and told him to ignore me if I ask for it back.

Please tell me which of these pull against one another, which only appear to, and how serious each real conflict is. Order them by severity, say for each what would have to give, and then tell me which single revision would clear up the most.
