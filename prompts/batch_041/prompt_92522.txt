I am writing the final examination for an upper-level course on early modern metaphysics, and this year's essay question is on Leibniz's thesis that this is the best of all possible worlds. The question must not be answerable by reciting the problem of evil or the familiar reply that evils are permitted for the sake of greater goods; the crux is rather the charge of vacuity — that if 'best' means only 'the world an all-perfect being would actualise', the thesis is true by construction and constrains nothing — together with the question of what Leibniz's own criterion of maximal variety governed by the simplest laws, or of the greatest quantity of essence, would have to supply for the thesis to have real content. Build into the question a short invented case for candidates to assess: two compossible worlds, one of monotonous regularity and one of great diversity whose laws can only be stated as a long list of special cases, which Leibniz's criterion appears not to rank. Candidates should be asked to say what formal property the ordering of possible worlds must possess for a maximum to exist at all, and whether Leibniz can defend that property or merely assumes it. Candidates will have ninety minutes.

Please supply the essay question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that names five moves a first-class answer must make, the marks each is worth, and the specific errors that should cost candidates marks — distinguishing sharply between a first and a 2:1. The answer should be written in British English.
