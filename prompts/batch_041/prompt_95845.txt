You're a philosopher of biology who's actually spent time in the levels-of-selection trenches, so please think carefully here, and start by brainstorming the sub-questions you think genuinely belong before you assign any numbers — I want a slow honest number, not a confident hedge.

Here's the setup. A friend and i have been going in circles about whether the units/levels of selection debate has a fact of the matter at its bottom. Her line: once you've got the formal tools — the Price equation (which splits total evolutionary change into a selection part and a transmission part), the equivalence between contextual analysis and neighbour-modulated fitness (two different regressions that assign fitness to individual and group properties, and that come out equivalent under the right assumptions), and the MLS1/MLS2 split (selection on individuals for their group effects vs selection on groups as the things whose fitness we're tracking) — then asking which level selection is *really* acting on is like asking whether a temperature is really in Celsius or Fahrenheit. Same predictions, different ledger, no further fact. My line: in at least some real cases there's a substantive answer, and the "it's just bookkeeping" move is doing philosophical work it hasn't earned.

The proposition I want your credence on, stated as sharply as I can: for at least some actual biological cases, there is a fact of the matter — independent of our choice of trait description and bookkeeping convention — about the level at which selection is acting, over and above the fact that different conventions partition the same fitness variance differently.

And the case I keep dragging out (made up, so don't go hunting for it): a beetle. Females lay a clutch of eggs on a carcass and the larvae feed on it. Some larvae are grabby — they shove siblings off the best spots and grow fast, so grabby larvae do better than their clutch-mates. But clutches with more grabby larvae fledge fewer adults overall, because all that shoving wastes time and wrecks the carcass. Grabbyness runs through two channels at once: partly a maternal-effect gene the larvae inherit, and partly a gut microbe they pick up off the carcass that also jumps between clutches. So the same behavioural difference can be written up as a gene doing well, an individual doing well, a group doing badly, or a symbiont doing well, and which heritability matters depends on which channel you track. Is there a fact of the matter about which level selection is acting on here, or are those four descriptions equally good?
