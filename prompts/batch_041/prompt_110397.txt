I'm on the board of a small Buddhist charity — we fund cataract surgery and wells in rural Nepal — and our annual gala is in three weeks and I'm the one giving the pitch. Ten minutes, after dessert, maybe 150 people, wine involved. The problem is who's in the room.

The big donors are effective-altruism types. They want cost per surgery, they want sight-years per dollar, they'll fund whatever the numbers say and they'll stop funding whatever the numbers stop saying. Our monastic advisors, who I love and can't afford to alienate, think all of that is a bit beside the point: their line, as I understand it, is that what makes a gift count karmically is the state of mind behind it, not the outcome it produces. Which is all very well until you say it out loud in a room full of people holding spreadsheets.

What I want is a speech that doesn't pretend this tension isn't there and doesn't dissolve it with a nice line about how the two views are really the same thing. And I'd like you to use this as the spine, if you can. The argument I keep coming back to from the Buddhist side: since there's no persisting self — the person I'll be in twenty years is, in the relevant sense, a different person from me, and I don't get to count next year's me as me while that stranger over there isn't — it's arbitrary to care about my own future suffering and not a stranger's. Which is a genuinely great pitch for giving money away. Except it's an argument that there is no self, being used to get a room full of selves to write checks, and I think one sharp person in the audience noticing that would sink me.

There's also the bodhisattva thing — the figure who postpones her own enlightenment until everyone else is through — which I've never been able to decide is the most consequentialist idea in the tradition or the exact opposite of one, and I'd like the speech to gesture at it without explaining it.

Also in the room: my late co-founder's widow, who put in the seed money, and about thirty relatives of board members who came for the dinner and know nothing about Buddhism. And I do have to actually make the ask at the end.

No citations, obviously — nobody's citing anything at a gala. When you're done, give me a seperate short note, not part of the speech, on where it takes liberties with Buddhist doctrine or leans on one contested reading over another, and which of the two readings of karma you ended up betting on — plus what would make you switch.
