I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I spent several years on the technology desk before that. I'm at home in the vocabulary of information security — cryptographic hashes and collision resistance, digital signatures and certificate chains, content-addressable storage, Merkle trees, append-only transparency logs, tamper-evidence, provenance and chain of custody — and I've read enough of the philosophy of art to be genuinely bothered by the debate over fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals. I'd like you to restate that debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of information security, and then give me a residue report: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in the security framing at all. I am not asking whether I ought to use the security framing; I'm asking what it carries and what it drops.

So that we work from the same material rather than from whatever version of the debate you happen to have stored, here is the position as I understand it:

1. The puzzle case: an original work and a forgery that no test run on the objects themselves — materials, technique, surface, even the maker's habitual marks — can distinguish, because the forgery was made to defeat the best expertise available.
2. One response: if nothing perceptible separates them, and the value of a work lies in the experience it affords, then the forgery is worth exactly as much as the original. Call this the surface view.
3. The opposing response: the original carries a value that is not located in its perceptible surface. It is bound up with its history — with having been made by that person at that time, with standing in a particular causal relation to everything that came afterward, with being the very thing people travel to stand in front of. On this view a perfect copy is a different object with a different value, however identical it looks and feels.
4. The explanatory puzzle: we pay enormous sums for originals even when we know the copy is indistinguishable, which the surface view seems unable to explain and the history view seems to explain by positing a value we cannot point to in the object.
5. A distinction between arts in which the history of production is constitutive (a painting, where a copy is not the work) and arts in which it is not (a score or a novel, where any faithful copy or performance is a genuine instance). The puzzle appears to arise only for the former.
6. A distinction between the physical artifact and the work it embodies.

Now the translation and the residue. For each item above, tell me what it becomes in security terms and how much is lost in the becoming. I am especially interested in the following. Does integrity — bit-identity, tamper-evidence — map onto authenticity, meaning the right history? Note that the two come apart precisely in the perfect-forgery case, where one can be flawless while the other is absent, and tell me what that separation shows. The choice of what to hash — the content, or the artifact together with a signed record of its history — looks to me like it reproduces the surface/history disagreement; tell me whether that is a real translation or merely a pun, and what would decide it. Provenance and transparency logs look like the natural home of the history view, yet their authority bottoms out in a root of trust — a signer, a registrar, a convention — and I want to know what that implies about whether the value of originals is a fact about objects or a fact about institutions. Finally, and most of all, I want the residue that will not translate at all: anything about why the history matters, anything about value, anything about the viewer's experience, and anything the security framing is constitutionally unable to represent. Then give me your verdict on whether the residue is a defect of the security vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical debate was never as clear as it looked.
