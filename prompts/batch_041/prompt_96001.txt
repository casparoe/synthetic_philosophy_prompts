Consider a practice widespread in public project appraisal: ranking candidate projects by a single number, their expected net benefit, and funding them in descending order of that number. Picture a particular agency's written rulebook, which contains three clauses. First, every candidate project is assigned one number — its expected net benefit — obtained by multiplying the net benefit of each possible outcome by that outcome's probability and summing over the outcomes; projects are then funded in decreasing order of this number. Second, if one project yields at least as much net benefit as another in every possible outcome, and strictly more in at least one, it must be ranked above the other. Third, if one project yields exactly x dollars more than another in every possible outcome, its number must exceed the other's by exactly x. The office treats the three clauses as mutually supporting: the expected-benefit number is meant to be the common currency in which the dominance clause and the additivity clause are cashed out.

Now the office has begun to review an unfamiliar instrument: a contract that pays, in dollars, a particular function of a fair coin tossed until it first lands heads. If the first head occurs on toss n, the payout is (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n — large and positive when n is odd, large and negative when n is even. Adding the outcomes' contributions in their natural order gives the alternating harmonic series, whose sum is the natural logarithm of 2. But the office's analysts have found that the very same outcomes, summed in a different order, can be made to total any real number whatever; they can exhibit one ordering on which this contract totals more than a second contract that pays exactly one dollar more in every outcome, and another ordering on which the reverse holds. Gambles of this shape are sometimes called Pasadena games.

I want to know what philosophical commitments the rulebook presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and choice it encodes, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does it presuppose that every gamble has a determinate expected value, or only that the gambles it happens to meet do? Does it presuppose that betterness can be represented as a real-valued sum over outcomes, or is that an optional convenience? Are the three clauses jointly consistent, or does the coin contract expose a genuine contradiction rather than a merely exotic case? If the first clause is abandoned, which of the others can stand on their own, and which of them was silently carrying the weight all along? And which rival theories of rational choice — for instance, theories that traffic only in comparisons rather than numbers, or that rank options by something other than an expectation — would also license the practice as written, so that the rulebook turns out to be underdetermined among them? Finally, if the office stopped assuming that every project has a determinate expected benefit, what would have to change in how it writes its rules and reaches decisions?
