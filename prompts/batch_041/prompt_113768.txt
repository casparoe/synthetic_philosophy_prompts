Question: In the debate between Mises and Hayek, on one side, and Lange, on the other, what exactly is the economic calculation problem, and does Lange's market-socialist model solve it? In your answer, distinguish the static allocation problem from the dynamic discovery problem, explain the role of the Central Planning Board's trial-and-error price adjustments, and assess whether the model can generate the kind of knowledge that market prices convey.

Student answer: The socialist calculation debate is about whether a centrally planned economy can allocate resources as efficiently as a market. Mises argued that without private ownership of the means of production, there can be no genuine market prices for capital goods. Since capital goods are not exchanged in a market, planners have no way to compare the relative importance of different production plans, and so rational economic calculation is impossible. Hayek did not simply repeat Mises; he shifted the emphasis to knowledge. The knowledge needed for economic coordination is dispersed, tacit, and often local, and no central board can assemble it. Lange's market-socialist model is the most sophisticated reply. In Lange's model, the Central Planning Board sets prices for capital goods and consumer goods, while households and workers choose their consumption and labor supply. Firms are instructed to produce at minimum average cost and to set output where marginal cost equals price. If shortages or surpluses appear, the CPB adjusts prices by trial and error, much like a Walrasian auctioneer, until equilibrium is reached. This solves the calculation problem because the CPB does not need to know all preferences and technologies in advance; it can learn them from the market's responses. Lange also addresses incentives by allowing managers to receive bonuses for following the pricing rules. Hayek's point about tacit knowledge is a serious challenge, but Lange's trial-and-error process can in principle incorporate local information because firms and consumers respond to prices, and the CPB observes those responses. The main remaining objection is that a real CPB might be politically captured, but that is a practical problem, not a theoretical one. So Lange's model shows that socialism can be efficient in theory.
