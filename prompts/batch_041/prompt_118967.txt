I've talked myself into a position about aggregation and I want to know how exposed it is. I accept the core of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if each person's preferences over social outcomes satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and social preferences satisfy them too while respecting the Pareto principle, then any defensible social evaluation is a positive weighted sum of individual utilities. What I reject is the further step that converts this into utilitarianism. I don't think the impartial observer has to be modelled as equiprobable behind the veil, so the weights need not come out equal. I think they should be read off the strength of each person's claim, with the theorem then giving me a disciplined way to trade off claims that pull against each other.

What should count against this? I want defeaters internal to the derivation, not general complaints about utilitarianism: which axiom or step does the most damage if dropped, and what would have to be true of us -- our preferences, our moral psychology, the structure of moral claims themselves -- for dropping it to be forced rather than merely permitted? Two things I'm especially unsure about. First, does the weighting move buy me anything, or does it just relocate the hard question while borrowing the theorem's authority? Second, does a framework whose weights can be tuned to yield any verdict I antecedently favour make itself unfalsifiable, and if so, is that fatal or just the price of this kind of view? Tell me what my version predicts that plain equal-weight utilitarianism does not, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a sympathetic defender would concede and which they would dismiss.
