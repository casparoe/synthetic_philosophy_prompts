I am preparing a short research proposal for a departmental work-in-progress grant that would fund one semester of full-time writing, and I would like you to draft it. The project concerns the rule-following considerations standardly associated with Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein — the skeptical argument that no fact about a speaker, past or present, determines that they mean addition rather than some deviant function that agrees with addition on every case they have actually computed but diverges on some large number they never reach. I do not want to rehash the familiar standoff between dispositionalist, primitivist, and communitarian replies, which has been rehearsed to the point of exhaustion. My half-formed thought is this: the skeptical argument is usually presented as a single challenge, but it is really two, and running them together is what makes the dialectic look more intractable than it is. One is a metaphysical question — whether there is any fact about an agent that constitutes their meaning one thing rather than another, independently of what anyone could tell. The other is an epistemic question — whether anyone, the agent included, could ever be in a position to know or justifiably assert which rule is being followed. My suspicion is that each of the standard responses quietly answers one of these while helping itself to an answer to the other, and that separating the two questions reorders which objections have force against which views and exposes a position that the fused formulation makes invisible.

Turn this into a proposal. State the central question so that it has a determinate answer rather than an air of profundity; make the case that the existing positions leave it genuinely open and say what, in the received debate, has kept the two strands fused; describe the method concretely enough that a reader can picture the semester's work — what you would do first, how you would test whether the separation is real rather than a verbal distinction, and how you would tell whether a given response answers the metaphysical question, the epistemic one, or both; say what a successful result would look like and, just as honestly, what would count as the project failing or collapsing back into the original standoff; and give a realistic scope for one semester, flagging which part you would cut first if the argument turns out to need more room. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation — refer to positions and arguments by description and I will supply the references myself.

Keep the entire proposal under 850 words, and put the precise statement of the question in the first two sentences, before any framing or motivation.
