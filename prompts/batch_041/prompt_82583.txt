I'm a novelist — fourth book, contracted, due in about a year — and the book is, more or less, about time: a man is told that all of his moments exist at once, no before and after, and he finds this consoling, which is the part I can't get past. I read Borges in my twenties and never got over it — the essay where he tries to refute time outright, the one he wrote in the forties and eventually stuck in Other Inquisitions, the story about the garden where all the branches happen, the one about the man granted a whole year inside the instant before he's shot. My closest friend is a philosopher and at dinner he keeps insisting the consolation is incoherent: if the entire life is equally real, then there's no asymmetry between dread and relief, and my protagonist has no more reason to be comforted by his past being settled than to be terrified by his future being settled. I dont think that objection lands, but I can't say why, and I've started to suspect it's because I don't know which questions in this neighbourhood are live and which were settled thirty years ago. I also notice that every device of his I actually love is about the telling rather than the thing told — time running backwards in the narration, loops, an afternoon described twice — and I've never understood the Leibniz move, the bit where two afternoons identical in every detail supposedly just are the same afternoon, if I'm even remembering it right.

So here's what I'd like. Take the role of a philosopher of time who also knows Borges's corpus backwards, and think carefully before you answer. Start by brainstorming the candidate open questions in the vicinity of his denial of time — the exegetical ones (what exactly hes claiming, whether the two arguments in that essay can be pulled apart, whether the stories and the essays even agree with each other) and the live metaphysical ones (whether my friend's asymmetry objection has any teeth, what a tenseless picture of the world owes to our attitudes toward past and future, whether a denial of time bears at all on the time of narrative versus the time of clocks). Then rank the four or five that matter most, for me specifically: I've got maybe three hours a week for the next year, no physics, and a novel to finish.

Tell me which questions are upstream of which, which would genuinely change what I write versus which are just exegetical rabbit holes, which are tractable for someone with my brain and my schedule, and what argument or result over the next year would move each one up or down the list. Skip the reading list — I want the reasoning behind the order, and I'd much rather have a ranking you're willing to defend and be argued out of than everything hedged into a tie.
