I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced seminar on the history of economic thought, and for the past two weeks our class has been working through Hume's essays on money and trade — the ones in which he insists that a nation's true wealth consists in its goods, its labour, and its productive capacity rather than in its stock of coin, and in which he describes money not as one of the wheels of trade but as the oil that lets those wheels turn smoothly. Last Thursday, during a discussion of whether a government can impoverish its own citizens merely by printing money, a classmate repeatedly invoked the phrase "money is a veil" as though it settled the matter: on his reading, since money is only a veil draped over the real economy, no quantity of it can make a country genuinely poorer, and anyone who fears inflation has simply confused the veil with the thing veiled. I am uneasy with this, but I cannot put my finger on what is wrong, and I would rather understand the slogan properly than win the argument. I would therefore like you to act as an expert in the history of economic thought and monetary theory, and to reason carefully and explicitly before you answer; if it helps, begin with a brief brainstorm of the candidate meanings before committing to any verdicts.

What I want is a menu — not a single interpretation defended at length — of the distinct, precise claims that the one-liner "money is a veil" could be making, arranged from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict attached to each. At a minimum I would like you to separate: the claim that money is neutral in the long run, so that changes in its quantity alter only nominal magnitudes; the claim that money is not itself any part of a nation's real wealth; the claim that economic analysis may be split into a real part and a nominal part that can be conducted independently of each other; the claim that money functions merely as a unit of account or numeraire, so that its own value is a matter of convention; and the claim that monetary institutions cannot explain why some economies prosper while others stagnate. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or incoherent; say what further premises each reading would need in order to license any conclusion about a country being made poorer by printing money; and, where you can, note whether the reading is genuinely Hume's own position, a later doctrine read back into him, or something he would have rejected. Where a reading holds only in the long run, or only under idealised conditions, say so plainly, and tell me which of the readings my classmate most plausibly intended and whether that reading actually supports the strong conclusion he drew from it. Please close by flagging any readings I have not thought to include, and lay the whole thing out as a numbered list that I can bring to office hours, so that I can ask my teacher precisely which reading he had in mind when he first introduced the phrase.
