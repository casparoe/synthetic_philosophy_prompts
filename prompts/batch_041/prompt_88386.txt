I chair the policy committee of a state sentencing commission, and I need the decision memo I will circulate before next week's vote on what we have been calling Amendment 9.

Some background, since the memo has to be usable by members who have not read the file. Our guidelines produce two numbers for every defendant: a Culpability Score, built from the conduct and mental state of the offence itself, and a Risk Score, built from actuarial predictors of reoffending. Judges must consider both, but the preamble to the guidelines states that the Culpability Score rests on the principle that an offender deserves punishment in proportion to her wrongdoing, and that this desert holds independently of any expected gain in deterrence, incapacitation, or rehabilitation. The amendment was prompted by a case our commission took up last spring: a defendant convicted of a serious assault, with a documented history of severe deprivation from infancy, whose Culpability Score put her near the top of the grid. At the hearing, one commissioner asked aloud whether anyone in the room could say the defendant deserved what the grid prescribed, given that she had not chosen the childhood, the temperament, or the circumstances that made her the person who acted.

A committee member has now circulated a proposal to strike the desert language from the preamble and remove the Culpability Score from the binding part of the grid, leaving the Risk Score and a set of forward-looking purposes. Her supporting memorandum credits a short argument to Galen Strawson, which she renders in four steps: (i) a person acts as she does because of the way she is; (ii) to deserve anything for her action in the basic sense, she would have to be responsible for being the way she is; (iii) no one can be responsible for being the way she is, since that would require her to have chosen her own character before she had one to choose with; (iv) therefore no one ever deserves anything for what she does. She concludes that whatever our guidelines may accomplish, they cannot honestly claim to rest on desert, and that a purely forward-looking rationale is the only defensible one.

Draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the amendment as written, reject it, or adopt it with specified conditions — and it should be a document the committee can vote on, not an essay. Beyond that, I need it to do the following work.

First, take the argument seriously enough that its friends would recognize their position, and then locate precisely where it can be resisted. In particular, distinguish three questions that the committee member's memorandum runs together: whether step (i) is a causal claim about how actions are produced or a claim about what agency amounts to; whether the desert our preamble invokes really requires the kind of ultimate self-authorship the argument demands, or only something weaker, such as the offender's action having expressed a quality of will that was answerable to reasons; and whether the argument, if sound, refutes only a libertarian picture of free will or also the compatibilist desert that most guideline drafters have actually relied on. Say which reading of the argument makes it strongest and which makes it weakest, and do not pretend the steps are obviously equivalent.

Second, address the objection that the argument proves too much. If it succeeds, what happens to the praise we extend to offenders who turn their lives around, to the earned-release and good-time programming that depends on the idea that they have earned something, to the accountability language our parole officers are trained to use, and to the ordinary moral criticism we direct at people outside the criminal justice system? Is that a cost the commission should simply accept, or evidence that the argument has overreached?

Third, confront the institutional question directly. Suppose the committee concludes that the metaphysics cannot be settled by a sentencing commission, and that reasonable people on both sides are arguing in good faith. May a public body permissibly retain desert language for its expressive or motivating function while privately doubting its metaphysical basis? If you think it may, say what distinguishes that from a noble lie, and if you think it may not, say what the commission should do instead when it cannot resolve the underlying question but must still set a grid.

Finally, give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due — at least a paragraph that a member who disagrees with your bottom line would accept as a fair statement of her case — and note the single piece of evidence or argument that would most reasonably change your recommendation.

Write it as a formal memorandum, roughly a thousand words, with a clear recommendation in the first paragraph and the reasoning following. Assume the committee has the argument as summarized above and nothing else; do not cite literature, cases, or studies, and do not tell us what anyone else has written on the subject.
