For the past several months I have been embedded, as research for a novel, in the back office of a welfare-to-work contractor in a mid-sized town: I sit in on caseload reviews, watch the paperwork that turns a missed appointment into a loss of income, and listen to the advisers talk among themselves. The sentence I hear most often, in a dozen variations, is that what people need is a hand up rather than a handout — that you teach a person to fish instead of handing them a fish. It is said warmly, by people who mean it, and it is also said in the moment before a decision that the person on the other side of the desk experiences as punishment. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether the sentiment is generous or cruel. I am asking what, precisely, someone commits themselves to when they say it, because I have to write these people as neither villains nor saints, and because my narrator is one of them.

Give me a disambiguation. Lay out, as a list, the distinct claims the line could be making, arranged from the nearly empty to the very strong, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false as it stands. I expect the readings to fall into different kinds, and I would like them sorted by kind: claims about what is empirically true of people who receive money without conditions; claims about what is owed, independently of consequences; claims about what a state may require of its members; and claims about what the metaphor itself assumes, since a hand up presupposes that there is somewhere to be lifted to, and a fish presupposes a pond, a net, and someone willing to buy fish. For each reading, say what would have to be shown for it to hold, and which concrete policies it would actually license — refusing cash in favour of training, making payment conditional on appointments attended, subsidising the wages of the low-paid instead of paying them a benefit, or none of these.

Two things I especially want. First, say which of the readings are claims about consequences and which are claims about entitlement, because in that room the two are swapped for each other mid-sentence. Second, tell me whether any of the readings is only conditionally true — true in a town where the jobs exist and pay better than the benefit plus childcare, and quietly empty in one where they do not — because that is the town I am writing about, and I need to know whether my advisers are wrong or merely irrelevant. Close by telling me which reading the sincere ones most likely mean, and which the strategic ones are leaning on, since my narrator's arc depends on her hearing the difference in her own mouth.
