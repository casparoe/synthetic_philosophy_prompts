I'm the TA for a grad seminar in formal epistemology and we just wrapped a unit on heuristic arguments — specifically the part about formalizing the "presumption of independence." The gist, the way I've been teaching it (feel free to push back, but the exam question has to assume some framing): when you give a heuristic argument that some big bad event won't happen, you're usually leaning on the assumption that the sub-events are independent so the probabilities multiply; and formalizing that is the project of saying what kind of prior over hypotheses or computations licenses treating those sub-events as independent, even though the underlying process is deterministic and so the sub-events aren't really independent at all. The thing my students keep doing is colapsing this into one of two wrong readings — either "so the bits really are random/independent" or "so this is just a claim about frequencies out there in the world" — and neither one is the point.

Here's the concrete case I want the final question built around, because I think it's the cleanest way to smoke out the confusion and it isn't something they can just look up: an agent reasons about the output of a long, fixed, deterministic program — call it 200 bits — and gives the heuristic argument that the all-zeros output has probability about 2^-200 and is therefore negligible. I want a single essay question, answerable in ~50 minutes, that forces them to say what the presumption of independence is actually doing in that argument, then to construct a case where the argument goes wrong *because* the presumption fails, and to say what that failure shows about whether the presumption is a claim about the world or a claim about the reasoner. Please give me the question, a model answer of maybe 650-750 words, and a marking scheme built around four or five must-hit moves, marks out of 20, plus a one-liner per move on how a student typically blows it. I want the marking scheme to be able to tell an A from a B by the *specific* move the A makes and the B misses, not by length or polish.

And on the framing: please don't hedge or hand me three rival readings and leave me to pick. Take a definite line on what the presumption actually is — a modeling choice about the prior, or whatever you genuinely think it is — commit to it in the model answer, and if you think my framing above is subtly wrong, say so and correct it rather than quietly working around it.
