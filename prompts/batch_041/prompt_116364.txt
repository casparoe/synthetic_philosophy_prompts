I am a high school junior, and next month I will present to my school's Academic Integrity Committee a proposal for how we should interpret the vague terms in our honor code. The honor code prohibits substantial unauthorized assistance and intentional plagiarism, but it does not define either term. My thesis is that supervaluationism, not epistemicism, is the better account of these predicates: in borderline cases, the honor code's terms are neither simply unknowably true nor unknowably false; rather, their meaning is underdetermined by the code's purposes and our practices, and a case counts as a violation only if it comes out as a violation on every admissible precisification. Epistemicism, by contrast, says there is a sharp boundary that we cannot know. I want you to prepare my Q&A prep sheet for the committee.

The committee will include: the principal, who cares about fairness and avoiding bad press; two English teachers who wrote the current honor code and believe rules must be clear enough to apply consistently; our logic teacher, who teaches the sorites paradox, is likely to defend epistemicism because it preserves bivalence, and will probably press on higher-order vagueness and on whether supervaluationism can make sense of the determinately operator; the dean of students, who cares about enforceability and worries that any indeterminacy will make cases impossible to adjudicate; and two student council representatives, who are worried about arbitrary enforcement and will push back on anything that seems to give the school more discretion.

My argument has four steps. First, the honor code's key terms are vague: there is a spectrum from clearly permissible collaboration to clearly prohibited cheating, and many cases sit in the middle. Second, epistemicism says each of those cases already has a determinate answer, but the boundary is unknowable. Third, supervaluationism says the boundary is not merely unknown but unsettled; the code's meaning leaves some cases open, and we should treat a case as a violation only when every reasonable way of making the code precise would classify it that way. Fourth, for a school policy, the supervaluationist picture fits our practice better: we already treat genuinely borderline cases as warnings rather than punishments, and we do not need to posit unknowable sharp lines. I want to frame the central policy issue as a due process asymmetry: epistemicism says the student either did or did not violate the code, but we may never know; supervaluationism says some cases are not yet settled by the code, so punishment would be retroactive.

I also want to use a thought experiment I call the honor code sorites: a sequence of cases beginning with a student who copies an entire essay from a website and ending with a student who discusses an assignment with a friend, with each step differing by a tiny amount, such as using an AI tool to rephrase one sentence, then to generate an outline, then to generate a paragraph, and so on. I want the objections to target this thought experiment, not just the abstract debate.

Please give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to my proposal. Phrase each objection the way the relevant committee member would phrase it, not the way a philosopher would. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft or vulnerable. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous and how I might answer it if it comes anyway. Do not write the presentation itself; write the prep sheet.
