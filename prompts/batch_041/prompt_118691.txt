I keep running into a pattern in arguments about the long-run consequences of an action, and I can't tell whether it has a name. The shape is always the same. Someone proposes doing something whose near-term effects are good. An objector replies that the far-off effects could be enormous and could be bad, so the near-term good cannot settle the matter. The proposer grants this but adds that those far-off effects are as likely to be good as bad, so they cancel out, and we should decide on the near-term effects. What bothers me is that the "as likely to be good as bad" is never argued for -- no one has any distribution over those outcomes; the quantity is simply unknown, and it is being quietly treated as zero. Worse, the treatment flips with the arguer's goal: when the aim is to block an action, the unknown long-run effects count as a live danger; when the aim is to permit one, they count as neutral. So I want to know whether the specific move -- assigning an unknown consequence a value of zero, and doing so asymmetrically -- has an established name. It feels close to a few ideas I have only half-absorbed (something about indifference, something about precaution, and the general complaint that we cannot total up consequences), but none seems to be quite it: indifference is about assigning equal probabilities whereas this assigns none, and the general complaint is about our inability to know rather than about the default we slip into once we have given up. If there is a name, what is it, and which nearby concept is it most often confused with? If there is not, please coin one and mark its edges -- in particular, tell me whether the asymmetry is essential to the phenomenon or merely a common way it gets deployed.
