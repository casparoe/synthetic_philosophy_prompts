I spent my career in the natural sciences and have recently begun reading moral and political philosophy, and I would like a philosophical reading of Joshua Oppenheimer's documentary The Act of Killing that focuses on what the film shows about the conditions under which a state apology for historical wrongs is possible. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.

The film concerns the Indonesian killings of 1965–66, and it follows Anwar Congo and other members of a death squad as they re-enact their killings in the style of the gangster films and musicals they grew up on. No one apologizes. The perpetrators are celebrated, and the state that would have to apologize is, in effect, the perpetrators' state. I am not interested in the familiar observation that Indonesia has never reckoned with this past. I am interested in the film's method. By handing the perpetrators a camera and a stage, Oppenheimer seems to produce an apology-substitute: a confession that is also a performance, an acknowledgment that is also a boast. I have been trying to formulate a distinction that I have not seen stated in quite this way. On one view, an apology is a transaction: it discharges a debt, restores a relation, and is complete when the offended party accepts it. On another view, an apology is a re-description: it changes the public meaning of the past so that the wrong can no longer serve as a source of legitimacy or pride. A state apology for a historical wrong is usually both, but the two can come apart. The Act of Killing looks like an experiment in what happens when the transactional side is impossible — there is no legitimate apologizer, no recognized standing for the victims, no shared moral community — and only the re-descriptive side remains. Does the film accomplish that re-description? Or does it fail because it lets the perpetrators narrate their own crimes in the genres that made them feel heroic, so that the film becomes another chapter in the mythology rather than a break with it?

I would like you to read the film through this distinction and through the philosophical literature on apology, collective responsibility, and historical injustice. Where does the film dramatize something the philosophical accounts miss? Where does it resist the reading? I am especially interested in the final scene on the rooftop, where Anwar returns to the place where he killed people and is overcome by something — nausea, remorse, or merely the body's revolt. Does that scene constitute the beginning of an apology, or does it show the limit of what an individual body can do when the political community has not asked for one? And what should we make of the film's own complicity: Oppenheimer gives the perpetrators the means of production, and the result is that they become movie stars. Is that a betrayal of the victims, or is it the only honest way to show how impunity works?

I am not looking for a summary of the film or a survey of the literature. I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical problems seriously, and that ends by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not. If my distinction between transactional and re-descriptive apology is not the right one, say so and propose a better carving. Please do not quote from the film at length, and please do not give me a bibliography.
