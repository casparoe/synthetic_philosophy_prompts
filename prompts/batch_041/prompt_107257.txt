Two colleagues in my department got into a dispute about a sentence in a disaster report. The report said that if the safety valve had been replaced, the explosion would not have occurred. One colleague, a historian, said this counterfactual is a causal claim and can only be assessed by comparing the actual world with a hypothetical world in which the valve was replaced; she added that the corresponding indicative, that if the valve was replaced the explosion did not occur, is a different kind of claim, because it concerns what actually happened and can be checked in the archive. The other colleague, a statistician, said the distinction is overstated: both conditionals are evaluated by conditional probabilities, and the only difference is which event is held fixed. The historian replied that conditional probability cannot capture the counterfactual's dependence on a specific intervention, and that the statistician's view makes the counterfactual true whenever the valve and the explosion are merely correlated in the data. The statistician answered that the historian's possible-worlds story is untestable unless it is cashed out probabilistically, and that if it is, the indicative and the counterfactual converge in every case that matters. They have asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, saying who is right on each point and what both are missing. Do not simply say they are talking past each other; identify the substantive issue about the relationship between indicative and counterfactual conditionals.
