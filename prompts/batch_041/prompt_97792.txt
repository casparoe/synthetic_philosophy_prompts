One of my grad students came out of a cognitive-science seminar and handed me this summary of pain asymbolia, and I want to check it before it hardens into a lecture I give next term:

"Pain asymbolia is the condition — usualy after a stroke or some kind of brain injury — where people just can't feel pain anymore in the normal sense: you can pinch them, burn them, whatever, and they don't flinch or recoil. What's weird is that when you ask, they'll say something like yeah there's a pain in my arm, but it doesn't bother me, I don't care about it. So what this shows is that pain is really two things stuck together — a pure sensation (how intense, and where) and a seperate emotional reaction of unpleasantness. Asymbolics have the first without the second, so it proves the badness of pain isn't part of the pain sensation at all; the sensation itself is neutral and all the awfulness lives in the emotional add-on. Which means philosophers who insist pain is intrinsically bad have got it wrong — what's bad is really the suffering about the pain, and in principle you could have pain that isn't bad at all."

Some of that sounds right to me and some of it is making me itch, but pain isn't my area and I don't want to correct him on vibes. Can you go through it sentence by sentence — what a pain researcher or a philosopher of mind would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's just false — and then tell me specifically whether the "two components" business actually gets him to the conclusion he draws about the badness of pain, or whether he's overreaching? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question.
