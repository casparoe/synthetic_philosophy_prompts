Having recently been appointed to my municipality's commemorations advisory committee, I need a principled way to gauge how much the man-of-his-time defence should temper moral criticism of figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus. Please rate each of the three on a 0–100 scale of residual culpability after the defence has been given its fullest reasonable weight, where 0 means the defence wholly neutralises culpability and 100 means it leaves culpability untouched. Before giving the numbers, disaggregate the defence into at least four separable claims: (i) factual ignorance—what the person could not have known given the state of knowledge; (ii) moral ignorance—whether the wrongness of the conduct was unavailable in the person's own moral vocabulary; (iii) structural inevitability—whether the conduct was simply what their position required, as opposed to a personal initiative beyond the norm; and (iv) available dissent—whether contemporaries already articulated the objection. State which component carries the most weight for each figure, then rank the three from the case in which the defence is strongest to the case in which it is weakest, with a brief justification for each placement. Finally, identify which of the three I should be most cautious about defending in a public statement, and say what that caution implies for how the committee should word its findings.
