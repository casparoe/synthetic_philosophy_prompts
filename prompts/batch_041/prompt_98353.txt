You are a philosopher of mind with a specialism in the philosophy and psychophysics of temporal experience, and a knack for finding the exact joint at which an argument that looks airtight is quietly hinged. Before you answer, think the problem through carefully and brainstorm: set out, in your own words, the strongest case for each of the premises below before you attack any of them. Then do not merely gesture at a flaw. Locate it, explain why the step looked innocent, and say what a defender of the argument must pay to repair it.

I built the following argument after a seminar and have since grown suspicious of it, because its conclusion is one I cannot accept. I would like you to tell me which premise or step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

Background. In the colour phi phenomenon, a dot is flashed at one position in one colour and then, a fraction of a second later, at another position in a different colour. Observers do not report two separate flashes. They report a single dot travelling from the first position to the second and — this is the striking part — they report the colour change as occurring somewhere along the middle of the path, as though the dot changed colour in transit, before the second colour could possibly have been received. The effect has relatives in other modalities: in the tactile 'rabbit' illusion, taps delivered near the wrist and then near the elbow are felt as a series of taps marching up the arm, intermediate taps included, although no taps were delivered there.

The argument:

(1) In the colour phi case, the experience of the colour change is either (a) a genuine experience occurring at the midpoint, which would require the brain to have the second colour before it arrives, and is therefore impossible; or (b) an experience assembled only after the second flash and then presented to the subject as having occurred earlier; or (c) an experience in which the first colour was genuinely experienced unchanged, with the change inserted into memory afterwards. Call these the anticipatory, the Stalinesque and the Orwellian readings. The live choice is between (b) and (c).

(2) The Stalinesque and the Orwellian readings entail exactly the same predictions about every possible observation: the same momentary reports, the same button-presses, the same later recollections, and the same readings on any instrument we could build. Whatever experiment we devise, the two readings agree on its outcome.

(3) When two hypotheses entail exactly the same predictions about every possible observation, there is no fact of the matter as to which is true.

(4) Therefore there is no fact of the matter whether the change was experienced at the midpoint or whether the earlier experience was rewritten. When a content is experienced — its place in the stream — is not an objective feature of the world.

(5) Now take any two rival hypotheses about the content of an experience that likewise entail exactly the same predictions about every possible observation: that the subject experienced one shade of red rather than a neighbouring shade they would describe, sort and act upon identically; or that a word flashed too briefly to be read carefully was experienced as 'duck' rather than as some indistinguishable token they would report as 'duck'.

(6) By (3), there is no fact of the matter in these cases either. And since for any experience one can always construct a rival content with the same observable footprint, no experience has a determinate content beyond its observable consequences.

(7) Therefore consciousness has no determinate intrinsic character: all there is to an experience is its functional and reportable profile.

(8) But (7) cuts the ground from under the argument. Reports, button-presses and recollections count as evidence about experience only if there was something it was like for the subject, with some determinate character. If (7) is true, the phenomena that set the argument going are not evidence of anything at all.

I am most reluctant to give up (3), because without something like it I do not see how the original colour phi puzzle is even a puzzle; and I am equally reluctant to accept (7). Tell me where the argument goes wrong. If you judge it valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what the bill comes to. If you judge a step invalid, name it, explain why it looked fine, and say what the repair requires. Finally, address whether anything changes if the second flash's colour is fixed by a random process only at the instant of that flash, with observers still reporting a midpoint change matching the colour so fixed.
