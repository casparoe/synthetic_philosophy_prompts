Write me a short story — like 1000 to 1500 words, dont go over — set in a made-up country that had a civil war about 40 years ago. Pick your own name for it, and don't make it a stand-in for one specific real country; I want a place that's a bit of all of them.

Quick setup, use it or tweak it: the winning side put up a big concrete memorial in the capital square in the mid-80s. It's ugly, it's triumphalist, it lists the winning side's war dead by name, and it says nothign about the mass graves out in the countryside. The peace deal came with a blanket amnesty and an unwritten deal that everyone would stop digging. Now it's the 40th anniversary and there's a five-person committee deciding what to do with the thing, and your protagonist is the chair who has to break the tie.

The four other committee members should each carry one of these arguments, and I don't want you to name the real countries they're drawn from — the reader should just feel it:

- One says: leave it exactly as it is, forever, and add a plaque. The state's job is to keep the crime in public view even when it's boring, even when the grandkids are sick of hearing about it, even when it's bad for tourism. He'll say something like a country that gets to feel finished with its past is a country that's lying.
- One says: shrink it. Not deny it — she'll be genuinely offended if you call her a denier — but she thinks permanent official mourning keeps a society frozen, that her generation keeps getting billed for a debt it didn't run up, and that other countries which went through this eventually got to be normal, and that being normal isn't a betrayal.
- One says the official story is basically correct and the memorial doesn't go far enough; he's also got the law on his side, and there's a real cost to contradicting him in public.
- One says it's about heritage: his grandfather fought for his homeland, the memorial honors him, and tearing it down or adding to it is erasing his family. Make him hard to dismiss. Don't let him be a cartoon.

Stuff I want the story to actually chew on, through arguments about concrete things — the wording on a plaque, who pays for the floodlights, whether to add a second wall with the other side's names on it — rather than little speeches:

- whether guilt can be inherited, or whether the real question is who's still benefiting
- whether forgetting can be something a society does on purpose and does well, rather than just a failure
- whether a monument owes anything to the dead at all, or only to the living
- whether ritual reverence is a substitute for actually knowing what happened

End with the chair casting the tie-breaking vote and doing something concrete. Take a side in the story's own logic — don't end on everyone has a point. And no quoting real documents or speeches, make it all up.
