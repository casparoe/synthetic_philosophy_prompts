I run compliance for a mid-sized international development NGO: about forty staff, projects in a dozen countries, and a rulebook on gifts, hospitality, and small payments that was largely inherited from donor templates rather than thought through. My office screens two or three borderline cases a week. A wrapped gift presented to visiting staff at a partner organization's launch; a supplier who is a district official's cousin; a modest payment to move a stalled permit; a contribution to a colleague's family wedding; a per diem that a local partner quietly tops up from its own funds. The amounts are usually trivial and the relational stakes are not. I have perhaps twenty minutes per case, no anthropologist on staff, and a country director who signs off on anything above a token threshold.

My unease is conceptual. Our current test asks whether someone turned a position of trust into personal advantage, and I suspect that formulation smuggles in a distinction between office and person, and between a gift and a bribe, that many of our partners do not draw. The result may be a rulebook that penalizes ordinary reciprocity while missing the arrangements that genuinely distort decisions.

Please write a decision procedure my office can actually run on a case, grounded in what the relevant scholarship establishes about gift exchange and reciprocity, patronage and clientelism, and the contrast between clientelist and programmatic political linkage, as well as the principal-agent and collective-action framings of corruption, which I suspect recommend different remedies. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning made usable by someone without the theory.

Specifically: the questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped; where the procedure stops with a no; where it hands the decision to the country director, or to a person, because the question is one of values rather than facts; how it should treat a practice that is a shared local norm an individual employee cannot unilaterally opt out of without real cost; and where I can register a this-rule-is-donor-parochialism objection without the whole framework collapsing into anything goes. Tell me which way the burden of proof should run by default, and who bears the cost when the procedure is wrong in each direction. Mark the places where I will be tempted to think I am applying a rule when I am in fact making a judgment.

I have three axes I am considering building it around: whether a transfer creates a continuing obligation or discharges a debt; whether reciprocation is specific and timed or diffuse and open-ended; and whether the transfer is public and legible or concealed. Tell me whether these carve at the joints or whether I have the wrong axes, and if the latter, what the right ones are.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the wrapped gift at the launch, the cousin-supplier, and the customary payment to a village authority that is the fastest route to a signature we are legally entitled to anyway. At least one verdict should cut against my sympathies, and I want to know which instinct the procedure is overriding and on what grounds.

Finally, say how confident you are in the conceptual claims the procedure rests on, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Please write in American English.
