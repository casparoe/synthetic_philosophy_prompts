I have been following the argument over the Ellsberg paradox for a long time, and it never resolves, and I have started to suspect that it is not the kind of argument that can. The setup is the familiar one: an urn holds ninety balls, thirty red and sixty that are either black or yellow in a proportion you are not told; you choose between a bet that pays on red and a bet that pays on black, and most people, myself included, take red; then you choose between a bet that pays on red-or-yellow and one that pays on black-or-yellow, and most people take black-or-yellow. The standard objection is that the two preferences cannot both be right, since the second choice is just the first with the same yellow event added to both sides, which is precisely the reversal the sure-thing principle forbids -- and which critics say leaves you open to a bookmaker who trades with you over time, a charge the defenders of the aversion reject. And yet the aversion does not feel like an error; it feels like the honest response to the fact that you genuinely do not know the proportion, and that any sharp number for black would be invented rather than discovered.

What I want is not a verdict on whether ambiguity aversion is rational. I have read enough of the literature to know that both answers have been sitting on the shelf next to each other for decades. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it substantive or verbal -- do the two sides mean different things by rational, one meaning coherent with your own credences and the other meaning defensible in the face of your own ignorance? Are they answering different questions under a single heading -- a descriptive question about why people behave this way, a normative question about whether the behavior is defensible, and a formal question about what rule would represent it? Is a hidden metaphysical dispute doing the work, namely whether a credence can be anything other than a single sharp number, or whether ambiguity is a feature of the situation at all rather than of the agent's information? And does the reductive move -- treating ambiguity as a second-order probability and the aversion as ordinary risk aversion over that -- settle the matter or merely rename it?

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is downstream of a disagreement about what a credence is, and that the two sides cannot argue each other out of it because each treats its own account of credence as the definition of rationality rather than as a substantive commitment that needs defending. If that is right, say what could actually move things -- a distinction, a formal result, an experiment -- and if it is wrong, tell me the real structure of the dispute and which features of the case each diagnosis turns on. I am especially interested in whether the experimental work that separates aversion-to-ambiguity from distrust-of-the-source and from the mere feeling of comparative ignorance changes the normative picture or only the descriptive one.
