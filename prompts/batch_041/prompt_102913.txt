I am in a small philosophy-of-physics reading group that keeps colliding with a slogan, and I would like to know what it actually commits its users to. The slogan is "shut up and calculate," and I have now heard it do three quite different jobs in the space of a month. In the first, a visiting speaker argued that because no interpretation of quantum mechanics yields a prediction that distinguishes it from any other, work on the measurement problem is idle; a colleague replied with the slogan, evidently meaning that we should stop asking whether the wavefunction is real and simply use the formalism to get numbers out. In the second, the slogan was turned against a student who had argued the opposite way — that since Everettian and collapse-based accounts agree on every experimental prediction, the disagreement between them is merely verbal and therefore nothing to worry about. In the third, my supervisor used it while I was struggling with the mathematics of a two-state system, and there it seemed to mean only: learn the formalism before you start philosophizing about it. I cannot tell whether these people are making one claim or several incompatible ones, and I suspect that the slogan is doing rhetorical work that none of its users could defend if pressed.

What I would like is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on which interpretation is correct. Set out the distinct precise theses that "shut up and calculate" could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical: for instance, claims about what physics is for, about what a physical theory is a theory of, about whether there is a fact of the matter regarding interpretation, about whether interpretive questions are empirical questions in disguise, about whether agreement in prediction entails agreement in content, and about who is entitled to ask which questions when. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or simply false, and — where a reading is defensible — what it would take to establish it rather than merely assert it.

Then, most importantly, tell me which of those readings each of my three encounters would need in order to license the conclusion that was actually drawn from it, and whether the slogan, on that reading, does in fact support that conclusion. I am especially interested in the second case, since there the slogan was used to make an interpretive dispute seem harmless, whereas in the first it was used to make the same dispute seem pointless — if the same words can do both, I want to know where the equivocation lies, and which reading of the slogan, if any, would make the debate between Everettians and their opponents either empirically empty or philosophically urgent.
