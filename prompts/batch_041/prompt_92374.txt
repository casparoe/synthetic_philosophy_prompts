Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about a question we never managed to state cleanly: whether a rational agent should size her risks so as to maximize the expected logarithm of her wealth — the growth-optimal, or Kelly, fraction — or whether that entire way of thinking, which my friend insists on calling geometric rationality, is a superstition dressed up in asymptotics. We could not even agree on what would count as settling it. I would rather see the disagreement staged than summarized, so I have a request for you.

Take the role of a literary novelist with a serious amateur's command of decision theory: someone who can make a rate of growth feel like a fate, and who knows that a character who lectures is a character the reader stops believing. Think carefully before you write. Begin your reply with a short planning section — the characters and the position each one embodies, the scenes, and the numbers you intend to use with the arithmetic worked out — and then the story itself. The notes may be rough; the story should not be.

Here is the premise I would like you to build on. Halloway Reach is a small fictional fishing town whose Society of the Reach is three hundred years old. By charter, every member on entering must name a single fraction of her estate, one number fixed for life, to be staked each year in the Society's annual venture. The terms are announced only on the morning of the staking, and they vary; two kinds have recurred for the century that the records can be reconciled. In the first, the staked portion comes back half again larger on one outcome and at sixty percent of itself on the other, each with probability one half. In the second, the stake doubles or is lost entirely, the favorable outcome occurring three times in five. The charter forbids any member to revise her number, so a fraction that is right for one kind of venture is wrong for the other. A young clerk has been hired to prepare the tercentenary ledger, and in reconciling it she finds that over the century the arithmetic mean estate of members has multiplied many times over while the median estate has dwindled, and that the mean is carried by a handful of improbable lineages. Then a syndicate makes a single offer: a fixed sum now for the Society's rights, an offer that amounts to one unrepeatable wager on the whole endowment and will not be made again.

I want the following from the story.

- Dramatize rather than explain. No more than a sentence or two of exposition about what a growth-optimal fraction is, and no lecture under any circumstances. The ideas should live in the charter, the ledgers, the dialogue, the weather.
- Every number that appears must be right. If a character computes an optimal fraction or a growth rate in front of the reader, the reasoning should be shown in a way a careful reader could check, and it must check out. The optimal fractions appear nowhere in the records; the characters have to derive them. In your planning notes, state the growth-optimal fraction and the resulting growth rate per round for each of the two recurring ventures, to two decimal places.
- Let the positions be embodied: the member who stakes everything, the member who stakes nothing, the member who wants one number that is defensible against whatever terms the morning brings, and the one who insists that the whole criterion is a theorem about long sequences and therefore silent about a single unrepeatable wager. Show the two ventures side by side at least once, so that the fixed number looks absurd for one of them.
- Do not resolve the dispute. The story may turn on a decision, but the reader must not be told which criterion is correct. Resist the moral.
- No real people, no citations, no named schools of thought, no appeals to what anyone has written. My friend took the side of the character who stakes everything, on the grounds that the average is what counts, and I took the other side; I do not want the story to vindicate me.

Roughly 2,500 to 4,000 words.
