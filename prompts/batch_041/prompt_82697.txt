ok so I need a plan, not an explanation. Thursday, 75 minutes, my 300-level philosophy of science seminar, twenty-two students, half of them pre-med, they've had one logic class and the reading and nothing else in the area. It's the incommensurability session and I've taught it badly three years running, so assume I know the material (I've been teaching Kuhn for fifteen years) and tell me how to teach it.

What I want, in order of how much it matters to me. First, the sequence: what has to be on the board before what, and why each piece is load-bearing for the next one. Every year I get fifteen minutes in and realize they can't even hear the claim because they're missing something I assumed was obvious, and I'd like to know what that thing is before I open my mouth. Second, the single case I build the whole session on, and what it has to have and not have. I've been toying with figure skating: they scrapped the 6.0 system after the 2002 pairs scandal and replaced it with a code of points where every element gets a base value plus a grade of execution and the performance gets broken into five separate components, so there's no second mark for artistic impression anymore and a 5.8 has nowhere to live — and I gather the old marks were ordinal anyway, ranking skaters against each other rather than measuring anything absolute, which I think makes the case even better but check me on the details. Is that the right shape (a whole vocabulary of appraisal disappearing rather than being mismeasured), or does it fall apart the second some smart kid says the new system is just better? And no Ptolemy/Copernicus, no phlogiston, no duck-rabbit unless you can actually defend it — they've met all three and can recite them, and the duck-rabbit seems to me to teach them that this is about seeing rather than about what words mean. Third, the misconceptions in the order they'll actually form them, with the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. My guesses: they collapse incommensurability into incomparability, i.e. anything goes; they think it's the same as disagreeing about values; and they walk out thinking a paradigm shift is a conversion with no reasons on either side, which is the opposite of what I want, so tell me what has to be in place before that so the rationality survives. Fourth, one question for the last ten minutes that tells me whether they've got it, with the wrong answer and the right answer spelled out, because I can never tell the difference between them being quiet and them being lost. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to do at this level and say so out loud — I'm thinking the world-changes metaphysics and the whole Kuhn/Popper soap opera, but the later narrower version has to land somehow (it's about partial translation between local vocabularies, and it explicitly isn't incomparability), and I don't want to stand there summarizing the secondary literature at them.

Finish with one line that reads Verdict: followed by the case I should build the session on, five words or less, so I can paste it into my notes.
