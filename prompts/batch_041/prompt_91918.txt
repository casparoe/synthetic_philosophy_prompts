I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I have spent the term reconstructing an argument that I believe to be sound and that I find intolerable. I want a diagnosis, not reassurance. What follows is my reconstruction of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem together with the consequences I cannot shake. I can reproduce the proof, and I believe (1) through (5), though I would not swear to every detail of (3) and (4).

(1) A voting rule is strategy-proof just in case, for every voter, submitting their sincere ranking weakly dominates every other ranking available to them, whatever the other voters submit.

(2) Suppose a rule is defined over at least three candidates; admits every profile of strict individual rankings; can elect each candidate at some profile; and always names exactly one winner.

(3) Take any such rule and any voter who can sometimes change the winner. Fix the other voters' ballots and two candidates, a and b, such that this voter's own ballot is decisive between them: one ballot of theirs yields a, another yields b. Strategy-proofness then forces the rule to give this voter whichever of a and b they sincerely rank higher. Were the rule to give the other, the voter could profit by misreporting, contrary to (1).

(4) The relation just established, whereby this voter's preference over this pair governs whenever their ballot is decisive over that pair, propagates pair by pair and voter by voter until a single voter governs every pair at every profile. The rule is dictatorial.

(5) Hence every rule satisfying (2) is either dictatorial or manipulable. There is no third possibility.

(6) Actual rules are not dictatorial. Plurality rule, the Borda count, approval voting, instant-runoff: none of them hands one voter's ranking authority over every pair.

(7) Therefore every actual rule is manipulable: for each of them there is some profile at which some voter does better by lying about their ranking than by telling the truth.

(8) A rule that can be gamed by a lying voter does not report the preferences of the electorate; it reports those preferences as filtered through whatever strategic story the voters happen to be running.

(9) So the announced winner of any actual election is an artefact of the ballots cast, and there is no fact of the matter about which candidate the electorate really prefers.

(10) So the idea that an election discovers a collective preference is a fiction, and elections should be redescribed, without loss, as non-violent procedures for settling a conflict.

I would rather keep (1) and (6) than (9). I have heard the standard escapes named — restricting the domain of admissible rankings, permitting lotteries, weakening strategy-proofness to equilibrium or to Bayesian incentive compatibility, and dropping the requirement that every candidate be electable at some profile — and I cannot tell whether those are repairs to the proof, repairs to (6), or something else entirely.

Here is what I am asking for.

Name the step that does load-bearing work the argument needs and does not have, and state precisely what goes wrong there: an equivocation between two senses of a term, a scope or quantifier shift, a slide from a property of a mechanism to a claim about what exists, a condition silently dropped between steps, or nothing at all. If more than one step is defective, name the one that carries the weight and explain why the others are secondary.

Second, the prices. For each repair you consider — the four I listed and any you would add — say what it buys, what it costs, and who would accept that price.

Third, the residue. Tell me what the argument is entitled to conclude once repaired, stated so that it no longer proves too much, and which of (1) through (10) survive.
