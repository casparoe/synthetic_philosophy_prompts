I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, working at the intersection of formal epistemology and the philosophy of logic, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on logic and cognition. I would like a Q&A preparation sheet for the discussion period.

The paper defends the following position. The so-called problem of logical omniscience is not a defect of the idealization that epistemic logic makes; it is an artifact of an equivocation. We use the phrase having beliefs for two quite different things: (a) having a determinate doxastic state, understood as a body of sentences closed under the consequence relation the agent accepts; and (b) being disposed, in real time, to assent to or assert particular sentences. My central claim is that every alleged counterexample to omniscience is either a case of closure-absence (the agent has no determinate doxastic state of kind (a) at all, as when it holds no position on the sentences in question) or a case of relation-shift (what is called a consequence is a consequence only in the theorist's logic, not in the agent's), and that there is no third kind of case. My constructive claim is that we should relocate rather than relax the idealization: a determinate doxastic state is always omniscient relative to some logic, so the substantive questions are which logic an agent is answerable to and what makes accepting a logic rational. A corollary I draw is that an agent cannot be criticized for failing to draw a consequence that its own logic licenses, and hence that the normativity of logic cannot rest on closure obligations.

The audience is not homogeneous, and I want the objections I will actually get, not the ones I would get from a room of philosophical logicians. Present will be two epistemic logicians who work on awareness-style and impossible-worlds semantics, who will press soundness and completeness and who think the remedy is a better semantics; two Bayesian cognitive scientists who build resource-bounded models of reasoning and think belief should be graded rather than represented as a set of sentences; and one philosopher of mind with interpretivist sympathies, for whom any talk of a determinate doxastic state is a theoretical posit that stands in need of defense. A couple of graduate students will also ask the flat-footed question.

Please give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do if I could not answer them, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should concede rather than fight, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session. Assume I have no access to the literature in the room, so keep everything self-contained and give me the shape of any technical result rather than a citation. Include, at the end, the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
