I've been arguing about the non-identity problem in a forum thread for three weeks now and I want to try running the usual result backwards, so bear with me — I need you to actually build the case, not just talk around it.

The standard setup: an act looks obviously wrong, but the person affected can't complain, because if you'd done otherwise they wouldn't exist, and you can't be worse off than never having existed. Fine, I know it. I want the mirror image. I want a case where a person-affecting principle — the kind thats supposed to be the humane, anti-aggregation one — *condemns* something nobody thinks is wrong at all, and where the condemnation falls out of the same machinery that makes the standard case work.

Specs. One paragraph, ordinary life: no sci-fi, no medicine, no illness or disability angle, no resource depletion, no climate, no third parties who get hurt. Same number of people in both branches — exactly one child either way, just not the same child. The kid in the actual branch has a life that's plainly good and clearly worth living; in the branch not taken, the kid would have been somewhat better off. Keep that gap smallish — I don't want the case doing its work by making one life bad. And the difference should come from something the parents can't be blamed for: timing, luck, the way the world happened to be, not from them knowingly rolling the dice on a bad outcome.

Then do the actual work. Give me the verdict each of these gives and why: (1) the narrow person-affecting view, where "worse off" only ever compares a person to alternatives *for that same person*; (2) the version where you compare the actual kid to whoever would have existed instead — the "you had better luck available and didn't take it" principle; (3) plain totalism. My guess is 1 permits and 2 and 3 both condemn, which is the funny-scary part, because a view built to avoid aggregation ends up extensionally the same as totalism whenever the number of people is held fixed. Tell me if that's right, and say which feature of the case is doing the work. Then: if you pull that feature out, does the case collapse back into the ordinary non-identity puzzle? I want to know whether the mirror case is really a different case or just the same case with the arrow flipped.

Then tell me how someone who likes person-affecting views will attack it. My guess is they say the principle in (2) was never person-affecting to begin with and I've just dressed totalism up in a trench coat — but give me the strongest version of that objection, not the version I already thought of. And: does the case need the parents to have known the alternative was better? If it only condemns when they knew, does the whole thing still work, or does it degrade into a boring point about negligence?

And please, if this can't be built — if every version of the case smuggles in aggregation, or if (2) turns out to be a straw man nobody actually holds — just tell me that straight instead of producing a case anyway. I'd rather find out the ask is confused than get a tidy answer to a bad question.
