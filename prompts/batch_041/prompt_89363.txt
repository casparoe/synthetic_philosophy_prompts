I keep running into the experiments in which a person's brain shows a slow electrical build-up a fraction of a second before the person reports having decided to move, and I want to know what a serious theory of conscious intention would actually have to do before I'd count it as adequate. Please give me the requirements list. I mean the specific cases it has to get right (a sudden urge I resist, a decision reached only after long deliberation, a hand that moves because an electrode triggered the muscle rather than because I chose to move it, and the trials where the build-up appears but no movement ever follows), the distinctions it must not collapse (between an urge and a decision, between forming an intention and executing one, between "my brain produced it" and "I did it"), and any laboratory results it cannot simply contradict. For each requirement, tell me the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are genuine demands and which are assumptions smuggled in by one camp. I most want to know whether some pair of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable -- if preserving any real causal role for conscious choice cannot be squared with honoring the timing data, say so plainly, since that would be the most interesting outcome. Skip the scorecard of particular theories; I want the specification first.
