Next week I'm presenting to a room I'm a bit nervous about: two anaesthetists from a pain clinic, a palliative care consultant, a clinical psychologist who runs a chronic pain programme, and two philosophers of mind, one of whom is a representationalist about pain and wont let that go. My thesis is that pain asymbolia shows the hurtfulness of pain isn't intrinsic to the sensation. Background for whoever needs it: asymbolia is rare, usually turns up after brain injury or psychosurgery, and the patients say the stimulus hurts, can say where it is and roughly how intense it is, but it doesn't bother them at all -- they aren't distressed by it. So my claim is that the sensory-discriminative side and the affective-motivational side genuinely come apart, that the badness rides on a separable affective state, and that suffering rather than intensity is the right target of analgesia.
