I am a novelist at work on a book that follows two versions of the same river town across a century, and I keep colliding with a question I cannot settle on my own. Historians and economists speak of path dependence — the thought that early, small, even arbitrary events can fix an outcome that later becomes effectively irreversible — and I want to press on whether "locked in" names anything real or is merely the shape hindsight gives to a story. Here is the distinction I have been turning over and cannot get to do the work I want: some outcomes are sticky because reversing them would be costly (increasing returns, network effects, sunk investment), while others are sticky because the alternatives were not made expensive but destroyed — the people, skills, and institutions that could have sustained a different outcome no longer exist. Call the first friction and the second foreclosure, if those labels help. My question is whether that difference is a real difference in how history works or only a difference in how it feels from inside. Write me an essay that takes a position: are there outcomes that genuinely would not have arrived anyway, and if so, does the friction/foreclosure distinction earn its keep — or does the whole vocabulary of lock-in collapse into a truism once you ask what would have happened instead? Weigh the strongest reasons on both sides, but do not end on the fence; I want a verdict and the grounds for it. If my framing is confused — if the distinction is one without a difference, or if the question as I have posed it has no determinate answer — say so plainly rather than humoring me. Give it to me as exactly five numbered sections and nothing else: no preamble, no conclusion, each section one move in the argument, a few sentences apiece.
