My school holds an independent research symposium each spring, and proposals are capped at one page, so this is the document I have to get right. I would like to write mine on a puzzle about herding that I keep running into and cannot find posed the way I want to pose it.

The standard story about information cascades runs roughly like this: when people decide in sequence and each can see what earlier people chose but not what those people privately knew, a short run of early choices can lead everyone after them to set aside their own information and follow the group, and the belief the group lands on can be arbitrary, fragile, and flatly wrong. I understand the mechanism well enough. What I cannot work out is what the mechanism is supposed to show, because it seems to me to run together three things that can come apart: whether the group's procedure actually tracked the truth, whether the individuals inside it were justified in believing what they believed, and whether the belief they arrived at happened to be correct. My half-formed idea is that most of the alarming conclusions drawn from cascades — that crowds are unreliable, that herding is a failure of rationality, that going along with the majority is epistemically spineless — trade on sliding between these three. The case I find genuinely interesting is the one where nothing is wrong with any individual's reasoning, no one is insincere, and yet no one in the group knows anything. I would like to know whether that is a defect in the people, a defect in the situation, or neither. A second thing I want to fold in: cascades differ in whether they are self-correcting once someone with a strong enough private signal arrives, and I suspect that whether we should say the crowd was reliable depends on which kind we are looking at, and on whether we are counting correct outcomes across many possible worlds or asking whether this group's process tracked the truth in the actual one.

Please turn this into the one-page proposal I will submit. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing treatments leave it open — as far as I can tell, both the formal models and the popular retellings of them simply assume that being right by accident is not the same as knowing, without saying what is missing; set out the approach, which for me would be reconstructing the smallest possible cascade by hand and enumerating the cases, sorting them by which of the three properties they have, and then defending a criterion for when a herder who followed the group was nonetheless unjustified; say what a finished result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest scope for the eight to ten weeks and roughly four thousand words I actually have. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review — I will add references myself, so please talk about positions and arguments rather than citing works.

I want a committed proposal rather than a survey: commit to one formulation of the question, take a definite view about which of the distinctions is doing the real work and which is decoration, and say which answer you would defend and why, instead of laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Keep the whole thing to at most seven hundred words.
