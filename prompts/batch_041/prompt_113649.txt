I have been reading Machiavelli's The Prince on my own — no coursework behind this, just a copy I picked up and a habit of underlining — and chapter 25 has stopped me cold. I can follow the river simile well enough, but the arithmetic and the counsel seem to pull in opposite directions, and I would like help working out what the chapter is actually claiming rather than what it is usually summarized as claiming. Here is the stretch I keep returning to: "It is not unknown to me how many men have had, and still have, the opinion that the affairs of the world are in such wise governed by fortune and by God that men with their wisdom cannot direct them and that no one can even help them ... Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her." Later he adds that "a prince may be seen happy to-day and ruined to-morrow without having shown any change of disposition or character," that "he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times," and that a man is seldom "sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it."

My confusion is roughly this. He opens with what sounds like a fixed division of labour — fortune arbitrates one half of our actions and leaves us the other — but the river image seems to say something different: fortune shows her power precisely where valour has not prepared to resist her, which makes her share of any given outcome depend on how much preparation is already in place, not on a standing half-and-half ratio. If the second reading is right, the first line begins to look less like a thesis than a concession to the people he is arguing with, and I want to know which way to take it. The difficulty deepens further on, where success is located in a fit between a man's methods and the times, while the man himself is said to be unable to change his methods, since nature inclines him one way and past prosperity makes him unwilling to abandon what has worked. That seems to leave very little of the half we are supposedly directing: if my disposition is fixed and success turns on a correspondence between that disposition and circumstances I do not control, then prudence starts to look less like a cause of success than a lucky congruence. And when he says he holds his view "not to extinguish our free will," I cannot tell whether that is a serious claim about human agency, a defensive nod toward his readers' theology, or a quiet joke at the expense of those who say fortune governs everything.

What I would like is a patient reading that keeps these three strands in view at once — the half-and-half formula, the river and its dykes, and the spirit of the times — and tells me whether they can be made consistent or whether the chapter is deliberately holding them in tension. Specifically: does "fortune" name a single thing across the chapter, or does it shift between an unpredictable external shock, a general background of contingency, and the fit between a temperament and a moment? Is the "other half" something an individual prince can direct by an act of will, or does Machiavelli think it can only be secured collectively and in advance — by fortresses, alliances, institutions, the sort of thing a republic can manage and a single man cannot? And how should he answer someone who objects that his advice to build dykes while the weather is fair is itself available only to the man fortune has already spared, so that the counsel quietly presupposes the very luck it is meant to tame? I do not need a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the argument of the chapter made as clear as it can be, with the places where it genuinely strains marked rather than smoothed over.
