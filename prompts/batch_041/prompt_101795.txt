I have been locked in a long-running argument with a friend in our philosophy of mind reading group about Dennett's quining qualia essay, and we have reached the point where we need numbers rather than more hand-waving. The proposition I want your credence on is this: qualia, understood in Dennett's restrictive sense — as properties of experience that are simultaneously ineffable, intrinsic or non-relational, private, and directly or incorrigibly apprehensible — are an incoherent posit, meaning the concept cannot be made sense of even in principle and should be eliminated rather than merely found to be uninstantiated. My friend puts this at about 0.85; I am at about 0.4. I want your number, but more than the number I want the decomposition: the sub-questions the credence rests on, a rough probability for each, how they combine, and which one the final figure is most sensitive to. Please do not answer by saying it depends on your theory of introspection or on what you mean by qualia; I am asking you to take a stand on the concept as specified and then show your work.

The sub-questions I would like separated out are these. First, whether introspection could ever deliver a property genuinely independent of all the subject's dispositions to discriminate, judge, and react — the pressure the Chase and Sanborn coffee case is meant to apply, where two tasters both report that their relation to coffee has changed but cannot, from the inside, say whether the taste itself changed or only their liking of it. Second, whether that case establishes indistinguishability in principle or merely that these two subjects are poor introspectors, and how much rides on that difference. Third, whether a successful argument here shows the concept is incoherent or only empty — a coherent but necessarily uninstantiated predicate — and how much of your number turns on that distinction. Fourth, whether the incoherence, if it exists, is local to the four-property package or spreads to any notion of phenomenal character, including representationalist and higher-order theories that retain a deflated notion of what it is like. Fifth, whether some coherent reconstruction — qualia as relational, dispositional, or representation-dependent — preserves enough of the original notion to count as partial vindication rather than elimination. And sixth, a variant we invented and could not resolve: a subject who reports no change in the taste of coffee and no change in liking it, but whose detection threshold for a bitter additive has shifted. Does the four-property notion even have the resources to say whether his quale changed, and if it cannot, is that a defect of the notion or of the case?

For each sub-question give a probability and one line of justification, then state how you combine them into the final credence, name the sub-question you are least confident in and what argument or piece of evidence would move your number most, and finish with the number itself in the machine-readable form \answer{0.XX} on the last line. No citations, no reading list, and no survey of who has said what — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
