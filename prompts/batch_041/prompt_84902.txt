I've been reading about Mill's day job at the India House — he went into the Examiners' Office there as a teenager and ended up running it right before the Company was wound up after the 1857 rebellion — and I keep running into a defense of colonial rule that goes like this: governing a backward people is like raising a child. They're in what Mill called their nonage, not yet capable of deciding for themselves, so a guardian has to decide for them; and just as a parent's authority is legitimate only because it aims at the child's eventual maturity and independence, so despotism over a backward people is legitimate only if it aims at their improvement and their eventual self-government. Mill says something very close to this in On Liberty, and he spent most of his working life administering India, so it isn't just a stray line. My cousin thinks this settles it — the analogy shows colonial paternalism isn't exploitation, it's just tutelage, and the only real question is whether it's done well.

I'm not so sure the analogy can carry that weight, but I also don't want to just wave paternalism-bad at it. Can you audit it properly? Lay out the mapping — who's the parent, who's the child, what plays the role of immaturity, what the goal state is, who's accountable to whom — then say which parts of the analogy actually hold up for Mill's purposes and which break, which features of real parenting the analogy quietly imports that colonial rule lacks (or the reverse), and finally whether the tutelage conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
