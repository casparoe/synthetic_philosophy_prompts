You are an expert philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of comparative neurobiology, and you are good at designing cases that do exactly one job. I want one constructed thought experiment, built to the specifications below, together with a demonstration that it meets them.

What the case is for. I am writing a section on multiple realizability — the claim that a single mental state type can be realized by physically very different systems — which is standardly used as an argument that mental states are individuated by their causal or functional role rather than by their physical make-up. The objection I want to test is that the functional description you need in order to cover two very different systems is pitched so coarsely that it will also cover systems that plainly lack the state, and that if you pitch it finely enough to exclude those systems it no longer covers both of the originals. I want a single case that puts this trade-off under maximum pressure, with everything except the grain of the description held fixed.

The case is to contain three systems, A, B and C.

Specifications:

1. Choose one mental state with a reasonably agreed functional profile — a sensory state, a motivational state such as thirst or hunger, or pain. Do not choose phenomenal consciousness as such, since that is what the underlying dispute is about.
2. A and B are both in that state, and are as physically unlike each other as you can make them while keeping the case realistic.
3. C is a control. It must match A or B on whichever dimension is most tempting to treat as the mark of the state — input-output profile, or gross architecture — and it must plainly lack the state.
4. A and B must differ in at least three independent physical respects: the medium that carries the state, the architecture that carries it, and the dynamics or timescale of the state. In addition, A and B must not have inherited the mechanism from a common ancestor; it must have arisen in each of them independently, so that their sharing the state cannot be explained by shared history.
5. Realism constraint: no silicon brains, no nations or crowds wired together, no angels, no aliens, no fictional species, no science-fictional devices. All three systems must be ones we have good reason to think exist or could exist, and at least two of the three must be actual living organisms.
6. No stipulated verdicts. The fact that C lacks the state must follow from structural facts you state about C — for instance, that its internal states are not caused by the relevant inputs, that they play no part in generating its behaviour, or that its behaviour is produced by reading off a finite table. You may not simply assert that C is not in the state.
7. Everything except the grain of the functional description is to be held fixed. Do not vary the state, the systems, or the inputs between parts of your answer; only the description varies.

Deliverables:

(a) The case, in one paragraph.
(b) A single statement of the functional role, with the level of description made explicit, plus one sentence on why this statement is more than a description of behaviour.
(c) Verdicts and reasoning: what the functional-role view says about A, B and C; what the view that mental states are identical with physical state types says; what the view that only systems of the right biological kind can be in mental states says; and what a view that individuates the state by its typical causes and effects says. Where two of these agree, say why.
(d) The grain check. Coarsen the functional description until it covers A and B comfortably, and say what it now wrongly covers. Then fine it until it excludes C, and say which of A and B it now wrongly excludes. State plainly whether the trade-off is a genuine feature of the case or an artefact of how you wrote the description.
(e) The load-bearing feature: name the one feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed or reversed.
(f) The reply. Give the strongest way for a defender of the functional-role view to attack the case — in particular, whether they can say that the case shows only that you chose the wrong level of description rather than anything about the view — and say whether that reply succeeds.

Before writing the case, brainstorm briefly: list two or three candidate states and two or three candidate pairs of realizers, note for each how sharp the grain trade-off would be, and pick the sharpest. Show that brainstorming as a short list, then write the answer up in full. Think carefully at each step; the value of this answer lies in the case being genuinely constructed rather than stipulated. I do not need references, reading lists or citations — build the case from scratch.
