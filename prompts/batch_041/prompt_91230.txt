Ok so I've been lurking on the rationalist blogs for like a decade and I have read definately too many free will threads, and I'm sick of the same determinism-vs-indeterminism rerun that everyone writes. So I cooked up a case and I want a dialogue between two people who are both actually good at this, neither one a strawman.

Here's the case. Mira is 30. When she was two, her parents enrolled her in a program run by an outfit that we stipulate is genuinely benevolent, and the program installed in her a strong disposition toward honesty and toward helping people in distress. It is not a compulsion: she can lie, sometimes does, and she can walk past someone who needs help. But acting against it feels to her like betraying who she is, and she is fully responsive to reasons for and against doing these things -- give her a good enough reason and she'll act on it, and she deliberates like anyone else. After years of therapy she has looked hard at the implant and whole-heartedly endorses it; she says it's the best thing about her. She never chose it, and as a toddler she couldn't have.

The two characters:

A is a compatibilist. She thinks Mira is morally responsible in the ordinary sense for what she does -- take two acts, lying to protect a friend, and going way out of her way to help a stranger. A's line is roughly that responsibility tracks how the will actually operates and whether the agent endorses her own springs of action, not where those springs ultimately came from.

B is an incompatibilist. She thinks Mira is not responsible (or not ultimately responsible -- make her precise about which, and about why that qualifier does or doesn't matter) because Mira's springs of action were installed by an outside agency she had no say over, and she holds that this is a difference in kind from ordinary upbringing, not just a difference of degree. She needs a principled line, not just "manipulation is bad."

I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues. Have A press that B's principle proves too much, since none of us picked our genes or our childhood. Have B come back with an actual distinction instead of hand-waving -- something about the history of the process versus the mechanism it produces, or about whether the cause worked by giving her reasons versus bypassing her agency -- and then have A try to absorb that distinction too and see if she can. Let them fight about whether the designer's benevolent intentions matter, whether Mira's endorsement of the implant does any real work or is just the designer's plan running smoothly, and whether anything changes if we swap the implant for an equally strong disposition she happened to get from a blow to the head. Don't let either one win by fiat, and don't let them talk past each other.
