So for philosophy club we've been doing these little thought experiments and last week we did the one with the chairman of the board. His VP comes to him with a new program that'll make a ton of money but will also wreck the environment, and the chairman says he doesn't care about the environment at all, he just wants the profit, and then they run the program and sure enough the environment gets wrecked. Everybody in the club said he harmed the environment on purpose. Then our teacher flipped it around — same program, but now it *helps* the environment and the chairman still says he doesn't care — and suddenly everybody says he didn't do it on purpose, he just didn't care either way. She said this is a famous result called the Knobe effect and that it proves our moral judgments basically run the show, that we decide whether somebody did a thing on purpose based on whether we think they're a jerk, and only afterwards dress it up as a judgment about what they "really intended."

Here's what's bugging me though. If that's true, isn't the whole idea of intent in the law kind of a joke? In a murder trial everything turns on whether the guy meant to do it, and if our judgments about "meant to" are really just our moral gut reactions wearing a costume, then mens rea is basically the Knobe effect in a wig, and every jury is just deciding "was this a bad thing" and then back-filling the intent to match. My friend says that means we should scrap the intent requirement entirely and convict people based on outcomes, wich sounds insane to me but I honestly couldn't tell her why she's wrong. So, is she right? Is the Knobe effect proof that the concept of doing something on purpose is just incoherent, or that we're all hopelessly irrational about it? And does it actually have anything to do with how courts think about intent, or am I mixing up two totally different things? I have to hand something in for class next week and I don't want to turn in a mess, so any help sorting out what the experiment actually shows (and what it definately doesn't) would be great.
