I'm a trustee at a small grant-making foundation and somehow I've ended up as the person who has to write the paper for our grants committee, and I'd rather not wing it in front of people who'll notice. Can you help me draft it?

Quick context, then the ask. We hand out about £2m a year in small grants. This is the last allocation before a strategy review in eighteen months, and there are only two proposals left standing in the current cycle, both asking for roughly the same money — call it £400k each — so it's genuinely one or the other, or a split.

Proposal A is unglamorous but solid: a well-evaluated public-health programme with a track record, decent cost-effectiveness numbers, no surprises. Proposal B is from a small research group. They say their approach has, on their own estimate, at best about a one-in-a-billion chance of producing an outcome they call 'civilisational' in scale — something on the order of averting a catastrophe that would otherwise set humanity back generations, or a cure for a disease affecting billions — and that once you multiply it out the expected value is thousands of times larger than A's. They're upfront that the estimate is soft and that they can't show you the mechanism working yet.

One trustee has seized on this: the maths is the maths, she says, and we'd be negligent to fund the bed nets when B dominates in expectation. Another says this is just being mugged with extra steps — that if we take it seriously we'd have to fund every crank who turns up with a big number and a tiny probability, and we'd never fund the sure thing again. Neither of them will move and I have to write something both can live with.

So the deliverable is a decision memo I can circulate before the meeting: recommend fund A, fund B, or split, give me a concrete course of action with a time frame, and give me reasons the losing side could actually respond to rather than just asserting 'expected value' on one hand or 'we can't be mugged' on the other. Please engage the strongest version of each position properly, not a strawman of the one you end up disagreeing with.

Where I especially want your judgement: is there a principled way to handle cases like this that isn't just a fudge — a floor on the probabilities we'll count, a ceiling on the utility we're willing to plug in, a requirement that the probability be independently checkable by someone other than the applicant, treating the sure modest good as lexically prior to the speculative huge one — or are all of those just elaborate ways of refusing to do the arithmetic? If a rule like that is defensible, I'd like it stated precisely enough that next cycle's committee can apply it to a different proposal without re-litigating the whole thing.

You're a philosopher who's worked on decision theory under uncertainty — expected value with unbounded or astronomical payoffs, the various responses to mugging-style cases — so write from that. Think it through before you commit; start by laying out the arguments on both sides as strongly as you can, and only then write the memo. Use British English.

And be straight with me about how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the one point you're least sure about. I'd much rather know where the soft ground is than get a confident-sounding memo that hides it.
