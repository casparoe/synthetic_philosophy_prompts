I am a doctoral candidate in comparative political economy, and I want to develop the divergence between Haiti and the Dominican Republic — the two states that divide the island of Hispaniola — as a hard case for the institutions theory of development. Take that theory in its strong form: the claim that a society's long-run economic trajectory is fixed by whether the colonial order installed inclusive or extractive rules, and that this one variable does most of the explanatory work. I want the case run in both directions, with the mapping done first and done honestly.

Begin with the mapping. Name the institutions the theory needs on each side of the border, say roughly when each was put in place, and trace the mechanism by which it is supposed to have produced the present gap — the French plantation regime and the indemnity France extracted in exchange for recognizing Haitian independence on one side, the Spanish colonial legacy and the cattle-and-frontier economy on the other. Be explicit about which of these facts the theory is treating as evidence and which it is merely assuming, and do not let the labels inclusive and extractive do work that a concrete account of property rights, labor systems, and legal enforcement could not do on its own.

Then tell me where the lens breaks, and do not spare the theory. The border gives me variation, but not variation I can assign: the two sides differ in colonizer, in terrain and soil, and in the demography of settlement all at once, so on a single case the three rival hypotheses — institutions, geography, and culture — are observationally equivalent, each fittable to the same outcome after the fact. Press on the endogeneity of institutions directly. If the rules the colonizers left are themselves downstream of the disease environment, the terrain, the crop mix, and the settlers' prior beliefs, what is left for institutions to explain that geography or culture would not explain equally well? Say whether Hispaniola is better read as evidence for the theory, as a tautology dressed as an explanation, or as a case the theory never really anticipated, and whether an identification strategy built on historical instruments escapes the circularity or merely relocates it. Finish by describing what a disciplined causal claim about Hispaniola could actually look like, and what such a claim would have to leave unsaid.
