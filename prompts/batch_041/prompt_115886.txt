I am a high school student in my school's philosophy club, and I am trying to write a paper on whether the idea of sphexishness is a threat to free will. The illustration I have been working with is the digger wasp. After paralyzing a cricket, the wasp drags it to the mouth of its burrow and leaves it outside; it then goes in to inspect the burrow, and only when it comes back out does it haul the cricket inside. If, while the wasp is inside, you nudge the cricket a few inches away, the wasp will retrieve it, bring it back to the entrance, and then inspect the burrow all over again, and the whole sequence can be repeated many times without the wasp ever simply carrying the cricket in. The wasp's behavior is the standard example of a fixed routine running to completion, and it is where the term sphexishness comes from. I understand the story may be a simplification as biology, and I do not want to rest anything on the entomology; I am using it only as the picture of what a blind routine is.

A friend and I have built an argument that seems to show that we are all sphexish. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot find the error. It runs as follows.

(1) An action is free only if it is not dictated by a fixed, blind routine. The wasp, which cannot deviate from its sequence, is the clearest possible case of unfreedom.
(2) The wasp's behavior is fixed in exactly that way: when the sequence is interrupted, it simply restarts, and the wasp's apparent goal does not survive the interruption, which shows that the goal is not represented anywhere in the wasp.
(3) Any behavior that looks flexible can nevertheless be redescribed as a routine with more branches, a routine that takes in more features of the situation and produces the corresponding response.
(4) A routine with enough branches can reproduce any finite record of behavior, in the way that a large enough table of inputs and outputs can reproduce any pattern you have gathered.
(5) Therefore, for any stretch of behavior whatever, there exists a fixed routine that would produce it.
(6) Therefore no possible observation could distinguish a free action from the output of a sufficiently elaborate fixed routine.
(7) If no possible observation could distinguish two things, then nothing in the world answers to the difference between them, and the distinction is empty.
(8) Therefore there are no free actions, and the difference between the wasp and us is only a difference in the number of branches, not a difference in kind.
(9) We are all sphexish.

My friend thinks this settles the free will debate in the negative. I am unwilling to accept it, and not only because it is demeaning. If it were true, then praise and blame, regret, and the entire project of trying to become a better thinker would be empty; and, worse, my accepting the argument would itself be nothing but the firing of a routine, so I would have no reason to believe it. I would rather keep (1) and (2) -- the wasp really is unfree, and being dictated by a blind routine really is incompatible with freedom -- and I will not accept (9). That leaves everything in between, yet each of (3) through (8) looks airtight to me when I read it on its own.

Please tell me which single step I should reject, and why it looked so plausible that I missed it. Then tell me what rejecting it costs: what I have to say freedom is, and what else I have to give up, in order to block the argument. If more than one step is a candidate, I would like to know which is the cheapest to abandon and which repair leaves the most of the argument standing.
