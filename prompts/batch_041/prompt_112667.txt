I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on metaethics and the philosophy of action, and I keep running into a slogan that I now realize I do not understand in any precise way. The slogan is "you can't get an ought from an is." I first met it in a seminar on practical reason, where a commentator used it to brush aside a paper arguing that once we see how certain social arrangements produce avoidable suffering, we have all the facts we need to conclude that we ought to change them. Since then I've heard it used for at least four different jobs: to reject a definition of "good" in natural terms, to deny that any descriptive premises can logically entail a normative conclusion, to insist that facts alone cannot motivate anyone to act, and to claim that normative properties are not part of the natural world. I suspect these are not the same claim and that some are trivial while others are controversial or even false, but I can't get the distinctions straight enough to say which.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single favored interpretation, the distinct precise theses that "you can't get an ought from an is" could be expressing? I would like each reading stated in a way that makes its commitments explicit -- for example, whether it is a claim about formal validity, about meaning and analyticity, about epistemic justification, about motivation, about metaphysics, or about the pragmatics of accepting a conclusion -- and then a verdict on each: which are platitudes no one should deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false, and which are simply confused. I am especially interested in how the slogan relates to what gets called the naturalistic fallacy, since I think people around me use the two interchangeably, and in whether any of the readings can do the work my seminar commentator needed. To make that last question concrete: suppose someone argues that because a practice causes avoidable suffering at low cost, we ought to abolish it; which reading of the slogan would have to be true for that inference to be illegitimate, and does that reading survive scrutiny? It would help if you also considered potential pressure points -- thick ethical concepts that seem both descriptive and evaluative, institutional facts that appear to be normative all the way down, instrumental principles that license an "ought" from a desire plus a fact, and the difference between entailment and conversational implicature. No citations or reading list needed; I want the conceptual map and the verdicts.
