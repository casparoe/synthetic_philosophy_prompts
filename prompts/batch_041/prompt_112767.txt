Write an essay on presentism in historiography, addressing the question whether historians can escape the standards of their own time. The essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides and end with a defended verdict rather than a survey of positions. (Note that presentism here is not the metaphysical thesis that only the present exists; keep the two senses apart, and be alert to the fact that the term is used both as an accusation — present-minded distortion of the past — and as a defense — history must speak to present concerns.)

Begin by distinguishing the claims that a thesis of inescapability might be making, since they fare very differently. Consider at least: that historians inevitably select and frame the past according to present concerns, so that no narrative is neutral; that historians inevitably judge past agents by the moral and political standards current in the historian's own era; that historians are epistemically confined to the standards of evidence, argument, and explanation available in their own intellectual milieu; and that there is no trans-historical standpoint from which any set of standards could be certified as correct, so that the standards of one's own time are all anyone ever has.

Then argue both sides. For inescapability: the historian inherits a language, a conceptual vocabulary, and a set of disciplinary norms that they did not choose and cannot fully inspect from the outside, and the very act of posing a question to the past imports present categories. For escape: source criticism, attention to actors' own categories, comparative history, and the deliberate recovery of alien conceptual schemes look like techniques built precisely to loosen the present's grip, and the historian can subject their own standards to criticism, which suggests those standards are revisable rather than confining.

Distinguish, too, the standards of the historian's era from the standards of the historian's discipline; the two can come apart, and the phrase the standards of their own time tends to blur them.

Test your reasoning on a case. A historian concludes that some past institution was unjust, though almost no one at the time thought so and no criticism of it can be recovered from the period's own moral vocabulary. Is that judgment available to the historian, and on what basis? A second historian replies that it merely records the first historian's own era's standards in the guise of a finding about the past. Say who has the better of the argument, and why.

Finally, consider whether the inescapability thesis is self-undermining, since the claim that standards are always historically local is itself advanced as more than a local preference, and whether the distinction between being caused by one's time and being justified by it dissolves the problem.

Conclude with a verdict about which version of inescapability, if any, is both true and philosophically interesting, and about whether the phrase conceals more than it reveals.
