Explain what the banality of evil thesis is supposed to be, say how it relates to the idea of radical evil she had used earlier in The Origins of Totalitarianism, and assess whether the thesis makes Eichmann's guilt look smaller than it should."

And here's what the student wrote:

Arendt's subtitle is meant to puncture the image of Eichmann as a monster. Watching him in the glass booth, she saw not a sadistic fanatic but a mediocre bureaucrat who spoke in clichés and couldn't think for himself. Banality here doesn't mean the crimes were trivial — it means the man behind them was, and that evil on this scale can be done by someone who never really wills evil as such, just keeps his career moving and follows the rules. His invocation of Kant's categorical imperative is the clearest illustration: he took the rule to mean obeying the law, and for him the lawgiver was Hitler.

This is where the break with her earlier book comes in. In The Origins of Totalitarianism she had used the phrase radical evil for the men who built the totalitarian system, implying they were genuinely monstrous, that their evil had roots. In the Eichmann book she seems to give that up: evil turns out to have no depth at all, it's banal. So the two books are basically in tension, and I read the later one as her correcting the earlier one.

On responsibility, the banality thesis is often read as an excuse, and Arendt does suggest that Eichmann's guilt is somehow lessened because he was just a cog in the machine, following orders like countless others; she only insists he's guilty because he could have refused. So really the thesis is a claim about how ordinary people get drawn into atrocities — about the absence of thinking rather than the presence of hatred.
