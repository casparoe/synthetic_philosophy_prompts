I have no formal training in philosophy — I'm a software engineer by trade — and I've been trying to get a solid grip on a pattern I keep running into: situations that are bad for everyone caught up in them, that everyone involved can see are bad, and that nevertheless keep going. The pattern has a name (a multipolar trap, or a race to the bottom), and it's the thing people have in mind when they talk about Moloch. I'd like to build one case to my specifications and have you show that it actually works, rather than just gesturing at it.

Here are the specifications. The setting must be ordinary — a workplace, a professional body, a market for some mundane good, a group of neighbours — with no science fiction, no evil masterminds, and no external villain. The scenario itself should fit in one paragraph. Every participant must prefer the cooperative outcome to the status quo, must know that the others prefer it too, and must be behaving neither irrationally nor on the strength of private information the others lack; there should be no deception anywhere in the story. Assume, further, that the current participants can monitor one another perfectly and retaliate instantly, so that outright cheating is fully deterred. Up to this point the case looks like an ordinary collective-action problem: the standard analysis says these players are stuck in a prisoner's dilemma and the obvious fix is a binding agreement. And indeed, if you froze the current participants and had them write a binding agreement, they would all sign it, and none of them would break it.

Your job is to make the agreement fail anyway, and to make it fail for one specific reason: not because anyone cheats, and not because anyone's mind changes, but because the set of participants changes. Entities that hold to the agreement fare worse than entities that don't, so they shrink, exit, or are displaced, and the practice the agreement was meant to eliminate comes back in through the new arrivals, who were never party to it. The individuals keep their preferences and their discipline; the population drifts out from under them.

Once you've built it, walk me through what each of two analyses says about the case. The first treats the participants as fixed and asks where the equilibrium lies — tell me what it predicts about the agreement and why. The second treats the participants themselves as the thing being selected, and asks what survives — tell me what it predicts and why. Then take a definite stand on which of the two explains why the trap persists, and name the single feature of the case that does the explanatory work. If that feature were removed, I want to know precisely what the case would collapse into; my suspicion is something in which the binding agreement simply holds, but I'd rather you told me. Finish by arguing, as forcefully as you can, the case against your own verdict — put yourself in the shoes of someone convinced that a binding agreement among the current participants is sufficient, and give me their strongest objection.

One note on what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I want to use it to explain to a colleague why "why don't we all just agree to stop doing this" is sometimes not enough. A case that stipulates the bad outcome rather than generating it from the structure is no use to me.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered steps, no bold text, no tables — the way you'd lay it out in a long email.
