In the policy discussions I follow, one analogy does most of the argumentative work on the legalization side, and it is deployed so routinely that nobody bothers to defend it. It runs as follows. The United States tried to prohibit alcohol; the attempt failed, and failed necessarily rather than contingently, because a substance that a large fraction of the population wants cannot be kept out of circulation by law, and the effort to do so produces a black market, criminal violence, adulterated and unpredictably potent product, and the mass criminalization of otherwise unremarkable people. Repeal followed, the harms that had been predicted did not materialize, and the state acquired a regulated market and a tax base in place of an enforcement bill. Cannabis prohibition, and by extension the prohibition of other substances, is the same story at an earlier stage, and the rational response is to skip to the end.

I would like an audit of this analogy, not an argument for or against legalization. Give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what in the alcohol case is supposed to map onto the temperance movement, the bootlegger, the speakeasy, the repeal coalition, the Depression-era fiscal emergency, and the post-repeal regulatory settlement, and what in the current case is supposed to play each of those roles. Then separate the mappings that hold from the ones that are being imported from the source without warrant.

I am particularly interested in four points. First, the analogy's central mechanism is the claim that failure was necessary. Is that what the alcohol case shows, or was failure contingent on the prior legal status of alcohol, on its cultural centrality and majority use, and on the intensity and selectivity of enforcement? A prohibition imposed on a substance that was long legal and widely consumed is not obviously the same experiment as one imposed on substances that have been illegal for generations. Second, the claim that prohibition merely fails to suppress a market may understate its effects: the incentive structure of a black market rewards potency per unit of volume and weight, so prohibition may reshape demand toward concentrated or synthetic forms rather than leaving it where it was. Does the alcohol case support that mechanism or undercut it, and does either answer bear on the conclusion? Third, the actual drivers of repeal appear to have been fiscal and macroeconomic, together with a desire to undercut organized crime, rather than a considered verdict that prohibition had failed as such; and the settlement that replaced it was itself restrictive, with a mandated separation of production, distribution and retail in most American states and state-run retail monopolies in some. If the real lesson of the source case is about regulatory design rather than about the impossibility of prohibition, then the analogy has been doing different work than its users suppose. Fourth, alcohol remains among the leading causes of preventable death in the very countries that legalized it, a fact the analogy is normally taken to license ignoring.

Finally: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does it matter whether the analogy is being used predictively, to say what will happen, or normatively, to say what should be done? If the alcohol case is a poor vehicle, say what would be better — pharmaceutical regulation, gambling, or the Portuguese arrangement, which decriminalized possession while leaving supply illegal — and be explicit about which disanalogies bear on which of those conclusions and which are merely decorative.
