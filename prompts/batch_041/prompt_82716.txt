I'm in a seminar on the history of morals, and for the last session I have to circulate a one-page statement of my own position, followed by a short note on where the position might be unstable, and then eight people get to pick at one line each. I'd rather you write the note than I. The position, compressed: (1) there are facts about right and wrong that do not depend on what any individual or culture accepts, and our moral reasoning at its best is a fallible means of tracking them; (2) there has been real moral progress — the end of legal dueling, of chattel slavery, and of hereditary debt bondage are improvements rather than mere changes; (3) the arguments for those improvements were mostly available for a century before anything moved, and what moves a society is a change in what is honored and what is shameful, together with the interests and the enforcement capacity of the groups that keep the practice alive; (4) so the generation that actually drops a practice usually cannot argue the case for dropping it — the practice has simply become unthinkable, and they report that it always was; (5) whether a moral belief was caused by honor, by interest, or by wishful thinking is irrelevant to whether it is true or justified, since provenance is one question and warrant another; (6) all the same, the people who defended such a practice to the end were blameworthy for it, not merely badly positioned; and (7) the mechanism I have described is direction-neutral — the same honor dynamic can produce a revival of dueling or a new caste humiliation as easily as it can produce an abolition.

The case I keep using to test this is constructed, so don't go looking for it in the literature: a country ends hereditary debt bondage within about a decade, and the record shows that (i) the abolitionist arguments had been in print and in the legislature for eighty years, (ii) what changed was that the landlord caste lost the ability to enforce the bonds and at the same moment began to find the practice embarrassing rather than dignified, and (iii) within a single generation everyone, former landlords included, reports that the practice was always obviously monstrous. I want to call this a moral revolution and an improvement, and to say that nothing in (i)–(iii) either supports or undermines the claim that the practice was wrong.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which pairs among (1)–(7) are genuinely in tension, and how badly — rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. Which pair only looks like a tension, and why it dissolves. For each genuine tension, the cheapest revision that removes it, what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list, and which single revision would clean up the most at once. I would rather be told that one of these has to go than that they can all be reconciled with sufficient care. Finally, tell me what the package as it stands predicts about the constructed case — whether it comes out as progress, and on what grounds.
