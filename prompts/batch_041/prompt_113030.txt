I'm in a small writers' group that does deadpan institutional satire — fake internal reports, leaked memos, that sort of thing — and I want to do a piece on something that came up in our group chat and turned into a three-day argument: whether destroying habitat actually reduces the total amount of suffering in nature. The rough shape of the argument, as I understand it, is that a hectare of rainforest holds some standing population of animals, most of them small and short-lived, and the ones that do live tend to die badly — starvation, parasites, being eaten while still alive — and for the ones that breed in huge numbers, the overwhelming majority of the offspring die within days. So if you clear it and put in a soybean field or a mine or a parking lot, the number of animals alive at any given moment falls a lot, and maybe that's... good? Even though it's obviously not good for the animals who are there, and even though it destroys whatever was going well in their lives. My friends said this was a gross thought experiment and I said that's the point, so now I want to write it up.

What I want from you: a fake Environmental Impact Statement — the kind of thing a consultant submits to a planning authority or a developer's board — for a fictional project you invent (a pulp plantation in a tropical country, an open-pit mine, whatever suits), in which the consultant, with total professional calm, argues that the clearance is a net improvement in animal welfare. Around 800–900 words, using the usual sections: project description, affected area, assessment methodology, findings, mitigation and residual impacts, monitoring plan, recommendation, plus a sign-off block with a made-up name and title. The humour has to come from the reasoning being done correctly, not from the consultant being a loon: it should distinguish suffering-per-animal from total suffering, take seriously the difference between killing animals who exist and preventing animals from ever existing, admit that a cleared field is not empty of animals (mice, insects, whatever gets farmed there — so "fewer animals" isn't the same as "less life"), and stay honest that everything turns on one assumption, namely whether a wild animal's life is on balance worth living. He should never be a villain or a crank — he's just running a welfare calculus with the same earnestness as any other consultant. I'd also like a section where he considers and defuses the objection that this reasoning would license clearing every last bit of habitat, with a deflection the reader can see is doing way too much work. Don't have him sneeze, twirl a moustache, or tip the piece's hand in a winking final line — the reader should have to work out for themselves what's wrong here, and ideally someone who actually thinks about wild animal welfare should find it fair rather than cheap. No citations, no named researchers or studies, real or invented (the document can say "the welfare literature" generically); I can't check references anyway. Finish with a short plain-language public summary of the assessment, since that's the bit that should be the most disturbing.
