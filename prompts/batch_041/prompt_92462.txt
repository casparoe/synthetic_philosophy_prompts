Question (10 marks). A team of astronomers detects a previously unknown body and introduces the name "Aurex" for it, fixing the reference by pointing at the body itself -- they know which body they mean, though not yet what it is. Only later do they establish that Aurex is a comet. (a) Is "Aurex" a rigid designator? Explain what rigidity amounts to in this case. (b) Is the sentence "Aurex is a comet" necessary, contingent, or neither, and is it knowable a priori or only a posteriori? (c) Explain the difference between the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori, and say why they are not the same phenomenon. (d) Does the fact that the astronomers could not have known a priori that Aurex is a comet tell us anything about whether Aurex could have failed to be a comet?

Student's answer. (a) Yes, "Aurex" is a rigid designator. A rigid designator is an expression that picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists -- and, on the usual formulation, nothing at all in worlds where it does not exist. Because the astronomers introduced the name by pointing at the body itself, it is not equivalent to any description such as "the body the survey happened to detect first"; it is attached directly to that individual, so it goes on referring to that very individual when we consider counterfactual circumstances. A description like "the first body the survey detected," by contrast, would have picked out a different object in a world where a different body was detected first, and so it is not rigid. (b) "Aurex is a comet" is both necessary and a posteriori. It is necessary because "Aurex" is rigid: the name fastens onto that very body, and whatever properties that body actually has belong to it essentially, so being a comet is one of the things that could not have been otherwise for Aurex. There is therefore no possible world in which Aurex -- that very body -- is not a comet. It is a posteriori, since the astronomers could establish what Aurex is only by observation; no analysis of the name, or of the concept comet, would deliver the answer. (c) The necessary a posteriori is a truth that holds in every possible world but can be known only on the basis of experience; "Aurex is a comet," read as above, is an instance. The contingent a priori is its mirror image: a truth knowable independently of experience that nevertheless does not hold in every world. A familiar illustration is "the standard metre is one metre long," where the reference of the term is fixed by a convention we can know without observation, yet the resulting claim is not necessary. The two are not the same phenomenon because they concern different pairs of notions: the necessary/contingent distinction is about the modal status of the proposition, while the a priori/a posteriori distinction is about how the belief can be justified. The pairs are orthogonal, so all four combinations are available. (d) Yes -- and this is the subtle part. The astronomers arrived at their conclusion empirically, which means the evidence might have gone the other way: it might have turned out that Aurex was not a comet at all but a rocky asteroid. So there is a genuine sense in which Aurex could have failed to be a comet -- the sense in which the world could have presented us with different data. The necessity we identified in (b) is therefore best understood as the necessity of the conditional "if Aurex is a comet, then necessarily Aurex is a comet," rather than as a categorical necessity attaching to the proposition itself.
