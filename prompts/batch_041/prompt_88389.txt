I want a short story — 1200 words or so — that puts a real philosophical knot on stage instead of explaining it. Heres the knot: if what an experience feels like never causes anything, if it's just along for the ride while something else does the work, then how do we end up talking about it so reliably? The talk has to come from somewhere, and if it isn't the feeling, then whatever produces the talk is tracking the feeling closely enough to report on it — which makes you wonder what "along for the ride" was ever supposed to mean.

Setting: a near-future clinic that offers what it calls muting. The procedure leaves your experiences untouched but cuts them out of the causal chain, so your body keeps acting and your mouth keeps reporting as though nothing happened; the reports now come out of a backup routine the clinic calls the scribe. Muted patients feel normal, act normal, and insist nothing's different. The narrator is a technician whose job is to catch, from the outside, the moment a patient's reports come loose from their experiences — and who figures out that her test would fail on her too, and that nothing checkable from the inside could tell her either way.

Keep it fiction: scenes, a person, some pressure. Don't resolve it by handing the narrator a magic third-person window, and don't end with a paragraph explaining epiphenomenalism to me.
