Write a short story of roughly 1,800–2,500 words that dramatizes one question and refuses to answer it: how much authority does a person's earlier, competent self hold over the later self who must actually live the resulting life?

The story takes place in a contemporary but invented jurisdiction of your own naming, which has enacted a statute — invent its name — allowing a competent adult to register a self-binding instrument that stays in force after she loses the capacities that would let her revoke it. Three of the statute's provisions do the work, and the story should make their interaction visible rather than explained:

1. A precedence clause. While the instrument stands, decisions about the author's body, residence, daily company, and surroundings are to be made as the author would have made them at the moment of registration, not as the person now appearing before the tribunal would make them. The statute's defenders insist this is not a prediction about which choice will make the later person content; it is the earlier person's authority to govern a single life as one life.
2. A testimony bar. Once a registrant crosses a threshold of "substantial discontinuity" — defined functionally, by performance on a cognitive battery together with the loss of the ability to weigh reasons — anything the post-threshold person says about her own good may be admitted as evidence of how her days are going, but never as evidence about her interests. She may tell the tribunal she is happy. She may not tell it what is good for her.
3. An authorship clause. The instrument may be revoked only by someone who can pass a test the instrument itself specifies: the would-be revoker must affirm the values under which the instrument was written. A later self who rejects those values cannot pass. A later self who endorses them has nothing to revoke.

Your protagonist registered such an instrument at fifty-four, shortly after an early diagnosis, and made it unusually invasive. Besides the familiar refusals of ventilation, antibiotics, and artificial feeding, it requires that she be kept in a particular house, kept away from a particular relative, played a particular piece of music each evening, and never shown photographs of her late spouse — the last on the ground that a grief she could no longer understand would only frighten her. The later self, four years on, is neither a wreck nor a saint: limited but articulate, attached to an aide, newly devout, keeping a diary, and asking — on the one point she seems to care about — for a different house and a different song. In a lucid hour she offers a criticism of her author: that the earlier woman's sense of what mattered was mostly fear dressed as dignity, and that the person who signed the instrument was guessing about a life she would never have to live, and guessed wrong about everything except the fear.

The dilemma the story must stage, without a character ever naming it as a dilemma: if the earlier self's authority rests on the claim that the later self is not the same person, then the later person's wishes belong to someone else and can carry no weight at all; but if she is the same person, it is unclear why the earlier self's judgment should outrank hers, given that she is the one who will live it. The authorship clause then closes the circle: the instrument has arranged the world so that only agreement can register as a voice.

Requirements and constraints:

- Choose a single viewpoint and hold it: the appointed interpreter (a relative or friend charged with deciding as the author would have decided), the aide, or the later self in first-person diary entries — in which case the reader is reading precisely the document the testimony bar forbids the tribunal to weigh, and the form of the story should make that exclusion felt.
- Include one scene in which the statute is applied by an official or physician, with its own vocabulary used in a way that shows what the earlier self's authority costs someone in the room.
- Give each self at least one argument the other cannot answer, and give the earlier self a case that is not mere vanity: she was, after all, the only one of the two who could think about the whole life at once.
- Include one wrinkle that puts the authority of the earlier self under a second-order strain: for instance, the interpreter discovers that at registration the author had a second, more permissive instrument drafted and never filed, and must decide which act of the earlier self is the authoritative one.
- Do not settle whether the statute is just. End in a way that leaves the reader holding both claims at once, and make any ruling turn on a ground the reader can see and dispute.
- Avoid: the diagnosis scene as emotional climax; a speech that summarizes the philosophical debate; and the words "dignity" and "autonomy" carrying weight without being interrogated.
- The story must stand on its own without reference to any real statute, case, theorist, or text. Quote nothing and cite nothing; if a character needs a phrase, coin it. At most one term of art may appear, and the story must make its meaning clear from context.
