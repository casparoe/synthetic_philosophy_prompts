I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book about Hobbes — the exile years, the squaring-the-circle fiasco, the decades-long pamphlet war with the Oxford geometry professor who kept taking his proofs apart in print. I've hit the scene where my Hobbes has to explain out loud why he thinks politics can be proved the way geometry is proved, so I wrote the argument out properly, to give the speech real bones. Now I'm worried it proves something he'd have hated — that no argument in the book can ever settle anything. Here it is, tidied up, in his voice:

(1) In geometry we start from definitions and follow the consequences. The point, the line, the figure are things we make ourselves, and we know what we make — that's why geometry is the one science that's demonstrable.
(2) But a definition is a name we assign. Nothing in the world forces one set of names on us rather than another; we simply agree to speak that way.
(3) So a demonstration only shows that anyone who has agreed to speak as we do cannot deny the conclusion without talking nonsense.
(4) So a demonstrated conclusion is true of our words, not of bodies. It records the consequences of names, not the causes of things.
(5) Therefore geometry's certainty is the certainty of a well-built vocabulary, and nothing more.
(6) And since he says civil philosophy is demonstrable in the same way, the absolute sovereign is proved only inside a vocabulary.
(7) Which means the Oxford man, when he says the circle hasn't been squared, isn't wrong — he's speaking a different language. Hobbes can call him inconvenient, not mistaken.
(8) But Hobbes plainly does think he's wrong, and thinks the sovereign's necessity holds of real commonwealths, not just of readers who've accepted his definitions.
(9) If (1)-(5) stand, nothing he writes refutes anybody, and the argument that proves absolutism proves equally that no argument proves anything.
(10) Something has to give. Either geometry does give knowledge of real bodies, or demonstration is only verbal — and if it's only verbal, my third act doesn't work.

So where does it break? I'd rather not give up (2) — my Hobbes is a man who thinks words are tools and that he's a craftsman, and that's most of his charm. (5) is where I feel the floor move but I can't say why; it feels like it got smuggled in earlier and I just didn't notice. And if the fix costs me (1), tell me what I'm paying, because (1) is what makes him a mathematician instead of just a pamphleteer.
