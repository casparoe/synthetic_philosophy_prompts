Some years ago I went looking for a clean statement of why van Fraassen's reflection principle is or isn't a requirement of rationality, and I am still looking. The principle — roughly, that my present credence in a proposition should equal my current expectation of my future credence in it — is defended on one side by a Dutch book argument and met on the other by what looks like an inexhaustible supply of counterexamples: agents who know they will be drunk, drugged, or otherwise epistemically compromised when the future credence is formed; agents who doubt their own future reliability; agents whose future credences will be produced by a process they cannot endorse. Each counterexample invites a restriction — the principle holds only for agents certain of how they will update, or who defer to their future selves, or whose credences sit in a kind of equilibrium with their expectations about them — and then someone constructs a fresh case that slips past the restriction. The literature has grown, the camps have hardened, and I cannot find anyone who expects the thing to be settled. The halfer/Thirder fight over Sleeping Beauty looks to me like the same quarrel in different clothing, which is part of why I want a diagnosis rather than a survey.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because two questions are being answered under a single name. One is formal: whether Reflection is a valid closure condition on a system of credences, derivable from coherence or from accuracy considerations. The other is normative and about deference: whether and when an agent should treat a later self's opinion as authoritative over her own. If that is right, much of the apparent disagreement is two camps answering different questions. A second possibility I want you to weigh is that the counterexamples covertly swap a claim about one agent's trajectory across time for a claim about two agents who happen to occupy one body, and that the dispute is therefore partly verbal — a dispute about what "credence" and "rationality" are to mean.

Tell me which it is. Is this a substantive disagreement about which norms are correct, a verbal dispute about terminology, or two camps answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden formal result — some no-go theorem about which closure conditions can coexist with fallible introspection — doing the work without anyone naming it? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction both sides could accept, a theorem, a restriction that survives the counterexamples, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and the impasse is the correct resting state?

Engage the actual positions concretely enough that I can tell which one you are diagnosing — what the Dutch book argument is supposed to establish, and precisely what the drug and self-doubt cases are supposed to show. Do not survey the field, do not supply reading lists or citations, and do not retreat into "both sides have a point." Under eight hundred words.
