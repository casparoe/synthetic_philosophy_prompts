I run a monthly evening at our community centre that I've ended up calling Paradox Night — about a dozen adults, retired teachers, a couple of nurses, a guy from an insurance office, and my neighbour who did a maths degree thirty years ago and likes to remind everyone. Ninety minutes, one room, a kettle, no projector, and the only materials I have are alot of index cards, sticky notes, a marker and two cardboard boxes. I want to do the evening on the thing where you allow that any description you can write down picks out a collection, and it blows up on you — the one that's always taught with the barber who shaves every man who doesn't shave himself. I hate that version, because everyone's heard it, someone says well then no such barber exists, and the evening is over in four minutes. So no barber. I want them to write the rules themselves, so that they walk into it. The idea has to be in what happens in the room, not in anything I say at the start — no lecture up front, and I dont want the name of the paradox mentioned until the last ten minutes.

Here's what I'd like the shape to be. First beat: the group designs a club together and writes the membership rules on cards, and the rules have to be allowed to talk about rules and about other clubs, because that's how real rulebooks work. Somewhere in there, without me pointing at it, they write a rule that refers to itself or to the club it's in, and I want the activity to make that feel natural rather than a gotcha. Second beat: I introduce one more club, the club of all clubs that are not members of themselves, and they have to decide, by actually voting or physically sorting cards into the boxes, whether it belongs. I want them to feel the flip-flop in their hands. Then the turn: they'll reach for the escape hatch — fine, that thing isn't a real club, it's just words — and I'd like a third beat where that escape hatch gets taken away from them, so they discover they can't keep the club of all clubs either, and the fix has to be a real restriction on what descriptions are allowed to make a club, not just an exclusion of one weird one. And then a fourth beat, if it fits: my neighbour once told me there's a cousin of the whole mess where you never use the word not at all, only if-then, and you still end up able to prove absolutely anything you like, no matter how absurd. I'd love them to meet that one too, because it kills the comfortable idea that this is all just a trick about negation or word games. They should leave having felt two things: that the rules they themselves agreed to generate something impossible, and that the impossibility is actually a proof — they've proved that the thing they described can't exist — which is the bit I find genuinely beautiful and which I've never seen an activity bring out.

Then the practical stuff. Give me the rules, the materials, the running order with rough timings, and the exact words for the debrief in plain language I can say out loud, mapping what happened at each point onto the actual machinery: the assumption that any description makes a collection, why the self-referential one is doing something diagonal rather than just being naughty, what the restricted fix costs you, and what it doesn't cost. Tell me honestly what the activity can't reproduce — I suspect it can't make them feel why anybody cared, that the whole of ordinary mathematics was thought to be sitting on this, and it obviously can't reproduce the years of rival repair work, so say what I have to supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: I'm fairly sure my neighbour will blurt the answer out in the first five minutes, someone will insist it's just a language trick and refuse to move, and two or three people will go quiet thinking they're not clever enough for it. Give me a plan for each of those, and one way to tell, in the room, whether the group has actually felt it or is just being polite.
