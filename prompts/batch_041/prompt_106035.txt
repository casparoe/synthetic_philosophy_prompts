I am a philosophy professor with a joint appointment in a cognitive science program. My own work is on the later Wittgenstein, especially his treatment of religious belief, but the seminar I am teaching this term is full of students who work in formal epistemology and decision theory; they think in terms of credences, priors, likelihoods, conditionalization, expected utility, model selection, and Bayesian agents. I can follow a likelihood calculation, but I do not work in the formal literature. I want to give them something better than a summary of Wittgensteinian themes, and I have decided to ask for a systematic translation. Act as a philosopher who is equally competent in the later Wittgenstein and in Bayesian decision theory. Restate, as faithfully as you can, the family of views about religious belief commonly associated with Wittgenstein—that religious belief is not ordinarily a hypothesis answerable to evidence in the way a scientific belief is; that it functions more like a picture that regulates a life, a practice, or a form of life; that the believer and the non-believer may not be disagreeing about a matter of fact in any straightforward sense—in the vocabulary of Bayesian epistemology and decision theory. Lay out that target vocabulary first, then translate step by step. Be honest: do not silently convert the Wittgensteinian view into a thesis about unusually high priors or unusual utilities and then present that as a translation. Where the target framework forces a distortion, say so explicitly.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which elements of the Wittgensteinian position translate cleanly into credences, likelihoods, utilities, model uncertainty, or learning dynamics? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly is lost? Which have no counterpart at all—for example, the thought that a religious belief might function as a hinge or framework commitment rather than as a proposition with a probability; the difference between believing in something and believing that something is the case; the role of practice, training, and conversion; or the possibility that the relevant propositions are not well-defined until a form of life is already in place? For each residue item, say whether the failure lies in the Bayesian framework's inability to represent that feature or in the Wittgensteinian position's reluctance to specify what the cognitive state is supposed to be.

To keep the exercise concrete rather than generic, use as a test case a Bayesian agent whose hypothesis space is not fixed in advance and who can undergo something like model expansion or framework change, not merely conditionalization within a fixed space. Ask what happens to religious belief in that setting: is the form of life the prior, the likelihood, the model class, or something the formalism cannot place? I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of secondary literature; I want a rigorous conceptual translation and an equally rigorous account of what does not survive it. Think carefully and proceed in stages: first the target vocabulary, then the translation, then the residue report, then a short verdict on whether the residue is a defect of the target framework, a defect of the source position, or both.
