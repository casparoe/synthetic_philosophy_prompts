Three of us got to arguing over dinner about whether there is a fact of the matter as to which collections of matter are single objects. Two of my friends ended up on opposite sides and I couldn't referee them.

Sam said the whole question is a fuss over nothing. "Object," he said, is a label we hang on whichever chunk of the world it is convenient to talk about, so a cloud, a heap of sand, and a coral reef are objects when we have a use for them and not otherwise. There is no deeper truth about whether the heap is one thing or a trillion.

Dana said the labelling has to answer to something. Her test: a collection of parts is one object just in case the parts are knotted together causally, so that changing one makes the others adjust, and the boundary of the object is wherever that mutual adjusting stops. On her account a working engine is one object and the same engine taken apart into a tidy pile of components is not; a hurricane is one object; a heap of sand is not, because nudging a grain leaves its neighbours where they were.

Sam came back with three things. He said nudging one grain does move its neighbours, through friction, just imperceptibly, so the heap passes her test unless she is quietly deciding how much adjustment is enough. He said a flock of starlings passes it too, since every bird tracks its neighbours, yet nobody calls a flock a single object. And he said a corporation, a chess game, and a long argument all pass it, at which point her view collapses into his.

Dana answered that the flock and the corporation really are units of a kind, and that if it strikes us as absurd to call them objects, that is a prejudice about objects needing skins rather than a counterexample. She added that friction between grains is not the right sort of adjustment, and that Sam knew it.

Sam's last word was that "the right sort" is doing all the work, and that any line she draws will have borderline cases, so her answer is his answer with a fuzzy line drawn through the middle of it.

I would like a move-by-move verdict rather than a winner: which of Sam's objections land and which do not, whether Dana can distinguish the flock from the engine without help from the intuition she says is mere prejudice, whether Sam's vagueness point is fatal to her or merely a cost, and what both of them are overlooking.
