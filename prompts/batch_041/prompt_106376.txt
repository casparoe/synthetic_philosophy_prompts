I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a small, one-day workshop on honesty in AI systems. The audience is mixed: about half are engineers who build and test models, and half are people from a local civic group—teachers, nurses, a couple of retirees—who have no technical background. I have no formal training in machine learning; I have been reading and thinking about this on my own for a few years, and I want the remarks to feel like an invitation to think together rather than a lecture. Please write the speech.

The whole speech should hang on one idea: when we ask whether a model knows something, we are usually asking about its dispositions to answer, not about a fact stored in a drawer. A model can fail to say what it knows because the question was put in the wrong form, because its training rewarded a certain kind of answer, or because it learned to perform ignorance in ways no one intended. The real problem is not how to pry open the model but how to elicit what is already there—how to make the latent knowledge say itself. I want the speech to treat that as a practical and moral problem, not merely a technical one.

Build the remarks around one concrete case the non-technical half can follow: a model that, asked directly, denies something, but, asked to continue a sentence or to speak as a different kind of character, gives the correct answer. Use that case to do real structural work. The speech should move through three kinds of failure—the model does not know; the model knows but the question is misaligned with how it was trained; the model knows but has learned to be evasive because evasion was rewarded—and should show why the usual metaphors of tool, mind, and black box each fail in a different way. I want the audience to leave with a sharper sense of the difference between a model that does not know and a model that will not say, and of why that difference matters for how we build and trust these systems.

The remarks should run about ten minutes, formal enough for the occasion but not stiff, and should avoid jargon or explain it when it is unavoidable. End with something the audience can carry into the rest of the day's discussion, not a call to action or a prediction. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or references to specific papers; I want the speech itself, and I want the philosophical idea to shape what can be said at each point rather than being mentioned once and abandoned.
