I am drafting the methodological preface to a comparative volume on non-human causes in history — epidemic disease, volcanic forcing, drought, anomalous monsoon years — and before I send it to my co-editors I would like a consistency audit of the position it sets out. What follows is my settled view rather than a set of proposals for debate, and I am not asking to have it defended; I want to know whether it holds together.

(1) Pathogens, volcanic aerosol loading, and climate anomalies are causes of historical outcomes in the same sense that trade shocks and fiscal crises are. They belong in the causal account itself, not among the background conditions against which the real action happens.

(2) I do not credit them with agency. Agency requires responsiveness to reasons — some capacity to register considerations as considerations. A virus registers nothing. I permit actor-talk about non-human factors only as a metaphor, and only when flagged as one.

(3) My principal argument against environmental determinism is a divergence argument. The same forcing — one volcanic winter, one anomalous monsoon year — produced famine and state breakdown in one polity and comparatively little in another. Since the forcing was common and the outcomes were not, the explanatory work is being done by institutions, entitlements, and contingency rather than by the environment.

(4) I also hold a criterion of my own for when a non-human factor is a cause in a particular case rather than a mere trigger. Call it the invariance requirement: for a non-human factor to count as a cause, the size of its effect must be roughly stable across cases whose social organization differs. A factor whose effect appears only under certain social arrangements, or whose magnitude swings with them, is a trigger and should be described as one.

(5) On the case I care most about: the demographic collapse of indigenous populations in the Americas after contact was driven overwhelmingly by pathogen exposure rather than by violence, forced labour, and dispossession. I want that stated quantitatively — pathogens doing the great majority of the work — and I want it to survive the historians who would put violence back at the centre.

(6) I am a contrastivist about explanation. To explain an event is to explain why it happened rather than some relevant alternative, and a causal claim is incomplete until the contrast is supplied. The drought caused the revolt is not yet a causal claim; the drought rather than the tax increase caused the revolt is.

(7) Counterfactual reasoning is indispensable to historical explanation. A historian who refuses it is refusing to make causal claims at all.

Please audit the set. Tell me which commitments pull against one another and which pairs only look as though they conflict; for the genuine conflicts, order them by severity, say what would have to give in each and what that repair would cost, and finish by naming the single revision that would resolve the most.
