For my school's philosophy club I am putting together a handout on the special composition question — when do some things add up to a further thing? — and I would like you to build the centrepiece: a decision tree that takes a classmate from their verdicts on concrete cases to a position on when composition happens and what it is. Please write it as something I can photocopy and hand out: the tree itself, followed by a short note on each leaf.

Constraints:

1. It must sort people by what they say about cases, not by the labels they like. No node may be phrased as "do you accept unrestricted composition?" or anything else that names a view. Every node should be answerable by someone who has never read any philosophy: a verdict on a case, or a yes/no on a plainly stated claim.

2. Keep apart two questions my classmates constantly run together. The first is the condition: what must be true of some things for them to compose something. The second is the status of the composition itself: whether it is identity, a further fact over and above the parts, or something about which there is no fact of the matter. Build the tree so that answering the first does not settle the second, and say so explicitly wherever it matters.

3. Use these cases, and add others only if they earn their place: two people holding hands; a heap of sand fused by lightning into one lump of glass; a termite mound and the colony that built it; a lichen, one fungus and one alga living as a single thing; a slime mould that lives as separate single-celled amoebae and then aggregates into a slug that moves and feeds as one; a marble block and the statue carved from it; everyone in my school standing in the gym at assembly; and a data centre of thousands of machines, none of which does anything alone, jointly running one program.

4. Handle vagueness honestly rather than dodging it. Where a position needs a cutoff — how many grains, how much organisation — ask what it says at the borderline, and offer the options: the cutoff is itself vague; the cutoff is precise but nobody could know it; or vagueness here is a reductio of the position.

5. For every leaf give the position in one sentence; a name if you are confident of one, otherwise a plain descriptive label; and the commitment that would embarrass its defenders most. I do not need sources or a reading list — I could not check them anyway.

6. Mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and say briefly whether each is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Eight or nine well-motivated leaves are better than twenty I cannot explain.

7. Finish with a sanity check: if someone works through the tree and lands on "no composite objects exist anywhere," which earlier answers should already have excluded that, and where is the tree most likely to mis-sort someone?
