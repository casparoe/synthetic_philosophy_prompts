OK, bit of background so this makes sense. I'm 67, retired a few years back after 35 years in physical chemistry (thermodynamics, mostly), and since then I've been sitting in on the philosophy of mind seminar at the local university. I've gotten a bit obsessed with the mental causation stuff, which is a little embarrasing at my age, but there you go.

Anyway, the professor is letting me write one of the essay questions for the final. Upper-level undergrads, one essay, 90 minutes, out of 20 marks. And I definately want a real question, not one a student can answer by having memorized the lecture.

Here's what bugs me. Every version I draft can be answered by someone who's memorized "the physical cause is sufficient, so the mental is either overdetermining it or just an epiphenomenon." That's recitation, not thinking.

So here's the angle I want to build around. Back in my field we talked about causes at different levels all the time — "the temperature made the reaction go faster" — and nobody ever thought the molecular collisions and the temperature were two rival causes fighting over the same effect. They're the same happening described at different levels of grain, one coarse, one fine. I want a question that takes that intuition seriously for the mental case: the idea that a mental cause isn't a competitor to its physical basis, it's the same cause picked out more coarsely. I think philosophers call this the determinable/determinate move, or causal grain, something like that.

The question should make them do four things:

(a) state the exclusion argument properly, and name the premise that actually does the work, not just "the physical cause is sufficient";
(b) explain the different-levels-of-grain reply and say exactly why it's supposed to block exclusion;
(c) take a side — does it work, and if it does, is mental causation now just ordinary higher-level causation, like the temperature case, or has something been quietly given up;
(d) and this is the bit I really care about — make them decide whether this move is just the old special-sciences point (Fodor, the multiple-realization guy) in new clothes, or whether it's doing something the special-sciences point can't. Because if it's the same move, then either biology and economics get saved along with the mental, or the exclusion argument proves far too much and we should all be epiphenomenalists about the special sciences too.

Please give me the question, a model answer of around 600-700 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, I want five must-hit things a top answer has to do, marks that add up to 20, and for each one a one-liner on how students usually blow it. And don't let the model answer just recite Kim and stop — I want it to actually pick a side and defend it.

No citations or reading lists, please; they answer from memory and the question should carry whatever setup it needs. Thanks.
