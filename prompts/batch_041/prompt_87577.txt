I teach a large introductory astronomy course and, with four teaching assistants, mark roughly two hundred short-answer explanations per assignment; the marks feed a curve that decides who stays in the major. Our key is a three-tier rubric — correct, partly correct, wrong — and it is failing in a specific way: the answers students actually write are rarely simply wrong, they are simplifications, and several of the simplifications in our own model answers are, read literally, false. I want a marking procedure that ranks an answer by how close it comes to the truth rather than by how closely it matches the key.

Ground the procedure in the philosophical problem of verisimilitude — the idea, from Popper onward, that the aim of inquiry is not truth but getting nearer to it — and make it encode the lessons I have been reading about and want to take seriously:

(i) Closeness has two dimensions that can pull in opposite directions: how much of what an answer says is true, and how much it says at all. A vague answer is not automatically safe; a detailed one is not automatically better.

(ii) The obvious scoring rule — count the true claims and subtract the false ones — is disqualified. An answer that says almost nothing ("it is a combination of several effects") has only true consequences and would score perfectly under that rule, and a student can raise their score by padding with hedges and disjunctions. Only claims that bear on the question asked should count, and even then the exchange rate between the two dimensions has to be set out in the open rather than smuggled in.

(iii) Closeness can be compared only relative to a fixed question and a fixed vocabulary. Two answers that are each right in their own framework, and answers that are metaphors or category mistakes rather than candidates for truth, cannot be ranked by any such procedure. I want those routed to me unscored rather than guessed at.

Give me a numbered procedure that four TAs with no philosophy training can run in about thirty seconds per answer and reach the same verdict on: the tests in order, what counts as passing each, where it stops with a zero, and where it hands the answer to a person. Grades are appealable, so a step that cannot be shown to a student is not worth having.

Then run it on the three answers below to "Why is the sky blue?" and say plainly which of the verdicts I will not like — our TAs' instinct is to reward effort and to give credit for anything that is not false — and why the procedure is right anyway.

(a) Sunlight contains every colour. Air molecules scatter short wavelengths more strongly than long ones, so blue light is redirected toward our eyes from every direction; that is also why sunsets look red.
(b) The atmosphere absorbs blue light from the Sun and then re-emits it in every direction, so we see blue all around us.
(c) The sky is blue because of the way the air affects light.

Finally, tell me how the procedure handles an answer that is not a candidate for truth at all — "The sky is blue because the air is jealous of the sea" — and what the procedure cannot do: which marking disputes it will never settle, and which of its steps are judgment wearing the costume of a procedure. Answer in no more than 800 words, beginning with the one sentence a TA should keep in mind when the numbered steps run out.
