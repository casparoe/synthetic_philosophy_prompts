I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and the thread I keep pulling on is the one about which kinds of free will are actually worth wanting — whether the freedom that matters is the leeway to have done otherwise, or the rather different good of being the genuine source of your own actions, with your history and your values and your deliberation actually making the difference to what you do. Almost every discussion I find either stays at the level of abstractions or reaches for the same three or four stock thought experiments, and I've started to think the ideas only come alive when they're embodied in a life. So I'd like you to write me a short story.

Here is a setting I've had in mind, though you should feel free to change the details if they get in the way. Somewhere in the near future there's a clinic — a volitional-architecture practice, if you like the sound of that — that offers what it advertises as settled selves: a procedure that takes a commitment you make now (to a marriage, to a child, to a vocation, to a cause) and renders it unconditional in your future self, not by locking you in a room but by ensuring that you will wholeheartedly and unconflictedly want to keep it, with the capacity to reconsider quietly excised. Clients come to it precisely because they distrust their own future fickleness and would rather bind themselves than drift. Make the protagonist a technician at the clinic, or a client, or someone who has already been through it and is now watching a friend consider it — whichever lets the pressure land hardest.

What I want from the story is that it takes seriously, without resolving, the competing intuitions: that a person who cannot do otherwise is less free, and that a person who is not divided against herself, whose action flows from a unified and endorsed will, is in some sense more free — or at least free in the sense that might actually be worth wanting. Give me at least one scene in which a character argues that a settled commitment is more truly hers than any wavering one could be, and at least one in which a character argues that it is not hers at all but the clinic's, or the earlier self's, or a stranger's. And bring in the harder case: if the clinic shaped what you wholeheartedly want in the first place, does that make the resulting action less yours, or does the fact that it now expresses your deepest commitments make it more yours? Please don't have anyone explain the philosophical positions to anyone else, and don't let the story end as a tidy verdict; I want the concepts to show up as pressures in the characters' lives rather than as theses they announce. I'd rather close the book unable to say which of them was right, and a little unsure which one I am.
