I'm a senior software engineer at a company that runs customer support for a handful of mid-sized retailers, and I've spent the last year building an internal system on top of a large language model. The first version only proposed things: it drafted replies, flagged tickets, suggested refunds, and a person looked at each suggestion before anything happened. Three months ago we gave it hands. It can now issue refunds under a set amount, edit our help-center articles, close tickets, and open follow-up tasks for the human team. Our internal rule is that reversible actions can be automated and irreversible ones need a person, so refunds happen automatically (they can be clawed back) while anything that touches a public statement gets a human author. My design review is in two weeks and I have to sign off on the next round of permissions.

Two things bother me and I can't tell whether they're one worry or two.

The first is a question I can't phrase properly. With an ordinary tool — a compiler, a script, a spreadsheet — I know what it does because we specified what it does, and when it produces garbage, the fault lies in the specification or the input. This thing's behavior is learned. We can describe it statistically: it is right about 94% of the time overall, noticeably worse on non-English tickets and on customers who have been escalated before, and we have no way to say what it will do in a case we haven't seen. So when it issues a refund it shouldn't have, did a tool malfunction, or did something make a bad call? My colleague says that's a distinction without a difference, and that the only real difference between a tool and an agent is where we point when things go wrong.

The second is the approval step we kept for everything that wasn't automated. The queue is real, the log is real, the median time from suggestion to approval is under ten seconds, and roughly 98% of suggestions are approved. My manager's position is that we're fine, because a human is in the loop and there's an audit trail. My colleague's position is that we've built a machine for turning decisions into nobody's decisions: the reviewer is a formality, the log is an alibi, and the whole business case depends on the reviewer not really reviewing. If we asked reviewers to genuinely check, the speed advantage would vanish, so the value of the system seems inseparable from the fact that the oversight is nominal.

What I want from you is not a checklist but an argument about what is actually going on. Is the human in the loop genuinely taking on the responsibility, or is that a story we tell to keep the org chart comfortable? If the review is nominal, does its being nominal matter — or is the honest move to delete the step and openly admit we've automated the decision? And is there a principled place to draw the line about what the system may do on its own, whether by reversibility, by how bad the worst case is, or by who bears the cost of an error — or is that line always just a negotiated settlement about who gets blamed?
