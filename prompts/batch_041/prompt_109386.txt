OK so I'm a novelist, I'm about two hundred pages into a book that's basically a custody fight over a bronze head, and I've realized I don't actually have a theory of who should get it, I just have vibes and a stack of books. Before I write another scene I want the spec sheet, not the argument.

So: put on the hat of a legal philosopher who actually works on cultural heritage and property, and take this slow. Before you write anything, brainstorm out loud for a bit — list the cases you think a real theory would have to survive, including the ugly ones — because I'd rather watch you find the hard cases than get a tidy list that skipped them.

What I want is this. If I'm going to call a theory of "who owns the past" adequate — something that tells me when a museum should hand an object back and when it should keep it — what does it have to deliver? Which cases must it get right, which puzzles must it dissolve, which distinctions must it refuse to collapse? For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that puts it on the list, so I can see why it's a requirement and not just taste.

The cases I keep tripping over, roughly: the clean ones (stuff taken in a war, no paperwork, obviously looted — Benin, the marbles, whatever); the filthy ones (bought openly and lawfully under the law *at the time*, and that law was the colonial law, so the sale was "valid" and coerced at once); objects that changed hands three times before the museum ever saw them, including a local ruler who'd taken them off someone else in an earlier war; objects whose source community no longer exists, or is now split in two and both halves claim it; and the ones where nobody can prove a thing about provenance either way.

The distinctions I don't want it to flatten: legal title versus custody/guardianship; individual property versus group cultural heritage versus the "heritage of humanity" line the big museums run; restitution versus repatriation versus reparation (these are not the same move and I keep catching myself using them interchangeably); and the object's meaning in its original setting versus its meaning in a vitrine.

Constraints it can't just wish away: that the acquisition was often legal when it happened; that cultures aren't frozen and the "source community" of 1897 isn't the one asking now; that these museums are, at least on paper, trustees for a global public; and that for a lot of objects the provenance is genuinely, permanently unknown.

Then two things I really care about. First, tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement — and flag any pair you think *can't* be jointly satisfied. My money's on the rule-of-law / repose side colliding with the correct-the-injustice side, and on group cultural rights colliding with the fact that groups argue with themselves; tell me if I'm wrong. Second, which single requirement does the most work, i.e. knocks out the most popular theories on its own.

And at the end, briefly, no more than a paragraph each, how the usual suspects do against your list: the universal-museum view, the cultural-nationalist view, the return-anything-with-a-tainted-chain view, and the stewardship/guardianship view. Brief — the list is the thing I'm paying for.

Don't send me a reading list, I've got enough books. Just the spec.
