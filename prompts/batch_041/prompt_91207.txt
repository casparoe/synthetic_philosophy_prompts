I am the deputy head at a large secondary school, which makes me the person who arranges the things that are meant to take staff and students by surprise: the quiz a class is told to expect "sometime this week", the compliance visit a department is told will fall on one unannounced day this term, the fire drill that has to be announced in advance for insurance reasons and is still supposed to catch people out. A colleague in the maths department takes pleasure in explaining to me that the whole business is incoherent. If you announce that something will happen on a day nobody expects, he says, then the last possible day is ruled out, because by the evening before, everyone would be expecting it; but once the last day is ruled out, the day before it falls to the same step; and so on backwards until the announcement promises something that cannot occur. Yet the quizzes happen, the inspections happen, and people are surprised. He says the pupils' chain of "if I knew it was going to be then, it couldn't be then" must go wrong somewhere, but he cannot say where, and I cannot tell whether he means that the announcement defeats itself, or that the reasoning is invalid, or both.

What I want is not the name of the quiz puzzle. I am sure that puzzle has a name and a small industry attached to it, and I suspect it is the tidy special case of something larger that I keep running into. The wider pattern is this: a body with authority makes a public statement whose content is partly about what its audience will and will not know at some future moment -- "there will be an unannounced inspection"; "the paper will contain something you have not been taught to expect"; "the party is on Saturday, but you will not know when to arrive". And these statements meet two very different fates. Some survive being thought about; others dissolve the moment the audience works through them. The party is the case that puzzles me most, because my sister was told in so many words that a party was being arranged for her and was nonetheless completely surprised, since what she lacked was not the fact of it but the when and the by-whom. So the difference cannot be simply that the audience does not know, and it cannot be simply that the audience has not thought about it, because she had.

Is there an established concept for the structure I am circling, or is the quiz puzzle really all there is? I am after the carving rather than the label. What exactly makes an announcement of this kind work or collapse; whether the dissolving argument is a genuine proof that the announced thing cannot happen, a misuse of the assumption that the audience trusts the announcement, or an equivocation between "the audience does not know" and "the audience knows that it does not know"; which well-worked-out ideas come nearest and how each of them fails to fit the party and the inspection as well as it fits the quiz; and whether my three cases are one structure or several that merely resemble each other. If no existing term covers the wider pattern, I would rather have a coined one with a definition and a couple of borderline cases than a nearby term that turns out to cover only the tidy version.
