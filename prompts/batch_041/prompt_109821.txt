I write literary fiction — single POV, close third, no omniscience — and over the years I've built up a private vocabulary with my editor for how a story-world holds together. There's what's rendered and what's merely asserted. There's what's on the page versus what the reader is licensed to infer from it. There's the difference between my narrator staying quiet about a room and the room not being there. And there's the fact that my narrator is a presence in the book even in the paragraphs where she reports nothing, so the world feels like it keeps ticking while my character sleeps — even though, strictly speaking, I've never written that house at 3am in my life. The house is definately there while she sleeps, or at least that's the effect I'm after.

Now take Berkeley. To be is to be perceived, no matter sitting behind the ideas, the infinite mind keeping the furniture in place while we're not looking. Plus the master argument, the one that's supposed to show you can't so much as conceive of a thing existing unconceived. I want the whole package restated in my vocabulary, as honestly as you can manage. One thing I'll ask you not to do: don't get there by calling it all fiction. I know exactly what fiction is and Berkeley isn't saying the world is made up, so if my vocabulary can only reach him by quietly turning him into a novelist, that's a finding, not a translation.

Then the residue report. What survives clean, what you had to fudge, what I've got no words for at all. And your verdict on the leftover: is it something my way of talking about stories structurally can't say, or is it the spot where Berkeley was thinner than he sounds? I'm shaky on the argument itself — I think the trick is that the conceiving is itself a kind of perceiving, so you never get the unconceived thing in view — so correct me where that's wrong. Same goes for the bit about us having notions of minds rather than ideas of them; fold it in if I've remembered it right.
