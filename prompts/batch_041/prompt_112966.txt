I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and I keep running into 'the banality of evil' in two settings where it seems to be doing two entirely different jobs. In a seminar I'm TAing, a student used it to explain the mid-level administrators who implement a state's detention and deportation machinery, and the discussion immediately collapsed into a fight about whether the phrase amounts to an excuse; in a reading group on the ethics of technology, someone else used it to explain why content moderators who enforce a platform's rules all day without ever asking what the rules are for should be seen not as cogs but as the very site where the harm occurs. Both times the phrase did all the work and nobody could say what work it was doing, and both times I suspected that 'banality' was being predicated of something different — the deed, the man, his motives, or our explanations of him. I've read Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial, so I don't want a summary of the book, a bibliography, or citations; I want an analytic map of the slogan itself, treated as a piece of language that has come loose from its original context and is now being asked to settle arguments it was never built for.

Please lay out a menu of the distinct precise claims 'the banality of evil' could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and give each one a verdict. State each reading in a single sentence in its own terms, then classify it: is it a claim about the psychology of perpetrators, about how large-scale wrongs are best explained, about the nature of evil as such, about culpability and mitigation, or about the limits of our capacity to understand atrocity at all? Then say which are platitudes nobody would deny, which are substantive psychological or historical claims that evidence could in principle falsify, which are conceptual or moral claims that no evidence could settle, which are plainly false or incoherent, and which are merely verbal. I would particularly like the grammatical sorting done explicitly — whether 'banal' attaches to the evil, to the person, to his motives, or to our account of him — and a verdict on whether the phrase survives as a substantive claim under each parsing.

Finally, tell me which readings would actually license the two uses I described and which would undercut them. My own tentative list, which I would like you to correct and extend rather than accept: (a) great wrongs are done by ordinary people rather than monsters; (b) the perpetrator's motives were unremarkable — careerism, obedience, vanity — rather than fanatical hatred; (c) the wrongness of the deed is independent of the perpetrator's inner life, so nothing about his mediocrity mitigates it; (d) thoughtlessness excuses, because a man who never really thought about what he was doing cannot be blamed for it. I suspect (d) inverts whatever the phrase originally meant, but I can't articulate exactly what it inverts, and I'd like the inversion spelled out. Flag any pair of readings on your list that cannot both be true, and any point at which the slogan smuggles in a contested empirical claim about the past — in particular, whether it commits its user to the thesis that ideological hatred was not the engine of the historical atrocity in question, a claim about the past that some later archival work disputes and that seems to me to be doing a great deal of hidden work in both of my cases. Give it to me as a numbered list I can bring to office hours, each entry carrying the claim, the kind of claim it is, my verdict, and which of the two uses it supports, followed by a short closing section on which readings people usually mean when they repeat the phrase and why it survives as a slogan at all.
