For an essay in my ethics class, I want to read Arthur Miller's The Crucible through Augustine's On Lying, and I want the reading to be honest in both directions rather than forcing the play to illustrate the theory. Augustine's standard is deliberately severe: what makes an assertion a lie is the will to deceive, not the falsity of the words or the harm they do, so a lie told to save a life is still a sin and its harmlessness only lowers its rank among lies; he allows you to conceal a truth, but never to assert a falsehood. Take that standard into Salem. Elizabeth tells exactly one lie, to protect her husband, and it is the lie that destroys his credibility; Abigail's lies destroy other people; Danforth asserts what is false without meaning to deceive, while Hale, who has stopped believing the accusations, ends up urging a lie; Tituba, under threat of hanging, confesses to whatever is put in her mouth; and Proctor buys his life with a false confession before tearing it up. On Augustine's terms, who in the play is actually lying, and does the play's dramatic logic endorse his verdicts or quietly overturn them — in particular, does it present the most Augustinian act in the play, Proctor's refusal to let the lie stand, as heroism or as waste? Say where the play resists the reading and what it shows about lying that Augustine's arguments alone do not, and point me to the two or three scenes my class should argue about.
