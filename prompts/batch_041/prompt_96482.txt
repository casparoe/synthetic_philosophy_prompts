I run a small reading group for graduate students in ethics, and next month's session is with a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas — the thesis that there can be situations in which an agent is subject to two obligations that cannot both be discharged, and in which this is not merely a failure of the agent's knowledge. I'd like to come with questions that treat her position as a live theoretical claim rather than an excuse to trade hand-wringing examples.

Please draft eight to ten questions, in the order you would actually put them, and give each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The set should press on the structure of the view rather than invite restatement, and it should not include anything biographical or any request for reading recommendations — she will be answering live, without a bibliography to hand. For each question, say in a phrase which job it is doing, so I can see that the coverage below is complete:

1. The standard objection from deontic logic: that if whatever one ought to do one can do, and if one who ought to do each of two things ought to do the two together, then obligations can never genuinely conflict — the conflicting pair would generate an obligation to do the impossible. Press her on exactly which premise she rejects, and on what she forfeits by rejecting it; the follow-up should anticipate the reply that she keeps the principle but restricts its scope.

2. The distinction between an agent who cannot tell which obligation should win and a case in which there is no fact of the matter about which should win. Get her to say which one her thesis requires, and why the weaker, epistemic reading will not do the work.

3. The guidance objection: that an obligation is supposed to be something an agent can deliberate from, and if neither option is uniquely correct there is nothing for deliberation to track. The follow-up should draw out whether she thinks an ideally informed and fully rational agent could still face a genuine dilemma.

4. Moral residue — the guilt, regret, or felt need to make amends that outlasts the choice. Press her on whether such residue is evidence of a genuine dilemma or can be explained away by an obligation that was overridden rather than violated, and have the follow-up make her commit on whether an overridden obligation can ground residue.

5. Whether her view needs the conflicting values to be incommensurable, in the strong sense that no comparison between them is available, as opposed to merely being very hard to compare; and what becomes of her argument if commensurable values can also generate genuine dilemmas.

6. The metaethical upshot: whether irresolvable moral conflict counts for or against the existence of moral facts. She will likely claim it cuts the opposite way from what her critics assume, so the follow-up should pin her down on why.

7. One question that distinguishes a genuine dilemma from a merely hard choice in a way an unsympathetic critic could not simply stipulate away.

8. A closing question on what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon the thesis.

Finally, tell me plainly if the setup I've given you is confused or if the task cannot be done well as posed — for instance, if the impossibility argument I sketched above is a caricature of what the objectors actually say, or if you suspect the whole dispute is verbal, with each side attaching a different sense to the word 'ought', so that there is no substantive disagreement to interview her about. In that case say so at the top and adjust, rather than manufacturing questions around a controversy that isn't there; and flag any question that would require putting a commitment in her mouth that the position does not actually carry.
