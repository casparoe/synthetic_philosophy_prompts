I'm a high school student working on a presentation for my school's philosophy club, whose theme this term is what biology can and cannot teach us about human affairs, and I've gotten stuck on a case I chose myself. I want to analyze the US–Soviet space race through the handicap principle: Sputnik, Kennedy's 1961 pledge to put a man on the Moon before the decade was out, Apollo 11 in 1969, and then the strange part — that after Apollo 17 in 1972 the whole enterprise was wound down and no human has been back since. The principle, as I understand it, says that a peacock's tail, a gazelle stotting at a predator, or any gift that ruins the giver can function as an honest signal precisely because it is costly: only a genuinely capable animal, house, or state can afford it, so the receiver can trust what the cost says. Please do the mapping carefully and explicitly rather than gesturing at it — who is supposed to be the signaler and who the receiver, what exactly the cost is and who bears it, why the cost is what makes the message credible, and what the theory says the actors were actually choosing between at the relevant moments.

Then I'd like the honest accounting, because three things bother me. First, a peacock regrows its tail every season and a gazelle stots again for every predator, whereas the Moon landing was paid for once; does the principle require a handicap to be continually re-paid, and if so, does the American decision to stop at Apollo 17 count as evidence against the signaling reading, as evidence that the audience had updated permanently and no further payment was needed, or as a case the theory simply never anticipated? Second, I've read that very secure high-status people sometimes conspicuously decline to compete, on the grounds that competing would imply they had something to prove — I've seen this called countersignalling — and the Soviet side after 1969 spent years insisting that it had never been in a race to the Moon at all; is that the same move, and does it fit inside the handicap framework or sit outside it? Third, I'm suspicious of the framing itself: I've heard historians argue that the "race" story is partly a later construction and that NASA's own institutional interests and ordinary domestic politics explain the budget at least as well as Soviet competition does, so tell me what would have to be true for the signaling reading to be the better explanation, and where the theory is simply silent — for instance, about whether spending roughly four percent of the federal budget on a signal was worth it, or about who pays a handicap when the signaler is a state and the bill falls on taxpayers. I've also read that the handicap principle itself was dismissed for years as a naive just-so story before being given a formal game-theoretic footing, so if that's right, tell me what the formal version actually licenses and where it is more modest than the popular version.

Finally, please be strict about separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I would rather have an answer that marks its own uncertainty than a confident verdict where the evidence does not support one: I want to know which parts of the handicap story are settled biology, which parts of the space-race-as-signal reading are disputed by historians or by biologists who think the principle is unfalsifiable, and at which points you are inferring rather than reporting.
