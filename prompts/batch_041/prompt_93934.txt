I am a high school student working through Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach on my own, and I have reached the part where he introduces typogenetics: a toy system in which strands are built from the four bases, each strand can be read off by a ribosome into an enzyme, each enzyme is composed of amino acids that function as commands (cut the strand here, delete a base, insert a base, switch on copy mode, and so forth), and the enzymes then act back on strands. The reader is eventually asked to design a strand whose coded enzymes, working together, produce a copy of that very strand. I have followed the mechanics, and I can appreciate that a single string of bases is doing two jobs at once, being both something acted upon and a set of instructions for action. What I cannot yet state clearly, even to myself, is what the promised isomorphism with Gödel's construction actually consists in. I understand the rough idea of Gödel numbering, that one assigns numbers to formulas so that arithmetic can be made to talk about arithmetic, but I do not see whether the parallel between that device and the way a strand carries a description of the enzymes that will act on it is a genuine structural correspondence or a suggestive analogy that collapses under pressure.

What I would like is an explanation, pitched at someone who is comfortable with formal systems only in the informal way that book presents them, of precisely what is supposed to be isomorphic to what. Please make the explanation concrete by working through two test cases I have been turning over. First, in typogenetics the ribosome, the thing that reads a strand and converts it into an enzyme, is supplied from outside the system by the reader, whereas in a real cell the machinery that reads and copies the DNA is itself encoded in the DNA, which is what makes the arrangement look like a loop rather than a two-step process. In Gödel's proof, is there anything corresponding to this difference? Is the machinery of the proof entirely internal to arithmetic, or is some of it supplied from outside the formal system in the way the typogenetical ribosome is supplied by the reader, and does the answer change how strong the analogy is? Second, I noticed that in typogenetics a strand, once an enzyme has acted on it, is no longer present in its original form, whereas in a formal system the older theorems survive the derivation of new ones; does that asymmetry matter for the analogy, or is it incidental to the point being made?

I am not asking for a reading list or for historical background about who established what; I would much rather have the conceptual content laid out directly. It would help if you would say plainly where you think the analogy illuminates something and where it strains, and if you would close by telling me what work the notion of a code is doing in each case, since the comparison seems to me to rest on the fact that in all three instances an arbitrary mapping allows one and the same object to be read in two different ways. If inventing a small modified version of typogenetics would sharpen the issue, please feel free to construct one and reason about it.
