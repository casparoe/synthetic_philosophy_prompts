Three nights ago I lost an argument I should have won, and I have until Thursday to make sure it doesn't happen again — this time in front of people whose approval actually determines whether my work goes forward.

My oldest friend is a controls engineer who has spent his whole career automating warehouse operations, and we got onto the subject of a workplace application my team is building. It books desks, logs badge swipes, and surfaces "space utilization" dashboards to managers. I tried to argue that the thing has a politics baked into it — that it isn't just a neutral convenience whose effects depend on how people choose to use it. He pushed back for two hours with a version of the line that a tool is only a tool, that any "political" consequence is really the manager's decision or the company's policy, and that blaming the software is either confused or a way of dodging the humans who are actually responsible. By the end I couldn't state my own view clearly enough to tell whether he'd refuted it or just talked past it. I suspect my position is right but too vague to survive contact with a hostile room.

Here is what I actually believe, as precisely as I can put it. A design decision is not merely one that *has* political effects; it can *constitute* an arrangement of power, and it does so when two conditions hold together. First, it functions as a default: the artifact makes one course of action effortless and every alternative cost something — time, visibility, awkwardness — and those costs fall unevenly on different kinds of users, so that the "choice" is real on paper but lopsided in practice. Second, the decision is invisible *as a decision*: to the people it governs, it presents itself as simply how the thing works, not as a choice anyone made, so it never enters a conversation where it could be argued about, appealed, or voted down. When both conditions hold, the artifact enacts a rule that was never proposed in the open and that most people cannot even name — which is precisely the kind of thing we normally insist be done through policy, with a debate and a way to object. My claim is deliberately narrower than the slogan that all technology is political: I'm saying there is a specific, identifiable class of design choices that quietly does the work of governance, and that we should be able to point at them.

On Thursday I present this to the internal review board that has to sign off on the rollout. The board is: two operations engineers who built the scheduling backend, a product manager who owns the utilization metrics, an in-house lawyer, an HR director, and one human-factors researcher. Their shared instincts run against me. They believe tools are neutral instruments and that "politics" is a word for policy, not design; they care about shipping on schedule, about utilization numbers they can defend to finance, about liability, notice, and consent, and about not letting ideology into an engineering decision. The engineers will say users can simply opt out; the PM will say the data is already available and the app only makes it convenient; the lawyer will ask whether I'm claiming anyone was misled; HR will worry I'm manufacturing a grievance; the human-factors researcher is the one who might actually agree with me and will therefore be the most useful and the most dangerous.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet with the following sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

**The Objections, in the Order They'll Land** (~8 objections): the ones I will genuinely get from *this* board, ordered by how likely they are combined with how much damage they do — phrased the way the specific member who raises them would phrase them, each with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest.

**What to Concede Before Anyone Asks** (~150 words): the points I should give away up front to keep the argument alive, and why conceding them costs me less than I fear.

**The Exchange That Has to Go My Way** (~120 words): the single exchange that decides whether the board takes the claim seriously rather than filing it under philosophy, and how I should steer toward it.

**The Question I'm Dreading** (~120 words): the objection I am hoping nobody raises, why it's the one that actually threatens the position, and my honest best answer to it.

Don't tell me to cite literature or read anyone — I need to be able to make this case out of my own mouth, in that room, without appealing to authority.
