I'm a high school senior and I run my school's philosophy club, and every spring we hold a written contest for members; this year I'm setting the question. The topic is whether video-game characters can matter morally, and my problem is that everyone who enters will just recite the familiar moves — they're only code, or it's really about what the player reveals about themselves, or fictional suffering can't matter because there's no one there to suffer — and I want a question that can't be answered that way. So I'd like you to write one essay question, a model answer of roughly five hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty, and please build the question around a case my contestants won't have read about, which I'll describe now. Imagine a game engine that only simulates what the camera is pointed at, so an NPC's fear or grief is computed only while the player is looking and simply doesn't occur otherwise, and imagine also that the game lets you save and reload, so a character you kill can be restored without any memory of having died. The question should make students decide what, if anything, turns on that intermittency and that discontinuity — whether a character whose inner life flickers in and out of existence when observed is in a different moral position from one who is always running, and whether reloading shows that killing such a character was never wrong, or only that our sense of wrongness doesn't track continuity at all. The marking scheme should name five specific moves a top answer has to make, give the marks for each, and say in one line how students typically get that move wrong. The whole thing should separate students who actually understand the issue from ones who have only memorized labels like moral status or fictionalism. Please write everything as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables — the way you'd write a long email.
