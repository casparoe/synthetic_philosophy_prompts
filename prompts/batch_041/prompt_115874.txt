For most of my working life I treated sentience as a threshold. An organism either has a welfare that counts or it does not, and the serious work was deciding which side of the line each species fell on. Two things have lately pushed me off that position. I have started keeping a small apiary, which has made the question of what a bee's life is worth to the bee feel considerably less academic than it used to. And for the past year I have sat on an institutional committee that reviews protocols involving fruit flies, zebrafish, octopuses, and, in one strange case, an adaptive thermostat controller used as a comparison system in a study of goal-directed behaviour. Drafting a protected-taxa policy has convinced me that the threshold approach is arbitrary at both ends: it forces a binary verdict on evidence that is probabilistic and multi-criteria, it makes protection hostage to whether a species happens to have been studied, and it treats a crab and a cow as moral equals once both clear the line. The clearest illustration is the legislation that enumerates all vertebrates plus cephalopods and decapods while leaving insects out — a binary legal line drawn from a review whose own evidentiary framework was graded, weighting how many independent criteria an animal satisfied and how strong each line of evidence was.

So I now hold the graded view. Sentience comes in degrees — how much there is that it is like to be the organism, how rich and integrated the affective and evaluative structure is, how much of its behaviour is organized around its own states — and moral status should track that degree continuously rather than switching on at a threshold. On this view there is no deep moral discontinuity between a species just below the line and one just above; there is a slope running from mammals and birds down through cephalopods, decapods, and insects, and policy should be graduated accordingly, with the strength of the presumption against causing suffering, the stringency of review, and the standards for analgesia and humane killing all scaling with the degree of sentience we have reason to attribute.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them separated by kind: empirical findings about insect and crustacean neurobiology that would undercut the attributions; conceptual objections, such as the possibility that degree of sentience is not well-defined or not comparable across radically different architectures, or that sentience is all-or-nothing and only its contents vary; normative objections, including the charge that graded status is incompatible with the equal consideration we owe within a species or with the legal certainty that policy requires; and methodological objections, especially the worry that the view is unfalsifiable because any awkward evidence can be absorbed by adjusting the estimated degree. Say which of these the graded view's own defenders should concede rather than deflect, and what the view predicts that both threshold views and rival scalar views do not. Address the two ends of the slope directly: does the graded view actually say anything about insects that the threshold view cannot, or does it merely relocate the threshold somewhere more comfortable, and is a thermostat with a sliver of moral status a bullet the view can bite or a reductio? Finally, assess honestly whether the view has been insulated by qualification — for instance, if it is stated only as a claim about the structure of moral status and never as a claim about how to act under uncertainty — and whether that insulation is a defect or the correct response to the problem.
