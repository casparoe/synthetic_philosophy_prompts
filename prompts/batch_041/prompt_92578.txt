Was Malthus Right but Early? On Timing Clauses in Long-Run Predictions

Malthus's central prediction — that population growth tends to outrun the growth of subsistence — is often defended with a single phrase: he was right, just early. I want to know what that phrase can possibly mean. I will argue that it has a legitimate use, but that the legitimate use is far narrower than its popularity suggests, and that in most of its uses it functions as a mechanism swap disguised as a schedule correction.

Any long-run prediction bundles three separable commitments: a trend claim (the direction of change), a rate claim (how fast, or by when), and a trigger condition (what has to be absent or present for the trend to run). These can fail independently and they draw on different evidence. Now, "early" is a temporal predicate: it compares an observed timing to an expected timing. So anyone who says a theory was early has already conceded that the theory carried a rate claim, or a trigger condition with a date attached. If it carried neither — if it said only that population tends to press against subsistence unless something changes — then there is no schedule for the world to have been late to, and "early" is doing no work at all. It is a way of saying: I still find the mechanism compelling.

Suppose, then, that the theory did carry a schedule. When it misses, three repair patterns are available. Parameter repair: the model contained a free variable whose value was mis-set, and the correction is a re-setting of that variable. Scope retreat: the theory is redrawn to cover fewer cases, or to apply only when its trigger condition is satisfied. Mechanism swap: the original causal story is replaced by a different one that happens to generate the same trend. Only the first of these licenses the description "right but early." The second licenses "right somewhere else," and the third licenses "a different theory is right."

Here is a test I want to propose. Call a missed prediction a case of early truth if and only if three things can be done: name the parameter whose value was wrong; show that the parameter was part of the original model and not an addition made after the miss; and show that the parameter can be measured independently of the prediction it is now being used to rescue. When all three can be done, "early" names an ordinary scientific occurrence — a free variable was badly estimated — and it is respectable. When any of them cannot, the phrase is a compliment paid to a theory that no longer exists. The third condition does the heaviest lifting: a parameter that is only ever read off the failure it is supposed to explain has no independent content, and the rescue becomes circular.

The vacuity danger sits mostly in the second repair pattern. If the trigger condition is specified as "absent technological substitution, absent new resource discoveries, absent institutional change," then no observation can count against the trend claim, because every observation of the trend failing is also an observation of the trigger condition failing. A theory that cannot fail cannot be early either: being early is a way of failing on schedule, and there is no schedule in play. I take this to be the real trouble with the perennial defence of Malthus, and it is a trouble with the logical form of the defence rather than with the demographic facts, which I am happy to leave aside.

Two further asymmetries. First, falsification debt: each rescue borrows against the theory's remaining credit, and the debt is repaid only out-of-sample, by predictions made after the repair and then tested. Re-describing a past failure is not a payment. Second, and more troubling for the phrase's defenders: "right but early" is available only retrospectively. At the moment of the miss, a theory that is right but early and a theory that is simply wrong are evidentially indistinguishable. So the phrase has historiographic content — it can tell us why a text is still worth reading — but no justificatory content. It cannot tell us to keep believing the theory's next dated claim.

The verdict I want to defend is therefore deflationary but not dismissive. "Right but early" is legitimate when it abbreviates a specific, independently measurable mis-set parameter, and illegitimate when it abbreviates a mechanism swap or a retreat into unfalsifiability. The interesting question about any particular long-run prediction is not whether it was early but which of the three repairs is being performed on it — and that question is settled by looking at the model rather than at how the history turned out.
