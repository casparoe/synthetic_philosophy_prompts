For a history seminar I am writing a satirical piece, and I would like help drafting it. The assignment asks us to parody a way of thinking about history rather than a person, so I have invented a fictional applied cyclical historian, Dr. Marlow Ashgrove, who runs a paid subscription advice newsletter telling readers how to live given that history moves in cycles. I want the column itself, not a description of it, and I would like it to be funny to someone who has read the cyclical theorists while remaining readable to someone who has not.

The satire should be aimed at a practice rather than a doctrine: the habit of matching present events to an earlier period and then treating the match as a prediction, together with the quietism this licenses, the suggestion that because the cycle will turn anyway there is nothing to be done. My invented columnist should be a fair target, so everything he says ought to be something a clever defender of applied cyclical history could actually say, and his evasions should be recognizable rather than stupid. I do not want a strawman, and I do not want him to slip into abandoning his own view halfway through unless that collapse is itself the joke.

His system should have three named parts, and the column must use them consistently. First, phase-lock: the claim that a present period has locked onto an earlier analogue closely enough to be read as its recurrence. Second, the residue: those features of the present that do not fit the analogue. Third, the standing wave: his view that a cycle can be ridden or anticipated but not broken.

The reader's letter should raise the strongest objections I can think of: that the theory cannot be falsified because the residue is always explained away; that we can never know we are not living in the first cycle; that a published prediction changes the behaviour it predicts; and that telling people to do nothing may itself be a moral choice with victims. Dr. Ashgrove's reply should answer all four without ever contradicting himself, and the humour should come from the content of his answers rather than from making him absurd. Please keep the older cyclical theorists we studied, Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler and Toynbee, in the background as the tradition he draws on, but do not put invented claims or quotations into their mouths, since I cannot check them and the assignment does not require real sources.

Please write it in this order.

1. Headline: the newsletter's title for this instalment, one line.
2. The reader's letter: about 120 words, in the voice of an ordinary worried subscriber.
3. Dr. Ashgrove's reply: 450 to 550 words, deadpan and confident, never winking at the reader.
4. Note on the Satire: about 150 words, in your own voice, saying which of his moves you judge to be genuinely the weakest and why.
