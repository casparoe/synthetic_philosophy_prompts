I am a novelist, and every spring I teach an upper-level seminar called Consent and Narrative, in which we read fiction and case law side by side and ask what it takes for an agreement to be a real agreement rather than a form of pressure with paperwork. I am writing the final examination now and need a single essay question on the validity of consent. The constraint that matters most to me is this: the question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar four-part checklist — competence, disclosure, understanding, voluntariness — and then dropping the case into the boxes. I want a question that forces students to do philosophy rather than taxonomy, and it has to be answerable from the scenario I supply and from their own reasoning, with no citations and no outside reading. I do not want a student's grade to depend on whether she happens to have read any particular author.

Before you draft anything, please adopt the role of an expert in bioethics and the philosophy of consent — someone who has served on hospital ethics committees and also thought carefully about what agreement is, metaphysically speaking — and work deliberately rather than quickly. Open with a short brainstorming pass in which you inventory the pressure points in the scenario below, say which two or three of them will do the discriminating work on the exam, and note what a merely competent answer would miss. Only after that should you write the question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The scenario, which is my own invention and which the students will see in full:

A composer in her late fifties has a degenerative neurological condition. A private institute offers the only intervention that might slow it. Every sentence of the institute's consent document is literally true: the procedure is described as a therapeutic intervention, the risks are stated, the mechanics are explained. What the document does not say is that the institute's governing purpose is to test a theory about neural plasticity, that its researchers expect the intervention to yield publishable data whether or not it helps her, and that they regard benefit to her as a hoped-for side effect rather than the point. She is competent; she understands the mechanics perfectly. She is not physically forced, but the intervention is ruinously expensive — she sells her house — and there is no comparable alternative. Asked afterward, she says she would have agreed even had she known the institute's real purpose. The procedure does what it does: it alters her sensibility so thoroughly that she no longer values the music she spent thirty years writing, and the person who emerges cannot understand why she ever agreed to it.

What I want from you:

1. The essay question, phrased as it would appear on the exam paper, with a stem that tells the student what to address. It should require a verdict on whether her consent was valid, and it should require the student to say what work is being done by (a) the difference between deceiving someone about facts and deceiving someone about purpose, (b) whether the deception has to be causally operative for the consent to fail, and (c) the time at which validity is assessed — at the moment of agreement or in light of the person who results. It should also ask the student to name one component of consent that this case shows to be carrying more weight than the standard checklist suggests, and to say whether the validity of her consent and the wrongness of what was done to her can come apart.

2. A model answer of roughly 750 words, written as a strong student would write it, that takes a definite position and defends it while showing awareness of the strongest opposing line.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20), a one-line note for each on how candidates typically lose it, and a closing sentence naming the specific moves that separate an A from a B. Include in the scheme one trap answer that sounds sophisticated but should score low, and say why.
