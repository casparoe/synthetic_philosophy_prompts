Question:
In the absent-minded driver problem, a driver is on a highway with two exits. At each exit he can either exit or continue. He cannot tell the exits apart and cannot remember whether he has already passed one. If he exits at the first exit he gets 0. If he exits at the second exit he gets 4. If he continues past both exits he gets 1. He must choose the same action at both exits, though he can randomize with a fixed probability at each. (a) What is the ex ante optimal probability of exiting at each exit? Show the expected value. (b) Explain the paradox that arises when you compare the ex ante optimal policy with what it seems best to do at an intersection. (c) Does the paradox show that expected utility theory is inconsistent? Explain.

Student answer:
(a) Let p be the probability of exiting at each intersection. The possible outcomes are: exit at first, probability p, payoff 0; continue first and exit second, probability (1-p)p, payoff 4; continue both, probability (1-p)^2, payoff 1. So expected value E = p*0 + (1-p)p*4 + (1-p)^2*1 = 4p - 4p^2 + 1 - 2p + p^2 = 1 + 2p - 3p^2. Take derivative: 2 - 6p = 0, so p = 1/3. So optimal exit probability is 1/3, continue probability 2/3. E = 1 + 2/3 - 3/9 = 1 + 2/3 - 1/3 = 4/3. So ex ante optimal expected payoff is 4/3.

(b) The paradox is that if the driver finds himself at an intersection, he should not follow the policy of exiting with probability 1/3. Given he is at an intersection, there are two possibilities: he is at the first exit or the second. Since he has no memory, he should think each is equally likely, so probability 1/2 each. If he exits, expected payoff = 0.5*0 + 0.5*4 = 2. If he continues, then if he is at first he will reach second and then exit (since he can plan to exit at second), getting 4; if he is at second he continues past both and gets 1. So expected payoff of continuing = 0.5*4 + 0.5*1 = 2.5. So continuing is better than exiting (2.5 > 2). Thus at the action stage he should continue, but if he always continues he gets only 1, which is worse than 4/3. This is the paradox.

(c) No, it doesn't show expected utility theory is inconsistent. It just shows that with imperfect recall, the optimal policy from the planning stage may not be optimal at the action stage. The driver should commit to a random device that makes him exit with probability 1/3 at each intersection, and if he can't commit, he might have a problem. But the theory is fine; it's a case of dynamic inconsistency.
