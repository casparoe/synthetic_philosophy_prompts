I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I've been asked to run a session of our democracy seminar in which we assess concrete institutional proposals rather than rehearse the usual arguments about the competence of voters. I'd like a comparative judgment from you, not a survey, and I'd rather you commit to numbers than hedge. Assume the setting is a large, rich, and fairly polarized democracy with roughly forty million adult citizens, and treat each of the following as a serious proposal that could be implemented over the next thirty years.

(1) Plural voting by examination: every adult has one vote, and anyone who passes a free, repeatedly retakeable general examination in history, elementary statistics, and basic economics gets a second vote, with the exam administered in every school and public library. (2) An epistocratic upper house: about a hundred members chosen by lottery from among those who score in the top 5 percent on that same examination, with power to delay any bill for up to two years and to oblige the government to publish a public justification of it. (3) A sortition-only legislature: the elected chamber is replaced by an assembly of several hundred citizens chosen by lot for one-year terms; experts may propose and answer questions but not vote, and agenda-setting sits with a randomly chosen committee backed by a permanent research staff. (4) A market-review procedure: any act expected to cost more than a fixed share of GDP takes effect only after six months, during which conditional prediction markets on its net effects run; if the market-implied probability that the act raises net welfare drops below a set threshold, a jury of randomly selected citizens reconsiders it with access to the traders' reasoning. (5) Delegable voting with a competence filter: citizens may vote directly or delegate their vote to anyone who has passed the examination in (1) and registered as a delegate; delegations are revocable at will and delegates are recallable by a majority of those who delegated to them. (6) A market in votes: each citizen holds one vote that may be freely sold, with a ceiling of twenty votes per person and a public register of holdings.

For each design, give me a score between 0 and 1 for how much you'd expect it to improve the quality of the legislation actually enacted, where 0.5 means no expected change from the status quo, 0 means substantial deterioration, and 1 means the best outcome you think is realistically available to such a country. Then rank the six from best to worst, keeping the ranking consistent with the scores, and give each one three or four sentences explaining where it lands and what its most serious failure mode would be. I'm less interested in whether a design is defensible in principle than in what you'd expect it to do in practice.

Two further things. Tell me which single design you'd adopt if you could adopt only one, and what you'd expect to happen to the quality of legislation in a country whose electorate is about as informed and as polarized as ours is now. And flag any design whose score rests mostly on empirical guesses rather than on anything we have reason to know, saying roughly how much of your confidence is of that kind.

Please write this as continuous prose, in British English, the way you'd write an email to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Don't restate the proposals back to me at length; I have them in front of me.
