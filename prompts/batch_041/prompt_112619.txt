I am preparing a handout for a final-year undergraduate seminar on the relation between the person and the community, and I want it to do more than list the usual positions. What I should like from you is a decision tree — a taxonomy built from diagnostic questions rather than from a list of names — that takes a reader from commitments she can answer for herself to a determinate position on whether individualism or collectivism gets the relation between person and community right.

The pedagogic purpose is to sort students by what they actually believe rather than by the label they would volunteer. So no branch node may simply ask which position the reader holds, and no leaf may be reachable only by a reader who already knows the taxonomy. Phrase each branch node as a question about a concrete case — a decision someone might have to make, or a single claim someone might accept or deny — such that a reader can answer it without knowing where it leads. Build the tree in the right order: the questions and the combinations of answers they allow first, then the positions located in the resulting space, so that it is visible which cells are empty.

I should like the tree to turn on at least these distinctions, because my students run them together constantly:

- the methodological question of whether the individual or the relation is the basic unit of social explanation;
- the constitutive question of whether a person is constituted by her relations or merely causally shaped by them;
- the normative question of whether individual ends are subordinate to the ends of a community, whether the community matters chiefly as the condition of individual flourishing, or whether the individual's good is lexically prior;
- the question of locus — family, locality, nation, or people — and whether the relevant community is the one that gives the person her identity or the one that commands her allegiance;
- whether autonomy is the central value, one good among others, or a parochial ideal to be rejected;
- the descriptive question of whether the East–West contrast is a claim about how people in fact think, about what the traditions' ideals require of them, or a piece of Western self-description.

The tree must be able to place at least these positions: an atomistic liberalism; a liberalism that grants thorough social embedding while keeping the individual's good lexically prior; communitarianism; Confucian role ethics; relational autonomy; a state-centred collectivism of the legalist rather than the familial kind; the interdependent self-construal understood descriptively rather than normatively; and the sceptical view that the dichotomy is an artefact. Where the space contains a cell that nobody occupies, include it and say in one sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Aim for eight to ten branch questions and twelve to sixteen leaves. Each leaf should carry a name, or be marked unnamed where nobody defends it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Add a worked trace showing how one combination of answers reaches one leaf and a minimally different combination reaches another. Close with two short notes: one listing the pairs of positions your tree cannot separate and explaining why the questions I have specified fail to separate them, and one on the two positions a reader is most likely to confuse.

I am myself inclined to think the East–West contrast is overdrawn, but the tree must not presuppose that verdict: the sceptical position should have to be earned like any other. No reading list and no citations, please; the seminar reads the primary texts separately and the handout should stand on its own.
