My friend Daniel and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the Pasadena game, and the argument has left me with a position I have to defend at a small workshop in three weeks. The game, due to Nover and Hájek (2004), is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time, and if that happens on toss k you receive (−1)^(k−1) · 2^k / k dollars. The k-th outcome has probability 2^−k, so the series the standard theory wants to sum is the alternating harmonic series, whose partial sums converge to ln 2 — but only conditionally, and its terms can be rearranged to converge to any real number at all, or to diverge; the game therefore has no expected value, and expected utility theory has no verdict to offer on what it is worth. Daniel's line, which he will be repeating at me over drinks afterwards, is that this shows the game is not a proper object of rational evaluation at all, and that any attempt to assign it a determinate place among sure sums is a stipulation dressed up as a discovery. I disagree, and my proposal is a comparison rule rather than a value: truncate a game at level n by voiding every payoff that arrives after the nth toss, and say that G beats H when the truncated expectation of G exceeds that of H for every sufficiently large n, that H beats G in the reverse case, and that the two are incomparable when the truncated expectations keep crossing. On this rule the Pasadena game has a perfectly definite place among sure sums — it beats $0.69 and loses to $0.70 — while never acquiring an expected value; and where the truncations never settle (a game that pays (−1)^(k−1) · 2^k when the first head falls on toss k has truncations alternating between 0 and 1 forever) I take incomparability to be a verdict rather than a gap to be filled in later. The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly decision theorists and formal epistemologists who know the literature on non-absolutely-convergent expectations and take the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms seriously; two probabilists who will want to know whether my relation is well defined and transitive and what it does when applied to mixtures; one behavioral economist who regards the whole topic as a museum of paradoxes; and Daniel. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long: the objection as the person who would raise it would put it, then my reply, with the weakest point in that reply flagged in a parenthesis — ordered by how much damage the objection does to me, except that the fifth must be the one I am most hoping nobody raises. No introduction, no conclusion, and do not restate my position back to me.
