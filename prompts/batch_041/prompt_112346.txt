The question was: State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, explain the sense in which it is a negative result about voting, and explain its relationship to Arrow's theorem.

The student wrote:

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is about social choice functions, that is, rules that take the reported preferences of the voters and return a single winning alternative. It says that any such rule which is strategy-proof, meaning that no voter can ever obtain a better outcome for herself by reporting preferences other than her true ones, and which is onto, meaning that each alternative can be made the winner for some profile of preferences, must be dictatorial: there is one voter whose most-preferred alternative is always the social outcome. Since dictatorship is unacceptable, the theorem implies that every non-dictatorial voting rule with at least three alternatives is manipulable by some voter in some profile.

The theorem needs at least three alternatives; with only two, majority rule is strategy-proof. It also assumes that voters have strict preferences and that every strict ordering is admissible, so it is a result about unrestricted domains.

The result is essentially a corollary of Arrow's theorem. Given a strategy-proof social choice function, we can read off a social welfare function by declaring that society prefers x to y whenever the choice function selects x from the pair x, y. The derived welfare function automatically satisfies everything Arrow requires: independence of irrelevant alternatives comes for free from the pairwise construction, and the strategy-proofness of the choice function guarantees that the derived relation is transitive and respects the Pareto principle. Arrow's theorem then delivers a dictator, which is exactly the Gibbard–Satterthwaite conclusion.

One should not misread the theorem as saying that voters will lie. It says only that they can profit by lying in some circumstances; whether they actually do depends on their beliefs about how the others will vote. In that sense the theorem is about incentives rather than behaviour, and it does not tell us how often manipulation is likely to succeed.
