I'm a retired organic chemist — thirty-five years at the bench, and since I hung up the pipettes I've been working through philosophy of decision theory, mostly by argueing about it with an old labmate who jumped ship to economics. He lent me a copy of Memento, which I'd somehow never seen, and told me to watch it with the "absent-minded driver problem" in mind. I've now read the problem a couple times: two intersections, the driver can't tell them apart and can't remember having passed the first, exiting at the first gets him nothing, exiting at the second gets him a good payoff, and sailing past both gets him a small consolation. The trick, as I understand it, is that because his information state is the same at both intersections he can't choose different actions at them, so he's stuck picking a single mixed policy, and the best one has him continue with some probability at each. Fine. What I want from you is a reading of the film through that problem, and a reading of the problem through the film — because Leonard seems to be the driver, except that his condition is worse and his notes and tattoos are an attempt to get around the very constraint the problem imposes. He leaves messages for his future self, which is precisely what the driver can't do. So does Memento show that the absent-minded driver problem is an artifact of a bad model of agency — one that assumes the agent has no way to leave himself notes — or does it show the opposite, that the constraint is real and Leonard's tattoos are just a crude way of buying back something like perfect recall at the cost of becoming a puppet of his own past self? And there's a moral question I can't shake: if your future self can't remember your deliberation, is he the same agent you are, and does that change whether the optimal policy is the one that maximizes the payoff of the "driver" or the one that maximizes the payoff of whoever wakes up at the second intersection?

I should say I'm suspicious of the usual framing, because a friend who does decision theory tells me the problem is really about "policy-based" versus "act-based" evaluation, and that it gets used as a weapon in the evidential-versus-causal decision theory fight, and I don't fully see why. So as you go, tell me what the film adds that the bare two-intersection problem doesn't: does it dramatize a case where the agent's own preferences aren't stable across time — Leonard seems to want the truth and then decides he'd rather have a meaningful quest — and if so, does that break the problem's assumption that there's one utility function being maximized? Where does the analogy hold, and where does the film trade on a confusion between forgetting your reasons and never having had them? Which scenes would you point to, and what would an honest critic say the film gets right about imperfect recall that the decision theorists' toy example misses?
