I run the excavation at a site we've been digging for three seasons — a small Neolithic cemetery on the edge of town, about forty burials, a few of them with grave goods: a handful of polished stone axes, a scatter of red ochre, and one woman buried with what looks like a carefully arranged set of pierced animal teeth. Nothing spectacular by international standards, but locally it's a big deal, and next Thursday the town's little museum opens a permanent display of the finds and they've asked me to give the opening remarks. The room will be a mix: the mayor and a couple of councillors, the museum's donors (one of whom payed for the display cases and has opinions), a handful of local history society people, two or three actual archaeologists from the university, and — this is the awkward part — a group from the nearby village who've adopted the site as part of their heritage, who've been coming out to the dig all summer, and who I think are hoping I'll say something about their ancestors' spiritual life. There's also a reporter coming who filed a piece last month under a headline about the world's oldest religion, which is nonsense but which has stuck in everyone elses heads, so whatever I say will get read against that.

What I actually want to talk about, without turning it into a lecture, is the gap between the objects in those cases and anything we can honestly say about what the people who made them believed. I can tell you where the ochre came from and that it was ground very fine; I can't tell you whether it was mourning or protection or decoration or all three at once, and I want to explain why that gap isn't just me being professionally cautious but is the actual shape of what archaeology can know — and maybe something about how even a word like 'religion' might be smuggling in categories these people wouldn't have recognised. Can you write me the speech? Five or six minutes, meant to be spoken aloud, warm rather than defensive, and it has to land for the donors and the heritage group as well as for the academics — I don't want the archaeologists in the room nodding along while everyone else feels lectured at, or worse, told that their feelings about the site are naive.

Please make definite choices rather than handing me three alternative speeches or a menu of tones — commit to one and tell me why you made the calls you did. And then, separately from the speech itself, give me a short note on where it takes liberties or leans on a contested view of what inference from material remains can actually support, so I know what I'm defending when the reporter corners me at the cheese table.
