I'm a graduate student in a cognitive science programme, and I've spent the term in a seminar on meaning and representation that keeps returning to the three-layer picture of messages: an inner message that carries the content, an outer message that specifies how the inner one is to be decoded, and a frame message that announces that there is a message here at all and marks where it begins and ends — the vocabulary that comes out of the discussion of meaning in Gödel, Escher, Bach. The recurring disagreement in the seminar is whether any of that layering does independent work. One faction says meaning is fixed by the producer's intention; another says it is constructed by the receiver; I suspect both are describing something else and that the layer is where the meaning actually lives, or at least where a great deal of it lives. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of language with a cognitive-science background, think carefully before committing to anything, and construct a thought experiment to order rather than an illustration. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate settings (an ordinary one: a hospital records office, a family's papers, a small-town archive, a school) and pick the one that lets the case work cleanly.

The case is to have four parts, and each part must be something a person could actually encounter, with its setting named, in no more than three sentences. (1) An identical-carrier case: two documents whose inner messages are numerically the same string, both fully grammatical, containing no lexically ambiguous word, no indexical and no demonstrative, and produced by parties with the same intentions (or by a machine with none), which nevertheless say different things — the difference must be traceable to the layer that fixes what kind of document this is and how it is to be taken. (2) An empty-carrier case: the inner message is destroyed or was never committed to any carrier, while the surrounding layer survives intact, and the content is still determinate enough that a competent reader can say what was asserted, promised, ordered or asked — so the layer alone can carry content. (3) An orphan-carrier case: the inner message is perfectly legible and complete, but the layer is unrecoverable, and the upshot is not mere ignorance but indeterminacy — nothing in the world fixes which content the string has. (4) A counter-case in the opposite direction: the layer is completely known and fully shared by everyone involved, yet the content is carried entirely by the inner message, with the layer contributing nothing to what is said and only to the act of reading — this is to mark the limit of the thesis. Constraints on all four: ordinary life only, no cryptography, ciphers, keys or private codes, no dead or invented languages, no translation between natural languages, no signals from space, no DNA, no gramophone records or magnetic tape as carriers, no Twin Earth, swampman, brain in a vat, Chinese room, telepathy or forgotten code, and nothing that depends on a real historical document; and the producer of at least one of parts (1) through (3) must be a non-intentional source, such as a machine, an accident or a natural process, so that intention-based accounts have nothing to grip.

Then do the demonstration, keeping the frame layer (which announces that a message is present and marks its boundaries) apart from the outer layer (which specifies the scheme of decoding) wherever the distinction matters. For each of the four parts: state the content, say where that content is located — producer's intention, carrier string, receiver's construction, or the layer — and say which of the three layers is doing the work. Then tell me which single feature of the whole set is doing the heavy lifting, what happens to each of the four verdicts if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the view that meaning is in the receiver's construction, or in the producer's intention, would attack parts (1) and (3). Do not cite any literature or list readings; work it out. Commit to verdicts rather than surveying options, and finish by saying where your reasoning is most likely to fail and which argument or consideration would move you to relocate the meaning somewhere other than the layer.
