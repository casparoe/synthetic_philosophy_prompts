Give me your credence that the following claim is true: self-masking dispositions are possible, and their possibility shows that the fink/mask distinction must be drawn by the mechanism of interference rather than by whether the interfering factor is internal or external to the disposition. For the purposes of this question, a fink is a case in which the stimulus itself removes or alters the disposition before it can manifest — the familiar case in which a wire is disposed to conduct when touched, but a detector cuts the current at the moment of contact — and a mask is a case in which the disposition remains but its manifestation is blocked by a separate condition, as when a fragile glass is wrapped in foam. A self-masking disposition would be one whose own causal basis, when the stimulus occurs, produces an inhibitor of the very manifestation that the basis would otherwise produce, while the basis itself remains in place. The claim is that such a disposition is not a fink but a mask, and that the internal location of the masker means the fink/mask distinction cannot be a simple internal/external distinction.

Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, giving a rough probability and one line of justification for each:

(a) the probability that a disposition can have a causal basis that includes a feedback loop triggered by its own stimulus condition;

(b) the probability that, in such a case, the causal basis can remain present while producing an inhibitor of its own manifestation;

(c) the probability that such a case is correctly classified as a mask rather than a fink;

(d) the probability that the internal/external source of the interference is a theoretically significant feature of dispositions, rather than a superficial one;

(e) the probability that ordinary disposition ascriptions and scientific practice would treat the case as a single disposition rather than as a composite of two;

(f) the probability that the possibility of self-masking dispositions creates a problem for a conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions;

(g) the probability that it creates a problem for an account that identifies dispositions with their causal bases.

Then give me the combined credence. Tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or empirical finding would move it most. I am at about 0.4; a colleague is at about 0.75. If you can, say which of those two credences your decomposition makes more reasonable, and where the disagreement most likely lives.
