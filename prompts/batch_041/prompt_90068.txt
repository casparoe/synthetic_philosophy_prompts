I am working through an eighteenth-century treatise on human understanding — I am deliberately not naming the author, because I want an answer that works from the text rather than from the standard commentary on it — and I keep getting stuck on one stretch of its chapter on the credibility of reports. Here is the passage, with the page-break marks of my edition removed:

A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence. In such conclusions as are founded on an infallible experience, he expects the event with the last degree of assurance, and regards his past experience as a full proof of the future existence of that event. In other cases, he proceeds with more caution: He weighs the opposite experiments: He considers which side is supported by the greater number of experiments: To that side he inclines, with doubt and hesitation; and when at last he fixes his judgment, the evidence exceeds not what we properly call probability. All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence.

To apply these principles to a particular instance; we may observe, that there is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other.

What I want help with is not the general drift — that trust in reports is answerable to experience — but the precise commitment of that second paragraph. Writers on testimony divide over a thesis I will call global evidentialism: the claim that a hearer is justified in accepting what she is told only if she has, or could have, independently established from her own non-testimonial experience that testimony is in general reliable. Against this, others hold that at least some testimonial beliefs are justified without any such grounding, because they can be as epistemically basic as perceptual beliefs. I would like to know which of these the passage commits its author to, and how securely. Begin by reconstructing the argument of the second paragraph in plain terms, then take up the following.

1. Consider the sentence "This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word." What is the point of conceding that? Does the argument need the causal classification, or is the concession designed to show that nothing hangs on it? Say what would change if testimony-based inference were classified one way rather than the other, and why the author might be content either way.

2. Consider "our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony." Is this a claim about how we in fact come to trust reports (an aetiology) or about what justifies us in trusting them (a warrant)? The paragraph appears to run the two together; tell me whether they can be pulled apart, and which reading the surrounding sentences force.

3. The next sentence appeals to memory, "an inclination to truth and a principle of probity," and our being "sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood," all "discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature." Does that yield an empirical generalization — people have been observed to tell the truth, so expect them to — or a natural instinct, that we are simply built to trust? These support quite different conclusions, and I cannot tell which the passage wants. Does the argument survive either way?

4. If the passage does assert global evidentialism, does it contain any resources for answering the objection that the position is unliveable — that one cannot have enough non-testimonial experience to ground the reliability of testimony in general, because most of what one counts as experience of the world has itself been acquired from others?

Finally, say what the passage leaves open: which further sentence or distinction, had the author supplied it, would decide between the reductionist and the anti-reductionist readings.
