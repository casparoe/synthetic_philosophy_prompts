I've been reading Kierkegaard's A Literary Review, the one usually called Two Ages in English, on my own and without any philosophical training, and there's a passage I can't get straight. As best I can paraphrase it: he says the present age is one of reflection and levelling, and that the public is a phantom, at once all-powerful and utterly insignificant. The public, he suggests, is brought into being by the press, and the press in turn addresses itself to the public; because of this, no single person is really answerable for what is said, since the public is not a person and no one is the public. He contrasts this with an age of action, where there are actual individuals who can be held to account.

What I don't understand is what work the word phantom is doing. Is he saying the public does not exist at all, or that it exists only as an abstraction that nevertheless has real effects? And why does he think the press brings the public about, rather than merely reporting on a public that was already there? I'd also like to understand why he treats levelling as a bad thing: if everyone is levelled, is that not just equality, which sounds like something to welcome?

Please explain the passage in plain terms and tell me where my reading is going wrong. I'm not after a reading list or citations, just an account of the argument itself. Could you answer in British English?
