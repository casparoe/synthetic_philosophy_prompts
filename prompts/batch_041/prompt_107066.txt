I serve on my hospital's ethics committee, which is redrafting our policy on determining death, and I've been asked to write the section on "what the science actually shows." The draft's central claim is that death is the irreversible loss of the brain's capacity to integrate the body's functions, that the older circulatory criterion is merely a bedside proxy we now know to be less accurate, and that because we can keep a heart and lungs running after the brain has stopped integrating, the body is already dead and the machines are only animating tissue. The committee's premise is that one of these criteria is simply the correct definition of death and the others are approximations, so our only job is to identify the one that matches the biological facts — and that once we have, the older criterion should be dropped outright, including for families who ask to wait for circulatory death. Is this the right way to frame the issue? If neuroscience has settled that the brain criterion is the correct one, does that settle the policy as well?
