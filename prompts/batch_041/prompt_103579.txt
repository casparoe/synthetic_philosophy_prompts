Two graduate students in my department, Dana and Ravi, spent most of an afternoon arguing after a seminar on causation, and they have asked me to referee. I would rather not simply declare a winner; what I want is a move-by-move assessment of the argument as they actually made it, since I suspect each of them is right about something and wrong about something else. Here is how the exchange went.

The case under discussion was this: a landowner fails to build a fence along a dangerous cliff on her property, and a trespasser who wanders onto the land falls to his death.

Dana opened by claiming that the landowner cannot, strictly speaking, have caused the death. Her reasoning was that causation is a relation between events, that an omission is not an event but the absence of one, and that an absence has no power to bring anything about. She added that this is precisely why the law is right to treat omissions as a special category: we are answerable for what we do, and only exceptionally for what we leave undone, and the exception is created by a duty rather than by any causal contribution on the landowner's part.

Ravi replied that this proves too much. We say, and say correctly, that the failure of the brakes caused the crash and that the drought caused the famine; if omissions could never be causes, every such statement would be false or merely loose. He proposed that causation is at bottom counterfactual dependence: an omission is a cause of an outcome just when, had the agent acted, the outcome would not have occurred. On that account the landowner did cause the death, since a fence would have prevented it, and the distinction between events and absences drops out as irrelevant.

Dana answered that counterfactual dependence is far too permissive. On Ravi's account, she said, my not having donated to the fire department is a cause of every fire that spreads, and the absence of rain in a distant country is a cause of my garden's failure, because things would have gone differently had I acted or had it rained. Something more is needed to single out the landowner's inaction from the endless list of absences. Her proposal was that the omission must be a failure relative to a norm or expectation — what the landowner was supposed to do — and that it is this normative element, not bare counterfactual dependence, that leads us to identify her omission as the cause.

Ravi responded that Dana had now conceded his main point: she was no longer saying that omissions cannot be causes, only that some omissions are causes and others are not, which is exactly what he had claimed. As for the norm, he said it governs which of many genuine causes we choose to mention when we speak; it is a feature of our explanatory practices, not of the causal structure of the world. The world does not change when an expectation is violated.

Dana said she was not so sure. If the concept of a cause is the concept of something that explains and grounds responsibility, then the normative element may belong to the concept itself rather than to our conversational habits, and if we stripped causation of every norm we might find we had stripped it of the very feature that makes it the kind of thing we care about. She conceded that she could not say precisely where the norm lives — in the world, in the concept, or only in our talk — but insisted that it is doing real work somewhere.

Please assess each of the moves in turn — Dana's opening claim, Ravi's first reply, Dana's counterfactual-dependence objection, Ravi's reply that she has conceded his point, and Dana's closing thought — saying for each whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and briefly justifying the rating. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relation between causation and causal attribution, and note where one of them has the better of the other on the specific issue of whether the landowner caused the death. Please write your answer in American English.
