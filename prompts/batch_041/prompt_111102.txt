I'm taking a democratic theory seminar this term and last week we did a session on futarchy — the idea where instead of voting on policies you vote on a metric you care about (GDP, life expectancy, whatever) and then let a prediction market pick the policy, on the theory that markets are better at aggregating beliefs about consequences than voters are at guessing them. The discussion went badly in an interesting way: half the room argued about whether prediction markets actually forecast well, the other half argued about whether it's acceptable for capital-weighted bets to bind everyone, and a third group (me) kept pointing out that those aren't the same objection. I want to write a handout that sorts the positions out properly instead of rehashing the same two takes, because right now "I'm pro-futarchy" seems to mean about six different things.

So: give me a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on governing by prediction markets. Build it from the logical space first — each internal node should be a question that genuinely splits the positions below it, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which leaf it leads to (so no nodes like "do you think markets work well?" or "are you a democrat or a technocrat?"). Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if it's a cell nobody occupies) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want around ten leaves. The distinctions I actually care about, roughly in the order I'd want them asked: (1) do markets decide policy or only inform a decider; (2) if they decide, what do they decide — the policy, a threshold or parameter, or the metric itself; (3) is the metric fixed in advance by a vote, or is it also up for market determination, and what happens to the regress if it is; (4) the mechanics of the conditional bet — when a trader bets that things go better under policy X, does the payout depend on X actually being adopted, and if it does, how do you stop the price from feeding back into the adoption decision it's supposed to be informing; (5) who gets to trade and how bets are weighted (raw capital, person-weighted, subsidized market maker) and what that does to the manipulation and last-trader problems; (6) whether the market is aggregating values at all or only beliefs, given that prices can't rank outcomes by themselves; (7) the legitimacy question — is a good track record enough, or does the decision rule itself need democratic authorization from the people it binds; (8) measurability and resolution for policies whose effects are diffuse and decades out.

Two more things. Where the tree can't separate two positions, say so instead of papering over it — I suspect "markets decide" and "markets advise" collapse into each other whenever the decider credibly commits to following the price, and I want to know if there are others like that. And for the empty cells, tell me why they're empty: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it for undergrads in the seminar — a few of them are econ majors, most aren't — so keep the jargon down and don't assume anyone has read the futarchy literature. Its for a two-page handout.
