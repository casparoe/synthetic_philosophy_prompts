The topic is causation by omission, and here is the case I want the essay built around. On a hot afternoon a child is struggling in the shallow end of a public pool. A woman sitting at the edge, a strong swimmer with her phone in her hand, does not move, and the child drowns. Everyone agrees that the woman "did nothing." But which nothing? She did not shout to the lifeguard twenty metres away, who would have reached the child in time. She did not dial the emergency number, which would have brought help too late. She did not jump in, which in fact would not have saved the child, because the child was trapped under a loose drain cover that one person could not lift. So among the many things that are all truly described by "she did nothing," one would have made the difference, one would have made no difference, and one would have made a difference only too late. Yet the world does not seem to contain a fact about which of these omissions she performed; it contains only a woman sitting still.

I would like you to defend a specific thesis: that the real difficulty for causation by omission is not the familiar worry that an absence cannot make a difference, but a problem of individuation — an omission is not a determinate item in the world the way an event is, so a claim such as "her omission caused the death" can be true only relative to a description of the omission, and the description is supplied by our norms and interests rather than by the causal facts. From this, conclude that causation by omission is a different kind of causal claim from ordinary event causation — interest-relative in a way that event causation is not — and defend that conclusion rather than retreating to the safer claim that omissions are causes just like events.
