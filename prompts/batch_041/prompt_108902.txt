I've joined a reading group at work — we're all software engineers, so we come at this sideways — and we've gotten stuck on whether what a person believes can change what she literally sees, and what would have to be true of a case for that claim to be more than a loose way of talking. I want to build one case for us to argue over, engineered to order. Please construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. First, it has to be an ordinary situation of the sort a person could actually find herself in — a hospital, a car, a kitchen, a classroom; no laboratory apparatus, no brain implants, no invented neurology. Second, the influence must be strong rather than weak: the representational content of the experience itself differs, so the subject is not merely attending to a different part of the scene, drawing a different inference from the same appearance, or resolving an ambiguous figure differently — the world looks different to her. Third, the influence must be synchronic, operating within a single perceptual episode, rather than arriving through a skill or habit built up over months of training; so no expert who has learned to see what novices miss. Fourth, the state doing the penetrating should be a belief, and the scenario should include whatever is needed to make the change in experience observable rather than merely stipulated — the subject's report, and a further condition in which the effect persists after she has been told that the belief is false. Fifth, keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

After the scenario, please write separate paragraphs that do the following. Walk through the case and show, specification by specification, that it satisfies each of the constraints rather than simply asserting that it does. Identify the one feature of the case that is doing the real work, and say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest reply available to someone who holds that perception is immune to this sort of top-down influence, including whether that reply can deflate the case by redescribing it as a shift in attention or in the standard the subject uses for reporting what she sees, and tell me honestly whether the case survives the reply. The purpose of the exercise is to have something concrete to argue about: whether perception is a neutral tribunal of evidence or is already shaped by what we think. Please write in American English.
