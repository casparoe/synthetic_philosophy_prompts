Question (15 pts):

(a) In this unit we distinguished thin adaptationism -- selection is one legitimate mode of explanation, to be used once the plausible alternatives have been tested -- from thick adaptationism -- the working assumption that a trait is an adaptation until someone shows otherwise. Which one does Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique target, and what is the critique's core, stated as a claim about method rather than about any particular trait? (5)

(b) A study reports that male skinks with larger blue throat patches win more fights, that patch size is heritable, and that patch size tracks body size almost one to one. A classmate concludes that the patches are an adaptation for winning fights. Using the critique from (a), say what this inference has going for it and what it does not yet establish. (10)

Student's answer:

(a) The critique targets thick adaptationism. Gould and Lewontin aren't saying adaptation never happens; their complaint is that adaptationists assume a trait must have been selected for whatever it happens to do now, then tell a plausible story about how it helped its bearers, which nobody can test because the story is fitted to the trait after the fact. The spandrels of San Marco are the example -- the triangular spaces are a byproduct of mounting a dome on round arches, but an adaptationist could still tell you a nice story about why they're exactly that size and shape. Their methodological demand is that you work through the alternatives first -- developmental constraint, drift, historical accident -- so until you've ruled every one of them out, "selection did it" is off the table.

(b) The classmate has something, because heritability shows the trait can evolve, and winning more fights with a bigger patch is at least a candidate function. But this is exactly the pattern the critique warns about, and Gould and Lewontin would say the patches are a spandrel: a byproduct of body size that later got co-opted for signalling, which makes it a genuine adaptation after all, so the spandrel point ends up cutting both ways. The study also only shows the trait is useful now, and current utility is precisely what adaptationists mistake for evidence of past selection. And since the patches track body size so closely, they could just be riding along with whatever body size is doing -- which is drift.
