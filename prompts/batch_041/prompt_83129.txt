I chair the medication-safety working group of a regional hospital network — nine pharmacists and clinicians — and until three weeks ago I would have described our agenda as entirely practical. Then a patient on my own service was nearly given a drug that one of our two formulary sources flags as contraindicated in her condition and the other expressly permits. The decision-support system had imported both assertions, silently ranked one above the other by source tier, and never fired the alert.

We meet next Thursday to settle what to do about the knowledge base, and I must circulate a written opinion beforehand. The proposal on the table would stop resolving such conflicts at import. The base would hold both assertions as a flagged pair; a rule would fire only where an assertion is present and is not contradicted at an equal or higher evidence tier; where the tiers tie, the automatic alert would be suppressed, both sources shown to the prescriber side by side, and a timer opened for human adjudication. Nothing downstream breaks, the argument runs, because we are no longer inferring from premises we know to be inconsistent.

The disagreement in the group is not really about software. One member, who taught logic before medical school, holds that adopting this commits the network to the view that some contradictions are true — that both sources are right — which he says we have no business asserting in a clinical document. Another insists the design is silent about truth: we may continue to believe that one of the two claims is false while conceding we do not know which. A third warns that if we keep the classical engine and merely stop resolving conflicts, we will be reasoning from an inconsistent premise set, and from a contradiction anything whatsoever follows, so every rule in the base becomes live at once.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. I need a clear recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — with reasoning the group can actually argue with, one concrete implementation step with a date attached, and a fair statement of the strongest case against whatever I end up recommending. It should also say plainly whether the flagged-pair design commits us to true contradictions or is neutral between that view and the ordinary one; I will be asked that within the first five minutes. I don't need the literature — I need the memo.
