I am a novelist, and the book I am writing has at its center a jazz pianist — a performer whose fluency is genuine and hard-won, and who can do at the keyboard what she cannot put into words. Because I want her inward life to be exact rather than merely lyrical, I have been reading in the philosophy of skill, and I have assembled five accounts of what it is to know how to do something. I would like you to rank them.

They are:

1. Knowing how is a species of knowing that: to know how to do something is to know the answer to a "how" question — a proposition, or set of propositions, specifying a way of doing it — with the skill amounting to that knowledge plus the capacity to apply it.
2. Knowing how is a distinct kind of knowledge, exercised in the doing and not reducible to any proposition one might entertain; it is practical through and through.
3. Knowing how is not a form of knowledge at all, but simply the ability or disposition to perform — to be able to do the thing is all there is to it.
4. Knowing how is tacit and embodied: a non-representational, perceptual-motor capacity that is in principle resistant to articulation.
5. Knowing how is two-factor: a propositional grasp of a method together with a trained perceptual-motor attunement, neither of which suffices on its own.

Rank these in order of how well each accounts for the single phenomenon I am trying to render — a performer who, in the moment, does the right thing; who can demonstrate a passage flawlessly and yet, when asked how she did it, offers either a halting and partial description or none at all; and who plays measurably worse the instant she attends closely to her own hands. Give me the ranking, a sentence or two on each explaining why it lands where it does, and say which of the accounts this case positively tells against, as opposed to merely leaving unsatisfied.
