I have no background in philosophy or economics, but I read Berkeley's Querist recently and have been chewing on it since. What struck me is that he asks whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, and suggests instead that the true idea of money is that of a ticket or counter — a claim on goods and on other people's labour rather than a value lodged in the metal itself — with a nation's real wealth lying in industry and commodities, so that money matters only insofar as it stirs up and circulates that industry. Ever since, I keep noticing people say, as though it settled the matter, that money has no intrinsic value. I have heard it used to argue that a gold standard is a superstition, that a government cannot print its way to prosperity, that bitcoin is either the purest money ever devised or not money at all, and that inflation is "merely" a change in the unit of account. I doubt these people all mean the same thing, and I suspect some of them are saying something Berkeley would have rejected.

Please separate out the distinct precise claims that "money has no intrinsic value" could be making — from the ones that are nearly trivial, through the ones that are substantive and genuinely contestable, to any that are simply false — and say of each whether Berkeley's sort of view would support it, contradict it, or leave it undecided. Then tell me which of these readings is actually doing the work in each of the arguments I listed above, and whether it really supports the conclusion the speaker drew. I don't need sources or reading lists; I want the claims pulled apart and adjudicated.

Give the answer in three sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: "The readings," a numbered menu of six to eight candidate claims, each in a short paragraph; "Verdicts," a sentence or two for each reading saying whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and where Berkeley stands on it; and "Which reading is doing the work," two or three hundred words matching readings to the arguments I described and flagging any argument that the slogan does not actually support.
