I serve on the ethics committee of a professional association of retirement-plan advisers; I spent my career as an actuary, but I minored in philosophy in college, which apparently makes me the committee's resident authority on the gap between what a sentence literally says and what saying it in a given context conveys, so the chair has asked me to draft the written opinion we will vote on at Thursday's meeting. I would like you to write that opinion for me.

The case is this. A member was asked by a client, point-blank, whether her money would be locked up if she put it into the annuity he was recommending. He replied that she could request a withdrawal at any time. That is true. What he did not say is that withdrawals during the first five years carry a surrender charge of seven percent; she discovered this when she took money out in the third year, and she has now complained that he misrepresented the product. Our code of conduct is unhelpfully terse on the point: it requires members to be truthful with clients and not to misrepresent material facts, and it contains no separate duty to disclose. Everything therefore turns on whether a statement that is literally true but leaves a false impression counts as a misrepresentation. Our vice-chair, who has read more of this literature than the rest of us, says of course it does, because a statement means what it conveys and not merely what it says, and she wants us to adopt an interpretation of the code to that effect. The member's defender on the committee says the opposite: that to misrepresent is to say something false, that reading implications into the code would turn ordinary tact, ordinary negotiating, and the protection of confidential information into violations, and that in any case one cannot reliably establish what a speaker intended his words to convey. A third member, who has been reading up on the topic, circulated a study whose citation she could no longer find, distinguishing three ways of deceiving someone — saying something false, staying silent when silence will mislead, and choosing true statements so as to create a false impression, the last of which the researchers apparently call paltering — and reporting that people who palter regard themselves as more ethical than outright liars while those they deceive feel just as deceived. I expect the vote to be close, and I would rather not spend the meeting relitigating first principles.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition for this complaint — dismissal, a private reprimand, a required corrective letter to the client, public censure, or whatever else you think fits — and say whether the committee should adopt an interpretive standard covering true-but-misleading statements, giving me the actual wording of any standard you propose and stating plainly what it would and would not reach. Where the disposition asks anything of the member, attach a deadline. Give the strongest case for whichever side you come down against and answer it rather than waving it away; the members will want reasons they can restate in their own words, not a literature review. Keep the whole thing to 750 words or fewer, and put the recommendation in the first two sentences so that I can read that part aloud.
