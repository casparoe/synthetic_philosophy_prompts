For about fifteen years now I've been a fairly obsessive reader of rationalist blogs and forums — the kind of person who keeps a running document of unresolved arguments and gets more excited about a well-placed distinction than about a conclusion — and I've recently drifted into the orbit of a small foundation that funds conceptual work on norms and institutions. I've somehow been handed the job of drafting the internal priorities memo for a two-year, roughly $500k program on a cluster of questions I'll call, for lack of a better name, the ethics of celebrating and cutting down the successful: the American-Dream story that excellence should be visible, rewarded and aspired to, set against the Scandinavian Jantelagen norm complex (the informal rules that say you shouldn't imagine you're anything special or imagine you're better than us) and its Anglophone cousin, the tall poppy syndrome, under which visible success invites informal punishment. I want the memo to be a genuinely defended agenda rather than a survey of who has said what, so please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I'll fill those in myself. What I need is your best judgment about which open questions in this area matter most and why, ranked, with the ranking argued rather than asserted.

A few half-formed ideas I keep circling, which you should treat as candidates to place, revise, or throw out rather than as a fixed menu. First, I suspect the whole debate is muddled by running together two different targets: resentment aimed upward at people who are ahead of you (the classic tall poppy) and the same behavior aimed sideways at peers (the crab-bucket pattern, which I take to be far more consequential for mobility and far less discussed). Second, I suspect the normative status of the leveling norm depends heavily on exit costs — that in a village with no exit, suppressing visible excellence may be a defensible insurance arrangement among people who will be stuck with each other, whereas in a city with cheap exit the same norm looks like something closer to pure spite. Third, there's a distinction I can't get to sit still between excellence in a positional domain, where your gain is my loss and celebrating you is celebrating my demotion, and excellence in a non-positional one, where the good isn't scarce at all; I suspect the leveling norm is tracking that distinction badly, and that much of the American-Dream-versus-Jantelagen fight is really a fight about which domains we think we're in. Fourth, I don't know how to tell whether the Dream is best read as a factual prediction about mobility, a motivational frame, or a legitimating myth, and I suspect which of those you pick determines whether its falsity is a political problem, a psychological one, or no problem at all.

So: give me the agenda. For each question you'd put near the top, tell me what makes it worth two years of someone's attention — whether it's upstream of other questions and which ones, whether it's the crux that most of the rest of the disagreement hangs on, whether it's tractable for a philosopher working alone with no lab and no survey budget, whether it's neglected or merely unfashionable, and how much of the answer would actually change what a foundation, a school, or an individual does. Be explicit about the trade-offs when those criteria conflict, tell me which of my four hunches you'd kill and why, and say plainly where you think the framing itself is a false dichotomy the memo should dissolve rather than adjudicate. Finish by naming the one or two questions you'd have me personally take on if I get a sabbatical and want to come back with something that wasn't already obvious, and tell me what would move a question up or down the list — what argument, finding, or piece of evidence over the next couple of years would make you reorder it.
