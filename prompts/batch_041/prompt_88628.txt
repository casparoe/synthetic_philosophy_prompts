Right, I need to get my head straight on something before I commit to it in writing. Here's the position I've ended up holding about testimony, and I want to know what would actually count against it.

I'm a reductionist. Someone telling me something only justifies me in believing it if I've got positive grounds, not themselves just more testimony, for thinking that this speaker on this occasion is reliable — plus no undefeated reason to doubt them. Not the global version where I just need a general reason to think people are reliable; the local one, where it's about the particular exchange. Trust isn't the default; suspension is, and trust has to be earned. I know the anti-reductionists think this is either impossible to carry out or collapses into their view the moment you soften it, and I don't want the standard objections listed back at me. I want the exposure mapped: what would have to be true for me to be wrong, which observations, arguments, intuitions or empirical findings should actually move me, and which of those a committed reductionist would themselves concede count against them. Rank them — fatal versus merely awkward. And tell me honestly whether the view predicts anything its rivals don't, or whether it's a norm that can't be falsified at all; if it's the latter, say whether that's a real problem for a norm of this kind or just a category error on my part for expecting it.

Two specific things I keep tripping over. First, I half-remember reading somewhere that the people studying this from the cognitive-science side think the actual architecture is neither blind trust nor suspicion but a sort of always-on vigilance — cheap filters for competence and good faith that run while you're listening, evolved for exactly this purpose. If that's roughly right, does it count against reductionism, or is the descriptive story simply irrelevant to the normative one? I can't tell whether I'm supposed to be worried by it. Second, I can't work out what reductionism is even supposed to say when the testifier isn't a person — say a chatbot or a recommendation system that's right most of the time but whose mistakes are correlated in ways I've got no independent way of detecting. There's no local track record I can build, no character to assess, and my only check on it is other testimony. Does the view have anything to say there, or does that show the whole framing was built for a world of people talking to one another and doesn't stretch?

Be specific about which of these are genuine defeaters and which are just the anti-reductionists being pleased with themselves. And write it in British English — the place this is going is British and they get funny about it.
