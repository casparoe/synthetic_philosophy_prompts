A friend of mine who reads widely in cognitive science but works in an unrelated field posted the following summary of the free energy principle on a forum we both frequent. Before I repeat any of it in conversation, I'd like to know how much of it to trust. Here is what he wrote:

"The free energy principle says that every self-organizing system, brains included, must minimize the surprise it encounters — surprise being the mismatch between what it predicts and what it actually senses. Because surprise can't be computed directly, the brain instead minimizes a bound on it called free energy, which is essentially the same quantity physicists use to describe systems settling toward equilibrium. Perception is the process of adjusting the model to reduce prediction error; action, or active inference, is the process of adjusting the world to fit the model. So perception is controlled hallucination: the brain plays a movie and corrects it only when the senses protest. That explains why we see what we expect, why illusions work, why anxiety is a failure to update predictions, and — since prediction error is just the brain's version of thermodynamic free energy — why life itself is a system that resists the second law. The catch is the dark room problem: if minimizing surprise is the whole point, the optimal strategy is to lie in a dark, quiet room forever, which is precisely what we don't do, so the theory either needs extra machinery bolted on or it is unfalsifiable. Friston's answer is Markov blankets: anything with a boundary — cells, organs, people, maybe even a rock — minimizes free energy, which is why the theory keeps getting called panpsychist. The upshot is that the brain doesn't represent the world at all; it only predicts and corrects, so the idea of a mind that mirrors reality is dead."

I have no independent grip on this literature. I've read the popular treatments and a few blog sequences and found myself nodding along, which is exactly why I'm suspicious of my own assent. What I want is an audit, claim by claim: which parts a working theorist in this area would sign off on, which are the sort of simplification that earns a wince but survives at a cocktail-party level, and which are straightforwardly wrong. Three things interest me in particular. First, whether the free energy principle and predictive processing are one theory or two, since the summary runs them together as though they were interchangeable. Second, whether the dark room problem is a decisive objection or a puzzle with a standard reply, and whether the summary is fair to either side in how it sets the issue up. Third, whether the closing claim — that the brain does not represent the world at all — actually follows from anything in the theory, or is a reading that a substantial portion of the field would reject. I would also like to know which of the empirical-sounding assertions, the one about anxiety especially, are established findings, which are live hypotheses, and which are merely stories that fit the framework. Please don't send me a reading list; I want to know what to believe and what to stop repeating.
