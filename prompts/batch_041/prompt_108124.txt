I have spent the better part of a term trying to write a short paper on the spectrum argument against the transitivity of 'better than', and the longer I sit with the literature the less I believe the parties to the dispute are disagreeing about the thing I originally took them to be disagreeing about. The argument, in the form Temkin made famous and which has been reworked ever since, lines up a series of cases -- lives, or worlds, or outcomes -- running from something unambiguously excellent at one end to something barely worth living at the other, arranged so that each case differs from its neighbour by a hair. Adjacent comparisons each seem to favour one side, yet transitivity would force a verdict at the extremes (or, in the loop versions, would force a case to come out better than itself), and that forced verdict is one we are strongly inclined to reject. Temkin's own response is to keep every step and abandon transitivity; a long line of critics insist the fault must instead lie in one of the steps, in an equivocation, or in the way the cases are described.

What I want from you is not a verdict on whether 'better than' is transitive, but a diagnosis of why this exchange has not settled after decades. My hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and would like you to test rather than assume, is that the impasse is not about the logic of the relation at all but about which pre-theoretic intuitions are permitted to function as data: each side treats its favoured intuition (the small-step judgements, or the transitivity of a comparative) as non-negotiable and the other's as dispensable, so that neither can be argued out of its starting point. I would like you to assess that against the alternatives: that the dispute is verbal, turning on a slide between senses of 'better than' (all-things-considered versus pro tanto versus relative to a covering value); that it is a real disagreement about the underlying structure of value, between an atomistic picture in which comparisons are built from independent magnitudes and a holistic one in which they can be irreducibly emergent; that the sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about the formal properties a value-ordering must have and one about which substantive theory of value is correct; or that the question is simply ill-posed. Say where each side is genuinely committed and where the appearance of conflict is an artifact of a shared word, and be concrete about what each camp actually accepts and rejects rather than surveying positions in the abstract. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result (a representation theorem, say, showing what a non-transitive ordering can and cannot preserve), or a change of question -- and, if nothing would, say so and explain why the impasse should be expected to persist. Do not lean on citations or reading lists; argue from the structure of the positions themselves.

Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a two-to-six word label for your diagnosis.
