I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been arguing with a friend in my department who works on social epistemology about whether medicine is objective. I came out of the argument realizing I don't actually understand the naturalism–normativism debate about health and disease as well as I thought. My friend's case runs like this: the American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from the DSM in the early 1970s; the DSM-5 dropped the bereavement exclusion for major depression in 2013; and when the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association revised their hypertension guideline in 2017, they moved the stage-1 threshold down to 130/80, so that a great many people who had been told they were fine were suddenly diseased. My friend says these are all cases where the medical profession simply decided that a value judgment was a fact, and that the naturalist picture — disease as a biological malfunction that exists independently of our values — has been refuted by the history of medicine. She concludes that normativism has won, and that therefore whether someone "really" has a disease is not a factual matter at all: it is just whatever the relevant institutions with the relevant values decide. My father was recently told he has stage 1 hypertension at 132/84 and was prescribed a low-dose medication, and I genuinely don't know what to tell him. If my friend is right, then my father's disease is just a social decision, and I should be telling him that the diagnosis is a political artifact rather than a medical fact.

So my question is this: if the normativist view has effectively won the debate, how should I — as someone who has to help a real person make a real decision — think about whether my father actually has a disease? Is the correct conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about whether he is diseased, and that the only remaining question is whose values get to settle it? And if so, how am I supposed to choose between the values of the cardiologists who lowered the threshold and my friend's values, given that neither side can appeal to the biology?

Please take on the role of a philosopher of medicine who knows the naturalism–normativism literature well, including the biostatistical approach and the main responses to it. Before you answer, please brainstorm the principal positions in the debate and how they differ from one another, then give me a considered answer in a way that would actually help me talk to both my father and my friend. I would especially like to understand what each position is actually claiming, what a naturalist can say about cases like the homosexuality reclassification and the hypertension guideline change, and whether accepting that the concept of disease is value-laden really commits me to thinking that disease attributions are arbitrary or merely political. No citations or reading lists are needed; I want the reasoning itself.
