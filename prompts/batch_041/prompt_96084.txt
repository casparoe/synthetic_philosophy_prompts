I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book in which a committee of unremarkable men is asked to settle a question none of them fully understands — whether a prisoner is the man he claims to be. For the intellectual atmosphere of the thing I have been reading Condorcet's Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix, published in Paris in 1785, and I have arrived at a stretch of argument where I cannot get my footing. I would rather not quote his French and risk mangling it, so let me set out in my own words what I take the passage to be saying, and you can tell me where I have gone wrong.

As I read it: suppose every member of an assembly has some fixed probability, above one half but short of certainty, of judging any given question correctly; suppose the members judge independently of one another; and suppose there is a fact of the matter as to which answer is right. Then the probability that the majority's answer is the correct one increases toward certainty as the assembly grows, and it can be computed from the size of the assembly, the size of the majority, and the individual probability of judging rightly. Condorcet then seems to treat that computed quantity as the probability that the decision is true, and to draw institutional conclusions from it directly — how large a body should be, how far a dissenting minority should defer, when a second vote is worth taking.

Three things defeat me, and they are not, I think, the usual ones.

First, what is the individual probability of judging correctly supposed to be? A fact about the voter, discoverable by counting how often such voters have been right before? Or a placeholder for our ignorance? The argument seems to need the first, while everything Condorcet says about the difficulty of the questions put to assemblies seems to give him only the second.

Second, and this is what actually stops me: the same book contains the demonstration that majority preference can run in a circle, so that no option commands a majority against every other, and the outcome depends on the order in which questions are put. How does a man write both of these? Does he take the convergence result and the cycling result to inhabit different levels — one about the truth of a single proposition, the other about the ranking of several — or does he feel no tension at all? If the second, what is he assuming that lets him not feel it?

Third, the independence condition. Deliberation is precisely the process by which voters cease to be independent of one another, and an assembly that deliberates is the only kind Condorcet could have been observing. Does he acknowledge that his theorem describes the aggregation of verdicts rather than the conduct of a debate? And if so, does he say anything about what follows for bodies that talk?

Tell me plainly how confident you are in what you assert here, and single out the point you are least sure of. I would rather have one honest hedge than a smooth account.
