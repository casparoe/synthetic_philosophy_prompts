My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the moderation calls my team makes have any principle behind them or are just the mood of the room, and it ended the way it always does: both of us invoking Mill's harm principle while meaning different things by harm. I would like to settle it by having the principle turned into something my team can actually run.

I lead content policy at a mid-sized platform. Roughly four hundred borderline reports a week reach reviewers with no philosophy training, about ten minutes per case, and five available interventions: label, downrank, remove, suspend the account, or leave the post alone and attach context. Our general counsel signs off on any removal, and our board has committed us in writing to restricting expression only where it causes harm to people other than the speaker.

Write the decision procedure that commitment implies. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and where the procedure stops with a no. Three things it has to face: whether a private company is even the kind of agent Mill's principle addresses, given that he aimed it at coercion and at the informal pressure of opinion, and that our five interventions are not all the same kind of act; how a reviewer who has never read Mill can separate harm from offense without the line collapsing into whoever complains loudest; and whether harm that arrives through the audience's own choice, or through their disapproval of the speaker, counts.

Then run it on four cases: a post from an account with four million followers urging readers to go find a named journalist at home; an anonymous post falsely stating that a local shopkeeper has a fraud conviction; a mocking hashtag about a politician's appearance; and a pro-anorexia account whose followers say it is the only thing that understands them. At least one of these should come out against what we would probably do. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a test.
