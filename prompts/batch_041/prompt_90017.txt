I am putting together a short anthology of poems about European integration, and I should like you to write one of them: a formal poem on the tension between the project of ever closer union and the democratic authorisation of that union.

The form is to carry the argument. Write it in terza rima — five tercets and a closing line, in iambic pentameter — and let the interlocking rhyme do the work of the thing it describes. In terza rima each tercet's middle line supplies the rhyme for the next, so the poem cannot come to rest until it chooses to stop; that is the mechanism by which integration has actually proceeded, the functional spillover in which a decision about coal and steel, or about the technical standards of a common market, creates the administrators, firms and organised interests that then demand the next decision, and the next, until the reach of the joint institutions is far beyond anything anyone set out to build. A reader who knows this literature should feel the chain tightening from line to line.

Then break the chain exactly once, and let the break fall where the democratic question enters. The counter-argument to have in view is the intergovernmentalist one: that states, not functional pressures, set the pace, and that they consent because they keep the ultimate authority — the Union may exercise sovereignty jointly, but it has never been given the competence to decide its own competences, and it is authorised by treaties that only the member states can amend. The poem should register the asymmetry that follows. Legitimacy that comes from what the Union delivers — peace, a market, mobility, common rules for the things that cross borders — accumulates faster than legitimacy that comes from a demos authorising the arrangement in advance. The old assumption, sometimes called the permissive consensus, was that publics would let the elites get on with it so long as the results were acceptable; the poem should catch the moment when that assumption is found to have expired and nothing has been put in its place.

Constraints:

- Five tercets in interlocking rhyme, then a single closing line. The break must be visible in the rhyme scheme itself, not merely announced in the sense.
- No proper nouns and no named institutions. Brussels, Strasbourg, the Commission, the Council, the Parliament, the treaties — none of them may appear; the machinery has to be rendered through its effects on the people who work inside it.
- One human voice, not a chorus and not an abstraction: a translator, a standards officer, a national official seconded to a working group, someone whose working life has been spent implementing decisions taken elsewhere. Choose one and keep to it.
- The closing line should leave the union closer and unfinished. No reconciliation, no elegy, no ironic shrug.
- Plain, well-made verse; no doggerel and no prose chopped into lines.
- British English spelling and idiom throughout.

The poem should be intelligible to someone who has never read a word of European law, and exact enough that a specialist recognises the argument rather than merely the vocabulary.
