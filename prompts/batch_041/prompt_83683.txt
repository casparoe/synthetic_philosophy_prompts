I work with population statistics for a living, which means I spend my days making generalisations about groups of people, and I have been trying to work out where my own line actually falls. What I want from you is the principle, or the best small set of competing principles, behind the verdicts below: which clause of the principle each verdict is doing the work for, what it predicts about cases I have not listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save. Take the factual content of the cases as stipulated — I am asking about what may be said, not about what the evidence shows.

- 'Norwegian men are on average taller than Guatemalan men.' Fine, no hesitation; I would put it in a report without thinking.
- 'Danes are more likely to cycle to work than Britons.' Fine.
- 'Men are more likely to die by suicide than women.' Fine, and I do write it.
- 'Men are violent.' Not fine. It is close to the same information.
- 'The Japanese are more group-oriented than Americans.' Fine if I hedge it — 'tend to be', 'on average'. Unhedged, I feel I have started claiming something about Japanese character, and I do not want to.
- 'Brazilians are warm.' Fine — and notice that I have dropped the hedge altogether; there is no 'on average' anywhere in it. It also feels like a compliment, and I suspect that is doing some of the work.
- 'Germans are humourless.' Not fine, and I cannot see that the form is any different from the Brazilian case.
- 'The French are rude.' Not fine. 'Parisian waiters are rude' — fine, or at least I say it without wincing.
- 'The Irish are good talkers.' Fine. 'The Irish are drunks.' Not fine, and as far as I know the second is not even the more common trait, so accuracy does not seem to be what is at stake.
- I am a Scot, and I say 'we Scots are dour' without a qualm. When my English colleague says 'Scots are dour' — the same proposition — I bristle. I cannot explain this by the content, because the content is identical.
- 'He was late because he is Brazilian.' Not fine. 'She is blunt because she is Dutch.' Fine, or nearly so.
- The one I keep going back and forth on: 'Nigerians are entrepreneurial.' I say it, I think it is fine, and then I catch myself wondering whether it is the same move as 'the Irish are drunks' with a friendlier adjective — and, if it is, whether the friendliness is the problem or the excuse.

Two things beyond the principle itself. First, I would like to know whether the account you give is about the content of what is said, about the speaker, about the audience, or about the effects; my suspicion is that my verdicts are tracking several different things at once and that I have been treating them as though they were one. Second, I would like to know what, if anything, survives of the idea of national character. If the best account of my verdicts leaves only distributions of traits, with no sense in which a people has a character, then I want that said plainly; if some notion of character survives, I want to know what work it does that a distribution cannot.

As a test of whatever you settle on, give me one case it permits and one it forbids that are as close together as you can make them. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the principle, the cases it turns on, and, if no single principle fits, a straight answer about which of my verdicts I should give up and why.
