I chair the Meritorious Service Review Panel of a regional emergency-services agency — five members, quarterly sittings, six to ten cases per sitting — and we have reached the point where I no longer trust our own minutes. The charter gives us three outcomes: no award, on the ground that the act fell within what the person's role already required; a commendation; and the agency's medal. The line between the first two is where everything goes wrong. A career firefighter who enters a burning building is doing his job; the same man, off duty and in street clothes, pulling two people from a wrecked car, is a hero. A volunteer who buys his own rescue equipment is thanked in public; a salaried officer who does the same is told he was merely diligent. We also get the mirror-image cases — conduct nobody could discipline but which leaves the panel feeling soiled: a dispatcher who follows protocol to the letter and keeps a caller on hold for eleven minutes while a colleague sits free, a paramedic who withdraws from a shift by two-line text the evening before. Our previous chair wanted a fourth category for such conduct; the agency's counsel refused, and I still do not know whether he was right about the concept or only about our exposure. And we know that whatever we reward, the training division begins to treat as expected: two years ago we medaled a lifeguard for a rescue outside his post's remit, and the job description was rewritten within twelve months. I am not sure whether we were honouring something or quietly converting it into a work rule.

What I want from you is the decision procedure I can put in front of the panel: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the matter back to us because the question is one of values rather than fact. Build it on the best thinking about supererogation — the distinction between what morality requires, what it merely permits, and what it permits while praising; the baseline problem, that is, how much is already owed by virtue of a role, a contract, or a general duty to rescue, and whether that baseline shifts between paid staff, volunteers, and people who hold no role at all; whether an act can count as beyond duty when the agent never grasped the risk he was running; whether mixed or frankly self-interested motives spoil the classification; and whether the panel's own practice can make obligatory something that was optional when we first praised it. Keep it usable by five people with no philosophical training who must justify every decision in a written minute that the union will read. Assume that decisions are quarterly, that a medal carries a small stipend and a public announcement, that we are not a disciplinary body and cannot put in a minute what we may think privately, and that I sign the minutes. No reading list or citations, please — the panel cannot check them and I need the substance.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (1) the off-duty firefighter, in street clothes, who pulls two people out of a car; (2) a nurse who stays four hours past the end of her shift to sit with a dying patient whose family is delayed in traffic, where I suspect the procedure will tell us the act was never supererogatory at all, because her duty of care already covered it, and I want to be told plainly if I am wrong; and (3) the dispatcher who followed protocol exactly, where the procedure must yield something the panel can defensibly write — including, if it comes to this, that the procedure gives us no grounds to say what we believe. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will predictably be gamed, by the union on one side and by the communications office on the other.
