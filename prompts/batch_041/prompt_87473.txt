In a reading group I attend, a colleague repeatedly ends debates by observing that one cannot doubt everything, most recently to dismiss my question of whether our reliance on so basic a rule as modus ponens could be justified without circularity; his position is that because the rule cannot be doubted, the demand for a justification is idle. I suspect the remark is being made to carry more weight than it can. Please distinguish the precise claims it might be expressing — ranging from a logical point about the presuppositions of doubt, through psychological and pragmatic points about what a person can actually doubt or act upon, to one or more distinctively Wittgensteinian claims about propositions that stand fast as hinges and are exempt from doubt and perhaps from knowledge altogether — and for each state whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and whether it would genuinely license my colleague's dismissal. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
