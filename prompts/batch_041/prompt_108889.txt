I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm closing a piece on the function debate with a poem, because the whole fight is really two answers to one question. When we say a trait's function is X, are we talking about its history -- it's here because X-ing got its bearers to breed -- or about what it's doing right now in this body, holding the system up? The two camps in the field think the other side is answering a different question, and I want the poem to stage that split instead of explaining it.

So: 12-16 lines, no fixed form, but built so the same lines can be read one way as an origin story (why it's here) and the other way as a present-tense description (what it does now). Reverse the order, bring a refrain back in a different context, whatever gets both readings onto the same words -- ideally the last line should work as the end of one reading and the start of the other. Hang it on one concrete case where the two answers actually come apart, not the textbook heart: something that plainly does something useful now but has no honest selection story you could tell, or something clearly selected for that's since gone idle, or a byproduct case, or a trait co-opted for a job its history never had (bone's whiteness and the X-ray would be one of these, if that helps). Dont let the words etiological or causal role into the poem itself, and dont let it settle which account gets to say what the function really is -- leave the reader holding both. Someone who teaches philosophy of biology should be able to tell the poem gets the difference, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem.
