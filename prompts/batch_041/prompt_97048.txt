You are to act as a philosopher of law with a specialty in self-referential and self-amending normative systems, and also as a competent formal poet. I would like you to think carefully before you begin: first work out, for yourself, the exact structure of the paradox the poem is meant to carry — where the regress starts, what each turn of it adds, and whether it closes or fails to close — and only then write the verse.

Some friends and I have been playing a game a couple of them call Nomic, and it has left me uneasy in a way I want to capture in verse. The game begins with a written set of rules. On your turn you propose a change to one rule — strike it, amend it, or add a new one — and the change takes effect if enough players vote for it. What unsettles me is that nothing is held back from this process: the rule that says how rule-changes are voted on can itself be changed by the very procedure it defines, and a rule can be rewritten so as to raise, lower, or otherwise alter the threshold for rewriting it. Some rules are marked mutable and some immutable, and an immutable rule is supposed to lie beyond amendment — but only because a mutable rule says so, and that mutable rule can be amended. So the question of what the rules actually permit keeps sliding one level up: whatever fixes the procedure can be unfixed by a procedure that the fixing itself authorized. It seems to me the same vertigo as any constitution that contains the clause for amending itself, or any law that governs how laws are made, and I want a poem that makes a reader feel that vertigo rather than merely be told about it.

Please write me a villanelle on this. Use the two refrains to carry the two halves of the self-reference — one line should be the voice of the rule asserting that it may legitimately be changed, the other the voice of the change asserting that it is the rule which grants that legitimacy — so that the form's habit of returning to the same two lines enacts the regress, the same question arriving again one level up. The closing quatrain should bring the two refrains together in a way that leaves the loop either sealed or broken, and I should be able to tell which choice you made and why it follows from the argument of the poem. Aim for strict meter and rhyme. A reader who knows what self-amendment does to a legal system should find the poem true to the problem, and a reader who does not should still find it a good poem. Do not gesture at real statutes, cases, or constitutions by name; keep the whole thing in the abstract.
