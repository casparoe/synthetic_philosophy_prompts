I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I've been asked to run a ninety-minute session on peer disagreement — conciliationism versus steadfastness — for an interdisciplinary reading group: statisticians, professional forecasters, and two people from an ML lab. They are fluent in probability and Bayesian updating and have no epistemology at all. I don't want the debate explained to me; I want a plan for teaching it in that room.

Start with the order in which the components have to go in and why the dependencies run that way: what has to be in place before the question even parses for this audience, and which of the usual building blocks can be compressed or skipped given what they already know. Then tell me what single case should carry the session. My current candidate is two forecasters with the same dataset, each aware of the other's track record, who end up at 0.75 and 0.40 on the same question — but I suspect that is a mistake, precisely because this audience will reduce the whole thing to a Bayesian update on the fact of disagreement. So tell me what the carrying case must have, and must lack, to survive that reduction rather than invite it, and whether a second case has to do work the first cannot.

Then the misconception map. What I expect: that conciliationism just means averaging the two credences; that the dispute is about how much to trust the other person rather than about what epistemic weight the bare fact of disagreement gets; that steadfastness is self-confidence or stubbornness under another name. Give me the rest, in the order they will actually surface, and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than left to sit. I also want the session to end with the self-undermining worry visible as a live problem rather than a puzzle with a known answer — the worry, roughly, that if a peer's disagreement should lower my confidence, I owe an account of what happens when the disagreement is itself about how to respond to disagreement. Tell me what must be established earlier for that to land, and give me one closing question that would show whether they have the shape of the problem or only a slogan.

Finally, say what I should openly refuse to cover at this level. And throughout, keep track of which of the positions I would be presenting as the standard framing are in fact contested among people who work on this: I don't want to teach my own leanings as the consensus, so flag where the plan should say out loud that the literature is split.
