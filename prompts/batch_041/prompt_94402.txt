I'm organizing the final meeting of a seminar on the unity of the proposition, where we've spent the term circling a single stubborn question: what makes a judgment or a sentence *one* thing rather than a heap of items that happen to sit next to each other? The problem keeps collapsing into Bradley's regress. Suppose we try to explain the unity of *a* and *b* by saying that a relation R holds between them. Now we seem to have three things instead of two — *a*, *b*, and R — and R is just one more item lying alongside the others, so it no longer connects anything. To make R actually relate *a* and *b*, we need a further relation R′ that ties R to them; but then R′ is itself one more item, needing R″ to tie it in, and so on without end. One student insists this is a trick of notation and that in a real act of judging the parts are already fused; I suspect she's half right, which is exactly why I don't want to begin with a summary. I'd like to open the session with a poem instead, so the group feels the trap before we argue about it.

Could you write one in the cumulative form of the nursery rhyme about the house that Jack built — the form where each stanza adds a single new element that hooks onto everything already accumulated, and the refrain grows longer each time? In your version each new element should be a *relation*. Start from something as flat and unmysterious as two items placed side by side, then add the relation that is supposed to bind them, then — because that relation is now merely a third thing sitting beside them — the further relation that is supposed to bind the first relation to them, and so on, each stanza reading as though it has finally supplied the missing connector while the reader who knows the problem sees that it has only lengthened the list. Let the refrain itself carry the argument: the same clause returning, one joint longer, still not one thing. Keep it rhymed and chantable, as if a child could skip to it, and let the innocence of the form do some of the work — this is a rhyme whose whole charm is that things pile up, and the poem should quietly reveal that the charm is the disease.

Two constraints on the ending and the manner. First, refuse the tidy culmination these rhymes usually get; no marriage, no final coupling that magically fuses the pile into a single object. The last stanza should either stop mid-reach, one connector short of closure, or turn and admit that the sentence it has been trying to complete cannot be completed this way — but it must not supply the alternative. Don't gesture toward unsaturated concepts, brute unities, or primitive ties; that's for the discussion afterward, and the poem shouldn't pre-empt it. Second, don't let the poem diagnose itself: no line announcing that this could go on forever, no footnote in verse explaining the regress. It should be a poem first. A philosopher who has felt this regress should find it exact, with nothing present merely to satisfy the rhyme, and someone who has never heard of it should still come away with a real poem — one that ends, unsettlingly, in the middle of trying to say one simple thing.
