1. The claim

Her core claim in the pamphlet is that to choose to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder. It's a narrower claim than it sounds. It doesn't say killing is always wrong, it doesn't say the bombings were unnecessary, and it doesn't say that fewer people wouldn't have died if the war had dragged on. It says that if the deaths of those civilians were the mechanism by which a surrender was to be produced -- and the design of the bombing was to frighten a government into surrendering -- then what was done was murder, whatever good came of it. So the historical question (did the bombs end the war?) is beside the point, which is why the historians' fight is not her fight.

2. The two defences

She takes up two stock defences. The first: Truman didn't kill anybody, he signed a piece of paper. The second: if killing the innocent is murder, then it's murder to shoot enemy soldiers while they sleep, since at that moment they aren't threatening anyone. Both, she thinks, get the description of the act wrong -- the first because signing the order was the act and not a mere antecedent to it; the second because a soldier isn't a private person with a rifle, he's part of a machine that's trying to kill you, and that's what makes him a legitimate target. I find that second answer interesting and a bit slippery: it makes targetability depend on a role rather than on what the person is doing at the time, and it's not obvious how to stop a role-based answer from spreading.

She also notes that the killing seemed necessary only because the Allies had insisted on unconditional surrender. Which is another reason to think the bombings were wrong.

People usually read her as invoking double effect, with the civilians as the foreseen-but-unintended by-product of an attack aimed at war production. But double effect is a non-starter here. The deaths were foreseen, and on any sane version of that doctrine foresight is already enough to disqualify the act.

3. What the degree was doing

Grant all of the above and you still haven't got to the thing she was actually objecting to, which was the degree. An honorary degree isn't a book review, its an act. To honour a man for a deed is to do something with the deed: to ratify it, to put it into circulation as an object of emulation, to add a university's weight to the proposition that this is what greatness looks like. As far as I can tell she's careful not to say Truman was a bad man; her complaint is about the celebration. And that's a much cheaper complaint to make. The objector doesn't have to settle the counterfactual history or claim to know what was in anybody's head. It's enough that this is not the sort of thing to be celebrated.

4. The cost

Here's my worry. Her prohibition is supposed to be absolute, i.e. not to depend on consequences. But the argument about the degree seems to run straight through consequences: an honour is objectionable because of what honouring does -- it encourages, it entrenches, it tells the next statesman that the thing can be done and then decorated. If that's the shape of it, she's borrowed a consequentialist premise to reach an absolutist conclusion, and a critic can say: fine, so the degree is wrong only if those downstream effects actually obtain, which is an empirical matter about the world and not a fact about the description of the act.

Two repairs suggest themselves. One: the honour is wrong as an injustice to the dead, who are wronged a second time, not merely symbolically. Two: the honour is itself an act of the same species as the act honoured -- ratification is a way of doing the thing over again -- so no causal story is needed. I lean to the second, but it has a price. If ratifying is re-doing, where does ratification stop? The dean who signs, the porter who carries the certificate, the guests who clap, the donors who pay for the dinner. Anscombe would say something about what counts as one's own act and what is voluntary; I'm not sure the pamphlet has that answer, and the later essay on war doesn't obviously supply it either.

5. Where I land

I think her real contribution is the move from was the bombing right? to what are we doing when we celebrate? -- and I think that move costs her something, because the second question can't be answered in the vocabulary she uses for the first. Or maybe I'm wrong and an honour is just something a university says, and saying is a different kind of act from doing.
