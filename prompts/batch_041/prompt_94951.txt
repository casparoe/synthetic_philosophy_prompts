Ok so I mediate commercial disputes for a living — supplier rows, a couple of partnership breakups, one very long fight over a family business — and there's a pattern in how settlements actually land that I've never had a name for. I'd like the name, but honestly I want the thing itself carved up more than I want the label.

Here's what I keep seeing. Two sides come in with numbers: A wants X, B wants Y, and everything between X and Y is the range of deals. You'd think they'd meet in the middle. They usually don't. What they drift toward is the point where each side ends up with the same *fraction* of its own ask — A walks away with 40% of X, B with 40% of Y. Not equal dollars, equal proportion-of-what-you-wanted.

Three things I've noticed about it. One, the bigger your ask, the bigger your slice in absolute terms, so the side with the more extravagant opening position quietly does better, and nobody seems to clock that. Two, it all depends on where you set "no deal," because everyones measuring from their walk-away, so the side that can credibly make walking away worse for the other one gets more. Three, and this is the one that bugs me: if some new option turns up that would only help one of them, the whole split moves, even though neither of them actually took the new option.

Is there a name for this? I have a feeling there is, and that there's a rival rule that behaves differently — one that wouldn't care about the new option appearing, or one that equalizes dollars instead of proportions — and I want to know which of them my mediators' instinct is closest to. But please don't just hand me a label. If you give me a name, tell me exactly what rule it is, whether what I'm describing is that rule or a cousin of it, what it's normally contrasted with and on what grounds, and where its edges are — cases where it spits out an answer no real negotiator would accept. I'd also like to know whether I'm overeaching by suspecting that the same proportional logic is lurking in how families split an inheritance and in how economists have argued about how much tax different incomes ought to pay.

Take the role of someone who actually works on bargaining theory and cooperative game theory. Think it through carefully — brainstorm the candidate rules first and only then commit to one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question. And tell me what would change your mind: if you tell me it's rule Q, what would make you take that back, or where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong?
