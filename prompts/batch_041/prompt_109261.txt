Hey — quick one from my epistemology seminar that's been nagging me. We covered Fitch's paradox and I think I must be missing something, beacuse the prof's gloss can't be the whole story.

The setup as I got it: the knowability principle says every truth can be known, and Fitch shows this collapses into "every truth is actually known" — which is obviously absurd, since there's tons nobody knows. Ok, so the principle needs patching, fine. But here's where I get off the bus. Take one specific truth: the total number of grains of sand on Earth is even (say it is; nobody's counted, so nobody knows it). Fitch's move is that the proposition "the sand number is even AND nobody knows it's even" is unknowable — you can't know that conjunction, because knowing it would mean knowing both halves, and knowing the second half would make it false. Fine. But that seems to make the unknowability a fact about us — about nobody having bothered to count — and not about truth or knowability at all! If someone actually did the count and got the parity, they'd know the first half, the "nobody knows" half would just be false, and there'd be no paradox left over. So isn't the whole thing an artifact of us being finite and slow? Give us a big enough computer and infinite patience, and doesn't the paradox dissapear? The prof keeps insisting the unknowability is "structural" and not about our limits, but I honestly can't see the difference. Where am I going wrong?
