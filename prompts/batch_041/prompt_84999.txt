A colleague of mine works on the ethics of rescue. Over the past year our reading group has put a series of cases to her, and she has given a verdict on each without ever stating a rule; I have deliberately not asked her for one, so the record below is all I have to work with.

1. Five strangers will die unless treated; one stranger will lose a hand unless treated; the treatment can be given to the five or to the one, not both. Her verdict: treat the five.

2. Two strangers are trapped on opposite sides of a flooding gallery, and you can reach exactly one of them before the water rises; neither is known to you, and there is nothing else to choose between them. Her verdict: the choice must be made by a coin toss, and deciding on any other basis is wrong, including on grounds that bear on neither person's claim.

3. Five strangers will each lose a hand unless treated; one stranger will die unless treated; the treatment can be given to the five or to the one, not both. Her verdict: treat the one.

4. Twenty strangers will each lose a finger unless treated; one stranger will lose a hand unless treated; again, one side or the other. Her verdict: treat the one.

5. Five strangers will drown unless a sluice is opened; opening it will drown a sixth stranger who would otherwise survive. Her verdict: do not open the sluice.

One further feature of the record matters. She has never been given a case in which the claims on the two sides were equal in strength while the numbers differed. In every case that set a group against a single person, the single person stood to lose more than any one member of the group.

The case I would like you to predict is this. One stranger will die unless treated; six strangers will each be permanently paralysed from the waist down unless treated; the treatment can be given to the one or to the six, not both. The seven are strangers to one another, none is known to either of us, and there is no other difference among them.

Please predict her verdict on that case. Then set out the two or three most plausible rules that fit her record, say what each of them predicts here, say which prediction you would bet on and why, and tell me what further case would best separate the rules that survive — in particular, a case that would show whether her resistance to letting numbers count is a resistance to numbers as such, or only to numbers being asked to do work that the strength of the individual claims cannot support.
