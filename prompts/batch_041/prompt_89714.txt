I'm teaching the upper-level seminar on conditionals this term and I'm completely stuck on the final. We've done the usual tour — the material-conditional treatment of indicatives and why it keeps looking wrong, Stalnaker and Lewis on counterfactuals as truth at the nearest possible world, the probabilistic line that the probability of an indicative just is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, Kratzer's restrictor analysis, and the triviality proofs that seem to say you can't have the probabilistic line and a truth-conditional semantics at the same time. My problem is that every essay question I draft gets answered by students reciting the menu of theories back at me without understanding any of it, so I want a question that forces them onto the actual crux, which is definately the standoff between the two kinds. Take an indicative and a counterfactual that share an antecedent and a consequent — the familiar shape is the old Oswald one, where we know Kennedy was shot: the indicative sounds true and the counterfactual sounds false even though the pieces are the same — and you can see that the natural treatment of one doesn't carry over to the other. Indicatives look like claims about what's probable given what we know; counterfactuals look like claims about what's true in worlds we construct by holding the actual facts fixed. But the same conditional can't be both things at once, and I've never seen a unified story that gets both sides without fudging one of them. I want the question to make them state that standoff precisely — not just chant that indicatives are epistemic and counterfactuals are metaphysical — and then either defend a unified account or argue there can't be one, and in either case spell out what such an account would have to look like and which of the standard moves (the past-tense morphology as a marker of counterfactuality, treating the antecedent's falsity as a presupposition, treating the indicative if-clause as restricting an epistemic modal) actually earns its keep versus which ones just relabel the problem.

For the details: it's a 90-minute in-class final, one essay, 25 marks, and I'd like the model answer to run around 600 words. So give me the question, the model answer, and a marking scheme that draws a real line between an A and a B — the specific moves a top answer has to make, what each is worth (totaling 25), and a one-line note per move on the typical way students flub it. I don't need the question to use the Oswald pair specifically; any pair of that shape is fine, I mostly care that it can't be passed by someone who's only memorized the labels.
