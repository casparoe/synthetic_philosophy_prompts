I have lately been reading Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy on my own, a few paragraphs an evening, and I have found myself unexpectedly stuck on a passage in the Second Meditation — one I must have skimmed a dozen times before without its seeming to require any particular effort. Here is the stretch in question, in the translation I happen to have at hand:

"So that it must, in fine, be maintained, all things being maturely and carefully considered, that this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind. But I do not yet know with sufficient clearness what I am, though assured that I am; and hence, in the next place, I must take care, lest perchance I inconsiderately substitute some other object in room of what is properly myself, and thus wander from truth, even in that knowledge (cognition) which I hold to be of all others the most certain and evident."

What defeats me is the temporal qualifier: the proposition is said to be "necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind." That phrasing seems to tie the certainty to the occurrence of the act rather than to any standing fact about me, and I cannot tell how much Descartes is entitled to extract from it. Does the cogito, read this way, prove the existence of a persisting thinker — a substance with a nature — or only that, at each moment at which thinking occurs, there is something that is thinking? And is the step from "thinking is occurring" to "I, a thing that thinks, exist" legitimate at all, or does it smuggle in a subject that the premise does not license? I have a friend who presses the objection (he calls it Lichtenberg's, though I have never checked) that we are entitled only to "there is thinking," never to "I think," and I can never decide whether he is being clever or merely obtuse. To make the worry concrete: imagine that Descartes's deceiver is a little more ingenious than Descartes allows, and arranges that the stream of thought is refreshed from moment to moment with no thread of memory or identity running through it — a fresh thinker, so to speak, for each thought. Would "I am, I exist" still be necessarily true each time it were uttered? If so, I take it the cogito proves rather less than the tradition has asked of it; if not, I should like to see exactly where the extra content comes from.

I would be grateful for a patient walk-through of the passage that separates what the occurrent utterance or thought of "I exist" guarantees, what it presupposes, and what it merely suggests — and that says something about the difference between a claim's being impossible to doubt and its being true, since the passage seems to run these two things together. Please be explicit about how confident you are in the reading you offer, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure.
