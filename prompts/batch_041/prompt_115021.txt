I serve on the ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and I have been asked to draft a minority comment on a memo that has been circulating for the past month. The case is a woman in the middle stage of dementia. Several years ago, while competent, she signed a directive declining artificial nutrition and hydration in the event of advanced dementia. She no longer recognizes her family. She is not in pain; she eats with encouragement; on good days she says she wants to go on living. Because her swallowing is deteriorating, the team has proposed a feeding tube, and the question is whether to place it. The memo argues that the directive governs and that her present apparent wish to continue living cannot override it, and it makes its case with an analogy I keep running into: an advance directive is like a will. The competent woman was the testator; the document she signed is the instrument; the demented patient is the estate; the clinicians are the executors; and just as an estate cannot rewrite the will that created it, the present patient cannot revoke an instruction issued by the person she used to be. On that basis the memo recommends that her present wishes be treated as evidence about her welfare — data to be weighed — rather than as the authoritative voice of the person whose life is at stake.

Two labels will help, and I will use them throughout: precedent autonomy for the authority of the earlier instruction, and contemporaneous autonomy for the authority of the present self's wishes. I also want kept apart two things a directive might be doing when it binds: precommitting against weakness of will (where the later self, were she lucid, would endorse the earlier restraint) and precommitting against a change in values (where the later self, were she lucid, would repudiate the instruction and mean it).

Please audit the analogy. Make the mapping explicit, say where it holds, where it breaks, and whether the memo's conclusion can actually be carried by the analogy or only appeared to be. I would also like you to say which reading of the directive the analogy presupposes — an authoritative instruction, or fallible evidence of what she would now want — and whether it survives on the other reading. Please organize the response as follows.

1. The mapping. A correspondence table: testator, instrument, estate, heir, executor, probate, and the moment of death, with what plays each role in the clinical case, and a flag on any role that has no counterpart. About 300 words.
2. Where it holds. The features of testamentary succession that genuinely transfer to advance directives, and what work they do for the memo. About 250 words.
3. Where it breaks. The disanalogies, marked as to which ones bear on the memo's conclusion and which are harmless decoration. Please consider at least whether the property disposed of can be separated from the person it disposes of, whether the transition from author to subject has a determinate moment, whether the later self has been left the testator's power to revoke, and how much of a will's force comes from an apparatus of enforcement that the clinic does not have. About 400 words.
4. What the analogy quietly imports. The features of the source case it needs but the target lacks, and the conclusions that ride in on them. About 250 words.
5. Does the conclusion survive. Your verdict on whether the memo's recommendation is supported by the analogy or merely dressed in it, and what would have to be argued independently. About 250 words.
6. A better analogy. Assess the rival candidates I have heard floated — Odysseus at the mast, and an entrenched constitutional clause binding a later legislature — against the features that actually matter here, and offer your own if neither fits. About 200 words.
