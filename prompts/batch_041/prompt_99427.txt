Write a pantoum about the units of selection, in which a single act of apparent self-sacrifice among social animals — a sentinel that gives an alarm call and draws a predator's attention onto itself — is told three times over: once as a story about genes, once as a story about the individual organism, and once as a story about the group. I want the form's interlocking repetitions to do the argumentative work. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third of the next, and the poem closes by folding back onto its opening line; use that machinery to make the same words mean different things as the level of description shifts, so that the very lines that sound altruistic when the subject is the bird sound self-interested when the subject is the copies of its genes carried by its relatives, and sound like a public good when the subject is the flock. The refrain lines themselves should stay the same — only the level at which they are heard should change — and the last stanza, in returning to the first, should leave the reader unable to say at which level the act was really selected.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has followed the debate recognizes the positions it stages — gene-level selectionism, individual selection, and group or multilevel selection — rather than merely hearing them named, and it should engage, at least in passing, two of the debate's live joints: the difference between a trait's being selected and its being selected for, and the charge that group selection is either a genuine cause or merely gene-level bookkeeping retold. Aim for four or five quatrains. Do not let the poem settle the dispute in favor of any one level; the point of the repetition is that the same event sustains more than one accounting. It should read as a poem to someone who knows nothing about the debate and as a fair statement of the problem to someone who knows it well.
