I cover religion-and-politics for a magazine, and I keep a running list in my notebook of cases like the ones below, because my gut reactions to them don't add up and it's starting to bother me. These are all real clips or close paraphrases of things I've seen, with my own verdict attached:

A bishop testifies against a bill to bring back the death penalty. He opens with the sanctity of life in his tradition, then spends twenty minutes on wrongful convictions and the deterrence numbers. Verdict: fine, obviously fine, and I'd never dream of telling him to cut the religious part.

A state senator says on the floor that she's voting to ban abortion because her church teaches that life begins at conception, and when a reporter asks if she has any reason that doesn't depend on her church, she says she doesn't owe anybody a non-religious reason. Verdict: not fine. But I genuinely can't say what's different from the bishop.

A mostly-Muslim neighborhood association shows up at a zoning hearing to block a pork processing plant. Their case is basically: this is unclean, we don't want it here. Verdict: fine — weirdly fine, and fine in a way the abortion case isn't, and I can't articulate why.

A group campaigns against same-sex marriage, citing God's law. Verdict: not fine.

Same campaign, different spokesman, God never mentioned — it's all about children needing a mother and a father, and he cites a study I know has been shredded. Our opinion editor calls this reason-laundering. Verdict: not fine. But the reason can't be "he gave a secular argument," because he gave one.

A Catholic hospital won't perform a procedure it regards as complicity in killing, and asks the state for a carve-out. Verdict: fine, I'd fight for that one.

A pharmacist won't fill a prescription and also wants a carve-out. Verdict: not fine. These two look like the identical move and I keep insisting they're different.

A Quaker group lobbies against a war and cites the Inner Light. Verdict: fine. But I've got a clip of a pastor saying God told him to back a war, and there: not fine. Same structure, opposite verdicts, and I can feel you rolling your eyes.

An atheist argues against a policy from a full-on utilitarian worldview, repugnant conclusion and all. Verdict: fine. So whatever my rule is, it can't just be "no comprehensive doctrines allowed."

And a guy who says he opposes something because God says so, where the policy happens to be independently defensible on public-health grounds that he never bothers to mention. Verdict: fine, or at least I can't make myself care, which is awkward given the senator.

So: is there a principle that gets all of this, or close to it? The one I keep reaching for is "religious argument is fine when you're asking the state to leave you alone, and out of bounds when you're asking it to lean on somebody else" — but the pork plant and the pharmacist break it in opposite directions, so probly it needs repair. Test it against the list, fix it if it can be fixed, tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up, and then tell me what your fixed-up principle says about the case I actually have to file on next week: a wedding venue that won't host a ceremony because of the owner's faith.
