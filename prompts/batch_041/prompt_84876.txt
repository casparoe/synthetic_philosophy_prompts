"The Church-Turing thesis is the theorem, proved independently by Church and Turing in the mid-1930s, that every effectively calculable function is computable by a Turing machine. What gives it teeth is that Turing machines can simulate any other reasonable model of computation -- lambda calculus, recursive functions, whatever -- so the thesis really amounts to saying that all sufficiently powerful computers are equivalent, which is why your laptop and a supercomputer can in principle compute exactly the same things. Turing's version of the thesis, unlike Church's, is an empirical claim about the physical world rather than a mathematical one, and that's why it can't be proved: you'd need a machine that solves the halting problem to refute it, and nobody has built one. It also has a striking upshot for cognitive science: if the thesis is true, then the question of whether the mind is a computer is settled, and the only remaining empirical question is what kind of comptuer it is."
