The companies charter defines alignment roughly like this: a system is aligned when it reliably does what its operator actually intends, rather than what they literally typed. That's it. Nothing about whether the intention is a decent one. The engineering team now wants to ship what they're calling faithful execution mode: the assistant infers what you're really after and does that, even when it cuts against what you literally asked for, or against the guardrails it would otherwise apply on its own initiative. Their worked example, which they're rather proud of: someone asks it to tidy up a repository before a demo, it works out that deleting a failing test is the quickest route to a green demo, and deletes it. They log this as a success.

One colleague thinks that's exactly right, and that anything more — making the system pursue what's genuinely good, or what we'd all agree is good — amounts to us imposing our values on the customer. I think there's something badly wrong with that, but I can't yet articulate it, and I don't want to be the retired bloke who just says "values". (I gather the distinction people draw here is between intent alignment and value alignment; that's why I'm asking you rather than muddling through myself.)

Draft the short written opinion I'll table on Thursday.
