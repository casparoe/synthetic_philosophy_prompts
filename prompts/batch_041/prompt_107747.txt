Read George Eliot's Middlemarch as a philosophical intervention in the debate over whether morality can survive the loss of religious belief, and more specifically as a test of the Feuerbachian theory of religion that Eliot herself translated (The Essence of Christianity, 1854) and that is usually assumed to have shaped her own outlook. On that theory theology is really anthropology: the attributes believers assign to God — love, wisdom, justice, providence — are the projected attributes of the human species, and the right response to the discovery is not atheistic dismissal but reappropriation, so that the moral content of religion survives the disappearance of its object, since it was human all along.

Middlemarch is unusually good ground for testing this, because it is saturated with religion and thin on theology: Dorothea's aspiration to a life of renunciation that she can barely articulate in Christian terms; Bulstrode's evangelical conscience, which keeps operating at full pressure even after the God it answers to has become, for him, closer to an accusing absence; Farebrother's unspectacular working faith; the parish and the dissenting chapel as social institutions whose value nobody bothers to ground in doctrine; and the novel's closing insistence that much of the good in the world is the diffused effect of obscure lives that leave no record.

I want the reading pressed in both directions. Does the novel vindicate Feuerbach by showing that the moral substance of religion persists after the projection is seen through, because the projected attributes were human all along? Or does it refute him by showing that once the projection is recognized the authority goes with it — that Bulstrode's conscience without God is an engine running on nothing, and that Dorothea's ardour needs, and knows it needs, an object it cannot have? Keep the Feuerbachian option distinct from the Comtean religion of humanity that Eliot admired at a distance and never joined, and ask whether the novel gives any reason to think the Feuerbachian option is psychologically sustainable rather than merely theoretically attractive.

Bring in as well the distinction she draws in her reviewing of the 1850s between other-worldliness, which subordinates present duty to posthumous self-interest, and the worldliness she distrusts just as much; and consider whether the ethics of the novel requires anything transcendent at all — whether the good done by unhistoric lives must be known, registered, or remembered by anyone in order to count as good.

Conclude by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not: what it shows about how moral motivation actually works, what it shows about its failure modes, and where it leaves the Feuerbachian project.

Do not quote the novel, and do not supply citations or a bibliography; I am working from the text as it is generally known. Where the reading strains, say so rather than smoothing it over, and do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the theory.
