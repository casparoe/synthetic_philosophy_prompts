I am a novelist, and I have lately become an amateur type designer. My new book is set in a city whose guilds keep their ledgers in a script of their own, and rather than commission the alphabet, I have spent the better part of a year drawing it myself. It has twenty-three letters, each written in a single unbroken stroke so that a clerk with a reed pen can fill a page without lifting the hand; and because the book reproduces many ledgers and letters, I have drawn dozens of variants of each character, so that each scribe's hand reads as an individual. The same letters also have to survive being cut into the guilds' wooden signboards, where the strokes thicken and the small counters close up, and there is a blind archivist in the story who reads the older ledgers by running a thumb along the incised lines.

My trouble is this. I showed a sheet of my 'a's to a friend who designs type professionally, and she objected to one of them—a variant I am fond of, in which the bowl has been flattened into a shallow trough and the tail drawn up into a small hook. It sits in the same family as the rest, drawn at the same stroke weight and the same pen angle. She says it has stopped being an 'a' and become a private mark. I say that a reader meeting it in the middle of a word, with the other letters around it, would read it as an 'a' without hesitation.

The argument has grown larger than I expected, and I find I lack the vocabulary for it. She believes a letter has a core—some feature or set of features that every genuine instance must carry, and that my variant has lost. I am not so sure. When I look at the 'a's of the typefaces on my shelves they seem to share no single trait; the resemblance is more diffuse, shifting from one to the next the way faces in a family do, and what they may really share is only the fact that each has been made to sit beside the same other letters and do the same work. Yet I cannot simply say that whatever a reader will accept is an 'a', or the alphabet would dissolve into whatever I please.

How should I think about this? If a letterform's identity is not secured by any one feature, what holds it together? Is there a principled way to tell whether a variant has crossed a line, or is it finally only a matter of taste and convention? And does it matter that the letters form a system—that an 'a' is partly what it is because it must be told apart from a 'd' and a 'q'? I would be grateful for an argument I could actually use at the drawing table.
