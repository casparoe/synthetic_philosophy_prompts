I write about science and ideas for a living, which means I get ambushed by my smarter friends on a fairly regular basis, and last week one of them finally got me. Quick setup so you don't need any literature (and please don't go looking for any, this is a diagnosis job, not a bibliography one): picture a soldier — the case circulates under the name "the Judy Benjamin problem" — who is lost in the dark in a war zone. She's 50/50 on being in Blue territory vs. Red territory. If she's in Blue, she thinks she's either at Blue HQ with probablity 1/4 or in the second company area with probability 3/4. So overall she's 1/8 on HQ. Her radio crackles, and the operator says he can't tell whether she's in the second company area or in Red territory. Everyone agrees this tells her something — it kills off HQ, at least on the supposition that she's in Blue. It is not flat news that she isn't at HQ; he didn't say that, he said he can't tell second company from Red. Now here's what's bugging me: three of us worked out what she should now think about being in Blue and got three different numbers. Treat what she learned as the plain proposition "not HQ" and conditionalize, and Blue jumps to 4/7. Use the rule that learning a conditional shouldn't budge your probability for its antecedent, and Blue stays at 1/2. My friend who likes minimizing relative entropy gave a formula and insisted the answer is neither of those. All three recipes look principled. They dissagree.

My friend thinks this isn't a curiosity, it's a reductio, and that I should just swallow it. His argument, as I've reconstructed it: (1) Learning just is minimal revision — your new credences are the credence function closest to your old one among those compatible with what you learned, and ordinary conditionalization is only the special case where "compatible" carves the space cleanly in two. (2) What Judy learned is exactly the constraint that HQ is ruled out conditional on Blue, nothing more. (3) Infinitely many credence functions meet that constraint and disagree about Blue. (4) "Closest" requires a distance between credence functions, and nothing in (1) or (2) fixes which distance. (5) So (1) and (2) together do not determine Judy's posterior. (6) If the theory doesn't determine it, any distribution meeting the constraint is rationally permitted. (7) Every case of learning can be rewritten as a constraint of this shape — learning a conditional, learning that something is merely probable, learning something vague. (8) So Bayesian updating never determines what you ought to believe; it only fences off the forbidden, and only once you've already picked your distance. (9) But that's absurd: when you learn something flat, like that it's raining, conditionalizing isn't optional, and nobody thinks "believe whatever is compatible with the evidence" is a serious epistemology. So a step has to go.

I'd rather keep (1) and (9) if I possibly can — I like the minimal-revision picture, and I'm not abandoning the idea that evidence constrains belief — but I'll sacrifice (2) if that's the price; I'd just want to know what the radio message actually tells her if it isn't that. Please take the role of a formal epistemologist / philosopher of probability refereeing this like a submission: start by brainstorming where a chain of this shape typically leaks — an equivocation, a scope shift, an illicit generalization from one case to all cases — then commit to the step you'd reject, explain why it looked so fine to my friend, and price the alternatives. Be concrete about what each repair job predicts for Judy's number, and don't dodge the possibility that the argument is valid and it's (9) that has to go, because if that's the answer I need to hear it straight.
