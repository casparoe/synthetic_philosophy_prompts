I chair the bicentennial commissioning committee of our city's historical society, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's vote. Our endowment funds exactly one major public-facing project this cycle, and we are down to two finalists.

The first is a scholarly biography of the man who ran this city for nearly thirty years in the late nineteenth century — mayor, railroad director, hospital founder. His personal papers are enormous and largely unpublished: letters, a diary, drafts of speeches. The proposal argues that his life is the clearest lens on how the city's institutions were actually built, and that the biography can quote him at length in his own words.

The second is a microhistory of a single block of tenements demolished in the 1960s. There are no letters or diaries from the people who lived there. The two historians would reconstruct their lives almost entirely from records produced by the institutions that policed them — police ledgers, poor-law applications, court testimony, tax rolls — and they propose to read those records against the grain, looking past the officials' categories to recover the residents' own strategies and conflicts. They argue that this is the only way the bicentennial can speak to the parts of the city that never got a monument.

A colleague has already circulated a memo that I think reframes the choice. She says the real criterion is not elite versus ordinary but which project lets its subjects speak in their own voice, and that on that criterion the biography wins easily, because the mayor left us his own account of himself while the tenants survive only as objects of other people's paperwork. The microhistorians reply that a self-written diary is not a transparent window either — that the mayor's letters are themselves a performance aimed at posterity, and that their own "against the grain" reading can get closer to lived experience than a curated self-portrait can. I find both of these arguments stronger than the usual slogans about history from below, and I don't think the committee has faced them squarely.

Please draft the opinion I'll circulate. Recommend one project, with reasons, and give the strongest honest statement of the case for the one you reject — I want the losing side to feel that its best argument was actually met, not waved away. Our mission statement is genuinely ambiguous about whether the bicentennial should aim at scholarly significance or at broad public resonance, so you will have to say how you resolve that tension rather than assume it away. Do not include citations, a reading list, or references to specific scholars; argue the substance. Write it as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a careful letter to colleagues who will read it once before they vote.
