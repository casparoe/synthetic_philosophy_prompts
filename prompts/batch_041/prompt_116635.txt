I retired from geology about four years ago — forty years of core samples and seismic lines, mostly — and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which has turned out to be alot harder than the rocks. Right now I'm stuck on something a poker-playing friend of mine cooked up. He calls it the Long Toss, and here are the rules exactly as he gives them: you flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first head comes on toss number n, the house pays you (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / (n+1) dollars. So heads right away pays $1, heads first on the second toss costs you $1.33, third toss pays $2, fourth costs you $3.20, fifth pays $5.33, sixth costs you $9.14, and it keeps on like that, the prizes roughly doubling while the chance of ever getting to them halves. The textbook recipe says multiply each payoff by its probability and add, and you get 1 − ln 2, about 31 cents, so you should be willing to pay up to that to sit down. But my friend says that's a swindle: the terms only add up to anything because they alternate and shrink, and if you group the very same outcomes in a different order you can make the total come out to any number you like, or not come out at all, and the game itself contains nothing that tells you which grouping is the right one. He says the game has no expected value and the textbook just quietly picks an order of summation and hopes nobody notices. I know about the St. Petersburg version where the expectation runs off to infinity; this seems different, and in some ways worse, because there the number is at least honestly infinite. I cant tell whether my friend is cheating or whether he's found a real hole, and I'd like to see what happens if I take the hole seriously.

So here's what I'm after. Take the standard decision rules and rate them as a general policy for games like this one, not just for this one: (1) expected value maximization, compute it and go with it; (2) maximin, find the worst thing that can happen under each option and pick the option whose worst case is least bad; (3) minimax regret, pick the option whose worst-case shortfall against what you'd have gotten if you'd known how things would go is smallest; (4) maximax, go for the best possible outcome; (5) keep expected value but stop treating dollars as linear in value — replace money with a utility that flattens out, so no prize can be worth more than some ceiling; (6) simply refuse to play anything with an unbounded payoff or a non-convergent expectation; (7) compare games against each other rather than in isolation — look at the difference between two games' payoffs, which might behave itself even when each game's expectation on its own doesn't, and decide on that basis. Give each one a score out of 10 for how much you'd trust it as a general rule for money games, sort them into tiers and put the tiers in order, and give me a sentence or two on why each lands where it does.

Then, separately: which of the seven would you actually use on the Long Toss, and does adopting it make me give up anything I'd want to keep? I have a pension and a suspicious amount of faith in diversification, so if your answer is "refuse anything with an unbounded payoff," I want to know what else that throws out with the bathwater, and whether the rule I end up with is one I could actually defend to my friend or just a fancy way of saying I don't like the game.
