Could you take the role of a philosopher of mind who works on teleology and on what computational explanations do and don't explain — someone who'll be unsentimental — and give me comments? Before you start critiquing, spend a minute brainstorming what the strongest version of the view I'm sketching would be, so you're not just knocking down a strawman, and think it through carefully rather than giving me your first reaction.

The gist: everyone like to say that Darwin and Turing pulled the same trick in seperate domains. Darwin showed you can get the appearance of design without a designer; Turing showed you can get the appearance of thinking without a thinker. I want to argue that's too neat, and that the two moves actually pull in different directions.

Darwin's move is about production: comprehension isn't needed to make something competent. Selection does the work, and the understanding never has to show up anywhere in the history of the thing. Turing's move is about the exercise, not the origin: once you've got a mechanism turning out the right answers, the fact that nobody's home inside isn't an extra fact about it. The output is the whole story, and anything you add on top is a fifth wheel. So Darwin says you don't need comprehension to get competence; Turing says you don't need it to have competence.

Take both and comprehension starts to look like a word with no work to do — never the thing that explains the behaviour, either at the origin or in the running. That's the conclusion I want to resist, but without reaching for a soul in the machine. My fix, and the part I'm least sure of:

Distinguish two ways a competence can relate to its own rationale. A competence can track a rationale — the behaviour is well explained by saying the system is doing X because X works, but the system has no representation of that because. Or a competence can own a rationale — the system represents the rationale and can be assessed against it. My claim is that tracking versus owning isn't a difference in the competence itself, it's a difference in what the system can be wrong about. A system that only tracks a rationale can fail: it can get damaged, or the world can shift and it can stop working. It can't be mistaken. Being mistaken requires that there be something in the system that is answerable to the rationale, so that the rationale can serve as a standard the system is held to from the inside. Comprehension, then, isn't a further product of the mechanism; it's a further order of competence — a competence about competences, the capacity to represent the rationale that a first-order competence merely implements. That's supposed to show why the two inversions don't add up to eliminativism: they take comprehension out of the production and the exercise of first-order competence, and leave untouched the possibility of a system that is competent about its own competences.

The example I keep using: the slime mould that stops reacting to a repellent once you've dosed it enough times, and then starts reacting again after a rest. Nobody thinks it has an opinion about whether the stuff is harmless — but it also isn't just broken, it's tracking something real about its situation. Compare a researcher who announces she's concluded the stuff is harmless, because she confused habituation with tolerance. She's made a mistake. The mould hasn't. I want to say that's not a difference in how the two systems behave; it's a difference in what there is in the system to be wrong about.

What I'd like from you: (1) is the tracking/owning distinction doing real work, or is it the old representation-versus-mechanism line with a new name? (2) does the mistake-versus-failure move mark the line properly, or does it smuggle in exactly the normativity I said I wasn't assuming? (3) is the Darwin/Turing contrast fair, or am I just reading two thinkers' emphases as two logical points? (4) do I actually escape the fifth-wheel worry or only relocate it? And tell me where it's boring, not just where it's wrong. Also flag it if my use of rationale is doing something shifty — I keep wanting to say the reason the behaviour works and the reason the system has, and those are clearly not the same thing, which might be the whole paper or might be a sign I've confused myself.
