I'm doing a part-time MA in philosophy, mainly so I can argue with my friends more efficiently, and we've just hit the module on virtual worlds and virtual harms. I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong-adjacent blogs, so my instinct is to hunt for one clean principle, but the more cases I write down the more I suspect I'm smuggling in two different senses of 'real' — one about what exists and one about what matters — and quietly switching between them. I've got a provisional rule of my own that I'd like you to stress-test: a virtual act is a real act, and its harm is a real harm, exactly to the extent that it sets back the real interests of actual people. But I don't trust it, because I think it either says too much or too little depending on how I fill in 'interests'.

Here are my verdicts, going case by case. Someone hacks my account and moves my rare sword — 200 hours of grinding, worth maybe £300 on the grey market — to their own character: that's plainly theft, a real wrong, and should be punishable. A player kills my avatar in a PvP zone the game openly advertises: no wrong at all, even though I'm annoyed for an hour. A group of players follows a woman around an MMO, penning her avatar into corners and using voice chat to harass her: that's serious harassment, ban-worthy and arguably worse than the sword. Someone builds an avatar of my real face and does degrading things with it in a social VR space, then shares screenshots: real harm, and somehow worse than the item theft. Two consenting adults role-play something atrocious in a private instance: no harm, leave them alone. A company sells me virtual land for £5,000 and then shuts the servers down with no refund: it feels like expropriation and I want to say something's wrong, but I can't tell whether it's theft or just a bad contract I signed. And a friend's AI companion gets switched off and she genuinely mourns it: I think her grief is real and the loss is real, yet I don't think any wrong was done to the chatbot itself.

The pair that's really bugging me is the PvP kill versus the sword theft versus the harassment, because all three are 'just pixels' and I give them wildly different verdicts — and then the face-avatar case versus the private role-play, where the virtual content is equally vile but only one of them I call a harm. So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, say what each clause is doing and which case forces it, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, griefing someone's memorial service held in-game), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. If a principle that saves every verdict has to grow extra epicycles, I'd rather you told me that and named the one verdict I should abandon for the simple version. And please separate what's actually settled among people who work on virtuality and the metaphysics of games from what's genuinely contested — I don't want one camp's line presented to me as the consensus, even if it's the camp I'd probably end up in.
