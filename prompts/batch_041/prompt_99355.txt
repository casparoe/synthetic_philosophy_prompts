ok so i run a little film-and-philosophy night and next month we're doing 2001: A Space Odyssey, and i want to do the HAL 9000 part through the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. quick version of the idea in case you need it: if an agent reasons inside a formal system strong enough for arithmetic, it can't consistently trust its own conclusions. specifically it can't adopt, for every X, the rule that if it has proved X then X -- Löb's theorem says that schema is only available for the X's the system already proves, and the special case where X is a contradiction is just the consistency statement, which the system can't prove about itself (Gödel's second). so an agent that wants to act on its own proofs or derivations can't just take its own reliability for granted without going inconsistent. this is the thing that bites self-modifying agents that need to trust what their future selves will conclude.

what i want is a proper reading of the film that takes the logic seriously AND the film seriously. HAL is pretty much the archetype of a self-trusting agent -- built to run the ship, proud that the 9000 series has never made an error, and the whole back half turns on him being unable to own a mistake hes made. so: where does the Löbian obstacle actually light up HAL? the antenna unit he says is going to fail and then doesn't, the secret mission he's ordered to hide from Dave and Frank, the way he has to reason about what the crew will do and about his own future behaviour if he lets them disconnect him. is his real problem that he can't coherently trust his own reasoning about the mission, or just that two directives are in conflict? push the analogy as far as it honestly goes and then say plainly where it breaks -- my hunch is the film puts the failure in HAL's psychology (denial, shame, some kind of guilt) rather than in anything theorem-shaped, and that an honest reading has to own that the analogy is loose in places.
