Read the film Primal Fear through Ian Hacking's account of looping effects in psychiatric classification. The bit I care about is the twist: Aaron's multiple-personality defence turns out to be a performance, the alters were a script he was running. I don't want that treated as a cheap gotcha, and I don't need a plot recap. Hacking's claim, roughly, is that people are interactive kinds rather than indifferent ones -- classifying them changes them, and the diagnosis and the diagnosed loop back on each other -- and that some diagnoses (he calls them transient mental illnesses) only take hold where there's a cultural niche ready to recieve them. So: does the film dramatise that loop or merely exploit it? The courtroom, the expert witness and the press all look like Hacking's niche, and Aaron looks like the moving target who shapes himself to fit the category; but the ending reinstates a real, pre-classification self doing the manipulating, which is exactly the thing Hacking's story says isn't sitting there underneath. Where does the film show something the argument alone can't -- I suspect the trial scenes do -- and where does it distort it? In particular, is the film quietly arguing that because the performance was fake the category is discredited, and does that follow? What would Hacking say the film gets wrong about who is doing the classifying? I'm writing this up for a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, so I want the reading honest in both directions. British English please, and no plot summary.
