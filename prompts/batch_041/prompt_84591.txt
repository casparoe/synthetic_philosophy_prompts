Act as a scholar of late antique Christian thought — someone who has spent years inside Augustine's moral and pastoral writing and knows the grain of his arguments, not merely his conclusions — and reason in stages, carefully, before you commit to any verdicts. Begin by brainstorming: set out the principles before you apply them, and show your work as you go.

Here is my situation. I am a novelist. The central character of my new book is a woman who has made what I take to be Augustine's position on lying the rule of her life — that it is never permitted, however kindly meant, however gravely needed — and who is driven, over the course of the book, to test that rule against the worst that can happen. I want her conscience to be genuinely his, not mine in period dress. So I need to know what Augustine would have ruled on situations he never lived to see, and I need your reasoning to be anchored in what he actually held rather than in the modern assumption that a lie is sometimes the loving thing.

To fix terms: I am working from his early book On Lying, where he writes that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind," and locates the fault of the liar in "the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind." I read him as caring about the intention to deceive, not the falsity of the words. Correct me if I have him wrong.

I do not want a bibliography or a reading list. I want your reconstruction and your predictions, and I want to see how they are derived.

1. The framework first. Set out the load-bearing principles of Augustine's position as you understand them: his account of what makes an utterance a lie; his insistence that one may say something false without lying, and something true and still lie; his separation of lying from speaking figuratively or in jest; his grading of lies by what they aim at; his refusal of the well-meant "helpful" lie; and his treatment of the lie told for someone's spiritual good — the problem he took up in his later book against lying, written against those who wished to pass as heretics in order to expose heretics. Tell me which of these principles does the real work and which are corollaries, and flag anything you are unsure he actually held.

2. Then the predictions. For each case below — none of which he could have encountered — give me his verdict, the reasoning from his principles step by step, and an explicit mark of where you are extrapolating beyond anything he considered. Where a case does not fit his categories at all, say so, and say what distinction he would have had to invent to handle it.

(a) My own trade. A novelist invents events that never happened, in the mouths of people who never lived; a reader who believed every sentence would be badly misled. Is the novelist lying? Is the unreliable narrator who deceives the reader lying? Where does the intention to deceive sit when the convention of fiction is understood on both sides?

(b) The fabricated video. Someone manufactures footage of a real person saying something they never said, intending no one to be fooled — only to amuse, or to demonstrate how easily we are fooled. Is there a lie here, and if so, whose?

(c) The machine. A system assembles fluent false statements; it has no beliefs and no intentions at all. Can it lie? If not, has Augustine's framework any name for what it does?

(d) The medical lie. A doctor, believing the falsehood will steady her patient, tells a woman with a terminal diagnosis that she will recover.

(e) The lie to the state. A woman lies on a form to obtain a drug she cannot afford, for a neighbour's child. No one is deceived but a bureaucracy; the lie harms no one she can see.

(f) Speech indifferent to truth. The politician, the advertiser, the talker who does not care whether what he says is true so long as it produces its effect. Is this lying, or something his framework has no name for?

(g) The inward lie. Self-deception: the double heart turned on itself.

(h) The instrumental lie. Pretending to be what one is not, in order to draw close to someone and do them good — the undercover journalist, the missionary, the infiltrator. He condemned the version of this aimed at heretics; I want to know how far the condemnation generalizes.

3. The case I most want your honesty on. Lying to save a life: the householder who tells the officer at the door that no one is hidden in the attic. Most of my readers will find the lie obligatory and Augustine's prohibition monstrous. Tell me whether the monstrosity, if it is one, lies in his principle or in his application of it, and whether he has any move available to him that is not a surrender.

4. Last, the question that is really about me. If Augustine were to read my manuscript, would he tell me that the novel itself is a long lie — that I have no business making things up and calling it art? And if he would not, what is the difference between what I do and what my unreliable narrator does? End by telling me, plainly, what it would cost a person to live as my protagonist means to live, and whether Augustine would call that cost a price worth paying.

Aim for rigor rather than coverage. Where you must choose between fidelity to his texts and usefulness to my book, choose fidelity, and tell me that you have.
