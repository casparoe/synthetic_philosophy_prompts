I co-run a small tutoring center with my sister — eight part-time tutors, three rooms above a pharmacy, and a payroll that only closes if people are honest about their hours. Three weeks ago we found out that one of our tutors, a nineteen-year-old we hired last spring, had been billing us for sessions he never ran; it comes to a little over two hundred dollars spread across six weeks. He didn't deny it, he apologized, he said he was short on rent, and he asked to keep the job. Those are the whole of the facts, and they've turned into the worst argument my sister and I have had in years, which is strange, because neither of us much cares about the two hundred dollars.

What we actually disagree about is what we're running here. I want to let him go, and not out of vindictiveness — I was broke at nineteen myself. My view is that a place like ours is a machine for making habits, and that habits are built the way anything is built: by repetition, by scaffolding, by doing the honest thing until honesty is simply the shape you're in. You don't wait for a teenager to feel like being truthful; you arrange the shop so that truthfulness is the path of least resistance and then you hold people to it, because a person's raw bent is not yet a person's character. My sister thinks that is backwards and a little creepy. She thinks the kid is decent — that decent is what people are — and that what we saw was a decent thing that got squeezed: rent, no family money, two owners who weren't paying attention. Let him go, she says, and you haven't punished a flaw, you've confirmed a lesson. She'd rather fix the circumstances, sit down with him, and treat the whole thing as a relationship that got damaged and can be mended. She also insists that a rule followed only because someone is watching teaches nothing, and that if we answer one kid's bad month with cameras and hour-audits we'll rot the very thing we're trying to build. I say a rule that gets followed is doing its job and the motive catches up later; she says a rule followed for the wrong reason is just theater. Here is the part that keeps nagging me, and it's why I'm writing instead of flipping a coin. Each of us seems to hold the belief that ought to make the other one hopeful. She believes people are good at the bottom, and yet she is the one who talks as though a person can be spoiled by his circumstances — as though the good thing is tender, and can be starved or cut back, and once it's gone you may not get it again. I believe people left to themselves are unreliable, appetite-driven, not to be trusted with a cash box, and yet I'm the one who thinks any of them can be remade by the right regimen — as if nothing in a person is ever finally lost. We both sound inconsistent, and I suspect we're re-running an old argument that has a name, one of the famous ones, though I can't place it and I don't want to guess.

So I'd like two things, and I'd rather have the second than the first. Tell me who has the better position — and tell me honestly whether it bears on what to do about this kid at all, or whether the philosophy is just scenery and the real question is simply what kind of shop we want to run. Name the disagreement if it has a name; I want the substance, not a reading list. And be straight with me about my own framing: if the two positions collapse into one once you get precise about what each of us means by a person's nature, say so; if I've caricatured my sister, or if my tidy little reversal is too tidy, say that too. I'd rather be told my question is confused than be handed a verdict dressed up as a deduction.
