I'm a third-year undergrad in a philosophy of science seminar and we've just finished the induction unit — Hume, then Goodman's grue, then two weeks of Bayesian confirmation where the professor kept saying the paradox just falls out of your priors and then moving on, which drove me a little nuts. For my final project I want to hand the class a decision tree that sorts people into the positions on this rather than into the names of the positions, so: build me one. Start from a commitment I think everyone in the room actually shares — that the record of a hundred thousand green emeralds before some future date supports the hypothesis that every emerald is green, and supports the grue hypothesis (green if first examined before that date, blue otherwise) to exactly the same degree — and then make every node below that a question someone could answer without already knowing which view it leads to. The nodes should be the real fault lines rather than a list of authors: is the paradox a fact about the support relation itself, or about which predicates are allowed to enter it at all; if it's about predicates, is grue's problem that it isn't a natural kind, that it isn't entrenched in our practice, that it isn't causally hooked up to anything, or just that it's a gerrymandered mess; if it's about the support relation, is the move to justify induction, to show it stands in no need of justification, to replace it with something else (Popper-ish, prediction without confirmation), or to say the whole thing is a pseudo-problem about the word probable; and for the Bayesians, whether a prior over hypotheses plus some simplicity measure actually solves it or just renames it. Aim for around twelve leaves, and give each one the name of the position (or unnamed, if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on the answer it has to swallow, and no citations please, I don't need a reading list.

Two things I specifically want in there, because otherwise everyone will just recite the same four views. First, take seriously the case of a community whose language has grue as the basic predicate and green as the defined one — does your entrenchment leaf have to say that community is simply right, and if so is that a feature or an embarassment? Second, an automated mineral lab: a classifier trained on samples that all happen to have been collected before a certain date, now having to judge a new one, where the training set contains a spurious correlate that held for every sample in it, so its grue is baked into the weights rather than into anybody's language. Where does each leaf land on that one, and does the answer come from the same machinery as the emerald case or from somewhere else? Also include a leaf for the crowd who say there are no universal rules of induction and every inference is licensed by local material facts (Norton, I think? I forget the name) and one for the people who want a single universal prior computed from description length.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — my guess is that the entrenchment view and the natural-kind view come out identical on every question I can think of, and I'd like to know whether that's a failure of my imagination or a real fact about the two views — and note which combinations of answers come out empty, with a quick diagnosis of whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Handout format please: indented branches, the question at each node, the answers written on the edges. And don't open with do you think induction can be justified, that gives the whole thing away.
