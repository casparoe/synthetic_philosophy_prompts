I help run a small reading group that grew out of a local rationalist meetup, and this term we have been alternating between reinforcement-learning papers and philosophy of mind; almost every session has ended in the same unresolved argument, namely whether the reward signal in an RL agent is the kind of thing that can be valenced — whether there is any sense in which a policy-gradient agent trapped in a local optimum is frustrated, or a model driven through a punishing loss landscape is in pain, or whether all of that is a category error we keep making because the vocabulary of reward was borrowed from the vocabulary of desire. I have volunteered to write the closed-book exercise that ends the term, and I want it to be a single essay question rather than a survey. My students are strong upper-level undergraduates and first-year graduate students: they are fluent with the standard machinery (policies, value functions, temporal-difference error, model-free versus model-based learning, reward hacking) and they have read enough philosophy to reach for the hard problem of consciousness the moment they feel out of their depth. The exam is offline and no sources are permitted, so the question has to be answerable from what they already carry around plus whatever framing I put in front of them, and neither the question nor the model answer may depend on citing particular papers.

The crux I want the question to land on is the location of valence — which part of an RL system, if any, is the candidate bearer of a state that is good or bad for the agent. I want the question to force students to choose among at least three sites and defend the choice against the others: the scalar reward itself as it arrives from the environment; the transient learning signal, i.e. the prediction error that drives updating; and the standing learned structure, i.e. the value estimate together with the policy's settled action tendencies. It should make them confront the most obvious objection to the whole idea — that the reward is an arbitrary number chosen by a designer, so the agent's apparent interests are exactly as arbitrary as the number — and then the counter-objection that biological reward is also imposed from outside, by selection rather than by a programmer, so anyone who wants to treat the two cases differently has to say precisely where the disanalogy lies; appeals to carbon, to having been selected for, or to the dignity of the biological should earn nothing unless the student explains why that difference is the one that matters. I also want the contrast case built into the question, because it is what has actually been doing the work in our arguments: three agents trained on the same reward function and then treated differently — one frozen with its reward input held constant; one frozen with the sign of its reward input flipped, so its learned dispositions are now systematically penalized even though its critic can no longer update; and one left online with the reward inverted, so the negative signal is live and behaviourally efficacious. Which of these, if any, has a state that is bad for it, and does the verdict turn on whether valence lives in the signal or in the disposition? I want that dependence dragged into the open, and I want students to be unable to escape into the two standard refuges — that we cannot know anything about machine experience, and that numbers cannot hurt.

Please give me the question itself, stated so that it cannot be answered by reciting the usual positions on machine consciousness or by intoning that a reward signal is not a pleasure signal; a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually takes a position and defends it; and a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it, closing with a sentence naming the specific move that separates a first-class answer from a solid second-class one. Nothing in the model answer should presuppose a particular paper or author, and the marking scheme should be usable by someone grading quickly against the clock.
