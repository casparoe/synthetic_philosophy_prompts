I've drafted a short paper in the philosophy of psychiatry and would like your comments on the argument. Please don't send me a reading list or citations — I know the literature and can chase references myself, and I don't want a survey of who has said what. I want you to engage with the draft as it stands.

The draft is titled Closed-Loop Classification: What Happens to Looping Effects When the Classifier Learns. Here it is.

§1. Hacking's looping effects. When we classify people, the classified can come to know how they are classified, and that knowledge changes them; because they change, the category's extension and its characteristic presentation change too. The category and its members co-evolve. Hacking called such categories interactive kinds, contrasting them with indifferent kinds like quarks, which do not take note of how we describe them, and his examples were drawn from psychiatry and the human sciences: the succession of diagnoses for what was once called multiple personality, the shifting boundary of autism, the career of the concept of child abuse.

The structure of the phenomenon, as he set it up, is a loop between two things: a classifying practice — clinicians, manuals, institutions — and a classified population. The loop runs because there is a channel between them, through which the category travels to the people it names: the clinic, the case report, the memoir, the patient community. That channel is human, slow, and lossy, which is why the loop has a period of years or decades and why the category can drift in a direction nobody intended.

§2. The channel collapses. My claim is that when the classification is implemented as a predictive model, the loop stops being a relation between two things. The model's output is delivered back to the person as a description of what they are, and the person's subsequent self-report is collected as training signal for the next version of the model. There is no longer a lossy channel between a practice and a population; the practice sits inside the population's self-description, and the self-description sits inside the practice's training data. I call this closed-loop classification. The two relata of Hacking's loop have become a single feedback system with one objective function.

§3. The inversion. Hacking's worry about looping effects was broadly epistemic: if human kinds loop, then the categories of psychiatry are moving targets, and their failure to stabilize is evidence that they do not correspond to anything in nature — they are made, not found. I claim that closed-loop classification inverts this worry. Under closed-loop conditions the category tends to become more stable, not less: because the loop is trained to minimize prediction error on self-reports, and because patients are motivated to be legible to the system that is describing them, the population's self-descriptions are pulled toward the region of the space the classifier already separates cleanly. The target stops moving.

And that, I want to argue, is the worse outcome. Hacking's instability at least left open the possibility that the category was tracking something. A category that stabilizes because the classifier has been installed in the classified has stabilized for a reason that has nothing to do with whether it names a natural kind. Stability, under these conditions, is a measure of the classifier's grip rather than of the category's validity.

§4. So what. If this is right, the forms of psychiatric critique that descend from Hacking — the worry that categories are performative, that diagnosis shapes the diagnosed — are not wrong, but they are pitched at the wrong level once the classifier is a model. The question is no longer whether the category loops through the patient, but whether the patient's self-understanding has become part of the model's input in a way that makes the category's stability uninformative. This suggests a test for a category under closed-loop conditions: hold the classifier fixed and see whether the category's extension still holds up, or whether it was being sustained by the loop. If the latter, the category has no independent support.

What I want from you:

1. Is the central move — that closed-loop classification has one relatum where Hacking had two — a real structural claim or a metaphor dressed up as one? Tell me which, and give me the argument either way.
2. Does the stability-inversion argument go through? I suspect I am sliding between two senses of stability: the statistical reliability of a prediction, and the fixity of a natural kind. If the slide is real, say so, and tell me whether it can be repaired or whether it sinks §3.
3. Am I reinventing something? If a version of Hacking's view, or of the broader interactive-kinds literature, already anticipates the algorithmic case, tell me in plain terms what it amounts to — no citations needed — and whether it leaves anything for me to say.
4. Give me a verdict: which of the three claims (channel collapse, stability inversion, the diagnostic test) is the weakest, and what should I cut or strengthen.

Please take a definite position rather than laying the options side by side. If you think the central claim is wrong, say so and say why; if you think it is right but mis-argued, name the step that fails. I would rather have a committed reading I can argue with than a balanced survey.
