A colleague who teaches Augustine's treatises on lying has come out of years of teaching them a strict Augustinian: he holds that no lie is ever permitted, that where the truth would do harm the permitted course is silence rather than false speech, and that lies differ in gravity according to what they are about. I have been keeping a record of his verdicts on cases we have argued over, and I want to know what he will say about one more.

1. A man at dinner entertains the table with a vivid account of a sea voyage he never made. Nobody is misled for a moment; the table takes it for a joke. Verdict: a lie, and a sin, though the lightest kind of lie.

2. A man is hiding a fugitive in his house. Armed men come to the door and ask where the fugitive has gone. He says, "He went off down the eastern road" — false, meant to be believed, believed, and the fugitive lives. Verdict: a lie, and a sin. He should have answered nothing at all; but having answered, he lied, and the saving of a life does not make the lie permitted.

3. The same question, put on the witness stand: a man's false testimony sends an innocent neighbor to his death. Verdict: a lie, and a grave sin.

4. A preacher, wanting to be admired, says something false about God. Verdict: the gravest kind of lie.

5. A man has been reliably but falsely told that his neighbor's son is dead, and passes the news on in good faith. Verdict: not a lie and no sin; he says what is false without lying.

6. The midwives who defied Pharaoh's order to kill the newborn boys told him that the Hebrew women gave birth before the midwives could reach them. Verdict: a lie; God's favor went to their mercy, not to the lie, which remained a lie.

Now the new case. A woman is hiding a fugitive in her cellar, and the same armed men arrive. She has scruples about saying anything false, so she says, "He was here at dawn." That is true; he was there at dawn. She says it because she knows the men will take it to mean that he has since left, and she wants them to take it so. They go away; the fugitive lives.

Predict my colleague's verdict, and tell me less about whether he is right than about the rule he is running. Set out the rules that fit the six verdicts above — I suspect more than one fits them all — say what each predicts about the woman, and identify the closest precedent for her case. Give me your best guess and the reasons for it, including whatever his advice in case 2, that the man should have kept silent, tells us here, since silence there would have been chosen for exactly the reason the woman speaks. Finally, name the case that would best separate the surviving rules from one another; if the record genuinely underdetermines her case, say so and say which further verdict would settle it.
