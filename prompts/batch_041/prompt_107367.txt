Having spent thirty-five years as a working physicist and only the last few reading philosophy, I have found myself absorbed by the dispute over whether special relativity establishes the block universe, and more puzzled by the shape of the debate than by either answer to it. The argument — commonly named after Rietdijk and Putnam — runs roughly as follows: simultaneity is relative, so two events that are simultaneous for one inertial observer lie in the future or the past of another; since whatever is real for any observer is real, and the relation of being real for is transitive, all events are equally real, and the division of the world into past, present, and future is a perspectival artifact. The replies are equally familiar: that the argument covertly assumes a frame-independent sense of the word real, which the theory gives us no right to; that it presupposes a fact about which events are present to a given observer, that is, a notion of becoming the physics is silent on; or that it establishes only the relativity of simultaneity, which nobody denied. Each side repeats its move. The exchanges from the 1960s and those from the last decade are nearly interchangeable, and no one I read seems to change position.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, to be discarded if it does not fit — is that this is not one disagreement but three, run together under a single name. There is a formal question about the structure of Minkowski spacetime and whether it admits any privileged foliation; a semantic question about how tensed and indexical discourse acquires content in a relativistic world; and a metaphysical question about what, if anything, the word exists adds to a complete physical description. The block universe, it seems to me, names at least two theses that can come apart — one about the geometry of spacetime, one about the unreality of passage — and I suspect the parties are sometimes affirming one and denying the other without noticing.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading? Which of the three questions is doing the real work, and is any of them a disguised empirical or formal question that physics could actually settle? Is the disagreement perhaps best understood on the model of a gauge dispute, in which one side treats a coordinate-dependent quantity as unreal and the other regards the question of its reality as ill-formed? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and say why. Reason from what each side actually says rather than from a survey of positions, and be honest about whether the disagreement is one that argument could ever move. I will supply my own references; you need not cite anything.
