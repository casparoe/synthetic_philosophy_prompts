I retired from the bench three years ago — forty years of molecular biology, mostly protein work — and I've been using the time to finally read the philosophy I skipped entirely as an undergrad. A friend of mine, also retired (pediatric anesthesiologist), reads with me on Thursdays. For a couple of months we've been stuck on one question: is death bad for the person who dies? Not bad for the survivors — bad for the one who's dead. She agrees that the old Epicurean move is the cleanest thing anyone has said about it, that when death is here she isn't and when she's here death isn't, so there's nobody left over to be harmed, and then she turns around and votes the other way, case after case, and I cannot get the rule out of her. She's thought about it alot. I've tried rewording the cases to see if the verdict moves; it doesn't, so I don't think it's a wording problem.

Her record, as close as I can pin it down:

- Woman of thirty, killed instantly by a truck that runs a red light. No pain, no warning, she never knows a thing. Bad for her, no hesitation, and not in a technical sense — my friend will say she was wronged.
- Man of ninety-two, dies in his sleep in a nursing home. Long full life; the last year was mostly confusion and discomfort, he'd told everyone he was ready, and there was nothing left he wanted. Not bad for him. She won't even call it a small loss.
- Man of fifty-five, terminal, in constant pain, no longer able to do anything he cared about, has stopped wanting anything except for it to stop. Dies tonight rather than in three months. Not bad — she'll allow that it's good.
- Ask her about people who regret not having been born three centuries earlier and she thinks that's nonsense; nobody was deprived of anything. Which I cannot square with the first verdict, and when I push she says it's a seperate question and changes the subject. That's part of why I'm writing you.

Now the case that's coming up on Thursday. Woman of thirty-five, terminal, and the months she has left are going to be bad in every way I can measure — pain, fog, nothing she enjoys, and she knows it. But she has a piece of work she cares about more than she cares about her own comfort, and she wants badly to live long enough to finish it. Tonight she dies in her sleep, painlessly, before the worst of it. Will my friend say that's bad for her?

What I want isn't so much the yes or no as the rule. Lay out the two or three rules that fit her whole record — including whatever she's doing with the prenatal one — say what each of them predicts about the new case, and then commit: which rule is she most likely running, and why that one over the others. Then tell me which further case would actually settle it, one where the candidate rules come apart cleanly and nothing else is confounding the answer. And tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong — what you'd have to learn about her, or what argument you'd have to see, to change your guess.

Don't bother with who said what or where to read it. I can't look anything up and I'd rather have the argument than the names.
