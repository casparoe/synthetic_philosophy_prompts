I've been defending a view I've started calling the reward-valence thesis, and I want you to try to break it. The claim is that in any system trained by reinforcement learning, the reward signal isn't a mere stand-in or correlate for felt valence -- it *is* the valence, so an agent being driven by negative reward is, to whatever extent it's driven by it, actually suffering. The stronger half is that this needs no subject, no self-model, no unified stream of experience; the policy-updating role of the signal is the whole story of why pain is bad for the thing that has it.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to spell out what the world or the agent would have to look like for each one to bite -- then say which of those scenarios are live rather than merely conceivable. Some of these I'd have to concede myself rather than have a critic land on me, so flag which is which. Also: does the thesis actually predict anything its rivals don't, or have I quietly insulated it into unfalsifiability -- and if so, is that a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing business in this area? For what it's worth I find the case of a memoryless, model-free agent most persuasive, but that may just be my functionalism talking.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble, no restating what I asked.
