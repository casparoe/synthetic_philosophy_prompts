I run a small neighborhood association, and the decisions that actually matter are settled by a five-person steering group rather than by the whole membership. We choose among options by ranking them, not by voting for one. Our rule, adopted years ago and never revisited, works in two steps: each member ranks all the options; we then strike the option that finishes last on the largest number of ballots; and between the two options that remain, the winner is the one that more members rank higher. We thought this was fair, because no one's first choice is simply discarded and the second step is just majority rule.

I have since read that a result — I have seen it called the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — says, roughly, that any way of turning individual rankings into a single group choice is either open to manipulation, in the sense that some member can get a better result by submitting a ranking that is not their honest one, or else is essentially a dictatorship. I would like to see this made concrete with our own rule, so that I can understand what it actually forbids and whether we can escape it.

Please construct a specific example to the following specifications.

- Five members, three options, with the full set of rankings written out so I can check the reasoning; a table is fine.
- Exactly one member can improve the outcome by submitting a ranking that differs from their true preferences. Show the honest rankings, that member's dishonest ballot, and the winner in each case.
- The dishonest ballot must be a genuine reversal, ranking some option the member likes below one they like less, and not merely the honest ranking with their favorite moved to the top. The honest outcome must be strictly worse for that member, by that member's own ranking, than the dishonest one.
- No ties at any stage of the rule, and no two options that every member ranks in the same order relative to each other.
- Then give a second version of the same example, altered as little as possible — ideally a single ballot — in which the manipulation no longer works, so that the feature responsible stands out.

After the two examples, please do the following. Walk through both steps of the rule in each version and show why the winner changes, or fails to change. Identify the single feature of the profile that is doing the work, and say whether changing just that feature is what removes the manipulation in the second version. Explain how the example connects to the general result: which of its conditions the example relies on, in particular whether it needs the rule to be able to produce at least three different winners and whether it depends on the rule's not being dictatorial, and why the result guarantees that some such example must exist if the rule meets those conditions.

Then the part I most want to be straight with me about. A member of my association will object that this manipulation requires knowing everyone else's exact rankings and a knife-edge arrangement of preferences, so it is a curiosity with no bearing on us. Assess that objection. Does the result say that manipulation is easy, or likely, or merely possible somewhere? Is my example robust, or is it fragile? Say plainly if it is a knife-edge, and whether the objection defeats the point or merely relocates it.

Finally, if I wanted a rule that no one could ever gain from misrepresenting, what would the result force me to give up? One or two sentences, phrased so that I could explain it at our next meeting.
