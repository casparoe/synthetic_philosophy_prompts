Right, I'm setting the paper for my second-year seminar on the School of Names — we do the White Horse Dialogue in week 4, alongside a bit of Zhuangzi taking the mickey out of these people — and I need one proper essay question for the exam. 45 minutes, closed book, they've got nothing but their own heads.

You're a philosopher of language who also actually knows the classical Chinese material, so please think carefully before you write, and maybe start by jotting down two or three candidate cruxes and then picking the one least likely to be answered by a well-read student who's simply memorised the standard line (Gongsun Long equivocates on 'is not' — identity versus predication — and that's the end of it). I don't want a question that rewards recitation. The version of that standard line I'd expect them to reach for is that 'is not' wobbles between non-identity and non-membership; true as far as it goes, but that's the opening of the discussion, not the end of it.

What I actually want them to chew on: the advocate's opening move, where horse is said to designate shape and white to designate colour, and what designates colour is not what designates shape, so the conclusion follows. And then the later exchange, where the objector pushes the point that the world contains no colourless horses. Students should have to say what the argument is an argument *about* — concepts? terms? animals? the relation between a kind and a coloured sub-kind? — and whether the charitable reconstruction is a reconstruction of the original claim or a replacement for it.

To stop them freewheeling, I want the question to hand them one concrete test case and make them work it. Here's the one I'd like: the contrast between 'a white horse is not a horse' and 'a stone lion is not a lion'. Both look like the same pattern, but one is a paradox and the other is a truism, and a good answer will say exactly why — it should push them towards the difference between a term that narrows a kind (every white horse is a horse) and a compound that withdraws the kind altogether (no stone lion is a lion) — and then towards the question of which pattern, if either, the advocate's reasoning is really trading on. If they can also say whether the kind-term apparatus of modern semantics helps here or merely changes the subject, bonus, but don't require it, and don't let the question depend on any one scholar's reading.

Deliverables, please:

1. The exam question itself, phrased the way I'd actually put it on a paper — a couple of sentences of setup plus the instruction about what the answer must do. Under 120 words.
2. A model answer of about 700 words, written the way a strong student would write it in 45 minutes, and capable of full marks. No citations and no reading list — they can't look anything up and neither can you, so don't invent page numbers, editors, or attributions.
3. A marking scheme out of 20: five must-hit points at 4 marks each, with a one-line description of what a candidate has to do to earn each, and a one-line note on the mistake that typically loses the marks. Keep the A/B boundary explicit — say what seperates an answer scoring 17+ from one scoring 12–13.

Two constraints on the content: no bibliography and no dates, and don't let any of the must-hit points depend on knowing which chapters of the Gongsun Longzi are authentic — that's a rabbit hole and it isn't what I'm testing.

And spell things the British way, please (colour, analyse, recognise) — the students get tetchy otherwise.
