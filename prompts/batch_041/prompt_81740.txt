I am presenting next month to the combined pain-medicine service and clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital: about twenty clinicians, most of them anaesthesiologists and pain physicians who run opioid and spinal-cord-stimulator clinics, plus two clinical psychologists from the chronic-pain rehabilitation programme, a palliative-care consultant, a hospital chaplain, and one philosopher of a Benthamite cast who will be the only friendly questioner in the room. They are unsentimental about pain and deeply suspicious of utopian pharmacology; several have spent their careers arguing that the affective dimension of pain cannot be cleanly separated from the sensory one; all of them think of suffering as something patients can be helped to bear rather than something to be engineered out of existence; and the committee has a standing institutional worry about anything that looks like enhancement rather than treatment.

My thesis is a deflationary version of the hedonistic imperative. I do not argue for maximising bliss; I argue for abolishing the floor — removing the worst experiential states rather than raising the ceiling — on the ground that the badness of suffering is separable from the informational work suffering does. The argument has three moves. First, suffering is not identical with nociception: clinical material supplies states that are felt as pain and accurately localised yet not minded, as in pain asymbolia, where damage to the insula can strip the aversive quality of a stimulus while leaving its location and intensity intact, and it supplies states that are minded with no peripheral story at all. Second, the aversive-affective component is what does the motivational work — it is what makes damage matter to the sufferer — so any abolition programme must keep a signal in that functional role while subtracting its felt badness. Third, that replacement signal is the proper target of biotechnology: engineered affect that carries the information and drives the protective behaviour without the agony. I expect the clinicians to attack the first move as a laboratory artefact and the third as incoherent.

Give me my Q&A prep as a numbered list of exactly five items, ordered by how much damage each will do to the argument in this room. Each item should state the objection the way this audience would actually phrase it, in clinical and committee vocabulary rather than the vocabulary of the transhumanist literature, then give me the two-minute reply I should make and one clause naming the point at which that reply is softest. Include no introduction, no conclusion, no advice about delivery, and no objections I would only face at a utilitarian seminar. The fifth item must be the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one whose force I privately concede and for which I have no good reply — and I expect it to come from the chaplain or the rehabilitation psychologist rather than from a physician.
