I'm in a small reading group on climate ethics and we've been stuck for months on the individual-contribution question — whether my eating meat, or taking the flight, or voting, is wrong given that my bit makes no measurable difference to the outcome. There are four regulars and they've settled into positions that never seem to touch. One says: your burger doesn't change the number of animals raised (or changes it by a fraction too small to register), your vote doesn't decide the election, so there's nothing wrong with the act as such — if it's wrong at all it must be wrong for some other reason. Another says that's absurd, because the same reasoning applies to everyone, and the upshot is millions of animals, a wrecked climate, a bad government; it's the pattern that's wrong, not the token. A third thinks the whole framing is a distraction — you're complicit in a system that does the harm, and complicity doesn't require your individual contribution to be causally efficacious. A fourth insists that the first person is just attacking a straw man, because the "pattern" view is rule-consequentialism or Kantian universalisability in disguise, and once you see that the inefficacy objection evaporates. Six months in nobody has moved, and the arguements have got shorter rather than longer.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a genuinely substantive disagreement about what makes actions wrong, or is it partly verbal — the sides using "makes a difference" in different senses (a causal difference, a moral difference, a difference in what we're entitled to say about you)? Are they answering different questions under one heading — one about permissibility, one about blameworthiness, one about what kind of thing a moral reason is — in which case they might all be right and simply not addressing each other? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, about thresholds or tipping points or how individual behaviour aggregates? Or is it bedrock: some of us think morality is fundamentally about what you do and others that it's about how the world goes, and neither can argue the other out of that starting point? My own hunch — and I'd like you to test it rather than just agree with it — is that the third and fourth are talking past the first two rather than contradicting them, but I can't tell whether that's a real insight or just a polite way of avoiding taking sides. And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, an empirical result, a formal model of thresholds, or is this one of those disputes where the honest answer is that it won't budge and we should stop expecting it to?
