I spent most of last evening arguing with a close friend—a machine-learning engineer who has little patience for what he calls philosophical hand-waving—about a thesis that has been nagging at me since I first encountered it: the claim that analogy is not merely a rhetorical ornament or a problem-solving heuristic but the core of cognition itself. On this view, every act of categorization is an analogical mapping, every retrieval of a memory is a re-enactment of a past analogy, every application of a concept to a new case is a comparison of surfaces and essences, and even the most abstract reasoning is a chain of analogies built on analogies. My friend thinks the thesis is either trivially true or self-undermining: if everything is analogy, the word loses its explanatory bite; and if analogy really is the core, then a system that merely predicts the next token from surface statistics cannot be doing it, because it has no concepts to map in the first place. He kept returning to the example of a model that can complete a verbal analogy in the style of a standardized test but fails to see that a political dispute is structurally a family quarrel. I think the thesis is more interesting than that, and I want to know what a philosopher-psychologist of an earlier era would have made of the dispute.

Suppose William James could be brought forward to read the contemporary literature on analogy and to watch a large language model produce a plausible, even novel, answer to a conceptual problem it was not trained on. What would his position be on the following cluster of issues: first, whether the analogy-is-core thesis is a genuine discovery about the mind or a redescription of familiar facts; second, whether James would regard the model’s performance as evidence of analogical thinking or would insist that the stream of thought, with its felt transitions, selective interests, and practical orientation, is doing work that no purely statistical engine can replicate; and third, how he would adjudicate between my friend’s deflationary view and my own view that the thesis is a research program rather than a slogan. Please reason from James’s own commitments—his pragmatism, his radical empiricism, his account of the stream of consciousness, his pluralism—rather than from what he happened to say about any particular technology, since he had none. I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of secondary literature; I want a sustained, in-the-weeds reconstruction of what James would likely say, including the strongest objection he would raise against his own conclusion.
