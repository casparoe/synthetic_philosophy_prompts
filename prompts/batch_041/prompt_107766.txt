The Senate's rejection of the Treaty of Versailles is usually told as a story of Wilson's rigidity and Lodge's reservations. I want it analyzed instead as a case for American exceptionalism taken in all three of its guises at once: the descriptive thesis that the country's history and institutions are genuinely distinctive, the normative claim that it has a mission the ordinary rules of statecraft do not constrain, and the ideology that does work in political argument. Take the salient facts as given — Article X's collective-security obligation, the reservations Lodge attached to it, Wilson's refusal to accept them, the irreconcilables who would have voted the treaty down with or without reservations — and map the episode onto the theory, asking which strand the conduct of each side actually exhibits. Use a distinction I find useful here: exceptionalism as exemplarity, on which America is a model whose example the world follows, versus exceptionalism as exemption, on which America is the case to which the rules do not apply. Note that both the internationalists and the irreconcilables argued from American distinctiveness — the one that America alone could lead the world into a new order, the other that America alone was exempt from the old one — and then say what that does to the theory's explanatory power. If every position in the debate follows from exceptionalism, is it a hypothesis that predicts anything, or a vocabulary both sides speak? Run the analysis the other way too: does the episode vindicate the normative claim, or does a refusal to be bound by the institution one's own president designed count against it? Finish by saying where the theory is silent — what in the fight it cannot explain at all, and whether the material explanations (party advantage, personal antipathy, the immigrant vote) leave anything for exceptionalism to do.
