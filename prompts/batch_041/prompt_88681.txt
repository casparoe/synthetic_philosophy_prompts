I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and the utility monster is one of those topics I have watched litigated a hundred times without ever coming away with a settled view of what the argument actually establishes. Recently I read a chapter in a popular ethics book, the kind an academic writes for a general audience, that summarizes the whole business in a couple of pages. Here is the summary as I remember it, close to the author's own words:

Nozick introduced the utility monster as a decisive refutation of utilitarianism. The monster is a being who takes enormous pleasure in other people's suffering: for any unit of resource you hand it, it gets vastly more happiness out of that unit than anyone else would, and since utilitarianism tells us to maximize the total, the calculation always favors giving the monster what it wants, even if the rest of us are reduced to misery or wiped out. So utilitarianism is refuted; it commits us to feeding everyone else to the monster, which nobody can accept. The author grants that no such creature could exist in the real world, since no human being has an unbounded capacity for happiness, but he says the principle still needs patching. He adds that the monster is really the same problem as Parfit's repugnant conclusion, only with one enormous happy being instead of a great many barely happy ones, and that what the case ultimately shows is that utilitarianism treats people as mere means to the monster's satisfaction.

That is the summary as I recall it. I am a reader, not a philosopher, and I cannot tell which parts are load-bearing and which are the author's own gloss. Some of it sounds right to me; some of it sounds like the sort of thing that gets asserted in blog comment sections and then repeated until it looks like common knowledge. My instinct, from the forums, is that the monster is more of a mood-affiliation test than a serious argument, but I would like to be shown that I am wrong if I am.
