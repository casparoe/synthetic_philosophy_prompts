I am a retired experimental physicist. I spent forty years on measurement and instrument design and have spent the years since retirement reading philosophy of science, largely in an effort to understand what I was actually doing all that time. I have now been asked to chair a standing model-review panel at a national standards institute: roughly half a dozen times a year we must recommend one of two rival quantitative models for regulatory use, where both fit the available data to within its stated uncertainty and the choice will move a safety limit or a budget. Our members are capable scientists but not philosophers, we meet for two hours, and our recommendation goes to a director who will not read anything longer than a page.

I would like you to turn Occam's razor, understood through Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, into a procedure we can actually run. Give me an ordered list of steps: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a recommendation, and where it must stop and hand the matter to judgment, marked plainly as such rather than dressed up as a computation. It should be something a chair can work through during the meeting with a member who has never heard of algorithmic information theory.

Be explicit about which steps rest on actual results — the invariance of complexity up to an additive constant across reference machines, the asymptotic-only character of the Solomonoff mixture's convergence, the fact that no computable hypothesis is ever driven to zero prior — and which are engineering compromises, such as substituting a real compressor or a parameter-count penalty for a quantity that cannot be computed. Do not let the procedure smuggle in an absolute standard of description length; make it state whose language and what background corpus the length is measured against. Handle the case where one model looks simpler only because an auxiliary assumption has been moved out of the code, and the case where the two models are not nested, so that no parameter count comparison is even defined.

Then apply it to three cases: two rival dose-response models for the same toxicant; two rival parameterizations of a physical process, where the more complex one is the one I have always favored; and a case in which the data cannot separate the models at all. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether it can ever license the claim that the preferred model is closer to the truth rather than merely cheaper to describe — and be honest about which of its steps would collapse if we replaced our compressor with another.
