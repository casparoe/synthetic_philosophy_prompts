My reading group is doing practical rationality this term and last night we spent two hours on Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I'm still not sure what to think about it. Here's how my friend Marisol laid it out for us -- she's the one who's actually read the literature, so the rest of us tend to take her word for it: a billionaire will put one million dollars in your bank account if at midnight tonight you form a genuine intention to drink a glass of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin isn't lethal, it just makes you painfully ill for a day and then you're fine, and the money is already sitting in your account hours before you'd drink it, so you're perfectly free to change your mind and skip the drinking. Marisol says the rational move at midnight is obviously to intend to drink, since intending is what gets you the million and intending costs you nothing; and then the rational move the next afternoon, with the money already banked, is obviously not to drink, since all that drinking adds at that point is a day of misery. Two opposite verdicts about the same person and the same glass of toxin, she says, which proves rational choice theory is internally inconsistent and has to be thrown out in favor of something better. She also thinks it's basically Newcomb's problem in another outfit -- a case where the only way to collect is to become a certain kind of person, irrational if that's what it takes -- so if one-boxing is right you ought to be able to walk off with the toxin money too, and if it turns out you can't, that's a strike against one-boxing. Her punchline is that the money is just unobtainable: nobody could ever satisfy this billionaire, because satisfying him requires an intention that no one can have.

It all sounded airtight at the bar, but somethings bugging me and I can't tell whether it's a real objection or just me being slow. Could you go through her claims one at a time and tell me which parts someone who works on intention and practical reason would just nod at, which are defensible simplifications, and which are wrong -- and then whether her three conclusions (the theory is incoherent, one-boxers should be able to collect, the money is unobtainable) actually follow from anything true in the setup? I'm not looking for a reading list or a history of who said what, I just want to know what to trust. One thing that's been nagging at me: how much does the puzzle depend on the toxin being exactly a day of misery? If the billionaire sweetened the deal with a tiny bonus -- say ten extra bucks if you actually go through with the drinking -- does the puzzle dissolve, or just move around? And what happens if the toxin is lethal instead? I have a feeling those two questions get at what the puzzle is really about, but I can't tell if I'm overthinking it.
