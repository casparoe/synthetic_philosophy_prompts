Im a PhD student in econ and next week Im hosting a movie night for my department — we're watching Twelve Angry Men and I volunteered to do a five-minute intro talk that reads the film through the common prior assumption and Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result. I'd like you to act as a game theorist who also takes film seriously as film, and to think carefully before writing — maybe brainstorm three or four candidate readings first, then commit to the one that actually holds up.

The thing I keep going back and forth on is this. Aumann's theorem says that two rational agents who share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's opinions can't agree to disagree — they'll already be at the same posterior. So when the jurors start out split eleven to one and end up unanimous, is the film just dramatizing that theorem? The tempting reading is yes: the holdouts have different information, the deliberation is public revelation of evidence (the switchblade, the el train, the woman's glasses, the old man's testimony), and convergence is just everyone updating on common knowledge. But that reading seems to need a common prior, and the whole drama of the film is that these men don't share one — they come in with wildly different priors over whether a kid from the slums would lie, what a witness is worth, what counts as reasonable doubt. So is the film a counterexample to the common prior, a case where Harsanyi's assumption is a false idealization of how real people form beliefs?

And then there's the part that really nags at me. Aumann's result needs more than a shared prior — it needs a shared state space, common knowledge of rationality, and common knowledge of the information partition. In the film the jurors don't even agree on what's relevant. When Juror 8 buys a knife like the murder weapon and stages the re-enactment with the limping old man, is that Bayesian updating on new evidence, or is it the generation of hypotheses that weren't in anyone's model to begin with? And Juror 3's final breakdown — is that belief updating at all, or is it something the theorem has no room for? I suspect the film dramatizes the preconditions of the theorem rather than the theorem itself, and I want you to tell me if that's right or if I'm being too clever.

What I want from you: a reading of the film that's faithful in both directions — don't reduce it to an illustration of the theorem, and don't bend the game theory to make the film come out looking prescient. Say whether the common prior is presupposed, merely fitted, or genuinely fails here; which scenes to point the audience to for each; where a character's choice is illuminated by the formal idea and where it resists it; and whether the honest verdict is that the film is better read as a story about heterogeneous priors (and if so, what would have to be true for the common prior to be restored). I'm also curious about the descriptive-versus-normative gap: is the common prior a claim about how beliefs actually form, a consistency requirement we impose on rational agents, or a modeling convenience that buys the no-trade results? Say which one the film's jury would violate, and why that matters. End by telling me what the film adds to this debate that the theorem and its critics can't — the thing you can only show with twelve men in a hot room. No citations or reading lists, obviously, I just want the argument and the reading.
