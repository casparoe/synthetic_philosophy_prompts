My oldest friend has just come back after two years working abroad, and we spent most of a long dinner arguing about it. She had gone on crossing against the light at empty intersections because everyone around her did; she had kept tipping at the rates customary at home rather than the rates the people serving her expected; she had held her place in queues the way she does here, in a city where a queue is closer to a loose crowd than to a line; and she had walked on grass that everyone walks on and nobody is supposed to. She maintained that she had done nothing wrong — that these were local customs, that customs are not laws, and that a guest cannot be expected to absorb every local habit. I maintained that a guest who ignores the house rules is not a guest but a nuisance, and that the tipping case at least looked like something more than bad manners. We got nowhere, largely because we kept sliding among three different claims: that she broke a rule, that she was rude, and that she did something morally wrong. I am still not certain which of the three we were arguing about at any given moment.

The argument matters to me for professional reasons. I run international onboarding at a company that relocates about forty people a year, and I field these questions constantly and in miniature: whether a new hire must tip at the coffee counter, whether they may cross mid-block, whether they must wait for the pedestrian signal, whether they may walk on the lawn, whether they should bring a gift for the clerk processing their paperwork, whether declining a round of drinks counts as a snub. I answer from instinct and folklore, and I would like a procedure instead.

Please turn what philosophy actually knows about convention, and about the obligations of visitors, into something I can run at my desk in about ten minutes, with no philosophical training and no research. I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear yes or no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I am the one who signs off on the advice, and my staff will relay it as though it were a rule, so false confidence is expensive; mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. A short procedure with honest holes is worth more to me than a long one that pretends to be complete, and I would like to be told which distinctions you kept and which you dropped in the compression.

The procedure should rest on the real distinctions rather than on slogans. It should separate a convention that solves a coordination problem from one that merely marks membership; it should say when following a local practice is genuinely owed, when it is merely prudent, and to whom it is owed — the people in front of you, the wider practice, or no one at all; and it should be explicit about how far the when-in-Rome argument runs and where it stops. My friend's case is awkward for any simple answer, because she is not really a foreigner: she is a returning local whose habits were reshaped abroad. The procedure should say whether a returning resident and a first-time visitor are in the same position, and why.

Run it on four cases and show your work on each.

1. Tipping at a restaurant where the server's cash wage is set below the ordinary minimum on the assumption that tips will make up the difference, and where the diner's own view is that tipping is a bad system — so that the person who bears the cost of a principled refusal is the server standing in front of them. I expect this to come out against both my friend and me, and I want to see how the procedure gets there.
2. Crossing against the signal at an empty intersection in a city where nearly everyone does it and the law is on the books but unenforced.
3. A queue that is loose rather than single-file, where the newcomer's way of holding a place reads as cutting to the people behind.
4. A local practice that requires something the visitor believes is wrong — a customary payment to a clerk to have a form processed, say, or a convention about who may sit where.

Then tell me what the procedure cannot settle and where it will misfire: what to do when locals themselves disagree about their own convention; when the visitor is exempt in a way others are not; when following the practice would cost them something the procedure has no way to price; and when the convention is one that a group the visitor belongs to has good reason to resent.
