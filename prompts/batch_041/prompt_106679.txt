I am the compliance and ethics officer at an international NGO that runs health and water programs through twelve country offices. Three decisions land on my desk most weeks, and I currently make them by feel: whether to approve a declared gift or act of hospitality given or received by field staff; whether a locally ordinary arrangement must be stopped, tolerated, or restructured (a payment to move a shipment past a checkpoint, a salary a driver is expected to share with his extended family, hiring a cousin over an equally qualified stranger, a contribution to a chief's funeral); and how to triage a report that a colleague is taking. I get about thirty minutes per case, I have no training in anthropology or law, and I have to write a justification that will hold up both to a donor's auditor and to a village council. Gifts under $250 are mine to decide; anything larger, and every termination, goes to the general counsel and the country director. Assume I have no web access; I need reasoning I can use, not references.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Some material I would like the procedure to use rather than reinvent. The standard formula that corruption is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain is too coarse here, because the fights are almost never about whether a payment happened; they are about whether the power was entrusted by the right community and whether the gain was private when it went to a kin group rather than to an individual. In many of the places we work, people move between two normative orders: the state or the NGO, experienced as an alien structure from which extraction is not felt to be stealing, and the kin and community network, whose claims are felt as genuine obligations that outrank the institution's. My own staff are not outside this. There is also a live argument I cannot settle, that petty payments to officials grease the wheels where bureaucracies are broken and produce better outcomes than refusal, against the reply that they are a regressive tax, select for those who can pay, and corrode the institution over time. And I need a way to separate a gift from a price: my instinct is that a real gift is addressed to a person and would have roughly the same value whoever held the office, while a bribe is addressed to the office and calibrated to the decision at stake, but I do not know how much weight that test can carry. Field staff also insist that local people distinguish sharply among kinds of taking — a cut a clerk demands and everyone resents, a payment a family man is understood to owe his relatives, a tribute that flows upward and is expected — and I would rather exploit those distinctions than flatten them.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a customs official asks $200 to release a container of vaccines whose refrigeration is failing; the shipment is worth $40,000 and covers a district's routine immunization for a month, and a field officer has paid before. Second, a district health officer who sits on the committee choosing which villages get boreholes has asked us for $600 toward his mother's funeral, as many local organizations contribute, and separately for a laptop in recognition of his support. Third, a pharmacist at one of our clinics is reported to sell a small share of the donated drugs on the local market and give the money to the cleaning staff and to patients who cannot afford transport; he works unpaid overtime and the clinic depends on him.

Tell me which of the three the procedure answers in a way I will not like, and what it offers me there beyond stop or tolerate. Say where the procedure can only run on information the field office may not have — what the decision would have been without the payment, whether local people call the arrangement a wrong — and what to do when that information is missing. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me which way the procedure should err, given that letting corruption through and cutting off a program or firing someone for what the community regards as ordinary decency do not cost the same. Close by telling me what this procedure cannot decide.
