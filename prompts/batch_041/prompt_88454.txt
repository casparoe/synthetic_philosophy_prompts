hey — I'm in a third-year course on the philosophy of well-being (it's cross-listed with the Buddhism unit, which is why I signed up) and I've basically been converted to something the prof calls tranquilism: the only thing good in itself is the absence of suffering and craving, and pleasure doesn't add anything on top of that, it's just the removal of a lack, so once the lack is gone there's nothing left to add. I've definately started living like I believe it. Trouble is I've never checked it against everything else I think, and the essay is due in two weeks, so I want an audit of the whole set before I commit. Go through these and tell me which ones actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and how bad each real conflict is. Rank them by severity, give the cheapest fix for each, and then say which single change would clean up the most at once. Please don't just tell me they're all in tension — some of them I'm pretty sure sit fine together and I want you to say which ones those are.

1. Tranquilism as above. Absence of suffering and craving is the only intrinsic good; suffering and craving are the only intrinsic bad; positive pleasure is neither.
2. Craving counts as suffering — it's the felt pull of something being missing — so a life where all your desires get satisfied isn't the good life. The good life is one where you don't have the desires in the first place.
3. I'm a deprivationist about death: dying at 25 is bad for the person who dies because of everything they miss out on — the years, the experiences. It's not bad because of any pain in the dying.
4. I'm strongly anti-paternalist. If a competent adult wants to do something that will make them miserable, that's their call, and nobody gets to override them for their own good. Suffering by itself is never enough reason to take someone's choice away.
5. My flatline case: imagine someone whose craving and pain are both completely gone, permanently, but who also can't feel anything else — no joy, no interest, nothing, just a grey nothing. My gut says that's a tragedy rather than a perfect life, and I can't tell whether my gut is just wrong on tranquilism's own terms or is picking up on something the view can't see.
6. The one that bothers me most: wanting to be tranquil is itself a craving, and a craving is a suffering, so the more I want the only good thing there is, the more of the only bad thing there is. The prof said something about just cultivating it without wanting it, which felt like a dodge. Is there a non-dodgy version of this, or does it sink the whole project?

Two more I assume are fine but want checked anyway: I still love music and food and I'm not giving them up, and I think helping other people matters a lot even when it costs me — I'd take on suffering to spare someone else. Neither seems like it should clash with (1), but tell me if I'm fooling myself.
