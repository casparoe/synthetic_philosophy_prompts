I'm a doctoral student in political theory, and I keep running into a puzzle about conditional amnesty that I can't get to come out clean. The model I have in mind is the one pioneered in the mid-1990s and imitated, in various forms, ever since: a truth commission empowered to grant amnesty to individual perpetrators of gross human rights violations, provided the act was committed for a political objective and the applicant makes full disclosure of it. Nothing in the design requires remorse, apology, or any change of heart; the currency is information, not contrition. Those who receive amnesty are not merely spared prison — they keep their pensions and, in some cases, their professional positions — while the victims' families receive a modest state-funded payment and a name and a paragraph in the commission's published record, which the state then adopts as the official account of what happened. The same instrument that pays the families also extinguishes their civil claims, so the payment is not compensation in any ordinary sense but a substitute for it.

What I cannot make sit together is the surrounding posture of the state that does this. It goes on punishing ordinary violent crime severely; it insists in public that impunity is intolerable; and its own legal order treats the failure to punish atrocity as a standing embarrassment. Meanwhile the amnesty is defended in two uncomfortable registers at once — as a tragic concession, justice traded away for the sake of peace, and as a higher form of justice, restorative and victim-centred and somehow more truthful than a trial would have been. I would like you to reconstruct what the practice actually presupposes, and then to push on it rather than merely describe it. What theory of punishment is doing the work — is amnesty intelligible only against a background view that punishment looks forward (deterrence, reform, incapacitation), or can a desert-based or an expressive/communicative view accommodate it too? Whose wrong is taken to be at issue: does the state's ability to waive the claim presuppose that the primary injury was to the political order rather than to particular victims, and if so, what becomes of the victims' standing when they are handed a payment and a paragraph instead of a verdict? Does the design presuppose that the state's own title to punish was compromised by its complicity in the crimes, so that amnesty is less an act of mercy than a settlement among wrongdoers?

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories would equally license the same arrangements. Then say what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by a straightforwardly retributive one, and again by one that abandons desert altogether and treats perpetrators as risks to be managed. I am especially interested in whether the practice's own logic forces it toward a conclusion its defenders resist: whether, on its own terms, it is committed to something about the moral equality of perpetrators and victims, or about the state's standing to forgive, that it would rather not avow.
