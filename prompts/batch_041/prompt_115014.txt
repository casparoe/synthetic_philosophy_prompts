I've been keeping a running list of verdicts about which things really have states that are about something, as opposed to merely being describable as if they were, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The list started as a way of getting clear on the two-bitser thought experiment — the coin acceptor built to take US quarters — and it has grown to the point where I no longer trust the distinction between original and derived intentionality that was supposed to organize it. I'd ask you to treat the following as fixed data points for as long as you can, rather than as claims to be corrected. A bimetallic strip in a thermostat has no states that are about anything, not even in a watered-down sense; when I say it registers that the room is cold, that is my description, not its content. A coin acceptor built in Ohio and then shipped to a laundromat in Panama, where only Panamanian quarters ever circulate through it, has internal states that are about Panamanian quarters: the content shifted with the use, even though the design, the parts, and the wiring diagram are unchanged. Its twin, still in Ohio and still fed US quarters, has states that are about US quarters — so two devices with physically identical internal states have states with different content. A duplicate of the Ohio device that came into existence by a cosmic accident, every molecule in place but with no designer and no history of anyone ever feeding it a coin, has no states that are about anything at all, whatever it does when a coin drops in. A device in a US laundromat whose acceptor has drifted through wear so that it now takes Canadian quarters and rejects worn US ones still has states that are about US quarters; the Canadian acceptances are errors, not a change of subject. A parrot that says it wants a cracker when it is hungry, and says the same thing when it is not, has no states that are about anything, whereas a dog that goes to the door when I pick up the leash does have states that are about a walk — and the dog learned its bit by conditioning, just as the parrot did. And a corporation that announces it expects a competitor to cut prices really does have a belief about that competitor, even though there is no one place in the building where the belief is stored and every part of the mechanism is made of people.

Two of these are pulling against each other and I cannot see how to keep both. In the Panama case I let use and context fix the content, so the acceptor's state comes to mean Panamanian quarter; in the worn-acceptor case I refuse to let the same sort of drift fix the content, and I call the Canadian acceptances mistakes instead. In the first case I seem to be saying that content is whatever the state is actually recruited to track; in the second, that content is whatever the state was built to track and that everything else is error. Whichever way I go, something else on the list moves. If actual recruitment fixes content, then the bimetallic strip is about temperature after all and the line between the thermostat and the two-bitser becomes very hard to draw; if design intent fixes content, then the Panama acceptor never changed its mind and I was simply wrong about it, and the accidental duplicate may inherit content from being a duplicate of something designed, which I am sure is wrong. The parrot and the dog are the other pair I cannot reconcile, since whatever story I tell about the dog's conditioning appears to fit the parrot equally well, and the corporation verdict seems to require that content can be genuine while being located nowhere in particular, which is either a deep truth about group agents or a confusion I have not yet diagnosed.

So: is there one account of what it takes for a state to be about something that delivers all of these verdicts, or close to all of them? If there is, state it precisely, tell me which clause is doing the work in each case and which case forced that clause into the account. If there is not, tell me which of my verdicts to give up and what the repaired account then predicts for three cases I have not put on the list: a slime mold that stops responding to a repeated blast of cold air and then responds again when the interval changes; a chess engine's internal state, which we can probe and find tracking whether its queen is attacked; and a river channel that settles into the shape the last big flood carved and then routes the next flood the same way. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled — where anyone who works on intentionality would agree — and which parts are genuinely contested between teleosemantic, interpretivist, and phenomenal-intentionality treatments, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. I would rather have the argument with its costs laid out than a confident answer that papers over where the disagreement actually is.
