I have spent my working life in the novel — first-person narratives, mostly, all of them built on the hope that a reader will consent to live for a few hundred pages inside a life that is not hers — and I have a craftsman's suspicion of the stage that I would like you to test against a thinker I keep returning to. Restate Rousseau's case against the theatre in his letter to d'Alembert as fully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: point of view and the reader's identification, the sympathetic protagonist, the tacit contract between writer and reader, verisimilitude, the discharge of feeling that fiction allows, and above all the difference between a reader alone with a book — who may close it, skip ahead, or reread a page until she understands it — and an audience sealed in a lit room, which can do none of these. Treat my vocabulary as the target language, not as an authority. Then give me the residue report. Which of Rousseau's claims survive intact: that the playwright must please a paying public and therefore make virtue agreeable and vice interesting; that comedy trains an audience to laugh at the sincere man and side with the agreeable one; that the theatre substitutes a troupe of actors for a whole people, who in a true festival would be both actors and spectators at once; that our pity for staged suffering is a dry rehearsal that costs us nothing and leaves us less, not more, disposed to actual compassion? Which survive only if I distort them to fit a maker's concerns? And which have no counterpart at all in how a novel is made — the civic and political charge in particular, the thought that the injury falls on the body of citizens rather than on any single soul, and the objection to the actor's profession as such. Tell me honestly whether what is left over is a blind spot in my craft or a sign that Rousseau's real target was never fiction in general. And address rather than dodge the obvious embarrassment: the man wrote a wildly popular sentimental novel, so I suspect the stage was never the true object of his worry.
