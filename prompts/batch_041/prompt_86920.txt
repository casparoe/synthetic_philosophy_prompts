I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character has had the neural bridge between her cerebral hemispheres severed, and I am determined not to write the cartoon version. I do not need a reading list or a verdict; I need the document that the two camps in this dispute could jointly sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Let me name the camps as I understand them. The single-subject view holds that such a patient remains one someone: the surgery partitions what can be reported, remembered, and deliberately acted upon, but it does not multiply the subject of experience. The two-streams view holds that it does, and that the fluent, speaking hemisphere is merely the louder of two concurrent points of view, not the whole person.

Produce three things.

First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign. They should cover what the commissurotomy cases actually show and do not show; what the residual cross-cueing between the hemispheres — the two sides trading information by observing each other's behaviour rather than through any internal channel — does and does not establish; and how the several things that might be meant by unity (a single subject, a single phenomenal field, a single stream of access to action and report) come apart in these cases. I would rather have six claims that cost something than twenty that cost nothing.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements. State each in wording its own side would accept, and name the crux it turns on. I expect the cruxes to include whether the subject of experience is individuated by the boundaries of the organism or by the degree of neural integration; whether the speaking hemisphere's reports carry any evidential privilege, or are simply the only testimony on offer; and whether a patient can be right or wrong, from the inside, about whether her experience is unified.

Third, for each crux, the case or argument both camps would agree bears on it, and what each side predicts about that case. One such case I have been imagining for the book: a continuum running from a patient with no bridge between the hemispheres, through a patient with a very thin artificial bridge carrying only a few bits per second, to a patient with an intact commissure. Each camp must say where on that continuum the number of subjects changes, if it changes at all, and why the threshold falls where they say it does rather than somewhere else. If either side would prefer to deny that the number is well defined, I want that said plainly and defended.

Finally, if any part of this dispute is merely verbal, identify it, and say what would remain of the disagreement once the words are fixed.
