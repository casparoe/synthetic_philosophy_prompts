I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I have spent a good part of the last few reading the history of philosophy with an amateur's mixture of enthusiasm and pedantry. One episode has lodged itself in my mind: Hobbes's long campaign to square the circle, and the dismantling he suffered at Wallis's hands over it. My instinct, as a scientist, is that a man who cannot tell a proof from a confident guess has forfeited any claim to have written a science of anything. But I notice that readers of Hobbes divide sharply over how much this ought to matter, and I would like you to produce the document that establishes what can and cannot be agreed between them.

Set the two camps at the table. On one side, those who hold that Hobbes's mathematics and his philosophy are a single enterprise: that his conception of demonstration—reasoning as the manipulation of definitions, mathematics subordinated to geometry and geometry to motion, and a settled hostility to the new symbolic algebra—is where the method of his whole system lies most exposed to view, so that the collapse of the mathematical program diagnoses a defect running through everything, the political philosophy included. On the other side, those who hold that the mathematics is a separate and unlucky enthusiasm: that the geometrical method of the political works is a matter of expository order and borrowed prestige, and that the arguments about the state of nature, authorization, the laws of nature, and sovereignty stand or fall on their own, untouched by whether his quadrature was correct.

Draft their joint statement. I want: (1) the claims both can sign—about what Hobbes actually held, what his critics actually showed, and what the historical record settles—and I want these to be substantive rather than courteous; (2) the residual disagreements, each stated in a form its own side would accept, with any merely verbal element identified as such; and (3) the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect the cruxes include whether any argument in the political works depends on a distinctively mathematical premise or inference; whether the method of resolving a thing into its parts and recomposing it is, for Hobbes, mathematical in inspiration or a general epistemic procedure that geometry merely illustrates; whether the thought, often attributed to him, that we can demonstrate only what we ourselves construct—figures in geometry, the commonwealth in politics—is load-bearing or decorative; whether his rejection of symbolic algebra was a philosophical commitment or a technical lag; and whether the reception of his politics was genuinely driven by his mathematics, which suggests a counterfactual both sides can assess. For each crux, give the case, argument, or piece of the historical record that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts.

Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table with a column for the crux, a column for each side's own wording, a column for the test both accept, and a column for the two predictions. Write it so that a genuine defender of each position could sign it without wincing. I do not need a bibliography, and I would rather you reason from what is already well established than reach for citations you cannot check.
