Write a confidential investor memorandum, roughly 800 words, on behalf of a fictional firm that brokers moral compromise between parties holding incompatible value systems. The firm's premise is that moral disagreement is a source of gains from trade, and the memorandum should be written in the sober, numbered, faintly unctuous register of a real private-placement document: a thesis, a market-sizing section, a unit-economics section, a fee schedule, a client-intake and screening process, a risks section, and a growth plan. Nothing in the piece should wink. The humor must come entirely from the content, so that a reader who has thought about trade theory and about moral compromise laughs at the right places while a reader who has not still follows the business being described.

The memorandum should hold the following ideas straight and let them generate the jokes:

- Comparative advantage rather than comparative virtue. The firm's selling proposition is that two parties with incompatible values can both improve their positions if each concedes where concession is cheapest for it, even when neither is morally better than the other and neither changes what it values. The document should be scrupulous about the economics and should never slip into claiming that the parties converge in what they care about.

- The conflation on which the whole enterprise rests: a bargain over a fixed moral pie, in which one side's gain is the other's loss and the parties merely divide the spoils, versus a genuine reconciliation of values, in which something new is created. The memorandum should treat the second as though it were nothing more than an unusually efficient instance of the first, and should be proud of the identification.

- The moral remainder. The memorandum must include a mechanism for pricing and disposing of the residue left over when a party does something that is, on balance, justified but still costs it something — a specialized desk that absorbs, hedges, or on-sells this residue to counterparties who have least use for a clean conscience. The pricing should be internally coherent and the surrounding discussion should never ask whether the residue survives the transfer.

- Commodification. Whether an act retains its moral character when performed for a fee on another party's behalf is the question the document must answer, and it should answer it exclusively by appeal to liquidity, volume, and the depth of the market.

- Incommensurability. The memorandum should concede that some values cannot be ranked on a single scale, and then convert this concession into a pricing problem to be solved by a spread rather than a difficulty about the enterprise itself.

- The constitutive limit. The market cannot include parties who hold that compromise in matters of value is itself impermissible, since such parties will not trade. The document should treat this not as a contradiction but as a market-segmentation fact, a demographic to be reached later or written off, and this treatment should be the reductio the reader recoils from.

Include a fee schedule that prices acts by the residue they generate and by the number of parties who must be indemnified; an intake questionnaire that screens prospective clients for what it calls moral liquidity; and a risks section that names the real risks in the bland idiom of a genuine filing, among them counterparty risk, regulatory exposure, and the possibility that the firm's own integrity was the inventory it sold. Close with the growth plan. The target is the position that moral conflict is always in principle a mutual-gains problem — the piece should exaggerate that position's actual logic, not invent a sillier one, and a philosopher should find the satire fair.
