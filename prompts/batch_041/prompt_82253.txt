At a seminar dinner last week, two of my colleagues had an argument I could not referee, and I would like you to sort it out. Ravi works on the early medieval period; Ines works on formal epistemology. The dispute was over Ravi's contention that a chronicle composed some four centuries after the events it narrates — our only narrative source for a war between two kingdoms, and demonstrably wrong on several points we can check — can still be mined for what happened, on the ground that it reports things reflecting badly on the side the chronicler plainly favors: a rout, a king who flees the field, a treaty his own people break. A forger, Ravi says, invents victories; it is the awkward details that can be trusted. He calls this his embarrassment criterion. Ines resisted him at every step, as follows.

(1) Ines: the criterion presupposes that you know what a forger would and would not invent, but the king who runs is among the most familiar stock figures in the writing of the period; the detail you treat as too awkward to have been fabricated is precisely the kind of thing that gets fabricated.

(2) Ravi: some of it is conventional, granted, but the conventions are borrowed from earlier works, and where the chronicle's awkward details match no earlier model known to us, convention cannot account for them.

(3) Ines: known to us is doing all the work there. You are reasoning from the absence of a model in the surviving corpus, and the corpus is what happened to survive, not what once existed; the argument from silence is your weakest move, not your strongest.

(4) Ravi: we also have corroboration — a charter and a coin hoard that fit his account.

(5) Ines: only if they are independent, and you have not shown that. A charter from the same chancery, or a coin whose legend the chronicler may himself be echoing, can descend from the same lost source as the chronicle; the agreement between them is then one witness counted twice.

(6) Ravi: if a source may be used only once we have proved it owes nothing to any other, we can know nothing at all; and the likelihood ratio you keep demanding is one you could not supply for any testimony you accept in daily life, so it cannot be a condition of knowledge.

Adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then say what each of them is missing, and who has the better of the argument overall — not who wins every point.
