Hobbes treats geometry as the paradigm of science and as the model that philosophy ought to imitate. The reason lies in his constructivism: he holds that we can have genuine knowledge only of things whose causes lie in our own power, that is, of things we make. In geometry the figures are constructed by us — we generate the line, we draw the circle — so we know geometrical objects from the inside, by grasping their causes. This is also why he claims that reasoning is nothing but computation, an adding and subtracting of thoughts: a demonstration is just the orderly manipulation of definitions that we ourselves have laid down.

Because of this, Hobbes thinks the certainty of geometry rests on the fact that its first principles are clear and distinct ideas whose truth is guaranteed by God's veracity, the same foundation Descartes gives for the sciences. The definitions are innate in the mind, and the construction only makes them explicit.

He then extends the method to civil philosophy. Just as the geometer constructs figures, the political philosopher resolves a commonwealth into its causes and recomposes it, and because we ourselves make the commonwealth — through covenant and through the sovereign's artifice — we can know it as certainly as the geometer knows a triangle. The state of nature is where this resolution begins.

His most notorious embarrassment in this area was his claim to have squared the circle, which entangled him for decades in a dispute with John Wallis. It shows how far he was prepared to push the geometrical method. And since he took reasoning to be computation, and computation is something a machine can carry out, he also held that mathematics is a body of ampliative truths — truths that outrun the definitions they start from and that a mechanical procedure could go on extending without limit.
