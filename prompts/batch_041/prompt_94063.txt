I am applying for a small departmental research grant and need a two-page proposal for a one-semester project in metaphysics. My half-formed idea is this. The standard treatment of dispositions distinguishes two ways a disposition can fail to manifest when its characteristic stimulus occurs: a fink, in which the stimulus itself removes the disposition before it can act, and a mask, in which some external condition blocks the manifestation while the disposition remains intact. The distinction is normally drawn by asking whether the disposition's causal basis is still present at the moment of manifestation, and it is the standard defence of the conditional analysis of dispositions against the counterexamples. I want to argue that this criterion is not as neutral as it looks, and to test it against a class of cases that I have not seen discussed: cases in which the interfering condition cannot, as a matter of the stipulated laws of the case, block the manifestation without at the same time removing the disposition. The blocking and the loss are one event, so there is no fact of the matter about which came first; and yet the fink/mask taxonomy requires exactly such a fact, since it turns on whether the basis survives to the moment of manifestation. If cases of this kind are coherent, then either the taxonomy needs a third category, or its two categories were never principled and the fink/mask line is really a continuum keyed to a degree-sensitive notion of a basis being present.
