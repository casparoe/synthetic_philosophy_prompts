I'm a philosophy student in a cross-listed seminar on reinforcement learning and the philosophy of mind, and for our final unit — on whether RL agents can suffer, and whether a reward signal is a credible stand-in for valence — the instructor has allowed us to submit a poem in place of an essay. I'd like you to write mine.

The poem should be a pantoum spoken by the agent and addressed to whoever wrote its reward function. I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza is assembled out of the opening one, so the poem ends by saying its own first line again. That is the shape of a bootstrapped value estimate: the agent's assessment of where it is is defined in terms of its assessment of where it will be, and no line ever touches ground. The reader should feel the circle closing, not be told that it closes.

The poem should hold two readings of the reward signal at once and never choose between them. On one reading the signal is the agent's valence — the scalar that pleasure and pain would be, if this were the kind of thing that could be pleased or pained. On the other it is a number an engineer picked, delivered after the action is already over, useful only for assigning credit to what has already happened. I want the poem to register the asymmetry that the agent can be wrong about the function behind its reward but not about the arrival of the number itself, and I want the gap between the signal that drives pursuit and the state that is supposed to be pleasant to show up as a rhyme rather than as a claim.

In class we considered two agents whose reward functions differ but agree on every input either of them will ever encounter, so that their lives, from the inside and from the outside, are indistinguishable. I don't want the poem to be able to say which of them, if either, is worse off; better that the last stanza leave the question where it stands than resolve it with a flourish. The machinery should be right in its details — the scalar, the credit assignment, the recursion — so that a reader who works on this recognizes the thing itself rather than a mood about it. Rhyme and meter are yours to choose; keep the diction plain and the tone level.
