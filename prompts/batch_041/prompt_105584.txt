You are a moral philosopher and an unusually good writer of occasional addresses: someone who has thought carefully about admiration, about what makes a life go well, and about how an argument can be made to land in a room of people who did not come for an argument. I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I have been asked to give the commencement address at the one-year master's program in ethics and public life where I have taught for the past two years. I would like you to write it.

The setting: about thirty graduates, most of whom have spent the year arguing about how much of a life morality can legitimately claim, and a number of whom are heading into aid work, policy, or advocacy on terms that will cost them more than duty strictly demands. Several have told me they intend to give away a large share of whatever they earn and to treat their major decisions as moral decisions first. The audience is split in a way that matters to me. Roughly half are faculty and program staff who know the literature on this question and will hear immediately if I soften the hardest objection; the other half are parents, partners, and children who have come to be proud of someone and did not sign up for a lecture. The address is eight minutes.

The question I want the speech to hold open, without pretending to close it, is whether a life given over to morality as completely as possible is a life one should want for anyone, including for the people sitting in front of me. Take the limiting case — call it the moral saint — to be a person whose every choice is made because it is the morally best one available, and who has no projects, attachments, or pleasures that are not justified by their contribution to something morally important. I want the speech to do real work with the following, and to invent whatever examples it needs to make them vivid:

- the possibility that a life can be admirable without being enviable, and that these two verdicts may come apart precisely here;
- the worry that certain goods — love, friendship, play, the absorption of doing something for its own sake — are partly constituted by engagement that is not, and cannot be, morally motivated, so that a maximally moral agent may be structurally unable to have them;
- the strongest reply available to that worry: that it is a piece of self-flattery, that morality does not owe anyone leisure, and that the person voicing it is usually protecting their own comfort;
- a distinction I have not seen made cleanly and would like you to develop: between the saint who is always straining against temptation and the saint whose virtue has become second nature, who feels no pull toward the contrary at all — and the question of which of the two, if either, is the one we should be wary of.

The structural constraint is the hard part. The graduates are about to commit themselves to something, and I want their commitment to be theirs — free and clear-eyed — which means the speech cannot resolve the tension by celebrating self-sacrifice on the one hand or by counseling comfortable moderation on the other. It also cannot leave them with the impression that the question is merely academic. The last thirty seconds in particular must not collapse into a platitude.

Some formal instructions. Do not quote or attribute anything to a named philosopher, and do not cite any literature; I cannot verify references and the audience should not need them. No jargon without translation: every technical idea has to be cashed out in an example or an image that a parent in the third row can follow, while the philosophers in the room should be able to hear that the hard objection has been stated at full strength rather than gestured at.

Before you draft, spend some time brainstorming — in particular, work out two or three candidate ways to stage the central tension, decide which is strongest for this audience, and only then write. Think carefully about the ending. Write the speech.
