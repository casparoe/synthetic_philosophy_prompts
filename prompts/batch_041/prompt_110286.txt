I am writing to ask for help thinking through a situation involving a close friend. It has become a quiet crisis for me, and I suspect it is not really about her at all.

I have known her since we were nineteen. She trained as a nurse and now works for a large health insurer, reviewing prior-authorization requests against a checklist and issuing denials. She is quick, warm, and — this is the detail I keep returning to — very good at the job. She went after the role deliberately, for the hours and the money, and she is proud of her numbers. Over dinner she will describe a case the way one describes a difficult crossword: a sixty-eight-year-old with a rare lymphoma, a criterion satisfied on a technicality, a denial written in four minutes. When I have pushed back, she has offered four arguments. The requests would be refused with or without her. A physician signs off on every denial, so the decision is not hers. She has no authority to approve anything, only to apply criteria someone else wrote. And no single denial she issues can be traced to any one patient's decline, so the difference she makes is invisible.

Two things complicate this for me. The first is that the checklist contains a discretionary clause: she may request additional documentation before deciding, and she does so — but only in cases that look like fraud, never in cases where the clinical need looks real. The second is that she keeps, privately, a running list of the cases she believes were decided wrongly. She says she is a good person doing a bad job, and that the two ledgers do not touch. Meanwhile, one of her teammates left for a nonprofit, and another began invoking the discretionary clause aggressively and was eased out within a year, which makes it hard for me to believe that anyone in her chair would behave as she does.

I am a nurse practitioner myself, and I have said nothing, partly because I cannot work out what the charge against her would be. That she is cruel? She is not. That she is weak? That seems closer, but I do not know what weakness is supposed to explain, or whether the weakness is hers or the chair's. And every so often a stranger thought arrives: if the situation explains what she does, then it explains her kindness too, and I am no longer sure what is left that is actually her — and if nothing is left, I do not know what I have been judging.

What I want is not a verdict on her soul, and not a reading list; I would rather understand what kind of question I am asking. Which of her four arguments are genuine excuses, and which are merely descriptions of a structure she inhabits? What does the private list show — remorse, or a device for keeping her self-image intact while nothing changes? If someone in her position is largely explained by her position, is there anything left for me to hold her to, and if so, what? And what, concretely, should I do: say something, say nothing, or end the friendship?
