I am in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and I have to circulate a short paper next week; what follows is the argument as I currently have it, and I would rather have comments than a rewrite. The standard move in the seminar is to say that the no-free-lunch theorems hand Hume a formal proof: Wolpert and Macready's 1997 result, on its popular reading, says that averaged over all objective functions no search algorithm beats any other, so there is no a priori reason to expect any inductive method to succeed, and the problem of induction turns out to be a theorem rather than a puzzle. I want to argue that this reading gets the direction of fit backwards. The average in question is taken over the uniform distribution on the space of all functions from inputs to outputs, and that distribution is not a neutral vantage point: it assigns overwhelming probability to functions with no structure at all, since a uniformly drawn lookup table is incompressible with probability approaching one as the table grows. So the theorem's assumption-free standpoint is itself a substantive prior — the prior of an agent who is certain that the world has no pattern. Relative to that prior it is a triviality that nothing beats guessing; that is simply what having no pattern means. The theorem therefore cannot underwrite inductive scepticism, because it presupposes anti-induction in its choice of measure. Average instead over a hypothesis class that can be described briefly — the computable functions, or the smooth ones, or those satisfying a locality constraint — and a free lunch appears at once: a method that weights hypotheses by how briefly they can be described will, on average over that class, outperform one that treats all functions alike. So the theorems do not show that induction is unjustified; they show that a free lunch and an assumption about the environment's structure are the same thing described twice.

The obvious reply is to ask what justifies the structured prior rather than the uniform one, and here I want to press a distinction that I have not seen made cleanly, between a first-order inductive bias, which is a restriction on the hypothesis class, and a second-order bias, which is a prior over priors. The no-free-lunch result defeats the fantasy of first-order neutrality: some bias is unavoidable. But it does not stop there. Fixing the second-order prior is itself a choice made under uncertainty, and the same counting argument seems to run one level up, since for every prior under which method A beats method B there appears to be a mirror-image prior under which B beats A. I concede that the transfer is not automatic — the space of priors is not obviously closed under the symmetries the first-order theorem needs, and there is no uniform measure on an infinite-dimensional simplex — but the regress strikes me as real in whatever form it takes, and it terminates in something that is not itself a bias: either a brute contingent fact about this world, that it is compressible in the particular ways our priors anticipate, or an empirical fact about us, that our learning algorithms were selected in this world and so carry its structure in their priors. I find the first horn more honest and the second more explanatory, and I am not yet willing to choose between them. What I do want to claim is that the upshot is a relocation rather than a solution. Hume's demand for a non-circular justification for expecting the future to resemble the past becomes the question why the actual world is lumpy in a way that any briefly describable hypothesis class can exploit, and that question looks empirical through and through. Induction works because the universe is not a random lookup table, and that the universe is not a random lookup table is itself something we could only have learned by induction. I am tempted to say the circle is not vicious, because the demand for a non-circular justification was the mistake all along, but I suspect that is a rhetorical exit rather than an argument.

So: three things, and please be blunt. First, accuracy. Is the claim that the uniform measure over all functions is itself an anti-inductive prior stated correctly, and does the move to a briefly describable hypothesis class really deliver a free lunch of the kind I need, or am I trading on a vagueness about what makes a class small? Second, is the first-order versus second-order distinction doing genuine work, or is it the same regress restated with new labels — and if it is genuine, does the counting argument transfer to priors at all, or does the space of priors fail some condition that the space of objective functions satisfies? Third, I want the strongest counterargument available: what is the best case that the no-free-lunch theorems do bear on Hume's problem in a way my relocation misses? Please do not send references or a reading list; I have no library this month, and I would rather have one paragraph of real objection than three of summary.
