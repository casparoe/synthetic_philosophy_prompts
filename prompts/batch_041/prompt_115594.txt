I'm in a reading group that's been grinding through contemporary just war theory for a few months, and we've gotten stuck on the moral equality of combatants. The traditional/legalist line — the one that tracks the law of armed conflict, and that you'd associate with Walzer — says every combatant has the same permissions and the same liabilities: the soldier fighting for an unjust cause is just as permitted to kill enemy soldiers as the one fighting for a just cause, and neither is a murderer for doing so. The revisionists (McMahan and the crowd that followed him) say that's obviously false: if your cause is unjust, you aren't permitted to kill anyone in its service, you're the one doing the wrong, and the other side is permitted to kill you in self-defense; the "moral equality" is a legal fiction that got promoted into a moral claim. What bugs me is that this has been going on for decades, everyone knows everyone's moves, and nothing moves. The revisionist self-defense argument looks to me like it just wins on its own terms, but I can't shake the feeling the traditionalists are defending something real, and that both sides keep quietly conceding each other's points — the revisionists usually end up saying the law of war shouldn't actually be changed, and the traditionalists usually end up saying the unjust combatant does act wrongly but is excused. So I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not another round of the argument.

Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to test it rather than assume it. I suspect the two sides aren't answering the same question. The revisionists are asking what an individual may do, on the assumption that they know their cause is unjust; the traditionalists are asking what the rules of a practice should be, given that the people in it are epistemically limited, embedded in institutions, and can't reasonably be expected to adjudicate the justice of their own war. If that's right, then "the moral equality of combatants" is doing double duty — as a claim about individual permissions it may be false, as a claim about what the law or convention of war should say it may be true — and the debate only looks substantive because both readings are in play. I'd also like you to weigh a rival diagnosis I find tempting: that the real work is being done by a distinction the two sides handle differently, liability to be killed vs. culpability or blameworthiness. That would let the traditionalist be right that the conscript who's been lied to isn't to blame, while the revisionist is right that he's still liable, with "equality" quietly sliding between the two. Or maybe it's a straight normative disagreement about what grounds the permission to kill (liability vs. necessity vs. convention), or a hidden empirical question about how much soldiers can actually know about the justice of their cause, or bedrock intuitions about whether institutional roles can generate permissions that don't reduce to individual liability.

So: which kind of disagreement is this, really, and how much of it is verbal versus substantive? Use what each side actually says — I don't need a survey or a reading list, and please no citations, I can't check them anyway. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a change of question, some empirical work on what soldiers know, or nothing, because the thing is bedrock. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say so and tell me what the real axis is. I'm a historian by training so I'm probably missing the methodological moves here, but I'd rather have the structure of the disagreement than a summery of positions.
