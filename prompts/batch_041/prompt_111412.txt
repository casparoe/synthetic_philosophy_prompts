A government advisory panel I sit on proposes to adopt the average score on the standard international ladder question, in which people place their own lives on a ten-step scale, as our national well-being indicator, with the explicit aim of climbing the international ranking built from it. Explain the conceptual issue this raises about what such a score can and cannot mean. Distinguish three readings of it: a measure of how well people's lives are actually going; a measure of the gap between how people's lives are going and how well they expected them to go; and a measure of what people in that country take to be a normal thing to say about their lives. Say what each reading implies for a government that wants to raise the score, including whether a policy could improve people's lives while lowering it, and whether reaching the top of the ranking would be worth wanting. Assume I know the surveys; I want the conceptual structure rather than a literature review. No more than 500 words, in British English.
