I teach a continuing-education seminar for adults with no background in philosophy — lawyers, engineers, a retired nurse — and next month's session is on Adam Smith's impartial spectator. I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard, and I do not want you to explain the doctrine to me. I want you to design the explanation I will give. My worry is specific: the one-line gloss that my students will arrive with, and will leave with unless the session is built against it, is that the impartial spectator is simply Smith's name for conscience, or for an ideal observer who knows everything and feels nothing. I want a plan that cannot let that gloss survive.

Lay out the order in which the ideas have to come, and say what depends on what: which piece must be securely in place before the next can even be stated, and where the sequence would collapse if I ran it in the more obvious order. Then choose the single case or example that will carry the whole session, and tell me what it must have and what it must lack. I am inclined to build it around a whistle-blower whom his own community condemns and whose reasons no one around him can fully see; tell me whether that works as a vehicle, what it would need to be made to work, and why a more familiar case — a lie told to spare someone's feelings, say — would fail. Then give me the misconceptions the room will predictably bring or form, in the order they will surface, and the precise point in the sequence where I should head each one off rather than waiting until the end.

I want three things to be load-bearing rather than decorative, and I want you to tell me where each enters the sequence: first, the difference between the spectator who merely watches and the spectator who is informed of the case; second, Smith's contrast between the love of praise and the love of praise-worthiness, and its counterpart on the side of blame; third, his account of self-deceit — the tendency to see one's own case in a flattering light, and the way general rules are meant to correct it. I also want the session to end on the question I keep returning to myself: if the impartial spectator is in some sense internalized from the spectators we actually meet, what is left of it when the surrounding society is itself corrupt — does the figure Smith describes have the resources to condemn the standards of its own community, or not? Tell me how far to take that in the last ten minutes, given the audience.

Tell me what to leave out at this level and to say plainly that I am leaving it out, so that I do not bluff my way through a question I cannot answer. And give me one question to put to the room in the final stretch whose answers will show me whether the idea has actually landed, or whether they have merely acquired a new vocabulary for the gloss they came in with.
