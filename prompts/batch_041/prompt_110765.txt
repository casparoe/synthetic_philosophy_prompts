Okay so I've been chewing on the common prior assumption and I cant decide what to make of it. You know the setup: everyone's Bayesian, they share one prior over the whole state space (including each other's private information), and then Aumann's result kicks in — if their posteriors are common knowledge they can't agree to disagree. Great. But what kind of claim is the common prior assumption itself? Is it descriptive — any two genuinely rational agents with the same information must start from the same prior? Is it normative — that having a different prior is some kind of inductive failure, a lack of epistemic hygiene? Or is it just a modeling convention that buys tractability in mechanism design and has no deep epistemological content?

Here's the thing that nags at me. Take two people (or two AIs) who are both perfectly Bayesian, both coherent, both flawless at updating, and yet they start from different priors because they were, say, built with different inductive biases — one wired to favor simple hypotheses, the other wired to favor something else. Neither made an arithmetic slip; their priors differ at the level of what they even count as a live possibility, how they carve the space up. On the common prior assumption this has to be "different information" in disguise — the divergence must trace back to some difference in what they observed. But that looks like assuming the conclusion: it assumes everything that could make them differ is already sitting inside the state space as a signal, when the actual difference might be in the very carving of the possibility space, not in anything either of them learned.

So is there any non-circular way to motivate the common prior assumption? Does "agreeing to disagree" still bite once you allow priors that diverge because of different hypothesis spaces or different simplicity weightings rather than different data? Write an essay on this and actually take a side and defend it. Also tell me what would change your mind — what would have to be true about rational disagreement, or about what a prior really is, for you to end up somewhere else. And flag where your reasoning is weakest, because I suspect the whole thing turns on whether "different information" can be defined independently of "different prior" in the first place.
