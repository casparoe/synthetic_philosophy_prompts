Design a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of testimony. The seminar has just worked through the standard opposition. On the reductionist view, a hearer's warrant for believing what she is told must ultimately rest on non-testimonial evidence — induction over her own experience of speakers' reliability. On the anti-reductionist view, testimony is a basic source of warrant: a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told by default, and needs no positive evidence of the speaker's trustworthiness unless a defeater appears.

I want a case in which the reductionist comes out *more* credulous than the anti-reductionist — the reverse of the direction in which the disagreement is usually made to run. That reversal must not be produced by handing the reductionist a bad, circular, or ad hoc inductive base. It has to fall out of a genuinely sound inductive track record together with the anti-reductionist's own structure.

Constraints on the scenario:

- One paragraph, set in ordinary life (a workplace, a family, a neighborhood — not a courtroom, newsroom, laboratory, or hospital ethics committee), with an ordinary adult hearer and an ordinary factual claim. No reported miracles, no scientific or expert testimony, no children, no known liars, no authority figures.
- The hearer must have a real, non-negligible inductive track record bearing on this speaker or on claims of this kind, and that track record must not be an artifact of the hearer having been in a position to check only the easy or convenient cases.
- The hearer must also hold some independent evidence against the claim, weaker than the induction. Nothing in the case may make the claim's truth or falsity independently checkable.
- The scenario itself must not mention either theory or use its vocabulary.

Then, in labeled sections:

1. State the belief, the hearer's total evidence, and which facts about the case are epistemically relevant.
2. Give the reductionist's verdict and the anti-reductionist's verdict, each derived from the theory rather than stipulated, and name the specific commitment of each theory that produces its verdict.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work. Say what happens to both verdicts if you remove that feature, and what happens if you instead strengthen it. In particular, tell me whether there is a magnitude of the independent counter-evidence at which the two theories swap verdicts, and what that would show.
4. Give the strongest way for an anti-reductionist to deny that the case is a counterexample — for instance, by arguing that a defeater does not cancel an entitlement but enters a weighing, or by arguing that in this case the default entitlement was never triggered — and say what that reply costs the view.
5. Finally, say whether the case exposes a real structural asymmetry between the two views or only an asymmetry in how each is usually stated, and how I could tell the difference. If you can also construct the mirrored case, in which the anti-reductionist is the more credulous party, say whether it requires a different mechanism or the same one running backwards.
