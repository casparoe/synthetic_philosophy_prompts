I recently spent an evening arguing with a close friend about the argument from illusion, the argument from hallucination, and disjunctivism. We kept sliding between disjunctivism about experience and disjunctivism about evidence, which I think was part of the problem. My friend holds that these arguments force a common-kind view of perceptual experience, on which veridical perception, illusion, and hallucination share the same basic experiential core; I hold that disjunctivism is not a verbal dodge but a substantive alternative, and that the arguments only go through on an assumption that needs defending. We talked past each other for hours, and I came away thinking that the real problem is pedagogical: people are taught the argument from illusion with the same three examples, and they absorb the conclusion before they understand the premises. I have now agreed to lead a two-hour session on this topic for a reading group of cognitive science graduate students and a few engineers. They have some background in psychology and neuroscience, but almost no philosophy of perception. I need you to plan the session, not deliver it.

Act as a philosopher of perception who has taught this material to interdisciplinary audiences and who knows where non-philosophers reliably go wrong. Before you produce the plan, think carefully and brainstorm: list the candidate orders of ideas, candidate carrying examples, and candidate misconceptions, then choose among them and explain your choices.

The plan should include:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit. What has to be in place before the argument from illusion can even be stated? What has to be in place before disjunctivism can be stated as a live option rather than a denial? Where exactly does the common-kind assumption enter, and when should the audience be made to feel its pull? Where exactly does the distinction between disjunctivism about experience and disjunctivism about evidence belong, and how should I keep the audience from conflating them? How should the sequence make the dialectic feel inevitable rather than like a trick?

2. The one carrying example that should run through the whole session. I am tired of the bent stick in water and the Müller-Lyer lines. I want something less worn. Consider, as a candidate, a mirror-clock case: you look at a clock directly, and you look at the same clock through a mirror; in both cases you can have an experience as of a clock of a certain shape and time, and the two experiences may be subjectively indistinguishable, but in one case you are seeing the clock and in the other you are seeing its reflection. If that example will not carry the weight, say why and replace it. Specify what the example must have and what it must lack, and at which points in the sequence it should be revisited.

3. The misconceptions the audience will bring or form. I predict at least: that illusion and hallucination are the same phenomenon; that disjunctivism is just naive realism with a new name; that the argument from illusion proves that we perceive sense-data; that disjunctivism is an anti-scientific refusal to talk about what is shared; and that the debate is merely verbal. For each, say where in the sequence it should be headed off, what the audience will be tempted to say, and the exact move that should dislodge it without begging the question. Also list the live objections they will raise in the order they will raise them, and how the sequence should absorb each.

4. A check for understanding at the end: one or two questions or mini-cases that would show whether they have grasped the structure of the dialectic, not just memorized a label. Include what a good answer would look like and what a merely verbal answer would look like.

5. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and why. I would rather leave them with a clean puzzle than a fake completeness.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or bibliography. Do not explain disjunctivism to me; plan how I should explain it. Write the plan as a polished document I can use to prepare, with headings and a clear sequence.
