I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time now — long enough to have watched a particular argumentative move rise from a curiosity to something close to a house style, and I have never found a satisfactory name for it. The move runs like this. Someone advances a thesis: that beliefs are never among the causes of action, say, or that nothing is ever owed to anyone. An interlocutor does not answer with evidence. Instead they point at the act of asserting the thesis. You have just offered me a consideration in favour of your view, they say; in doing so you have treated considerations as things that can favour views; so your denial refutes itself. The first party replies that this is a trick — that they were describing how things are rather than recommending anything, that a sentence can be used without the user being committed to everything its surface suggests — and the thread dies there, each side convinced the other has cheated.

What interests me is that the same shape shows up with quite different joints. Sometimes the claim is that the denial cannot be thought, because the concepts it would need are precisely the ones under attack. Sometimes it is that the denial cannot be asserted without inconsistency, which is a narrower and more tractable claim about what the speaker has committed themselves to in speaking. And sometimes it is neither: the claim is that the denial cannot be lived, that the practice in question is inescapable, so the theory that would abolish it is idle rather than false. I have seen all three delivered in the same triumphant tone, and I suspect they are not the same move at all, and that the third is doing something much weaker than its users suppose. I cannot stop deploying the concept of a reason while I go about my day; does that bear in the slightest on whether reasons exist?

I have a vague sense that something ancient is in the background here — a way of defending a principle by observing that anyone who states a denial of it has already conceded it — and that various later writers have attached their own names to neighbouring versions of the move, some of them treating it as the most abused argument in philosophy and others building whole positions on it.

What I want is not the label. If an established concept covers this, I want to know how far it reaches and where it stops: which of the versions above it actually covers, which are cousins rather than instances, and what the nearest competing terms are, so that I can tell which one belongs to which case. If no single concept covers it, I would like a term coined, with a definition that survives the boundary cases — above all, whether there is a principled way to distinguish a move that shows a position to be incoherent from one that shows only that a position cannot be put into practice, and whether the second kind of move can ever give a reason to believe anything, or only a reason to stop talking. I am equally interested in whether the failure mode has a recognised name, because the same form of argument, when the presupposition it leans on is optional or merely local, strikes me as one of the most reliable ways of bullying a person out of a position. A bare name will not help me; I have collected half a dozen already and none of them fits.
