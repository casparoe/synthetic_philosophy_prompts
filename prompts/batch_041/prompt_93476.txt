I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about the question "what is this for?" — in biology, in medicine, and now in machine learning. I'm not a philosopher and I'm on deadline, so what I need from you is an audit I can act on, not a seminar.

The feature is built around a passage from a well-reviewed trade book on biology that I had planned to quote at length. Here it is:

"Ask what a heart is for and you will get two answers. The older answer, which runs from Aristotle through Darwin, says that a trait's function is the effect for which it was preserved: the function is written into the trait's history. The newer answer, which comes out of Cummins, says that a function is not a fact about history at all but a fact about a system: to give the function of a part is to say what it contributes to the capacity of the larger whole that contains it — the heart circulates the blood, which is what a circulatory system does. Function talk, on this view, is simply a way of decomposing a complicated system into working parts, and anything that can be decomposed that way has functions, whether or not anything ever selected it. A swamp creature's heart has a function; so does the pump in your car. The etiological view is the one that takes the normativity of function seriously: a heart that does not pump is not merely different from other hearts, it is broken. The causal-role view cannot say that, which is why most philosophers of biology have given up on it — and why medicine quietly keeps the etiological view in the drawer. In the end the two views are not really rivals: one tells you where a trait came from, the other tells you what it currently does. The only live question is whether selection is the right piece of history to fasten onto, and it is, because nothing else in biology can underwrite the difference between a working part and a broken one."

One of the philosophers I interviewed put the other side of it to me in a single line in an email: "The etiological account is design talk in Darwinian dress. It says a trait is for whatever it was once chosen for, which makes function a fact about the distant past and tells you nothing about the organism in front of you."

And a source of mine who works on interpretability for neural networks told me the whole debate has been settled by his field: nobody asks what a neuron in a trained network was selected for; they ask what it contributes to the network's performance; so function in the only systems we build from scratch is causal role, and as biology becomes engineering, the etiological account is a legacy concept.

Please take the role of a philosopher of biology who knows the function literature and the philosophy of medicine well, and who is used to telling journalists what they cannot print. Think this through carefully before you write, and begin by working through the book passage clause by clause, listing for yourself every point at which a specialist would pause; I would rather see your reasoning about the awkward clauses than a tidy summary.
