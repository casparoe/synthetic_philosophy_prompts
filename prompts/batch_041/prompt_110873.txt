I sit on my hospital's neuroethics committee and we vote Thursday on a protocol I can't get a clean grip on. A neurology group here wants a first-in-human trial for patients with a fast, fatal neurodegenerative disease: as regions of the brain die off, they replace the lost tissue with a prosthetic that does the same input-output job, one small chunk per surgery, spaced over roughly three years, until by the end most of the relevant circuitry is prosthetic rather than biological. The patients are terminal and have signed on.

What I have to write up is the consent design. Before every surgery the patient has to pass what the team calls a continuity interview -- life story, recognition of family, their own stated values, and a yes to "do you still feel like the same person" -- and only then can they re-consent to the next stage; if they fail it, everything stops. Their fallback is a directive the patients sign at the outset, saying that if they stop being themselves the team should finish the procedure anyway, since whoever comes out the other side has their memories and their unfinished business. Half the committee thinks that's humane, half thinks its a machine writing its own permission slip, because the thing we're supposed to be testing is the thing doing the testing.

Draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the vote: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a bottom line we can act on, and if it's conditions then the one or two changes that would flip you to a yes, with a deadline we could actually put in the letter. Don't just hand me the standard line that identity isn't what really matters -- I've taught that seminar and it doesn't tell me whether this consent structure is valid. I want your verdict, the strongest case for the side you rule against, what would move you off your position, and where your reasoning is most likely to break down.
