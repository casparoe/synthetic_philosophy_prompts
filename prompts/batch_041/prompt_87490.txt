I sit on the acquisitions and repatriation committee of a mid-sized encyclopedic museum, and for the past several months we have been circling a pattern that I cannot stop noticing across the whole sector. The same institutions that now return human remains to descendant communities with comparatively little resistance -- and that will sometimes hand back objects described as sacred, ritually used, or still caught up in a living religious practice -- remain extraordinarily reluctant to part with art: the Benin Bronzes, the Parthenon Marbles, Egyptian antiquities, and the rest, even where the original acquisition involved conquest, coercion, or a price that nobody involved would have called free. Our committee's working rule, which I suspect many museums share without ever stating it, is roughly that duress vitiates a title but a sale does not, and that the strength of a claimant's standing tracks whether the object is the sort of thing a person can be wronged through -- remains, relics, consecrated things -- rather than whether the claimant community was itself wronged in the taking. I would like you to tell me what philosophical commitments this two-track practice presupposes.

What theory of property does it require, and what theory of the value or ontology of objects -- that some things are persons-adjacent and others are mere chattel, that a community has a claim to its material culture as such, or something else entirely? Does the practice presuppose that cultural identity is bound up with specific objects, or can it get by with a thinner view on which the wrong is only ever a wrong to individuals? I am especially interested in whether the two tracks are consistent with one another: if the special status of remains rests on the dignity or continued personhood of the dead, does anything in that reasoning extend to a mask or a bronze that a community treats as a living presence, or does the practice quietly rely on two incompatible pictures of what makes a claim valid? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined -- which rival theories would license the same behavior. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we abandoned the presupposed view and adopted either a flatly possession-based account of title, on which long possession plus a bona fide purchase settles the matter regardless of origin, or the opposite view that a people's relation to its heritage is not a property relation at all; which parts of current museum practice would survive each replacement, and which would become indefensible overnight. I do not need a reading list or citations; I want the argument, and I would rather you reason from the practice as I have described it than reach for authorities.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
