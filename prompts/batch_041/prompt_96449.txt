I'm doing a chapter on dream skepticism and I keep circling back to Borges's "The Circular Ruins" -- the one where the guy dreams a whole man into existence at a ruined temple, and at the end walks into the fire and realizes it doesnt burn him because he's somebody else's dream. I want to read it against the Zhuangzi butterfly passage, but not the usual way, where the butterfly just gets treated as the ancient version of Descartes's dream doubt and that's it. What actually interests me is that Zhuangzi's little story doesn't end in doubt. The last move is to say there must be some difference between Zhou and the butterfly, and to call the whole thing the transformation of things -- so it closes with a claim, and the claim is about what a self is, not about whether we can know anything. (Same chapter, a bit earlier, even says you only recognize a dream once you've woken up from the big one.) So does Borges's story pull the same kind of move? The dreamer in the ruins is undone by learning he's dreamed, but the story plays it as release, not refutation. Where does the story track the Zhuangzi ending, where does it break from it, and which of the two, if either, is actually a skeptic? My suspicion is neither, and that the standard "ancient dream argument" framing flattens both of them. I want a reading that's fair to both texts in both directions, says where the analogy fails, and says what the fiction can show about the dreamer's identity that the philosophy on its own can't.
