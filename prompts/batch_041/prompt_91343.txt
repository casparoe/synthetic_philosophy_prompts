I have no academic background in any of this — no training in economics, statistics, or philosophy — but for about a year I have been turning over a problem that began as a family argument and has since grown into something I think about most days, and I would like you to set out the most important implications of a certain idea for it.

The argument is this. My brother-in-law and I follow the same football club, and for two seasons we have watched the same matches, read the same reports, and talked them over afterwards. He is convinced the team is genuinely strong and will go on winning; I am convinced it has been lucky and will fall back. We have each been willing to bet the other at odds we separately regard as favourable, and neither of us is a fool. What troubles me is that we can both be so confident while looking at the very same thing.

Someone told me that much of the formal work on belief rests on what he called a common prior: that underneath everything people start from the same basic odds, so that any disagreement between two of them reflects a difference in what they have learned rather than in where they began. I would like to know what follows if one takes that seriously — not for specialists, but for someone trying to understand his own arguments.

Specifically, and in plain language rather than mathematics, I want to understand: what it implies about whether two people who have seen the same evidence and are both reasonable can honestly disagree, and what I should conclude about my brother-in-law and myself if it holds; what it implies about whether a bet of the kind we keep making can exist at all, and why betting and prediction markets nonetheless appear to be full of activity; what it implies about how I should read the price of something on a market as a summary of what people know; whether the assumption is meant as a description of how people actually are or only as a convenient fiction, and how I might tell the difference; and what survives of all these conclusions if the assumption is dropped, since I suspect it is false but want to know exactly what I would be giving up by rejecting it.

Two things I cannot get past, and I would like you to address both directly. First, a friend who knows this literature tells me the common prior is not really a claim about what two people believe now but about what each of them would have believed in situations that never in fact arose — the branches of the world that did not come up. I cannot see how a claim about things that never happened could bear on whether my brother-in-law and I should be able to bet with each other at all, so please explain what is meant and whether it matters. Second, I keep being told that it is not enough for us to share a starting point; we must also each know that we share it, and know that we know, and so on without end. I do not understand what work those extra layers of knowing do that the first one does not.

Finally, tell me which of the implications you take to be the most important, and why that one rather than the others.
