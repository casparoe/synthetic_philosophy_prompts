I teach a first-year seminar, and a dispute in my section last month sent me looking for vocabulary: one student made a dismissive remark about another's accent, the target asked the dean's office to extract a formal apology, two classmates told him he should have dealt with it himself rather than run to an authority, and several others then faulted me for not escalating the matter sooner. I have been second-guessing my own handling of it ever since.

What I found was the honor/dignity/victimhood typology of the sociologists Bradley Campbell and Jason Manning, in their article on microaggression and moral cultures and their later book on the rise of victimhood culture: honor cultures locate worth in reputation and answer slights directly; dignity cultures hold that every person bears equal worth, so slights are to be tolerated and conflicts settled with restraint or through law; victimhood cultures confer moral standing on the wronged and route grievances to third parties and audiences. I am teaching Hamlet in the spring and want to read it through that typology, partly because I suspect the play will make my students more honest about the incident than the incident did.

So: where does the play dramatize the honor code and its logic of self-help — vengeance, the duel, reputation — and where does it turn on that code rather than endorse it? Does it offer dignity culture as a genuine alternative, or expose Claudius's court as just another arena of private interest? Is Ophelia the play's victimhood figure, and does the play grant her grievance the recognition such a culture would seek? I care less about tagging characters than about the question the typology forces: does the play rank these three moral orders, or refuse the ranking? Where it resists the framework — the ghost, providence, Horatio's commission to report the story — say so plainly rather than forcing a fit, and close with what the play adds to this debate that the sociology alone cannot. Please write in American English.
