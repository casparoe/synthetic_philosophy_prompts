I have no statistical training beyond what I have picked up reading on my own, and I keep running into what looks to me like a contradiction at the center of how research results are reported and judged. The practice, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. A study is carried out; the researcher computes the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as what was observed, on the assumption that there is really no effect; if that probability falls below a threshold, by convention one in twenty, the result is called significant and published, and if it does not, the result is called null and usually shelved. Every account I have read warns me, in the same breath, that this probability is not the probability that the hypothesis is false, and that a non-significant result is not evidence that there is no effect. Yet the surrounding institutions behave as if it were: findings are tallied by whether they crossed the threshold; a failed repetition of a significant study is treated as a refutation of the original; and the whole apparatus of preregistration seems built to fix in advance the very things — how many observations will be gathered, when the experimenter will stop — that the arithmetic is supposed to depend on. I have also read that some groups now ask for more than the conventional test: they want the interval of plausible values to exclude the smallest effect anyone would care about, and sometimes a ratio comparing how well one hypothesis predicts the data against another. I cannot tell whether these are three ways of saying one thing, three incompatible things bolted together, or one coherent thing wearing three masks.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of evidence and inference that this whole practice presupposes. What view of probability does a procedure defined over hypothetical repetitions of an experiment commit one to, and can that view be squared with the interpretation researchers place on their own results? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — for instance, a view on which only the probability of the hypothesis given the data matters, or a view on which the evidential import of a body of data is the same however the data were collected? Does the practice presuppose that inference is a matter of choosing between two hypotheses with controlled error rates, or of measuring the strength of evidence, or of estimating a quantity together with its uncertainty — and can any one of these bear the weight of all the institutional machinery, or does the machinery require several incompatible ones at once? I am especially puzzled by the stopping rule: is it strictly presupposed by the practice that the same observations mean different things depending on whether the sample size was fixed in advance or the experimenter kept collecting until the result pleased them, or is that merely an artifact of the arithmetic that the practice could shed without changing its conclusions? The notion of a replication troubles me in the same way: what does it presuppose about a stable true effect, and about repetitions being independent and interchangeable? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it equally well. Finally, suppose one replaced whatever view it presupposes with the most natural rival; tell me concretely what would have to change — which of our daily habits of reporting, publishing, and replicating would survive intact, and which would have to be abandoned.
