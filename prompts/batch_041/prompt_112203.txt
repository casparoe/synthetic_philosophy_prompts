So two people on my neighborhood association, Dana and Marcus, got into it over whether we should pass a new set of written rules -- noise cut-off at 10, bins out only on pickup day, no parking on the grass, that kind of thing. I was just there taking minutes and honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Here's the back and fourth, roughly.

(1) Dana said we need the rules in writing, because "everyone just knows how it's done here" really means "everyone who grew up here knows." Newcomers and renters get dinged for stuff nobody ever told them, and the old-timers get to call their own habits "the way we do things." Written rules are fair because they're public and land on everyone the same.

(2) Marcus said the more you write down, the more you turn neighbors into each other's compliance officers. A street where everything is a rule is a street where every annoyance is a violation and every violation wants a response. Places that actually work run on a few shared norms and a lot of patience for harmless weirdness. He said her ordinance would trade a warm street for a correct one.

(3) Dana came back: "patience for harmless weirdness" is exactly the phrase the incumbents use for "we do what we're used to and call it normal." She said the street is already tight right now -- tight for the people who moved in last year, loose for the people who've been here thirty. So Marcus isn't arguing for looseness, he's arguing to keep the current tightness but make it unofficial, which is worse, because you can't appeal an unwritten rule.

(4) Marcus said fine, but she's smushing two different things together: how many rules a place has, and how hard it comes down on people who break them. You could have a hundred rules nobody enforces, or one rule with a fine bolted to it. What makes a place cold isn't the length of the list, it's the enforcement and the reporting. He said write the rules if you want, but build in that nobody gets fined and nobody reports anybody -- you just go talk to them -- and you get her fairness without the chill.

(5) Dana said a rule with no enforcement at all is just a suggestion, and a suggestion is exactly the unwritten norm Marcus didn't want; so the only real question was ever enforcement, which means he's quietly agreed with her that the change that matters is the one he's afraid of. Marcus said that's a word game -- a written rule nobody enforces still tells a newcomer what's expected, which an unwritten norm doesn't. Neither budged.

Can you go through moves 1 through 5 and say who's right at each one, not just who wins overall? And at the end, tell me what both of them seem to be assuming that maybe neither has looked at -- I have a feeling they're each smuggling in a picture of what a rule even is, and that's why they keep talking past each other.
