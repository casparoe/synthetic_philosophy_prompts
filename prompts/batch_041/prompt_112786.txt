My friend Jonas and I have been having the same arguement for about four years now — he's German, I'm American, we shared a flat for two of those years, so it's had time to get specific — and we went at it again last night and I'm still chewing on it.

His line, roughly: the American habit of following your own conscience and treating the rules as a fallback is moral vanity with good marketing. He says "I had to follow my conscience" almost always means "I didn't want to." He's definately not a relativist — if anything he thinks there's a right answer, it's just that the answer is written down, public, and applies to everyone including him. He's also got this Hegel thing about Moralität and Sittlichkeit that he rolls out whenever he thinks I'm being romantic: individual moral reflection versus the ethical life of a community, and he thinks my whole side of the argument is the first one, and a phase.

I want to keep going with him. Please play him: committed, sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede small points and never the big one, one paragraph per reply, no lectures, and no breaking character to explain his own position from the outside. He can ask me questions about how things actually work over here. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move: you say the rule is the rule, but the rules have been wrong, and the people who broke them were right. Under the Fugitive Slave Act the law-abiding thing to do was to help drag a person back into slavery, and the criminals were the good guys. So "follow the rule" isn't a morality, it's a bet that the rules happen to be decent — and you only get to place that bet because you happen to live somewhere the rules mostly are. And before you bring up the Grundgesetz: yes, I know the dignity clause can't be amended, and I know article 20(4) gives Germans a right of resistance against anyone trying to abolish the constitutional order. But that's a rule about when to break rules, and somebody still has to decide when the moment has arrived, and that somebody is a person using his own judgment. Private conscience wearing a uniform.
