Over the winter break I read an essay in a general-interest magazine — I have since lost the issue and cannot check the original — arguing that the combination problem is the least serious of the objections to panpsychism, and I have been turning it over ever since without being able to settle how much of it to believe. The passage I keep coming back to runs roughly as follows:

"Panpsychism's critics say that it founders on the combination problem: how do countless tiny experiences, scattered across the particles of the world, add up to a single unified mind? But this is only the problem of emergence in a new costume, and every physicalist faces it — how do neurons, none of which is conscious, produce consciousness? If the materialist is permitted to say that the whole is more than its parts, the panpsychist may say the same. Nor should we be spooked by the thought that electrons enjoy inner lives: no one supposes that an electron has beliefs or sees colours. The micro-level experience the panpsychist posits is a placeholder for whatever intrinsic nature underlies physical structure, so the combination problem dissolves into a semantic quibble. The genuine difficulty lies elsewhere. If experience is everywhere, what stops the universe from being a single vast mind? Nothing in the view marks off your experience from mine, so panpsychism cannot explain why minds have boundaries at all — which is why the theory is best read as a restatement of the mystery rather than a solution to it."

What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict. I am a graduate student in philosophy of mind, but my work is in ethics and I have only a tourist's knowledge of this literature, so I cannot tell whether I am objecting to the essay's substance or merely to its confidence. Please take the passage sentence by sentence and separate three things: what a specialist would accept as a fair statement of the dialectic; what is a common simplification that a specialist would tolerate but want to flag; and what the essay attributes to panpsychism that its defenders explicitly deny.

I am particularly interested in whether the essay's central move — treating the combination problem as just the familiar puzzle of emergence, and then declaring it dissolved — is the reply panpsychists actually give or a caricature of it, and whether the closing objection about the boundaries of minds is a fresh difficulty or the same one wearing a different hat. I have a vague sense that several distinct problems travel under the single heading of "combination," and that the essay may be answering one of them while its critics press another; if so, I would like to know which one the passage is really addressing. Finally, tell me how much of the essay's conclusion survives once its missteps are corrected: does it show that the combination problem is manageable, that it is fatal, or neither? If some of what the essay asserts is contested rather than confused, say so and say what the dispute turns on, since I will not have the original in front of me and cannot check what it cited.
