So I'm in a reading group that's been going through Simone Weil, and we hit the line on the back of her book — 'A political party is a machine to manufacture collective passion' — and everyone nodded like that settled the matter. I'd like to know how much of it to actually beleive, and I want a number, not a shrug.

Here's the proposition I want your credence on: that political parties make the people inside them systematically worse at believing true things about politics and policy, and that they do this because of features intrinsic to what a party is — not because of corrupt individuals, and not because of ordinary human weakness that would have found some other outlet anyway. I'm at about 0.4. The guy next to me is at 0.85 and thinks I'm being naive. Where does our disagreement actually live?

Break it up. Weil's own sentence has a triple character packed into it, so treat these as three separate claims and give me a probability for each: (a) a party is a device for manufacturing shared passion, (b) it exerts pressure on the thought of each individual member, (c) its sole and unlimited aim is its own growth. For each one, how likely is it true at all, and how likely is it intrinsic to parties rather than to any competitive coalition — a faction, a firm, a school of thought, a fandom, a subreddit with a party line?

Then a distinction I care about more than I expected to: is the mechanism pressure or selection? Weil seems to think parties convert people — put a person in, apply collective force, out comes a party-liner. The alternative is that parties mostly sort: the ones who care about truth leave or never rise, the ambitious and the indifferent get promoted, and the party's beliefs look distorted even though nobody's thinking was bent at all. If it's mostly selection, does abolishing parties even help, or does the same sorting just happen in whatever replaces them?

Also tell me what the comparison class is even supposed to be — parties versus what? No organized politics, looser associations, factions inside a single assembly, some direct-democracy scheme with no parties at all? "Harder than they would otherwise be" is doing a lot of work in my sentence and I'm not sure I can fill it in.

And: does the whole thing only go through if you buy her theology of attention and truth as something you receive rather than manufacture? If the conclusion just falls out of that, then my credence should track the theology, which feels like cheating.

Finish with the sub-question my number is most sensitive to and what would move it most — a comparison across party systems, some psychology of loyalty and group reasoning, or just a good thought experiment about a party with no offices, no patronage and no careers in it.
