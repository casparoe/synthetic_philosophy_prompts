I'm in a seminar on Confucian ethics and we spent most of last week on one passage in the Mencius — the one in Jin Xin I (book 7A, chapter 35, I think) where Tao Ying asks Mencius: suppose Shun is Son of Heaven and Gaoyao is his chief minister of justice, and Shun's father Gusou murders someone. What happens? Mencius says, flatly, that Gusou gets arrested. Tao Ying presses him: wouldn't Shun stop it? Mencius says no — how could he, Gaoyao had the authority. And then Mencius adds that Shun would have looked on giving up the empire the way you look at a worn-out shoe, would have secretly taken his father on his back, fled to the edge of the sea, and lived there happily, forgetting the empire. I'm paraphrasing the translation we're using, so dont lean on my exact wording for anything.

What I can't get my head around is how the two halves of that answer fit together, and my professor's explanation — something about roles being constitutive rather than additive, and about Shun acting out of his role as son rather than stepping outside his role as ruler — went straight past me. Concretely: Shun does not block the arrest, which looks like a concession that the law's authority isnt his to override. But then he takes the fugitive and runs, which looks like he's using his remaining power to defeat exactly that law, or at least to put his father beyond its reach. If role ethics says obligations come from the relationships you stand in, Shun stands in two at once — ruler and son — and the passage seems to let the second one win without ever cancelling the first. Is that a coherent position, or is Mencius just accepting a tragic conflict and picking a side? And what work is the worn-out shoe comparison doing — is the empire being described as merely instrumental, or as something genuinely good that Shun nonetheless rightly gives up?

A classmate argued that Shun is basically a deserter, that a ruler who sneaks off with a convicted murderer has failed in his role and the passage is a warning about the limits of filial devotion. Another said thats exactly backwards — that Mencius is showing filial love as the root that makes any other virtue possible, so Shun isn't failing but doing the thing that makes him Shun. I'd like help working out which reading the text supports, and specifically: what is that detail — that he goes secretly rather than making a public renunciation — actually doing in the story, and does Mencius think Shun can still be a good ruler after this, or is the whole point that he stops being one? Walk me through it in plain terms, and if you think the passage is genuinely ambiguous, say where the ambiguity lives instead of just resolving it for me.
