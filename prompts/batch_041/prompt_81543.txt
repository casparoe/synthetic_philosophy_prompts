I'm giving a talk in two weeks defending expected-value fanaticism -- roughly, the claim that for any two finite goods there's some nonzero probability of the bigger one that outweighs a certainty of the smaller, no matter how tiny that probability gets -- to a seminar thats mostly economists who teach decision theory (they'll be thinking St Petersburg, Archimedean axioms, risk aversion, representation theorems) plus two philosophers who work on infinite ethics and one who thinks the whole position is a reductio of consequentialism. My line is that fanaticism isn't a separate premise you have to swallow on top of expected utility; it's what you get when you drop what I'm calling threshold parochialism, the assumption that there's some scale of stakes at which probability quietly stops mattering. My key move is a rescaling argument -- whatever threshold the anti-fanatic names, I build a case just below it where they'll admit the tiny probability should count, then push the stakes back up and ask what changed.
