I have been having a long, circular argument with my sister, who is a potter, and my brother-in-law, who designs board games, about when copying someone else's work is wrong — I am also in a graduate seminar on intellectual property this term, which has made things worse rather than better — and I have decided that what I need is not more examples but a statement of what my own views add up to. So here are my verdicts on cases, as honestly as I can give them. A stranger at a craft fair photographs my sister's mugs, copies the shape exactly, and sells his own at a lower price at the next fair: fine, or at any rate not wrong — nobody owns a shape. A different stranger buys one of her mugs, scans it, and 3D-prints copies to sell: not fine. A photographer friend posts a picture of a lighthouse at dawn; I drive to the same lighthouse, shoot my own frame from the same spot at the same hour, and sell prints: fine. I take her file and sell prints from it: not fine. My brother-in-law's new game turns on an unusual mechanic; I build a different game around the same mechanic and publish it: fine, though if I lift the wording of his rulebook I am not fine. I retype a novel I admire and put it online for free: not fine. I write a novel that follows another novel scene by scene — same events, same order, new names, sentences entirely my own: not fine, and I find I feel this is nearly as bad as retyping it, which surprises me. I write a song over the same four-chord progression as a hit record: fine. I tour a rival firm's warehouse, see how they have organized picking and packing, copy the whole scheme in my own warehouse, and take a good deal of their business: fine. I find an unpublished manuscript by a long-dead novelist in an archive and publish it under my own name: not fine, and this one matters to me, because nobody is out of pocket and nobody would have read it otherwise. The case I cannot settle is perfume: a house analyzes a rival's scent and brings out a molecule-for-molecule match. I want to say fine — it is only chemistry — and I also want to say not fine, and I cannot tell which of those verdicts is doing the real work.

My own working principle is this: you may not copy the thing itself, the particular file, text, or object that someone made, but you may learn everything you can from it and make your own. The wrong is in the copying of an artifact, not in the borrowing of an idea. That gets most of the list, but it says the scene-by-scene novel is fine, which I do not believe, and it says the perfume copy is wrong, which I am not sure I believe either.

What I want from you is the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best captures the list, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to cases that are not on the list, with the case that forces each clause named, and with an honest account of which of my verdicts has to go: I would rather lose a verdict than buy a principle with a clause invented to save it. Please also tell me what your preferred principle says about a case I have not thought through — someone publishes, without permission, a translation of a novel into another language, doing real work of their own in the process — and give me several further cases of your own devising, in the spirit of the list, together with your verdicts on them, so that I can see whether I really believe the principle. Keep every string short enough to read at a glance, and do not hedge in the "statement" fields.
