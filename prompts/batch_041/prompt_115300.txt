For the past few years I've been reading people who argue that the sheer number of insects — on the order of a quintillion alive at any moment, with vastly more born and dying each year — should dominate our moral priorities: even a very small probability that any given insect is sentient, multiplied by numbers that large, yields an expected quantity of suffering exceeding that of all the vertebrates we ordinarily worry about. Some of the people who take this seriously reorganize their diets, their donations, and their research around it; others think it shows that something has gone wrong in the reasoning rather than in our priorities, and reach for talk of absurdity, fanaticism, or the limits of expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty. I don't want a verdict on insects; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement. Both sides appear to accept the same neurobiology, roughly the same numbers, and the same premise that suffering matters whatever its species, so where is the impasse actually located? Is it a hidden empirical dispute — about the prior probability of sentience in a small nervous system, and whether any evidence could move that prior? Is it the familiar problem that a small enough probability of an enormous harm can swamp every ordinary consideration, so that the real disagreement is about how to reason under uncertainty rather than about insects? Is it a dispute about the form of the argument — whether moral status should be computed as a per-capita probability times a headcount at all, or whether sentience is better treated as a threshold than as a graded quantity that scales with numbers? Or is it a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for: that a conclusion this strange must have a flaw somewhere, against the thought that strangeness is not evidence? Tell me which of these is doing the work, which are genuinely separable from the others, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a better estimate, a decision-theoretic result about when this kind of expected-value reasoning is warranted, a distinction that dissolves the standoff, or an honest case that the disagreement is bedrock and should be expected to persist. And if the two sides are really answering different questions under one heading — how many minds there are, what we owe them, and how to act while uncertain about both — say so and show how their arguments diverge.
