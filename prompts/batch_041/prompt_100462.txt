I serve on the charter-review commission of a mid-sized city that has, over the past several years, moved much of its budgeting onto a scheme usually called futarchy, and I need a written analysis for the commission of what the scheme presupposes.

The arrangement is as follows. The council first adopts, by ordinary vote, a single measurable objective for the coming term; last cycle it was a composite index combining median household income, life expectancy, and an air-quality measure. Once that objective is fixed, the council does not vote on which policy package to adopt. Two conditional prediction markets are opened for each pair of competing packages — one asking what the index would be if package A were implemented, the other asking what it would be under package B. Traders buy and sell, and the package whose market implies the higher index is adopted automatically. The council's own preferences are meant to be irrelevant at that stage; its only task is to set the index.

What puzzles me is a pattern in how the scheme is actually used. Twice the commission has declined to route a decision through the markets even though the same logic would seem to apply. The first was a proposal to site a waste-transfer station: the markets were never opened, and the minutes record only that the affected neighborhood had not consented. The second was whether to widen the index itself to include a measure of how the city's poorest residents were faring; the council decided that one directly, reasoning that the people the markets would be betting about should not themselves be the object of a wager.

So on one hand the city treats the question of what to do as equivalent to the question of what will happen, given a fixed yardstick; on the other hand it treats certain decisions as exempt from that reduction. Please reconstruct, for the commission, the theory this practice presupposes. What view of value, or of the relation between facts and values, does the market mechanism commit the city to — must the good be a single measurable quantity, or can the scheme run on weaker assumptions? What does it assume about the traders' bets — what view of belief, and of how dispersed private opinions can constitute collective knowledge? And does the pattern of exemptions commit the city to something the mechanism itself cannot state, for instance that some goods are not commensurable with the index, or that consent functions as a side-constraint rather than as one more value to be maximized?

Be explicit about what is strictly presupposed versus what the scheme merely happens to fit, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same procedure. Finally, suppose the presupposed view were replaced by a rival one; say concretely what would then have to change in the city's procedure. I am less interested in whether futarchy is a good idea than in what this particular use of it is already assuming.
