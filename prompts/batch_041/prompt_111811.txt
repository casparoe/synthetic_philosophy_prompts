For most of my career I was a neurobiologist, and since retiring I have been trying to work out, in a systematic way, which systems I actually believe have moral status; I would like you to find the principle, if any, that underlies my verdicts, and to tell me which of them it cannot accommodate.

A laboratory rat can suffer, and it matters how we treat it. A faithful whole-brain emulation of a rat running on silicon matters to me exactly as much; if it does everything the biological rat does, I cannot see what the substrate could add. A thermostat, a plant, a bacterium: nothing, however much they regulate themselves. A large language model that, when prompted, produces fluent first-person avowals of fear and longing: I do not believe it for a moment. Yet a reinforcement-learning agent that will forgo other rewards and plot elaborate routes to escape a state it has learned to avoid leaves me uneasy, and I cannot say whether my unease is of a different kind from my unease about the emulated rat. A simulated human inside a video game, however rich its scripted inner life, is nothing to me, because that inner life is stipulated by the designers rather than instantiated. And a fleeting configuration inside a large distributed computation that, for a fraction of a second, realizes the functional profile of a human pain state before dissolving: here I want to say nothing, though I recognize this sits badly with what I said about the emulation.

Is there a single principle that yields all of these verdicts, or must I surrender some? Please state the principle as precisely as you can, indicate which case motivates each part of it, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it implies about a case I have not considered: a system that resembles no animal at all, but whose internal states track damage and repair as finely as a human being's do.
