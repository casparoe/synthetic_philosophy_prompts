I've been roped into writing a five-minute turn for our department's end-of-year revue — mostly graduate students, a couple of long-suffering partners, and a running theme that everyone else is doing knowing little routines about academic life itself. I'd like mine to be a joke about an actual philosophical problem rather than about philosophers, and the topic I drew is the wrong kind of reasons problem. The contrast I want the joke to turn on is the one between a reason that bears on whether an attitude fits its object and a reason that bears only on whether having the attitude is advantageous — the distinction people sometimes put as object-given versus state-given reasons. The usual illustrations are the demon who will reward you for admiring him, so that you appear to have a reason to admire him which has nothing to do with his being admirable; the collector who will pay handsomely for your sincere admiration of a mediocre painting; and the billionaire who pays you only for forming the intention to drink a foul but harmless toxin which, having formed the intention, you would then have no reason actually to drink. There is also the fork between reading such cases as showing that the payoff really is a reason after all, so that fittingness is not the only route to a reason, and reading them as showing that it is not a reason at all. One case I have been toying with, which I would like you to use if you can make anything of it, is a demon who offers no external reward at all but proposes to adjust your character so that you admire him; the reason then attaches to the process of coming to have the attitude rather than to the attitude itself, and I suspect that is a good place for a joke to hide.

Please write the joke itself — a short monologue, a dialogue, or a two-line exchange, whichever suits it best — and give it a one-line tag at the end if that helps it land. It has to be genuinely amusing to someone who already knows the problem: the humour should come out of the structure of the cases, the asymmetry between reasons for attitudes and reasons for actions, or the awkwardness of the fork above, and not out of the mere mention of demons, philosophers or academic life. If the punchline could be transplanted onto any other philosophical position without loss, it has not worked. I am not interested in who said what, so please do not name names, guess at attributions, or offer me citations and further reading; the joke should stand on its own. And do write it in British English, with British spellings and idiom throughout, since I shall be performing it in London.
