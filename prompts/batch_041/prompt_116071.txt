I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar in metaphysics, and I want a case study that forces students to apply the apparatus of dispositions -- finks and masks in particular -- to material they cannot simply look up in the usual anthology. My students will already have the standard vocabulary: a mask is a condition that prevents a disposition from manifesting without removing it, as packing material prevents a fragile glass from breaking; a fink is a condition that removes the disposition precisely when its trigger conditions are met, the stock illustration being a glass whose fragility a sorcerer would strip away the moment it is struck. The case I want them to work on is the collapse of the elderly-priming effect in social psychology. In the original study, participants unscrambled sentences seeded with words associated with old age while a control group unscrambled neutral sentences; on leaving the laboratory, the primed group walked measurably more slowly down the corridor. The result was taken to show that a stereotype can be activated outside awareness and that the activation then shapes behaviour, and it became the paradigm case of social priming, a body of work that supplied some of the most conspicuous casualties of the replication crisis. When other laboratories repeated the experiment the effect did not appear, and in one widely discussed follow-up the experimenters' own expectations were manipulated experimentally: the walking-speed effect showed up only among participants whose experimenter had been led to expect it. That last wrinkle is why I want to use this case rather than the more familiar ones.

I would like the analysis run in both directions, and I want it disciplined about whose disposition is at stake. Begin by formulating the candidate dispositional claim as precisely as you can -- a person of a certain kind, primed in a certain way, is disposed to walk more slowly -- and then say what each party to the dispute is committed to. If the effect is genuine but failed to manifest in the replication settings, what exactly is the mask, and how would we tell a mask from a fink, that is, from a case in which the very conditions that trigger the disposition are what destroy it? If the effect is finkish, is the original finding an anomaly for the dispositional framework or an ordinary instance of it? And if the finding was an artifact -- of experimenter expectancy, say, or of analytic choices made after the fact -- whose disposition was being exercised all along, and what does it take to say so without equivocation? I would also like a verdict on what the framework licenses the field to conclude about the original result, as opposed to what the statistics alone license.

Then turn the lens around. I am interested in the charge that the mask clause renders dispositional claims unfalsifiable, since any failure to manifest can be redescribed as masking or finking, and I want to know whether the priming episode supports that charge. If the dispositionalist needs a principled rather than a merely sociological criterion for what counts as a mask, say what such a criterion would have to look like; if none is available, say so plainly instead of manufacturing one. Be explicit about the places where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak -- in particular about whether the disposition is present but masked and there is no disposition are, in a case like this, empirically distinguishable claims at all, and if they are not, what follows for the dispositional analysis of contested findings.
