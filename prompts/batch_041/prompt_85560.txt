Im putting together a paper on whether everyday conventions are actually moral obligations, and I keep running into the same arguement, so I want to write it up as the joint statement two people could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The two of them: one thinks a convention's grip comes from the practice itself -- you're answerable to the standards of the community you're standing in, and those standards can't be graded against some outside yardstick -- and the other thinks conventions only bind to the extent they track something independent of them (fairness, not freeloading on benefits you're happy to take, not causing harm). Both use tipping, queueing and jaywalking as their test cases, so keep it to those.

Before they start agreeing, have them lay the cases out on two axes: is the norm actually conformed to and approved of by locals, and is it officially on the books (a law, posted rules, a manager's instruction)? Tipping is unofficial but near-universal; jaywalking is official but widely ignored; queueing is usually both; add a fourth cell if it earns its keep.

Then the document itself. Exactly five numbered agreed claims to open with, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up at the end. After that, a table of what they still disagree about, one row per crux: the crux stated in neutral wording, each side's position in the wording its own side would sign off on, the case or argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about that case. The visitor question matters most to me -- does someone from a no-tipping country owe the tip, should they queue when nobody else does -- so make it come out either as a real disagreement or a verbal one, and if it's verbal, say which part is doing the work. British English, please.
