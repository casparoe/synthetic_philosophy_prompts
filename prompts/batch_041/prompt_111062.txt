I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing with a friend who does complexity theory, and the argument has been nagging at me because it bears directly on how my team makes design decisions. I am the engineering lead at a mid-sized software company, and our design reviews have developed a bad habit: someone invokes NP-hardness, or the fact that a problem is NP-complete, and the room treats that as a conversation-stopper. My friend insists this is a category mistake—that a worst-case hardness result for a problem class tells us almost nothing about whether a particular structured, small, or average-case instance is solvable in the time we have, and that the reduction establishing hardness may have nothing to do with the instances we actually care about. The deeper disagreement, as I understand it, is about what a complexity class is a property of—a problem type, a distribution of instances, or a decision procedure—and whether tractability is a mathematical predicate or a pragmatic one. I agree in principle, but I also think it would be equally foolish to ignore complexity results altogether, and I do not have a way to tell my engineers when a hardness result should change a design and when it should merely shift the burden of proof.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run in a thirty-minute design review, before we commit engineering months to a feature. Fix the role: the procedure is for a technically literate but not theoretically specialized engineering lead, with no complexity theorist in the room and no time to consult one. The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question has become one of values—how much we care about exactness, how much risk of poor scaling we can tolerate, what we owe users when a heuristic silently fails. It must distinguish at least three different appeals to hardness that my team currently conflates: a proof that no algorithm can do better in the worst case, a conditional hardness result that assumes P is not equal to NP, and an empirical observation that the algorithms we have tried scale badly. It should also say what to do when the relevant result is about approximation, parameterized complexity, or average-case behavior rather than exact worst-case solvability, and it should be honest about which steps are mathematical entailments and which are engineering or product judgments dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on two proposals. First, an exact route-optimization feature for a delivery fleet, where the sales team has promised optimal routes and the operations team has about forty stops per route but wants the option to scale to several hundred. Second, a scheduling feature that reduces to a constraint-satisfaction problem with a few hundred variables, where the team is willing to accept an approximate or occasionally suboptimal schedule but wants a guarantee that the solver will always return something usable within two seconds. Include one application where the procedure gives an answer I will not like—perhaps telling me that the hardness result I have been using to reject a feature is irrelevant to the instances that matter, or that the exactness the sales team promised cannot be justified at any scale we are likely to see. End with a short, candid section on what the procedure cannot do: the questions it cannot answer, the cases where it will misfire, and the point at which my team simply has to run experiments rather than reason from complexity classes.
