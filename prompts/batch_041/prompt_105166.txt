I teach a second-year module — it's the one where we stop digging and start arguing about what the digging means — and I've got 90 minutes, 18 students, four tables, a flat seminar room, no site, no real finds, and one thing I want them to actually feel rather than nod along to: how badly objects underdetermine claims about what people believed. The gap I care about is the one between a static pattern in the ground and a past behaviour, and then the second gap between behaviour and belief. The same pit of forty near-identical clay figurines under a house floor will cheerfully support household cult, ancestor veneration, toys, a rejected batch from a workshop, or something we don't have a word for, and nothing in the figurines themselves picks the winner — whatever you choose is being paid for out of background assumptions and comparisons with living societies. They always agree with me when I say this at the end of a lecture. They don't feel it. I tried a version of it last term with a photo of a real assemblage and it turned into a slide show with me talking. So design me a runnable activity: rules, materials, roles if you need them, timings, a scoring or bidding scheme that doesn't need real money, and a debrief.

The shape I want is commit, then get the same evidence back as a rival reading that's just as good, then have to price the difference. My instinct is that each team has to write one museum-label sentence, 25 words max, claiming something about a belief or an institution — not just that they ate grain — for an assemblage you invent for me, and then cards get dealt that either save the label or kill it, and they have to revise in front of everyone and say out loud what they gave up to do it. You can restructure that if you've got something better, but I want them to have to be specific and falsifiable before the rug comes out. Somewhere in there I'd like a moment where they notice that calling a find obviously ritual is just the name we give to the residual pile. I'd also like an unprovenanced object in the mix — a bag of finds with no context — because losing context should feel like a physical loss of inferential power and I want them to be able to say exactly what they lost. Invent the site and the finds yourself (fictional is fine, I'll print the cards), and include one object that turns out to be a later intrusion or a fake, because I want them to see that the most confident label in the room is the one most exposed to a single card. Give me the actual text of every card, the label template, the scoring, and a minute-by-minute timeline. And put some of the debrief into why this isn't a licence to say anything goes — what actually does the constraining work when an inference is a good one, formation processes, sampling, what should have survived and didn't, independent lines that have to agree.

Please be honest about the seams. I want a frank closing paragraph on what this activity can't reproduce — I'm guessing the big one is that in the game there's a designer who knows what really happened, which is exactly what real archaeologists don't get, so the game can teach the shape of the uncertainty but not the vertigo — and on what will predictably go wrong the first time. My guesses: one team writes a vacuous label and defends it, one team goes full hyper-positivist and refuses to infer anything about belief at all, and the intrusion card lands as a gotcha after which the whole room concludes that we can't know anything, which is definately not the lesson. Tell me how to handle each of those. Write it as someone who's run a field school and taught this seminar for years — brainstorm the mechanics first, think carefully about what 18 undergrads will actually do in the room for 90 minutes, and don't lecture me about teh general shape of the discipline, I want the thing I can run on Thursday.
