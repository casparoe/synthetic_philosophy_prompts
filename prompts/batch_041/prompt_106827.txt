I'm a fourth-year PhD student in econometrics working on identification in causal models — observational equivalence classes of linear Gaussian graphical models, instrumental variables, and the standard Bayesian apparatus for comparing models. Last term I sat in on a reading group in my university's philosophy department, and the epistemologists there kept invoking the underdetermination of theory by evidence. My private view is that this is identifiability wearing a costume, and when I said so, a friend told me I had simply translated the interesting part away. I would like you to take the translation seriously on her behalf rather than reassure me. Restate, one by one and as faithfully as the translation allows: (i) the holist point that a recalcitrant observation never obliges you to abandon any particular hypothesis, only some member of the conjunction of hypothesis and auxiliaries; (ii) the thesis that every theory has an empirically equivalent rival, distinguishing the trivial version, which merely re-describes the same structure, from the non-trivial version, which posits a genuinely different structure with the same observable consequences; (iii) the distinction between underdetermination that better evidence could in principle remove and underdetermination that no possible evidence could remove; and (iv) the step from underdetermination to constructive empiricism or to anti-realism about unobservables. Render each of these in my vocabulary — likelihoods, the identifiability of a parameter given the joint distribution, observational equivalence of causal structures, consistency of model selection as the sample grows, prior sensitivity, the no-free-lunch theorems about learning algorithms, and the difference between a parameter that is unidentified and one whose posterior is merely diffuse — and tell me for each whether it maps cleanly, maps only with distortion and what the distortion is, or has no counterpart at all.

The residue report is what I actually want. If everything the philosophers say bottoms out in identifiability plus a choice of prior, then their disagreement is either technical or verbal, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is itself an artifact of the translation. So address directly: whether empirical equivalence is the same relation as observational equivalence or is strictly weaker, for instance because it quantifies over all possible total evidence rather than over a fixed data-generating process; whether holism has any counterpart beyond the ordinary fact that revising a model is a search problem; and whether the anti-realist conclusion does any work that a decision-theoretic argument about which model to adopt could not do just as well. Then tell me what my framework cannot say — whether the gap is merely expressive, as when I lack any notion of a theory as opposed to a model class, or substantive, as when my whole setup presupposes that a single data-generating process exists and that every rival can be measured against a common probability space.

Separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know both literatures: I do not want textbook identifiability results presented as though they had settled a philosophical dispute, and I do not want a live disagreement among the philosophers smoothed into a consensus. Keep the whole reply under 1,000 words.
