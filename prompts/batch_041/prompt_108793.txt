I have been trying, without much success, to work out how far the procreation asymmetry should govern the creation of artificial minds, and I would like you to lay out its most important implications. As I understand the asymmetry — and I would be glad to be told that I have misstated it — it holds that it is bad, and indeed wrong, to bring into existence a being whose life will be predominantly miserable, whereas bringing into existence a being whose life will be predominantly happy is not in itself a good thing to have done and is certainly not something anyone is required to do: declining to create the happy being wrongs no one, because there is no one to be deprived. A friend with whom I have been arguing about this insists that the whole doctrine is an artefact of a biological imagination, tailored to cases of pregnancy and population policy, and that it simply has no grip once the beings in question are made rather than born. I suspect he is wrong, but I cannot articulate why, and I am asking because I sit on a small ethics advisory group that will soon have to say something about whether our laboratory may train systems that might be capable of contentment or of suffering.

What I want from you is not a verdict on any single case but the most important implications of taking the asymmetry seriously in this new setting. If we can build minds, does the asymmetry tell against building them at all, or only against building ones liable to suffer? Does it deny that creating a happy mind can be a benefit to that mind, and if so, what follows for how we should treat such a mind once it exists — is shutting down a contented one more like ending a happy life or more like declining to begin one? Does the asymmetry have anything to say about a laboratory that would create many minds, most of them happy but a substantial minority wretched, or about one that obtains a single happy mind only by way of many failed and suffering prototypes? And which parts of the asymmetry are actually doing the work in each of these implications — the claim about the badness of a suffering existence, the claim about the non-goodness of a happy one, or the thought that an outcome can be bad only if it is bad for someone in particular?

Please be concrete about the implications themselves and about where the asymmetry, followed honestly, leads somewhere its usual defenders might not want to go. I am less interested in a survey of what has been written about procreation than in what the view commits us to once the beings being created are not our children.
