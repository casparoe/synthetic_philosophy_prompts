Alright, longtime lurker here (LessWrong, the ACX comments, all of it), and I've got a task I actually care about getting right.

So my little reading group has been going through philosophy of math and we've split hard over Benacerraf's dilemma. Camp A, the "the dilemma definately bites" people, think it's close to decisive: mathematical sentences seem to be true and to be about numbers and sets and so on, but we can't have any causal contact with abstract objects, so either we give up the truth/reference of math or we give up the idea that we could ever know it -- and they'd rather give up platonism. Camp B, the "dissolve it" people, think the epistemic horn only looks strong if you've already bought the idea that knowledge requires some causal or informational hookup to the thing known, and that once you drop that, platonism is fine.

They've been at it for months and it's going in circles. A keeps saying "but how do you KNOW about the number 7, like whats the actual mechanism," and B keeps saying "you're smuggling in an empiricist theory of knowledge." Nobody has moved an inch.

What I want: write the joint statement these two camps could both genuinely sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right -- the document both sides would put their names to. Include:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- what both accept about mathematical practice, about what a semantics for mathematical language has to deliver, about what truth is doing in math, and about whether the dilemma is even one argument or two arguments stapled together.
2. The exact residual disagreements, each phrased the way that side would want it phrased, so each side reads its own bullet and nods instead of bristling.
3. The cruxes. I'm pretty sure one is "does knowledge require a causal or informational connection to its object?" and another is "does the truth of a mathematical sentence require a domain of objects for it to be true of, or can you earn the truth some other way -- substitutional quantifiers, a modal translation, truthmaker-style grounding, whatever?" Add any others you find, and tell me which ones actually move somebody.
4. For each crux, something concrete both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use this test case for the knowledge crux, since I think it cuts through the fog: imagine a community of mathematicians, or a theorem-proving AI, that's causally sealed off from everything except its own formal derivations -- no perception of abstracta, no Platonic perception, just symbol-pushing that happens to be sound and complete for the relevant fragment. Does it know the theorems, or does it merely believe them truly? Both sides should agree this bears on the causal-connection crux, and I want to see what each predicts and why. For the truth crux, use something like: can the anti-platonist give a semantics for a quantified sentence like "every number has a successor" that the platonist will admit is a real truth-evaluable semantics, and not just a paraphrase that quietly changes the subject? Say what each side predicts and what would count as the platonist conceding, and as the anti-platonist conceding.

Also: if part of the fight is just verbal -- like if "exists" or "object" or "know" is doing different work on each side -- say which part and why, and say whether the residual disagreement survives once you fix the vocabulary. Put the disagreements in a table if that helps.

Keep it tight and non-vacuous. If a bullet could be signed by literally anyone, it's not doing its job.
