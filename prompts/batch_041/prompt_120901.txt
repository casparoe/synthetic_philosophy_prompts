I'm supposed to be writing a seminar paper on whether pain is bad in itself or bad for some other reason, and instead of reading more I've been trying to figure out what I actually think by going with my gut on cases, which my roommate says is backwards. My gut is pretty steady on some of them. Ordinary headache I'd take an advil for: bad for me, clearly. Post-workout soreness, or the burn from a really hot pepper: that's pain, and it isn't bad for me at all, it's part of what I'm there for, which already seems to break the idea that pain is bad just by being pain. A guy who seeks pain out on purpose, there's a whole scene for this apparently: I think his pain is still a little bit bad for him even though he wants it, in the sense that if the same sensation showed up without the ritual and the wanting he'd rather not have it, and I think he'd agree. Phantom limb pain: bad for me in exactly the way an ordinary pain is, even though there's no limb and nothing wrong with my body, and my friend's line that pain is bad the way smoke is bad, i.e. it's the signal that something's damaged, looks like it dies right there. And then the asymbolic patients, the ones who after a stroke or a lobotomy or on a big dose of morphine will tell you yes, it hurts, right there, sharp, about a 7, but who are just not bothered by it, who don't flinch or ask for anything or act like they're suffering at all: I'm sure they're in pain, and I'm sure the pain isn't bad for them, or barely bad, nothing like mine. But that seems to agree with the soreness verdict and disagree with the masochist verdict, and I don't know if I can have it both ways, because either badness tracks how much you don't want the state or it tracks something about the state itself, and each one costs me one of the verdicts I just gave.

The one I keep going back and fourth on is what I think of as the morphine-in-reverse case. I read somewhere that soldiers with pretty serious wounds in, I think, WWII often reported almost no pain, some of them reportedly even relieved or pleased about it, and separately I've read about a drug that leaves the pain exactly as intense as it was but strips out the awfulness, so the patient says it still hurts, it just doesn't bother me. If that's roughly what's happening in asymbolia, I can't decide whether to say their pain is bad for them and they've merely lost the ability to mind it, or whether a pain that nobody minds and that doesn't move anyone to do anything about it just isn't bad at all. The second option makes me nervous, because it makes the badness of pain depend on a reaction the patient might simply be too damaged to have.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? I'd like it stated so I can see exactly which clause is doing the work for which case, and I want to hear what it says about ones I haven't listed, e.g. a creature that yanks its hand off a hot plate but that I have no reason to think feels anything, a pain in a limb you don't think is yours, a pain you have in a dream, and whatever the drug case turns out to be. And please tell me which of my verdicts I should just give up, because I suspect at least one of them has to go. If there's a simple principle that costs me exactly one verdict, give me that instead of a monster that saves everything.
