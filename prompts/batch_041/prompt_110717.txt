I'm not an academic, just someone who's been reading about famines on and off for a few years, and I've noticed that everyone who argues about them reaches for an analogy, and the analogy is doing all the work. I'd like you to audit the analogies rather than tell me who's right about the history.

The two I keep running into: famine as natural disaster, i.e. blight, drought, bad weather, an act of God, nobody's fault, and the slightly darker version where famine is like predators thinning a herd, a natural check that the population had coming. And famine as crime, i.e. man-made, someone did it, someone could be tried for it. In Ireland the potato blight was real and the food was still being shipped out; in Bengal in 1943, as I understand it, there was a war boom in Calcutta, boats and rice stocks destroyed so the Japanese couldn't use them, and prices ran away from wages; in China after 1958 the state was taking the grain and denying anything was wrong. So all three cases get filed under one of those two pictures and the picture then decides the politics.

Heres my own attempt, and I want you to be hard on it: a famine is a run on a bank. The food is still there in the vaults, or at least not much less of it than usual. What collapses is peoples claims on it. Everyone with a claim tries to convert it at once, the ones at the back of the queue get nothing, and the people at the front walk away fine. The trigger is often a rumour or a panic, not a shortage. The landless labourer who has only his labour to sell is the depositor with the weakest claim, and when rice triples and wages don't, his claim is worth nothing and he dies.

Give me a correspondence table for all three pictures: what plays the vault, the deposits, the depositors, the queue, the panic, the lender of last resort. Then the breakages. I want to know which mismatches actually matter and which are just decoration. Some of what I can already see: a run is a transfer, the total is unchanged, only the money moved, whereas in a famine people are dead and that's not a transfer; food is eaten and rots, it isn't gold sitting in a vault; and a bank run can be stopped by a credible promise, which makes the metaphor point at restoring confidence rather than actually getting food to people, which might be the most dangerous thing it smuggles in.

Then the real test. Does the bank run picture survive China after 1958, where there wasn't much of a market, no queue of depositors, no panic, just a bank manager burning the reserves and announcing everything is fine? Does it survive Ireland, where the depositors were tenants with one crop and the claims were being cashed in London? And which picture, if any, can accommodate the thing I've heard said, that famines don't happen in countries with a free press and elections — because that sounds like a claim about confidence and credible guarantees, which is bank language, not weather language.

Finally, if none of the three holds up, tell me what the right analogy is, or whether the honest answer is that there's no single one and the politics lives in the seams between them. I don't need citations, I need the mapping and the breakages.
