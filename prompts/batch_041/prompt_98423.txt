I want your credence in a specific claim about time preference, followed by the decomposition that generates it — not a survey of the debate, but your number and its moving parts.

The claim, stated so that it has a truth value: the mere date at which a benefit or harm occurs is never, by itself, a reason to count it as less important. More precisely, suppose an increment of welfare of a fixed size and kind can be placed either at an earlier or a later point, with no uncertainty about whether it occurs, no causal or informational asymmetry between the placements, and no difference in who receives it; the claim is that it is never more rational, and never more morally fitting, to prefer the earlier placement merely because it is earlier. This is a claim about time itself, not about discount rates for consumption, capital, or risk — a positive discount rate of the latter sort is entirely compatible with it.

Give me your credence in that claim between 0 and 1, then decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification per term, and the combination. At minimum I want:

1. Well-formedness. Whether value or welfare can be compared across times in a way that makes "merely earlier" a separable factor at all, or whether the comparison collapses — for instance because what is good for a person is indexed to their life-stage, so no two genuinely identical increments exist to be ranked.

2. Substantive requirement. Whether any plausible substantive theory of practical reason entails temporal neutrality. Means-end rationality is silent here; the question is whether anything else is not.

3. The spatial analogy. The argument that disposes of spatial discounting — that the mere distance of a person from me is no reason to count their welfare less — either transfers to the temporal case or it does not. If it transfers cleanly, neutrality follows almost immediately. Assign a probability that the analogy is sound.

4. The asymmetry of influence. We can affect the future and not the past, and this asymmetry is not itself a preference for the near over the far. Probability that it grounds a rational asymmetry in concern without covertly smuggling in pure time preference.

5. Intrapersonal near-bias. The near future is vivid and the far future is abstract; if any theory must vindicate the resulting bias toward the near within a single life, does that vindication require a nonzero rate?

Add any further terms you think belong, especially any that would let a positive discount rate survive a zero pure time preference. Then give the combined credence, name the term your number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move it most.

Finally, a calibration of the disagreement: I put this claim at 0.85; a colleague puts it at 0.15. Tell me which sub-question our disagreement most probably lives in, and which of the two of us that sub-question favors.
