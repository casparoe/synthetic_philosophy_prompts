ok so I teach a 300-level political theory seminar, about 18 students, and I get one 90-minute block for the Machiavelli fortune/virtù material — the second-to-last chapter of The Prince (the one about how much fortune can do in human affairs), plus the Discourses passage where he says a man has to change with the times if he wants to keep his good fortune. Every year I stand up and say some version of "fortune governs about half our actions, virtù is the other half, and the whole tragedy is that when the times turn you can't change your nature to match them," and every year it just bounces off. They nod, they write it down, they don't feel it.

What I want is an in-class game where they actually live through it. The specific thing I need them to feel: two people can both be good at this, one cautious and one impetuous, and each prospers while the times suit his temperament — and then the times move, and the one who can't switch goes under, and he can't switch, because you can't deviate from what your nature inclines you to, and because a man who's been winning one way can't be persuaded to leave the road he's on. I want them to get locked into a mode of proceeding by winning with it, and then watch the ground move out from under them.

But I don't want it to collapse into "so it's all luck," which is the opposite error and not what he says. He says the prudent build dikes and dams before the flood. So there has to be real skill in the game — anticipation, preparation, hedging — that visibly pays off, just never enough to beat fortune. And please don't make the "times" pure coin flips; Machiavelli's fortune is the shifting circumstances, not a dice roll, so there should be eras or runs with some structure, so that foresight is possible in principle but you can't know when the turn comes.

Give me the setup, materials (I'm running this out of a tote bag, nothing that costs money), a payoff scheme, roles, and a round structure I can run off a single page. Then a debrief that maps the actual moments from the game onto the actual moves in the text — the ones where they got to feel smart, and the ones where it didn't matter. Be honest with me about what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about what this can't reproduce and I'll just have to supply myself. I suspect the game can only turn "you can't change your nature" into a rule, whereas his claim is really about character — tell me if I'm wrong about that, and what else it flattens (I'm worried it makes virtù into nothing but hedging, when alot of what he means by virtù is the audacity to seize the occasion, and maybe also the founder's power to impose new modes and orders, which is a whole different thing).

One more constraint: it has to work without them having memorized the text — they've read it once. And no lecture up front; they shouldn't hear me name virtù and fortuna until the debrief.
