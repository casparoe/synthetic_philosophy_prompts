ok so ive become convinced by accuracy-first epistemology and id like you to try to un-convince me. the position: accuracy — how close your credences are to the truth — is the only fundamental epistemic good, and the standard norms (probabilism, conditionalization, maybe even the ban on contradictory beliefs) get whatever epistemic authority they have from accuracy-based arguments, the dominance one and the expected-accuracy one.

what would count against it? and i mean the real defeaters, not "but what about understanding?" as a one-liner — which results, cases, or commitments should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which of them would the people who hold this view just concede?

three things i keep chewing on, tell me if theyre real problems or if ive got the view wrong:

- the whole framework seems to assume a fixed partition — a set of possible worlds over which credences are defined and then scored. what happens if i drop that? if my credence function is defined over a coarser algebra than the world actually settles, or two agents with identical evidence and identical credences end up scored against different partitions, does the dominance argument for probabilism survive? and if it doesnt, is the program broken or is my version of it broken?

- accuracy is a relation between a credence and a world; truth is a property of a proposition. does the program need the first, or is it quietly using the second when it says closeness to the truth? and if its really truth all the way down, does the credence-scoring machinery lose its point?

- does it have anything to say about knowledge at all? if it doesnt, is that evidence its not a theory of epistemic normativity but just a scoring convention for partial belief? related: the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization tells an agent to update by conditionalizing, but it seems to assume the agent trusts her own future updating — what happens if she knows shes a bad updater, or systematically overconfident? does the argument survive, or does it just prove something about idealized agents?

and then the honest verdict: is this the kind of view that can be protected from every objection by re-specifying what accuracy is, and if so is that fatal or just the price of any foundational story?
