I'm a retired field entomologist—spent most of my career on ant trail pheromones, of all things—and since I hung up my field boots I've gotten weirdly into philosphy of biology. I now volunteer as the science advisor for a small natural history museum and a podcast, which mostly means I'm the person who has to sign off on exhibit labels and scripts. Our team keeps writing things like the beetle's horn evolved to fight rivals, or the bird's alarm call evolved to warn its relatives, and I keep thinking about Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique of adaptationism—the argument that not every trait is an adaptation; some are byproducts, developmental constraints, drift, exaptations, or just side effects of selection on something else. The director wants a new gallery called Built by Evolution, and I can already see every label turning into a just-so story. I have about twenty minutes per script, the writers have intro bio, and I can't send them off to read the literature.

What I need from you is a decision procedure I can actually hand to a writer. Something like a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard no on the phrase evolved for, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a judgment call about evidence or rhetoric rather than a fact. I've been sketching a two-axis worksheet—current utility on one axis, historical origin on the other—and a four-box outcome: SELECTION, SIDE-EFFECT, CO-OPTED, UNKNOWN. Use that if it helps, but keep it usable by someone who last took biology in college. It should force a check for the usual alternatives—byproduct, exaptation, constraint, drift, correlated selection—before anyone is allowed to say evolved to. I also want it to say what language to use when the evidence is thin: currently functions as, is consistent with, may have been favored, we don't know, etc. And it should flag the places where judgment can't be eliminated, like how much comparative evidence is enough or whether a plausible developmental story counts as a byproduct explanation.

Then run it on three cases so I can see how it behaves. First, a label claiming a male beetle's oversized horn evolved for fighting rival males. Second, a script claiming a bird's alarm call evolved to warn its relatives. Third, a label about my own old study system—ant trail pheromones—claiming the trail evolved to recruit nestmates. For each, show what the procedure says, what evidence would actually be needed, and what the final label language should be. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me I have to cut a claim I like—the ant one is probably going to sting—and be honest about what the procedure can't settle. No bibliography or citations, please; I need something operational, not a reading list.
