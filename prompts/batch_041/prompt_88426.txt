I've been chipping away at a metaphysics paper and I've reached the point where I need someone to try to knock my view down instead of politely nodding, so I'm going to lay out what I actually think and ask you to tell me what would count against it. The topic is the special composition question — when do some things add up to a further thing, rather than just being a bunch of things arranged in some way? The usual answers (always, never, only when the parts are 'unified' in some sense) all strike me as too static, because they make whether a composite exists at a time depend only on how the parts happen to be arranged at that time. I want a view where composition is path-dependent, a bit like hysteresis in a magnet or a thermostat: two systems that are intrinsic duplicates right now could differ in whether they compose a further thing, because one of them reached its present arrangement through a process that locked a composite in and the other was simply assembled into that arrangement. And I want the thresholds to be asymmetric — it should take more 'integration' for a composite to come into existence than for it to keep existing, so there's a band of arrangements where a composite can hang on but could never have arisen there from scratch. I've been calling it the ratchet view, for the thing that only clicks one way. (A friend in the pub told me this is just Ship of Theseus stuff with extra steps, which I don't think is fair, but you can tell me if he's right.)

Two cases I keep leaning on. First, a machine bolted together in one go versus a machine that's grown or gradually assembled and ends up in the exact same configuration down to the millimetre — I want to say only the second is a genuine composite, or at least that the first is a marginal case, since the second had a history of being integrated that pushed it past the entry threshold. Second, an organism that 'dies' and is later restarted (suspended animation, CPR, whatever) — I want to say the organism was there the whole time, even if the state it was in would never have been enough to bring an organism into being if you'd started from a corpse. The ratchet goes up but doesn't come back down. Same for a ship that's rebuilt plank by plank versus one swapped out in a single afternoon.

What I need from you is the case against it, and not the easy version. Tell me what would count against the ratchet view specifically: which intuitions, which cases, which formal constraints it violates, and which results elsewhere in metaphysics it stands or falls with. I especially want to know whether it's forced to deny that whether some things compose a further thing supervenes on how those things are arranged at the time — because I think it is, and I want to know how much that costs me and whose toes I'm stepping on. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender would just concede as the price of the view. Also: is there anything that would actually count as the view being wrong, or is this the kind of thing that can absorb any case by quietly positing a convenient history? If it's insulated like that, say so, and say whether that's a fatal flaw for a view of this kind or just business as usual in the area. Push on the thresholds too — I haven't said whether they're sharp or vague, and I suspect that choice is doing more work than I've admitted, and probably some of the work is being done by me just not wanting to spell out where the band sits.
