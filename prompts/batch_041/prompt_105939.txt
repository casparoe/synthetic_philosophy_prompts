I teach an upper-level seminar in democratic theory — nineteen students, a mix of politics and philosophy, one eighty-five-minute session, movable chairs. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the force of the accusation that democratic backsliding is nothing but a partisan label: not hear me assert that the worry is live, but catch themselves issuing verdicts that track who the actor is rather than what was done, and then have to work out what, if anything, in the concept survives that discovery. What I have sketched so far: eight short case episodes, each a single paragraph of institutional facts with no party names in the body — packing a constitutional court by ordinary statute, stripping jurisdiction from courts that have ruled against the executive, a friendly business group buying up the main independent broadcasters, a late change to districting rules, criminalising one protest movement while leaving a comparable movement alone, an amendment relaxing term limits, a purge of the senior civil service, and one episode that is merely aggressive but recognisably ordinary politics. I prepare two packets: in one the actors are a left-of-centre coalition, in the other a right-of-centre one, with the institutional facts identical; half the room gets each, and neither half knows about the other until the reveal. I want the rules to make it impossible to declare everything backsliding — my own idea is a budget of credibility chips that each group must spend to make a public accusation, so that naming at most three of the eight forces a decision about what counts, together with a monitoring-body role that must decide whether to publish its finding, with the reputational cost of a false positive built into the payoff. If you have a better mechanism, use it.

Whatever the mechanism, each group has to commit to a numerical severity rating for every episode before the packets are compared. Design the whole thing: roles, materials, the payoff scheme with no real money in it, a timeline in minutes, and the debrief, which should turn on the comparison — which ratings moved when the label changed, which did not, and what the unmoved ones share. My own reading is that the episodes surviving the swap are those aimed at the opposition's capacity to compete, or those that use impeccable legal form to entrench the incumbent, while merely harsh or unfair measures do not survive; if you think that line is too clean or simply wrong, say so, and tell me how the activity would change if the criterion were the outcome rather than the act. I also want the debrief to make the standpoint problem concrete, since the seminar keeps sliding past it: who is entitled to apply the label at all, and whether an external body's finding is a description of a situation or an intervention in it.

Finally, be blunt about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure as it happens — I would rather hear the likely disasters now than discover them in front of the room. Second, which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself; my guess is that it cannot reproduce the slow ratchet of real entrenchment, in which every individual step is defensible at the moment it is taken, and that it cannot settle whether the concept really is more than a label, since showing that verdicts are partly label-driven is a fact about my students rather than about the concept.
