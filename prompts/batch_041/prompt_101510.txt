I am a doctoral candidate in political theory working on the afterlives of lost causes. In comparing the more familiar cases — the cult of the American Confederacy, the Masada narrative, the Kosovo myth, and the Dunkirk myth — I have become preoccupied with a more local and less literary version of the phenomenon. A deindustrialised city in a country I know well has, for roughly three decades, funded an annual commemorative fortnight for a failed general strike and the lockout that followed. The official programme includes a museum of the defeat, a school unit on the betrayal of the strikers, a civic procession to the old works, and a mayoral address that treats the defeat as the moment the community discovered its true character. There is no comparable public ritual for the negotiated return to work, for the later partial reindustrialisation, or for any subsequent victory. Politicians of every party invoke the defeat to argue against present-day reforms, and the local university has a small research centre devoted to what it calls the event’s ongoing moral meaning. I am not asking whether the history is accurate. I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes.

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice require a view on which a collective has a true self that is disclosed in defeat rather than in success? Does it require that suffering is redemptive, or only that it is identity-constituting? Does it presuppose a counterfactual claim — that victory would have been worse for the community — or can it get by without one? What does it assume about collective agency and responsibility: that the community was betrayed by identifiable agents, or that history itself was the agent? There is an apparent tension I would like you to address: the practice treats the defeat as an injustice that should not have happened, while also treating it as the source of the community’s distinctive value. If the defeat was a betrayal, the true community was not defeated; if the defeat made the community what it is, the betrayal was somehow constitutive. Which theories of meaning, value, and identity can hold both claims together, and which cannot? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — for example, whether a purely functional sociological account of solidarity and grievance could explain the practice without any metaphysical commitment to redemptive defeat. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its implicit theory with a triumphalist one, a constructivist one that treats all founding moments as invented, or a tragic one that refuses to make defeat meaningful at all. Please write your answer in American English.
