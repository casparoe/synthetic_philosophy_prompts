For the past eighteen months I have kept a prediction journal — nothing elaborate, just dated claims with confidence levels, mostly about my own field and my own results — and last month a colleague who runs our group's calibration reviews showed me that in one subfield my 90% intervals have contained the truth a little more than half the time. Since then I have been trying to work out what that fact is supposed to do to the particular claims I am currently making in that subfield, and I have found that I cannot even state my confusion precisely. I seem to hold at once that I should still believe what my evidence supports and that someone with my track record should not believe it, and I cannot tell whether that combination is a coherent position, a straightforward case of epistemic akrasia, or simply a sign that I have not thought hard enough. My department's epistemology reading group has agreed to take the topic up with me next month, and rather than have eight people trade intuitions for ninety minutes, I would like a classification instrument we can actually use on ourselves — something that sorts us by what we say about cases rather than by the labels we are fond of.

So: please build me a decision tree, or a taxonomy of the same shape, for the question of what higher-order evidence about one's own reliability does to one's first-order beliefs. Each branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and phrased as a concrete case or a concrete claim rather than as a piece of terminology; each leaf should carry a descriptive name for the position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want the tree built around a few distinctions that I think the usual discussions blur. First, whether higher-order evidence bears on the first-order question directly or only on your estimate of your own competence, with the first-order credence then shifting through that estimate. Second, whether the evidence concerns the inputs of your reasoning (you may have misread the data), the process (you may have reasoned badly), or the standards you are applying (your sense of what counts as a good argument may be parochial) — I suspect the same person answers differently across those three, and I would rather see that split than have it averaged away. Third, whether there is exactly one rational response to a body of evidence or several, since a permissivist can dissolve the akrasia without conceding anything to the conciliator. Fourth, whether the higher-order evidence can itself be uncertain — an underpowered study of people like me rather than of me — and whether it can therefore carry partial weight. Fifth, whether one can coherently require of oneself that one distrust one's own reliability without that requirement being self-undermining.

The leaves should include at least the conciliatory view, the steadfast view, the view that the first-order belief and the higher-order belief about what one ought to believe can both be rational at the same time, and the view that the question is ill-posed because belief is not governed by requirements in the way the framing assumes; add any hybrid you think I have missed, and attach a proper name to a leaf only where you are confident of the attribution — otherwise leave it descriptive, and do not give me a reading list. Please use, at minimum, four cases: my calibration-journal case, a case in which the higher-order evidence is a weak study of people relevantly like me, a case in which I come to suspect that the standards I apply were simply those of my training, and one case of your own choosing that you think separates two positions the other three do not. Aim for roughly eight to twelve nodes. Where two leaves cannot be told apart by any question you can phrase — I suspect the total-evidence view and straightforward conciliationism may be one such pair — say so rather than inventing a branch to pry them apart, and where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, note it and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied.
