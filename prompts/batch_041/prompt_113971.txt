Write a short story, of roughly 1,500 words, that stages the disagreement between Dennett and Searle over original and derived intentionality, and that ends by taking a side.

The premise: a transit authority, cutting costs, has replaced its coin-operated fare boxes with a unit its engineers privately call "the two-bitser with a mouth." Like Dennett's original, it can be fooled — a slug of the right weight buys a ride. Unlike it, it narrates itself: a small language model, reading the box's sensor states, emits a running first-person log — "I have been paid"; "this passenger means to cheat me"; "I am holding the gate for a child." The story turns on a hearing at which a rider contests a fine the box assessed, and the only evidence is that log. The question the hearing cannot settle is whose beliefs the log reports: the box's own, someone else's merely routed through it, or none at all. Let the regress do the work. The sensors were designed by an engineer working to a specification written by the authority to satisfy an auditor, and the narration is derived from the sensors as the sensors are derived from him; the story should let the reader feel the chain run backward until it either stops at the human beings (Searle's line) or discloses that they are themselves only more interpreters of a text they did not author (Dennett's).

Constraints: the words "intentionality" and "intentional" should not appear in the story. The philosophical content should be carried by plot and by the hearing's procedure, not by a character lecturing. A reader who knows the debate should recognize it; a reader who does not should find the story whole on its own.

End with a short afterword, 150–250 words, in which you state plainly which side the story comes down on and why, and note any place where it takes a liberty with the ideas or leans on a contested reading of either philosopher.
