You are a philosopher who specializes in population ethics and axiology, and you have been asked to set one question for the written component of a PhD qualifying examination in an advanced seminar on population axiology. The examination is closed-book and the candidates have no internet access, so the question must be fully answerable from the reasoning materials supplied below: it must not require recalling particular authors, titles, or page references, and neither the question nor the model answer may name specific published works or cite sources. Your deliverable is the question together with a full model answer and a marking scheme.

Work carefully and in stages. First, in a scratch space, brainstorm: identify the crux that separates a candidate who can only apply labels such as totalism, the repugnant conclusion, the sadistic conclusion, and the very repugnant conclusion from one who can actually analyse a proposed principle; check that the crux genuinely cannot be answered by reciting a summary of the standard literature; then draft the question, then the model answer, then the marking scheme. Present your final response under three headings: Exam Question; Model Answer (roughly 600 to 700 words); Marking Scheme (whose allocations sum to 20 marks).

Background to be given to the candidates:

1. The repugnant conclusion: for any population of lives at a very high level of well-being, there is a conceivable much larger population every member of which has a life that is barely worth living, and which a purely additive (totalist) axiology must rank as at least as good. Totalism takes the value of a population to be the sum, over all lives, of individual well-being.

2. Any axiology of the form value equals the sum, over lives, of a function of each life's own well-being, where that function is positive and bounded below by a positive number on lives that are worth living, will imply the repugnant conclusion as the population grows without bound. Escaping it therefore requires either non-additivity, so that the value contributed by one life depends on how many others exist, or a lexical or threshold structure.

3. The very repugnant conclusion: a principle may imply that adding a vast number of lives in agony to an otherwise excellent population can be an improvement, provided that enough excellent lives are added at the same time.

4. The sadistic conclusion: a principle may imply that it is better to add lives with negative well-being than to add the same number of lives with positive well-being.

5. A separability, or independence, condition: the value a principle assigns to adding or removing one life should not depend on the size or composition of the rest of the population.

The candidates must evaluate the following newly devised principle, which you should state precisely in the question:

The Population-Proportional Critical Level View. Fix a positive constant c. For a population with N members and individual well-being levels u_1, ..., u_N, define its value as V equal to the sum over i of the quantity (u_i minus c times the quantity one minus one over N). Lives below the critical level contribute negatively, lives above it contribute positively, and the critical level rises toward c as N grows.

The question must require candidates to do three things, each carrying part of the marks:

(a) determine whether the principle avoids the repugnant conclusion, and justify the answer;

(b) determine which, if either, of the two objectionable conclusions in items 3 and 4 the principle incurs, and supply a concrete numerical example;

(c) identify the deeper structural cost of letting the critical level depend on N, namely the failure of the separability condition in item 5, and explain why that failure is not a merely technical blemish.

The model answer should be what a top candidate would produce: it should work through the algebra, give at least one numerical example, and concede the strongest reply available to a defender of the principle before explaining why that reply fails. The marking scheme must distribute 20 marks across named must-hit moves, give the mark value of each, and add a one-line note on how candidates typically lose marks on each move, for instance by asserting that a rising critical level automatically blocks the repugnant conclusion without checking the limiting behaviour as N grows, or by reciting the stock claim that any threshold view incurs the sadistic conclusion instead of testing the principle against a worked example. This is a single essay question; do not produce multiple-choice items.
