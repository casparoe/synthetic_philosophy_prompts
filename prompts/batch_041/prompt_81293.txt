I'm a retired ecologist — thirty-odd years of aphid and parasitoid population work, life tables, mark-recapture, the usual — and since I hung up my field boots I've been reading philosophy, badly but enthusiastically. The thing that's been bugging me for about a year now is the memes business. I keep reading about it and I keep getting the feeling that the argument has been going round in circles since Dawkins coined the word, and that the people involved aren't even disagreeing about the same thing. As best I can make out there are three camps and a half. One says culture is Darwinian because there are replicators — units that get copied with variation — and if you doubt that you're just refusing to see it (Dawkins, Blackmore, Dennett when he's in that mood). Another says culture is Darwinian but you don't need replicators at all: give me a population of variants, some heritability, and differential transmission, and I've got evolution by natural selection whether or not anything is faithfully copied (the Boyd and Richerson and Henrich and Mesoudi crowd). A third says the resemblance between what I do and what my neighbour does is mostly reconstructed from scratch each time rather than copied, so the Darwinian vocabulary is a metaphor that's quietly doing damage (Sperber and the cultural attraction people, as I understand them). And then there's a fourth position, mostly from philosophers, that the question "is culture Darwinian?" is either trivially yes or not a real question — that we're arguing about what to call things, not about what's true.

Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me if it's naive. I think the fault line isn't really about the facts of transmission at all; it's about what the word "Darwinian" is a claim about. Let me put it two ways and see if you think this is the real split. Thin: "Darwinian" names a pattern — a population of variants, heritability of some kind, differential transmission, frequencies changing over time. Thick: "Darwinian" names a mechanism — resemblance between variants is caused by copying of a replicator, so that selection explains the resemblance rather than merely describing its trajectory. On the thin reading the cultural-evolution people win by default and Sperber has to deny that culture is a population process at all; on the thick reading the memeticists need copying to be doing real work, and how much of cultural resemblance is copying becomes an empirical question. But even if we sort that out, there's a leftover I can't dissolve, and it's the one that worries me as an old population man. In my field a generation and a fitness come free with the life cycle; in culture they don't, the generations are whatever the modeller picks. So a selectionist story can be fitted to almost any cultural change after the fact, and then I start to suspect the real disagreement is about what we want a theory of culture to do — predict, unify, or explain the diversity — rather than about culture.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Which of these is it: a verbal dispute about the word "Darwinian", a genuine empirical dispute about fidelity and reconstruction that nobody has ever measured seperate from the theory, a dispute about standards of explanation, or two or three camps answering different questions under one label? I've been reading alot of this stuff and I still cant tell. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than in a survey of the literature, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a measurement, a formal result, a change of question — or whether I should stop expecting the thing to move at all. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; I got enough of that in my working life.
