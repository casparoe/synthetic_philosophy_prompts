I keep landing on verdicts about slurs that dont hang together, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them. A slur used affectionately by someone about themselves, or about a friend who belongs to the group it targets: fine. The same word tacked on by an outsider to an otherwise friendly compliment: not fine, badly so, and the friendliness doesn't help. A slur in quote marks in a newspaper report of a court case, attributed to someone else: grubby but allowed. The same word unquoted in a novel's dialogue, spoken by a character the author plainly despises: also fine, I think — it's the character using it, not the author. The same word in a diary no one will ever read: I still wince, and 'nobody was harmed' doesn't move me. The same word as an example under a lambda in a semantics paper, there purely to show how the thing composes: that feels different from the diary, and I want to call it fine, though I cant say why. A slur invented for a made-up people in a fantasy novel, referring to no one real: fine? But the same invented word sneered at a real person who isn't a member of any such group seems not fine, which puzzles me, since there's no target. And someone using a slur of a person who does belong to the group, where the speaker doesn't know that and thinks he's insulting him for something else entirely: I'm torn, and I change my mind by the day. Can one account get all of this? What does it say about a case I haven't listed — quoting a slur in order to argue that quoting it is always wrong? And my two fines for the newspaper and the novel feel like the same verdict, while my two fines for the in-group use and the invented word feel like different ones; can a single story deliver that, or must I drop one, and which? Please write in British English.
