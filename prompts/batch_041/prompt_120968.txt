I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on decision theory and rationality — 16 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few from economics and cognitive science. We have done Newcomb's problem and the basics of causal versus evidential decision theory, so they have the vocabulary, but they have never encountered counterfactual mugging or XOR blackmail, and I want to keep it that way until the debrief. I need a runnable in-class activity, not a lecture and not a discussion prompt, that makes them feel the pull of both problems before I name either one. The two should be staged as separate rounds of the same session, and ideally the second should draw on what happened in the first, so that the connection between them — something about decisions that matter only in one branch of a branching possibility, and about the way a message's arrival carries information about your own disposition — emerges from the structure rather than from me saying it.

For the first round, I want them to face the counterfactual-mugging structure: a coin determines whether they are in the branch where they are asked to pay a small amount, and a large payoff in the other branch depends on what they would have done in the branch they are actually in. The point they should feel is the asymmetry between committing to a policy in advance and facing the choice once the branch is settled — the sense that the money is already gone, that nothing they do now can change the other branch, and yet that refusing feels like a betrayal of something. For the second round, I want the XOR-blackmail structure: a threat to release a secret if and only if they do not pay, sent only to people the blackmailer predicts will pay, so that receiving the threat is itself evidence about their own type. The point they should feel is the collision between the causal case for paying (paying prevents the release) and the evidential case against being the sort of person who pays (the threat's arrival tells you something, and the ex ante best policy is to be unthreatenable). I would like the second round to be seeded by the first — for instance, the blackmailer's predictions could be based on what people actually did in round one — so that the moment of recognition ("I am being threatened because of what I did earlier") lands as a discovery rather than an announcement.

The hard design problem is credibility of the predictor. I do not want to lie to them and I do not have a real Oracle. I am willing to use some stagecraft — sealed envelopes, a confederate, a pre-committed scoring rule applied to a short questionnaire they fill out at the start — but whatever I use has to be something I can honestly explain in the debrief, and it has to be credible enough in the moment that they actually feel the counterfactual pressure. Please give me the full runnable design: materials, setup, exact rules and payoffs for each round (tokens or candy, no real money), a timeline for the 90 minutes, roles if any, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the two problems and onto the distinction between causal and evidential or updateless reasoning. Tell me how to stage the prediction so that it is both honest and psychologically real, and flag the point at which the stagecraft is likely to show through. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the ways participants will try to game the rules, the moments where someone will say "this is just Newcomb's problem again" and derail the room, the points where the fiction will feel fake — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of these problems the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief; in particular, I suspect that no classroom game can reproduce the full force of the perfect predictor, and I want to know what that costs.
