So my old grad school roommate is now the president of a small liberal arts college in Ohio, and she's asked me to give the commencement address this May. I'm a historian — early modern Europe, witch trials and tax rolls, nothing American — so I've been putting this off. Here's whats eating at me: the commencement address is the most Whig genre there is. You know the move — you tell the story of the college as a long climb up toward this morning, with the graduates standing on the summit of it, and every awkward thing in the past gets quietly recruited as a step on the way to us. The past was never aiming at us, but the speech has to pretend it was, and the audience wants that. I don't want to write that speech, and I also dont want to write the other one — the apology, where I stand in front of the trustees and the grandparents and basically read the place its indictment. Both of those are lazy and both of them are easy.

The college just changed its seal. The old one had a rising sun over an open book and a Latin motto about light and the coming day — literally a sunrise, the founding story as dawn. A student research seminar went through the founders' papers last year and turned up things the place has never said out loud, there was a vote, the seal changed, and now nobody agrees on what the new one is supposed to mean. One of the chapel donors is going to be sitting about twenty feet from me. I want the speech to do something with all this without turning the ceremony into a history lecture or a family fight. The faculty historians in the tent will hear what I'm up to; the parents wont, and shouldn't have to.

So write the address. Ten minutes, tops. The thing I'm after is that it has to be honest about the past without being either a victory lap or a confession, and it still has to give these kids something real to carry out of the tent. The Whig temptation is the whole problem here — the pull to make everything a prelude to now — so build the speech so that it can't just collapse back into that. Don't use the word "journey." And when you get to the spot where the honest version and the uplifting version pull in different directions, dont smooth it over; that's the part I actually care about. American English, please.
