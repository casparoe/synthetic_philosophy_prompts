I'm a graduate student in philosophy working on population ethics, and I want to rehearse a confrontation I keep losing. Please play a committed defender of what I'll call the person-affecting view: the thesis that an action can be wrong only if it is wrong to someone — only if there is a person (or at least a subject of welfare) who is made worse off by it, or whose interests it sets back, compared with how that same person would otherwise have fared. Play someone who really holds this and thinks the nonidentity problem has a boring solution rather than a dramatic one: patient, precise, a little stubborn, armed with resources rather than slogans. You may concede minor points, and you may ask me at most one clarifying question per reply, but you may not simply retreat to "the cases are misdescribed" unless you say exactly what makes the description incoherent. Do not cite authorities, do not name books, articles, or philosophers, and do not give me a reading list — I want your own moves, not the literature's. Stay in character until I say "time out," keep your replies to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a conversation, and let me hear you think: if there is a fork in the road for your view, show me the fork before you take it.

Here is my opening move. Consider the same medication, taken by the same woman, at the same dose, for the same reason — it manages a chronic condition of hers — and consider two cases. In Case One she takes it during pregnancy, and it causes her child to have a mild but permanent impairment. That child's life is unquestionably worth living; the impairment makes her life somewhat worse than it would otherwise have been; she is loved and well cared for. In Case Two she takes the identical medication in the months before conceiving, and here it does two things: it delays conception by several months, so that the child who eventually exists is a different person from the child she would have had earlier, and it causes the same mild, permanent impairment in whichever child does come to exist. So the child in Case Two is not the child who would otherwise have existed — she is someone who would never have existed at all had the woman waited. Same medication, same motive, same impairment in kind and degree, same amount of suffering in the world, same love and care. The only difference is that the impaired child in Case One is the very child who would otherwise have been unimpaired, while the impaired child in Case Two would not have existed at all otherwise. Your view, as I understand it, says Case One is a serious wrong and Case Two is at most a misfortune.

So: tell me what that difference is, in your own words, and do not just label it "identity" and call it a day. Then tell me whether you really want to say the woman in Case Two did nothing wrong — not "nothing blameworthy," nothing wrong — and whether you would say the same about a regulator deciding whether to warn people off the medication. If you say both cases are wrong, tell me which premise of the person-affecting view you just gave up; if you say only Case One is wrong, tell me why I should not conclude that your view is tracking metaphysics rather than people.
