I am presenting to a joint session of a statistical mechanics seminar and a philosophy of physics reading group a paper arguing that Maxwell's demon does not need an information-theoretic exorcism: the second law survives the demon on ordinary thermodynamic grounds, because any measurement-and-memory apparatus the demon must carry is itself a physical system that produces at least as much entropy as the demon extracts, and Landauer's principle — that erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 — is neither necessary nor sufficient for that accounting. The audience is about fifteen people: roughly ten condensed-matter and stochastic-thermodynamics people who work on fluctuation theorems and treat "information is physical" as either a slogan or a tautology; three philosophers of physics who know the Szilard engine and the reversibility objections in detail and will want to know exactly where irreversibility enters; and one person from a quantum computing group who regards Landauer's principle as settled physics and has built a course around it. Nobody in the room cares about the mentalistic trappings of "information," and several will be hostile to any argument that sounds like it is sneaking consciousness back in through the demon.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way the person who would raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one about whether my accounting silently assumes the demon's memory is classical, finite, and erasable by an external agent whose own entropy cost I have not counted.
