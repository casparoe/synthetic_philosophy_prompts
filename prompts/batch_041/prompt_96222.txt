I've been working through Bentham's unpublished essays on sexual morality -- the ones where he takes apart the laws against sodomy -- and I keep bumping into a move I dont have a word for. The law bans an act; because it's banned, people come to be horrified by it; and then that horror gets cited as the reason the ban has to stay. I think he's after something stronger than "punishment has side effects": on his picture the disgust isn't an independent cost you can weigh against the pleasures of the act, because the law is what produced it, so the utilitarian sum is rigged from the start. Is there a name for this exact shape of argument? I know it's in the neighbourhood of the offense principle, of Durkheim-ish claims that punishment reinforces collective sentiment, of labeling theory and secondary deviance, and of adaptive preferences -- but none of those is quite it, since the thing here is specifically that the reaction gets laundered into a justification for the very practice that caused it. If there's no established term, coin one and tell me where its edges are: what it is not, how it differs from an ordinary secondary harm of a prohibition, and how you'd tell in a concrete case whether the disgust was manufactured by the law or was already there beforehand. I care about the carving, not the label.
