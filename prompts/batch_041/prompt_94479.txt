I've been sitting on the adivsory panel for our government's historical-justice programme for six years now — the bit of the machinery that decides which wrongs from the past get a formal apology in the legislature, which get a statement of regret with the word apology carefully kept out of it, and which get nothing at all. I've started to suspect we're running a rule I can't state cleanly, and I want you to tell me what it is.

The track record, names stripped out on purpose — don't go reaching for what you already think about the real cases, treat them as precedents and nothing more:

1. Mass internment and ill-treatment of a minority community during a counter-insurgency campaign in the 1950s. State forces, a datable campaign, victims and their children still living, some of the conduct unlawful even by the law of the time, and a public inquiry that had just reported. We recommended a full apology in the chamber, plus a capped compensation scheme.

2. The forced removal of the babies of unmarried mothers and their placement for adoption abroad, 1950s to 70s. State agencies and religious bodies; perfectly lawful at the time, nobody was ever prosecuted; mothers and children still living; a commission of investigation had reported. Full apology.

3. The transportation and deaths of a subject population after a rebellion in the 1840s. Datable and documented, but carried out by the colonial administration of the day, which answered to a different legislature; no living victims, though plenty of descendants; the facts still disputed by a stubborn minority of historians; no inquiry ever held. We recommended a statement of deep regret and advised the minister not to use the word apology.

4. Slavery and the plantation economy, and the wider colonial extraction. No living victims, centuries long, no single act to apologise for, and the Treasury's line — that an apology implies open-ended liability — was never quite rebutted by our panel. Nothing, on our recommendation. This is the one I've lost sleep over.

5. The killing of peaceful demonstrators by soldiers in a colonial territory in the 1910s. A discrete event, soldiers under the metropolitan government of the day, no living victims (descendants only), and an official inquiry that found the killings unjustified. Full apology.

Next week's case: a chartered company holding a royal charter from our Crown ran a territory in the 1890s and worked its population to death over about a decade, with our own officials knowing and doing nothing. A public inquiry has just reported, so the facts are settled. No living victims. The territory is now an independent state whose parliament has formally asked us to apologise, and there's a class action pending in our courts from the descendants.

What I want: which of the rules I've been half-consciously running actually survives the record — the living-victims rule, the discrete-act-not-structure rule, the continuity rule (we apologise only for what our own institutions did, not for what a chartered company or a separate colonial legislature did), the inquiry rule (we apologise once an official inquiry has settled the facts, and not before)? Say what each survivor predicts for next week, name the closest analog and why it's the closest, and give me your best guess at what the panel will do and what it ought to do if it wants to be consistent with itself. If two of the surviving rules disagree, tell me which further case would settle it.

Then be honest about the less flattering possibility: that the record is equally well explained by something with no principle in it at all — that we apologise when the claimants are a domestic political constituency and the liability can be capped, and refuse when they're foreign or diffuse. If the record can't seperate the principled rules from that one, say so, and tell me which case would. And whichever way we go, tell me what principle we're adopting by going that way, and what we're giving up.

One more thing, since I teach this: of the standard moves — the standing problem (who can accept an apology on behalf of the dead), the corporate-apology problem (whether a state can apologise at all, or only perform one), the non-identity point, the retrospectivity objection — which of them actually bear on the decision, and which are just seminar-room decoration? British English throughout, please.
