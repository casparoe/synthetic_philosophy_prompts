I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology to about sixteen students, and I want to run an activity on the Gettier problem that does not open with a lecture. What I have in mind is an adversarial definition-repair game, and I would like you to design it properly. The class splits into two teams plus a rotating chair. A working definition of knowledge is written on the board as a checklist — the subject believes the proposition; the subject's evidence supports it; the proposition is true — and by the rules of the game any case that satisfies all three boxes is to be entered in the register as knowledge. Each round, one team must amend the checklist so as to block the counterexample the other team has just produced, and the other team then gets a fixed interval to produce a fresh case that satisfies the amended checklist while being, by the class's own vote, not knowledge. Cases must be ordinary and local: no brains in vats, no evil demons, no science-fictional machinery. The game ends when the amendments stop being conditions an ordinary person could check, or when the time runs out. Students should not hear the word Gettier or the names of the standard cases until the debrief.

Design the activity: materials, the precise rules including how a proposed case is accepted or rejected and by whom, the round structure and timings, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the structure of the problem. In the debrief I want the distinction between cases in which one of the subject's justified beliefs is false and cases in which all the subject's beliefs are true but the truth is disconnected from the evidence, together with an account of why the obvious repairs — banning false steps in the reasoning, requiring that the evidence guarantee the truth — each close off one family of cases while leaving the other open, or, in the last case, cost more than the problem is worth. Participants should come away having felt that justified-true-belief definitions are cheap to state and cheap to break, and that they can generate the breaking cases themselves.

Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one team to try to win by making the case bizarre, and at least one to reach for the guarantee-the-truth patch — and say how to handle each. Be equally explicit about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself. No citations or further reading. Say how confident you are in the design, and single out the element you are least sure will work in a room of this size and length. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
