I run a small decision-theory reading group and next month I want to do the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation — the money pump / Dutch book family of arguements that say if your preferences aren't complete, transitive, continuous and so on, then you're irrational, because someone can build a sequence of trades that leaves you worse off by your own lights. I don't want another summary of Savage. I want the adversarial-collaboration document that two people who've been shouting past each other for a year should have produced. The two camps:

A. the coherence theorist: the axioms really are requirements of practical rationality, the pump and book arguments establish that, and an agent with incomplete or intransitive preferences is irrational — exploitable, and rightly called irrational for it;
B. the skeptic: the arguments either assume what they're meant to prove, or establish only that you shouldn't be *exploitable*, which is not the same as your preferences having to be complete; and maybe rationality is a matter of how you choose, not what your ranking looks like.

Write the joint statement both of them could put their names to. I want:

1. An agreed-claims section (numbered). What both accept about how these arguments are structured, what counts as a sure loss, what the representation theorems do and don't establish, and which parts of the dispute are about the axioms themselves versus about what follows from them.

2. A disagreements table: each row a claim, one column for the theorist's wording of their own side, one for the skeptic's — each phrased so that side would actually sign it, no strawmen. At minimum it should cover: whether the axioms are requirements of rationality or just of one particular model; whether completeness is required or only non-exploitability; whether intransitivity is irrational even for an agent who never in fact faces a pump; and whether the target of the argument is individual acts or policies/plans.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux give (a) the precise sub-question, (b) a case, argument or comparison both sides agree bears on it, and (c) each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance. Please make sure these four cases show up somewhere, they're the ones I care about:

- the agent who simply refuses every trade: immune to every pump, and still looks irrational — so what does that say about whether pump-immunity is the right criterion?
- an agent with lexicographic preferences over, say, safety and money: complete and transitive, but violates continuity, so no expected-utility representation — is she irrational, and is there actually a pump against her, or does every trade leave her better off by her own lights?
- the announced pump: the agent is told the whole sequence up front and declines the first trade. Irrational given her preferences at that moment, or rational because what she's really choosing between is plans rather than acts?
- an agent with genuinely incomplete preferences (no opinion between two options) who uses a choice rule that just picks a maximal element. Is there any pump left, and if not, does that vindicate incompleteness or just show the pump is the wrong test?

4. A short section on verbal disputes, kept seperate from the real ones. I suspect 'coherent', 'rational' and 'requirement' are doing different work on the two sides — say which disagreements are really about words and which aren't.

5. One paragraph each on what would actually make that side give up its position.

No citations or reading lists, please — I don't care who said what, I want the arguements themselves. And if one of the four cases above turns out not to discriminate between the camps, say so rather than forcing it into the table.
