My philosophical logic seminar is twelve upper-level undergraduates meeting once for ninety minutes, and I want that session to end with them having felt, rather than been told, why a constructivist will grant that one of P and not-P must hold and still refuse to assert the disjunction. Design the activity that gets them there. They have had a single course in classical propositional logic — truth tables and natural deduction — and have never encountered intuitionistic logic; nothing should be named until the debrief, not intuitionism, not constructivism, not the proof interpretation of the connectives.

The core should be a two-player challenge game over claims built from and, or, if-then, and absurdity, played with cards or counters rather than written proofs: one player asserts a claim and defends it, the other attacks; the attacker of a conjunction chooses which conjunct to attack; a defender who asserts a disjunction must name the disjunct she will stand behind; a defender who uses a conditional concedes its antecedent and hands the burden back. You will need to fix the remaining rules yourself — how challenges and concessions are recorded, when the burden changes hands, when a player loses, whether a round can end unresolved — and to check before committing to them that the notion of a winning strategy they generate makes exactly the intuitionistically valid formulas winnable and not the classically valid ones. Test your rules on P or not-P, on not-not-P implying P, on not both P and not-P, on not-not-(P or not-P), and on (P implies Q) implies (not-Q implies not-P).

Three constraints on the staging. At least one round must turn on a disjunction whose disjuncts nobody in the room can settle — a claim about a digit of pi far beyond any computation, or a question in number theory you have checked is still open — so that the refusal to assert is not merely a failure of imagination. Scoring must use tokens rather than money, and producing a witness should cost the defender something that feels like real work. And since the game is for two players, tell me how to run six boards at once and how the room reconvenes. A third role is needed as well — clerk, auditor, or scorekeeper — who records what was and was not established, so that a tally can be read out at the end.

Give me the rulebook and the materials list, a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, the exact wording of the two or three things I say at the start without giving the game away, and a debrief that ties particular moments in play to the difference between refuting a negation and proving by contradiction, to the disjunction and existence properties, to why modus ponens and ex falso come through untouched, and to why not-not-P is a weaker commitment than P. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time — the class concluding the game is rigged, a student insisting the defender lost rather than that the claim was never established, someone winning by stalling — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about the limits: the activity makes a stricter standard of assertion feel natural without supplying the meaning-theoretic argument for adopting it; it says nothing about the constructive reading of the quantifiers or about choice; a student can accept every rule and remain a classical logician; and the correspondence between winning strategies and intuitionistic validity is itself a piece of ordinary mathematics that the game does not make visible. A frank note on those limits is worth more to me than a game that overclaims.
