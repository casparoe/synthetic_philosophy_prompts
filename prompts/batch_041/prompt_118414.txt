I'm a third-year PhD student in distributed computing. My day job is consensus protocols and failure detectors, and I know the runs-and-systems formalism for knowledge well enough to teach it -- possible worlds over executions, knowledge as indistinguishability of runs at a point, all the results about when common knowledge can and can't be attained when messages get lost or the timing is unknown. My game theory is much shakier. I know what Nash and subgame-perfect equilibrium are, I know common knowledge of rationality is doing alot of the work in the backward induction story, and I know the coordinated attack problem's cousin in game theory -- the game where two players keep acking each other's messages and the cooperative action unravels as soon as there's any chance a message went missing. But I've never worked through the epistemic game theory literature propery, and the paradox about the player who reaches a node that common knowledge of rationality supposedly rules out has always struck me as suspicious rather than deep.

So here's what I want. Take that position -- roughly: common knowledge of rationality is what's supposed to underwrite the standard solution concepts, and the unreached-node problem is a real difficulty for it -- and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Runs, executions, local states, information sets, indistinguishability, knowledge and common knowledge as fixed points of the everyone-knows operator, reliable vs. lossy channels, synchronous vs. asynchronous rounds, what a process knows at a point in an execution, eventual vs. immediate knowledge. Do it as a systematic translation, move by move, not as an analogy: for each thing the game theorists do, give me the corresponding thing in my vocabulary, even if the correspondence is ugly.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which parts go through cleanly -- I suspect the knowledge half does, since I think we're both doing Kripke semantics with different accents, and I want you to tell me if that's right -- which parts go through only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. Rationality is the obvious suspect. In my world a process either follows the protocol or it's faulty, and the interesting failure model is a Byzantine process that does anything whatsoever; I have no notion of a process that reasons correctly from its own information and still does the wrong thing, or that optimizes against its beliefs about other processes' beliefs. So can the game theorist's irrationality even be expressed here, or is the nearest analogue -- the Byzantine fault -- a different category that I'd be smuggling in if I reached for it? Same question for belief: my knowledge is factive and truth is baked into the possible worlds, and I have no hierarchy-of-probabilities machinery at all, so I want to know whether the belief-based versions of these results are saying something my knowledge-only version can't.

And the verdict I'm really after: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary -- something distributed computing genuinely can't see about strategic interaction -- or is it a sign that the game theorists' formulation is vaguer than it looks, leaning on an epistemic assumption they never specify operationally? I'm not asking you to settle whether backward induction is sound. I want to know which parts of the debate are about something real that I'm missing, and which parts dissolve once someone has to write down an actual protocol. If a chunk of the disagreement turns out to be purely verbal under translation, say which chunk and why.
