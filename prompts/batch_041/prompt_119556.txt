I have arrived at an axiology I would like to stress-test, and I want to know what would count against it. Call it lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. Its premises are these: there is a single hedonic scale on which pleasure and pain are commensurable, running from the worst suffering through a neutral point to the greatest enjoyment; and somewhere on the bad side of that scale lies a threshold at which suffering becomes intolerable. The claim is that suffering worse than the threshold is lexically prior to every good above it — no quantity of enjoyment, however vast, can outweigh, compensate for, or justify even a single instance of it, so that comparison behaves nothing like an exchange rate. Suffering milder than the threshold, by contrast, trades off against pleasure in the ordinary additive way. The case that convinced me: forced to choose between one person enduring a year just past the threshold and a million people each gaining a large enjoyment, I judge the first to be worse, and no increase in the number of enjoyers changes that verdict.

What I want is a map of the view's exposure. Which observations, cases, intuitions, formal results, or empirical findings would count against it, and how decisive would each be — ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I am interested in defeaters of both halves. Against the lexical priority: arguments or evidence that it is absurd or unworkable, including any showing that it collapses into plain negative utilitarianism at one end of the threshold's range or into classical utilitarianism at the other, and any decision-theoretic cost of non-representable preferences or of an obligation to sacrifice everything for vanishing probabilities of crossing the threshold. Against the commensurability premise: evidence that pleasure and pain occupy separate scales, that pain has more than one dimension that need not trade off linearly, that no non-arbitrary neutral point exists, that the threshold cannot be located or compared across persons, or that the psychological asymmetry between negative and positive affect tells against a single currency.

Two further questions. First, which of these defeaters would a defender of the view concede as counting against them, rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding? Second, I suspect the position may be unfalsifiable in practice: whenever a counterexample bites, the threshold can be relocated or the scale redescribed so that no case ever tells against it. If that is so, say whether it is a defect of this view or a feature of any axiology with a lexical element — and whether that insulation is a reason to abandon the position or simply the price of taking suffering seriously.
