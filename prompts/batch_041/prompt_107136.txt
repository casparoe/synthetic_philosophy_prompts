I've recently been appointed to my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and I've been surprised by how often the hardest cases aren't the ones where the principles obviously conflict—they're the ones where everyone on the committee is confident, but they're confident in different directions, and I suspect that at least some of us are being led by something other than the merits of the case. A colleague suggested I look at Adam Smith's idea of the impartial spectator, and while I find the basic idea appealing—judge your conduct as an impartial spectator would—I can't figure out how to actually do that in a meeting, under time pressure, with people I like and people I don't, and with a strong pull to side with the clinician who reminds me of myself. I need something more concrete than simply resolving to be impartial.

Could you write me a procedure my committee could actually run on a case—a set of steps to go through in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision back to the committee because it's a values question rather than a factual one? I'm particularly interested in the parts of Smith's account that make this hard: the difference between wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy; the way self-deception works, since Smith says we're prone to see our own conduct in a favorable light and that the internal judge he calls the man within the breast is easily corrupted; and the way custom and fashion can make us blind to the moral features of a situation, which I worry hospital culture does as much as any other. Then run the procedure on two cases: one where a family is requesting a treatment that the clinical team thinks is futile, and one where a clinician has made a decision that seems right by the book but feels wrong to the nurses who have to carry it out. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I don't want to hear, and be honest about what the procedure can't do—where it just gives me a vocabulary for the disagreement rather than a resolution.
