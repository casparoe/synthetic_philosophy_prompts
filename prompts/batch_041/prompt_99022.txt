The question she set:

Explain the higher-order thought (HOT) theory of consciousness. Say what it claims makes a mental state conscious, how it differs from at least one rival higher-order view, and then assess the misrepresentation objection: that since a higher-order representation is itself a representation, it can get its target wrong, and say whether the theory can handle this.

The students answer:

The higher-order thought theory (HOT), associated mainly with David Rosenthal, says that what makes a mental state conscious is not anything about the state itself but the fact that you have a higher-order thought about it. So a first-order state, like a sensation of red, is unconscious by default, and it becomes conscious when a suitable thought to the effect that you are in that state is directed at it. This is meant to capture the intuition that conscious experience always involves some awareness of oneself as being in the state, which is what separates a conscious pain from a pain you have but never notice. Rosenthal develops this in terms of an inner sense: the higher-order thought is really a kind of perception-like monitoring of your own first-order states, which is what lets it represent them in the first place. This puts him close to Lycan and Armstrong, who also treat the higher-order state as a form of inner perception, though Rosenthal adds the further claim that the higher-order state does not itself need to be conscious.

The obvious objection is the misrepresentation objection. Since the higher-order state is a representation of a first-order state, it can misrepresent it, so the theory seems to imply that if you had a higher-order thought representing you as being in a state you are not actually in, the theory would still count that as a conscious state, which is absurd. The standard reply is that the higher-order thought is itself allowed to be unconscious, so the theory does not need a further conscious state to explain the consciousness of the first-order state, and this blocks the objection. So overall the theory survives the misrepresentation objection, and its real strength is that it gives a clean explanation of the difference between conscious and unconscious states.
