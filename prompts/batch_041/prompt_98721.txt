I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am trying to write a policy layer for an autonomous system that is supposed to operate under deontological constraints. I keep running into what I understand to be the paradox of deontology: if a constraint forbids an agent from harming someone even when doing so would prevent greater harms to others, then the constraint appears to forbid minimizing the very thing—harm—that makes the constraint worth having. If the point of the constraint is to protect persons from harm, why should it block a harm-minimizing exception? I have five candidate specifications for how the constraint layer could work. Please rank them in tiers (S, A, B, C, D) by how well each one resolves the paradox rather than merely restating it or relocating the difficulty. For each, give a concise justification and identify the residual problem it leaves. Assess the specifications on their merits rather than by who has defended something like them.

1. Lexicographic firewall: the system treats the constraint as a hard lexical priority over any aggregative harm-reduction objective. It may never intentionally harm a person, even to prevent several equivalent harms, unless the harmed person has explicitly waived the protection.

2. Commitment device under opacity: the constraint is justified as a rule an agent would pre-commit to because, in the actual epistemic position, the agent cannot reliably identify the cases where violating would minimize harm, and the long-run costs of a violation habit exceed the local gains.

3. Agent-relative boundary: the constraint does not forbid the agent from allowing harm; it only forbids the agent from doing harm. The agent is permitted to refuse to be the instrument of harm even when that refusal leaves more harm in the world.

4. Incommensurability stop: the constraint marks a place where the system refuses to aggregate, not because the forbidden act is infinitely bad, but because the values on either side of the comparison are not commensurable in a way that would license a trade.

5. Rightsholder-controlled override: the constraint is a default entitlement held by each person; it can be overridden only by the person whose entitlement it is, or by a procedure that treats each person's authorization as necessary. Aggregation across persons is never sufficient.

I would like the ranking to distinguish resolutions that actually address the normative force of the constraint from those that are really decision-theoretic or epistemic workarounds. Also tell me which single specification, if any, you would implement first, and what test case would most likely expose its weakness.
