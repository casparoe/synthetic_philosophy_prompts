For the last four months I have been running a fortnightly reading group on scalable oversight — a mixed crowd, mostly machine learning PhD students plus two philosophers who keep the rest of us honest — and two of the regulars have now spent three consecutive sessions arguing past each other about process-based versus outcome-based supervision. One of them holds that step-level supervision is a categorically different and better kind of signal: it tells the model which move was wrong rather than merely that the final answer was wrong, it locates credit and blame where they belong, and it is therefore the more promising route to overseeing systems whose outputs we cannot check. The other holds that this is largely an artifact of small-scale experiments, and that outcome supervision, once one can afford enough samples and enough search at inference time, will absorb everything process supervision buys you at a fraction of the labelling cost — and that in any case a step-level reward is just an outcome reward for a shorter task. I have stopped being able to tell whether they disagree about the facts, about what the words mean, or about which comparison is the fair one, and I would rather the group produce the document the two of them should have written than sit through another round of this.

What I want from you is that joint statement, written so that both of them could put their names to it. It should begin with the claims they can both accept: about what each kind of supervision actually supplies to a learner; about the places where the two signals arguably converge, since a step-level reward is always defined relative to some segmentation of a task and any final answer is itself a step in some larger process, which makes me suspect that part of the distinction is one of grain rather than of kind; about what the existing evidence can and cannot establish; and about the settings in which neither signal is doing any work. Then the residual disagreements, each phrased in the form its own side would accept, with no strawmanning in either direction. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. I would expect at least these to be among them, though I want you to add or discard as the argument requires: whether the advantage of step-level supervision is a learning-signal advantage or a verification-and-selection advantage, and whether the two can be separated at all; whether the phenomenon of models reaching correct answers through reasoning they did not in fact rely on, or rationalizing answers settled on for other reasons, tells against process supervision, which rewards steps that look right, or against outcome supervision, which rewards answers however they were reached; whether step-level training instils reasoning or merely the appearance of reasoning to a judge, and what observation would distinguish those; and what could possibly count as an equal budget when one side receives per-step labels on a handful of trajectories and the other per-episode labels on very many. For each crux, give the test, case, or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — for instance a comparison in which one base policy and one pool of sampled trajectories are split by labelling regime and the resulting policies are compared on held-out tasks that require the same skills but lie outside the training distribution, and a second comparison asking whether a policy that has learned to satisfy a step-level model carries its bad habits over to tasks where that model is absent. Where you judge that part of their dispute is verbal, say which part and why, and where you judge that a crux is not empirically decidable with present methods, say that instead of inventing a test. Close with what each side would count as evidence against its own position.

Write it in continuous prose, the way a circulated memo reads — no headings, no numbered list, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; if you need to mark where one part ends and the next begins, do it in ordinary sentences. No citations, dates, or reading lists: we all know the literature and I want the argument, not a bibliography. And keep the tone of a document two people actually agreed to sign rather than a referee report on one of them.
