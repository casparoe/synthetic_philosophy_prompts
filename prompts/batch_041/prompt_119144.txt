I run the policy shop at a state agency that administers what we call the Hardship Relief Fund — a capped pot, about $40M a year, that pays one-time grants to residents who've been hit by something bad. The statute says we may only pay for losses that weren't the applicant's own doing. The legislative history is vague about what "own doing" means, and our sixty adjudicators are not philosophers, they're claims examiners with about twenty minutes per case and a queue of four thousand. Right now they're making it up as they go, and the appeals board has started to notice.

You're a philosopher who works on distributive justice and has actually done applied policy work. Please turn luck egalitarianism into something my examiners can run. The idea as I understand it: inequalities that trace back to brute luck are unjust and ones that trace back to option luck are not, and there's a device where you ask what a prudent person would have insured against. I need a decision procedure — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands off to a supervisor because the question is one of values rather than facts. Two things I want taken seriously rather than papered over: first, examiners can't reliably tell a choice from a circumstance; second, the standard complaint that a strict version of this view abandons the imprudent — if our procedure tells a guy who gambled his rent away to pound sand, my director will want to know why that isn't cruelty with a footnote.

Start by brainstorming which distinctions you think have to survive the translation and which you're willing to drop, then give me the procedure, then run it on three cases: a trucker whose back was wrecked by another driver and who had skipped the disability coverage his union offered; a homeowner who rebuilt in a flood plain without flood insurance and got flooded again; and a woman who spent her savings on a gambling app and now can't make rent. One of these should come out in a way the agency won't like — say so plainly. Finish with what the procedure can't do. Under 900 words, and no citations, I dont need a reading list.
