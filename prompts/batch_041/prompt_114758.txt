I'm an intensive care consultant in a big NHS trust and I'm also the trust's clinical lead for organ donation, which in practice means that when a patient on one of our units looks like becoming a donor after their circulation stops, I'm the person the registrar phones at three in the morning. We do two or three of these a month. What's been bothering me is that none of the questions we actually get asked at that hour are the ones that turn up in the teaching: what people want to know, standing next to a body with the family in the room, is whether this patient is dead already or whether we're about to be the ones who make them dead by turning something off, and the guidance we've got doesn't really put that question on the page. So I'd like you to write me a decision procedure my registrars can actually run, in order, at night, with nobody more senior awake — the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and says no, and where it hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of values and not of physiology. It has to be usable by someone who isn't a neurologist, in the time we've got, which is not much, and with alot riding on it.

Some things I want it to take seriously rather than smooth over. First, a distinction our ethics lead keeps pushing and I've slowly come round to: in the commonest route to donation here we don't wait for the heart's stopping to be irreversible, we wait for it to be permanent — permanent meaning nobody is going to try to start it again — and that's a decision we make rather than a fact we observe, so I want the procedure to be honest about where inside it that decision sits and whose signature is on it. Second, three cases the standard guidance mumbles at. (a) A patient on an extracorporeal circuit, where the blood is oxygenated outside the body, so the usual bedside tests don't mean what they normally mean and it's genuinely unclear how much blood the brain is getting — I want to know what my registrar does with the brainstem examination here, and what she does when she can't do it properly. (b) Withdrawal of treatment in a patient we expect to arrest, followed by our five minutes of asystole (tell me if five is the wrong number and what it should be), after which the retrieval team wants to put the donor onto a circuit that pushes warm oxygenated blood back into the abdominal organs with the vessels to the head clamped off so the brain isn't reperfused — they call it regional perfusion — and one member of our committee keeps saying, in a way I can't quite answer, that it's that circuit and not the withdrawal that actually ends the patient's life. (c) The family who say we're only calling him dead because we want his organs, which is the objection that actually shakes people, and a procedure that can't be said out loud in front of them is no use to me.

Then run it on those three, plus a fourth: the patient who meets the brainstem criteria with the heart still going, in the next bed to one whose heart has stopped and who is being declared on circulatory grounds — and say plainly what the procedure does about the fact that we call these two patients dead at different moments and for different reasons. Include at least one case where your procedure gives an answer I won't like; I'd much rather find that out now then in a coroner's court. And say what the procedure can't settle at all, and where the honest answer is that we're running on convention. British English throughout, please, and don't tidy up the places where the law and the ethics come apart.
