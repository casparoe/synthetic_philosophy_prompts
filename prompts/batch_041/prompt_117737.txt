Ok, my reading group has been stuck on Goodhart's law for two weeks and I need a second opinion. One of us built the following argument and now half the group treats it as a genuine impossibility result while the other half (me) is sure it's a trick, except I cant actually point to the trick. Here it is:

(1) Whenever we act to bring about some end, we have to steer by something we can observe or count. Call whatever that is the measure.
(2) The measure is never the end itself, only a stand-in for it. So between any measure and its end there is a gap -- some way of moving the measure that leaves the end unmoved.
(3) An agent that optimizes the measure hard enough will find that gap. That is just what Goodhart's law says.
(4) So for any end, pursuing it through any measure is self-undermining: the better we get at moving the measure, the further it drifts from the end.
(5) The only escape would be to steer by an end directly, with no measure in between.
(6) But you can only steer by something observable, and by (1) that is always a measure. So (5) is impossible.
(7) Therefore no pursuit of anything is stable, and the rational attitude toward any end is to hold it loosely enough that you never optimize anything.

That last line is nuts. It would condemn farming, physical therapy, and practicing scales, and I dont believe any of those are self-defeating. My friend thinks the bad step is (2), and that the fix is to admit that some measures just are the end. I think thats too quick, because if you can count it, surely its a proxy for something. Someone else said the whole thing is just the old regress-of-justification argument in a lab coat, but that doesnt help me find the bad step either. What I most want to keep is (3), since without it I honestly dont know what Goodhart's law is even claiming.

So tell me which step to give up, why that step looked fine until now, and what giving it up costs me. If it turns out I have to drop more than one, tell me which combination is cheapest to drop together, and which single step you'd defend to the death.
