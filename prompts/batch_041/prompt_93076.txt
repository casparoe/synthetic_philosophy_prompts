I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session at a weekend ideas festival: roughly two dozen adults, intelligent generalists, no formal training in game theory, and one or two who will take pleasure in being difficult. I want to spend the session on an idea that has been circulating in the multi-agent reinforcement learning and cooperative-AI literature under the name of the safe Pareto improvement, and I want the room to feel it rather than hear about it.

As I understand the idea, the contrast is this. An ordinary Pareto improvement is a change that makes everyone better off provided everyone goes along with it. A safe Pareto improvement is a change to the rules that is guaranteed to leave every player at least as well off, whatever the others end up doing, and without anyone's having to hold beliefs about how the others will play. That guarantee is what makes the idea worth having: you can sign up to such a change before you know whom you are playing, and it cannot be turned against you. The delicate part, and the part I want participants to run into rather than be told, is that the guarantee always rests on assumptions about how the modified game will be played, for example that two games differing only in their labels will be played the same way, or that plainly bad options will be discarded. Where those assumptions hold, the guarantee is real; where they quietly fail, a change that looked safe is only an ordinary Pareto improvement in disguise.

Please design the session. What I need is a runnable activity for a room of that size and that time, built so that the participants discover the distinction in their own play and do not hear the term safe Pareto improvement until the debrief. The arc should include at least two announced changes to the rules of the game they are playing: one that survives the test of being acceptable in advance, before you know your counterpart, and one that is genuinely tempting and genuinely unsafe, in that it improves things only if the others play along. Do not mark which is which.

Include the rules in full, the materials (I would rather this ran on index cards and tokens than on screens), a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a matching protocol that works for two dozen people in a room without arithmetic beyond what can be done on fingers, a timeline to the minute, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the play onto the parts of the idea they correspond to. Tell me, plainly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce, the formal guarantee over all possible opponents as opposed to the handful in the room, and the idea of a modification applied across arbitrary games, and tell me which of those pieces I will simply have to state myself in the debrief.

One direction I can see, which you should feel free to replace if you find a better one: begin with an anonymous two-player game with a tempting betrayal, played repeatedly with rematching; then let players write short conditional instructions that their counterpart may read before playing; then let them stake something on their own commitment. I suspect the first of those changes is where the safety lives and the second is where it dies, but I would rather you work out the mechanics than inherit mine, and I would rather the failure modes be honest than the design elegant.
