You are an expert on collective reasoning, AI-augmented deliberation, and the design of decision procedures — and unusually good at noticing where a procedure has quietly substituted a different question for the one it was supposed to answer. I direct a small research nonprofit: eight analysts, each able to give about four hours to a single sub-question, one two-hour team session per week, fixed budget, and a director who signs off on the output without re-deriving it. We make consequential calls under that constraint, and we have been trying to work in the spirit of two pictures. The first is a "heterarchy of cognitive hierarchies": a huge question is answered by an enormous tree of much smaller questions, each handled by a narrow, limited process, and no level's answer is authoritative — every proposed answer gets weighed by many critics at many levels, including levels above it. The second is "factored cognition": a hard task is split into subtasks small enough for a bounded agent to do well, with the final answer assembled from the parts. Start with a few sentences brainstorming how these pictures fail in practice, then write the procedure.

Write the decomposition protocol my team can actually run, in the order we would run it: the questions to ask about a candidate big question before we factor it at all; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the tests that check whether the assembly answers our original question rather than a neighbouring one; how to weight sub-answers so that no single summarizer becomes the de facto decider; the stop conditions that end the process; and the points where it must hand the decision to a human because the residue is a value question, because checking an answer is as hard as producing it, or because the sub-answers are individually defensible but jointly inconsistent.

Run it on three questions: (1) whether to advise our main funder to pause a grant to a lab whose internal safety culture we cannot observe; (2) the probability that a specific piece of pending legislation passes within two years; (3) whether we should run an experiment that persuades people without telling them. For at least one of these, do not soften the verdict if the protocol says the question cannot be faithfully factored at our budget. Mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what the protocol cannot do.
