I am assembling materials for a cross-listed graduate seminar (philosophy of mind and machine learning) on inverse reinforcement learning, and I want the session organised around a single question: when we observe an agent's behaviour and fit a reward function to it, what have we learned? Please construct a decision tree of the positions on that question — internal nodes as questions, leaves as named positions — suitable for handing out as a diagnostic flowchart.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of schools. The root should be a commitment nearly everyone in the room shares: that fitting a reward function to observed behaviour is at least sometimes a way of finding out something about the agent, rather than a mere redescription of what it did. Every branching question below that should be answerable by someone who does not yet know which position it leads to; phrase them as concrete cases, or as first-order claims about what is going on, not as labels to endorse. In particular, no node should read like "are you a behaviourist about reward?"

I want the tree to turn on at least these distinctions, plus whatever else you judge necessary:

- whether the thing inferred is a state of the agent (a preference, a desire, a valuation) or a summary of its behaviour that need not answer to any state at all;
- whether the demonstrator is assumed optimal, boundedly rational, or systematically mistaken, and if mistaken, whether the fit recovers what the agent actually wanted or what it would have wanted had its beliefs been true;
- whether the object recovered is a scalar over outcomes or something with structure a scalar cannot carry — rules, several incommensurable objectives, commitments held regardless of consequences;
- whose preferences are at issue once more than one agent is in the frame, and whether a joint objective can be decomposed back into individual ones;
- whether the exercise is descriptive (predict what the agent will do) or justificatory (say what the agent has reason to do, or what it would endorse on reflection).

The following cases should each appear as the content of a node: a person who buys a gym membership and never attends; someone who orders the cheapest item on every menu; someone who refuses to lie even when lying would secure the outcome they claim to care about most; two flatmates of whom one always cooks and the other always cleans; someone who afterwards says they never really wanted what they chose; and a demonstrator who performs a task for a learner in the way they believe the learner would like it done. Where two of these cases pull in the same direction, say so rather than multiplying nodes.

The leaves should be labelled with a short descriptive name; use a familiar label where one genuinely exists, but do not attribute any position to a named researcher or a particular paper — I would rather the tree stand on its own arguments than on names I cannot verify. Aim for eight to twelve leaves and six to nine internal nodes. For each leaf, add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment: the case or consequence its defenders find hardest to accommodate.

Two further requests. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to pull them apart. Second, list the combinations of answers the tree permits but that no leaf occupies, and for each say briefly whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to develop it.

Please give the tree in outline form — indented, with the branch question stated at each split — rather than as continuous prose, and keep any commentary outside the tree to a minimum.
