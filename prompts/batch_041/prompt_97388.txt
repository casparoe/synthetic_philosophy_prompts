After thirty years as a sedimentary geologist I retired, and I have spent the last several trying to educate myself in philosophy, mostly the philosophy of science; I have just finished a slow pass through the logical empiricists, and Otto Neurath has become something of a fixation. What fascinates me about his picture-language project is the combination of a genuinely humane motive — make statistics legible to working people, strip the pomp out of academic prose — with a set of assumptions that strike me, as a working scientist, as deeply strange: that a pictorial notation could be international and neutral, that the act of choosing which quantities to draw and at what scale introduces no perspective of its own, and that whatever cannot be rendered as a row of little figures is either meaningless or not yet science. The earnestness of the whole enterprise is what makes it, to my mind, ideal material for satire, since the funniest version of it is one its authors might almost have written themselves.

I would like you to draft, for the winter newsletter of a small philosophy-of-science reading group I belong to, a parody house-style manual — approximately 900 words, formatted as a numbered list of rules with the occasional marginal annotation — issued in-universe by a fictional standards bureau that has adopted a Neurath-style picture language as its only official medium of communication. Invent the bureau and give it a plausible institutional history (a founding, a schism, a rival committee); the target of the satire is the position and the practice, never a named person, living or dead. Let the humor come from the internal logic of the program rather than from making its partisans fools: the drive to purge metaphysics by forbidding anything undrawable, the dream of a single unified visual encyclopedia of all the sciences, the confidence that pictures are more neutral than words, and the way an institution that distrusts rhetoric must nonetheless police its own house style. I would especially like the manual to contain one rule that, in the very act of eliminating an ambiguity, manufactures a worse one, and one internal contradiction the bureau is entirely unaware of; a reader who has read Neurath should recognize that you have, without your ever needing to cite him more than in passing.

Keep it deadpan and bureaucratic — the voice of a body that has no idea how funny it is — and keep it legible to a reader who has never heard of logical empiricism, so that the irony never depends on inside knowledge even though it rewards it. The document should be self-contained and should not rely on quotations, citations, dates, or the titles of real works, since I want it to stand on its own in a setting where no one can look anything up.
