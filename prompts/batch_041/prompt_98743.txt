For years I have followed the back-and-forth over the wrong kind of reasons problem — in journal articles, yes, but mostly in comment threads where two camps reliably talk past each other — and I want to see whether it can be turned into an adversarial collaboration rather than another round of mutual misreading. Please draft the joint statement that the two following camps could both put their names to.

The first camp, which I will call the Restrictionists, holds the fitting-attitude analysis of value: for an object to be good, or valuable, just is for it to be the fitting object of a pro-attitude, where fittingness is a normative relation between an attitude and its object rather than a matter of the attitude's costs or benefits. They grant that not every reason to have a pro-attitude bears on value — the reward cases show this — but they maintain that the class of value-relevant reasons can be picked out by a principled, non-circular restriction, paradigmatically the distinction between object-given reasons (reasons that bear on the merits of the object) and state-given reasons (reasons that bear on the costs and benefits of merely having the attitude).

The second camp, which I will call the Pessimists, holds that no such restriction can do the work it is asked to do. Every candidate restriction, they argue, either reintroduces the very notion of value or fittingness it was supposed to explain, or draws the line in the wrong place, letting in cases that should be out and shutting out cases that should be in. On their view the wrong kind of reasons problem is either fatal to the fitting-attitude analysis or shows that fittingness must be taken as primitive — in which case it does no explanatory work for value at all.

Both camps are sophisticated, and neither is a strawman. Write the document so that each would recognize its own view in the wording and would not feel that the other side's vocabulary has been smuggled in. The deliverable should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — everything both camps can sign, including claims about what the problem is, what would count as solving it, what the reward cases do and do not show, and what neither camp is committed to.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should give a precise point of remaining disagreement, stated first in neutral wording that neither side would reject as slanted, then in the Restrictionist's own preferred formulation, then in the Pessimist's.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — state the case or argument both camps agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about how it comes out. I suspect one crux concerns whether the object-given/state-given distinction is itself normative all the way down, and another concerns cases in which a reason to have an attitude is at the same time a reason that the object be as it is.

4. A short final section identifying any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, and saying what each side would gain or lose by adopting the other's terminology.

I want the statement to engage the following cases, which I have constructed so that neither side can simply cite precedent:

- The Sponsorship. An athlete comes to value a sport only because a sponsor pays her contingent on her valuing it, and the payment is not contingent on the sport's being valuable. Does her valuing make the sport good, or only make her better off? Is her reason of the right kind?
- The Reversed Sponsorship. A benefactor will pay an agent only if the agent's pro-attitude is fitting — that is, only if the object is genuinely valuable. Does the prospect of payment give the agent a reason of the right kind to have the attitude? If not, does that show the object-given/state-given line is tracking something other than value?
- The Self-Fulfilling Valuation. An agent's valuing of a relationship is itself what makes the relationship valuable. The reason to value it is at once state-given (valuing benefits her) and object-given (valuing makes the object good). Does this break the distinction or illustrate it?
- The Confidence Pill. A pill induces in an agent a high credence that a proposition is true, and separately a reward is offered for holding that credence. Does the wrong kind of reasons problem arise for credences in the same way it arises for desires, and does whatever each camp says here constrain what it may say about value?

Where a camp's prediction on one of these cases would commit it to a position on another, say so explicitly. Keep the whole document constructive: its point is not to settle who is right but to make the residual disagreement exact and to put both camps' commitments on the record.
