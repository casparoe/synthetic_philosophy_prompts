I am the head of collections at a maritime museum that also maintains one large historic wooden vessel as a working exhibit, and I chair the committee that decides what we accession, what we lend, how we label an object, and what we insure. We keep having the same fight: whether a thing is still "the original." Our own ship has been repaired continuously for well over a century, plank by plank and fitting by fitting, so that almost no timber from the 1800s survives in place; separately, we stored the rotted-out timbers as they came off over the decades, and a private yard has now built a vessel out of that stored material, which some donors insist is the "real" ship; a third party has offered us a replica built to the original plans entirely from new wood; and a fourth object is a "restoration" whose hull is a patchwork of our own timbers and timbers salvaged from a sister ship of the same class. My board wants a written decision procedure it can rely on, so that these calls stop being settled by whoever argues loudest, and I have to be able to defend each outcome to donors, insurers, and harbor authorities.

We once had a philosopher address the committee, and a few ideas stuck with me that I would like the procedure to make usable by people who never studied this. One is that "the same" may be relative to the kind of thing you are asking about — the same vessel-as-artifact, the same vessel-as-historical-witness, the same vessel-as-sailing craft — and that the verdict can legitimately differ from one kind to the next. Another is that different theories give opposite answers here: one says a thing is destroyed the moment it loses any original part, another says it survives as long as the history of replacement is continuous, and a third says an object is really a four-dimensional whole whose earlier and later stages are all equally parts of it. And a last one is that in some of these cases there may simply be no fact of the matter, in which case the honest move is to say so rather than manufacture a verdict.

Please write the procedure for us: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it returns a definite verdict and the point at which it returns "no determinate answer," and which steps are conventions we are choosing rather than facts we are discovering. It has to be runnable in a single committee meeting by people with no philosophy background, and it must say who has to sign off when the procedure cannot decide. Then run it on our four objects — the continuously repaired ship, the reassembled original timbers, the new-wood replica, and the patchwork restoration — and be willing to give me at least one answer I will not like. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do.
