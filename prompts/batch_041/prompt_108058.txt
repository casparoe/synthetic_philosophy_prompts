Ok so this has been bugging me for a while and I finally want someone to think it through properly. In Switzerland a popular initiative can rewrite the federal constitution: you collect 100k signatures, and if a majority of voters and a majority of the cantons both say yes, it goes in the constitution. The cantons each count as one in that second majority, so a yes vote in some tiny canton ends up weighing far more than a yes in Zurich. At the same time the Federal Supreme Court basically won't strike down a federal statute for being unconstitutional, it can review cantonal law but not what parliament itself enacts. And yet the one thing the people apparently can't vote for is an initiative that violates peremptory international law, something authorizing torture or genocide, those can be declared invalid before they even reach a ballot. So: the people can rewrite the supreme law, no court gets to check parliament against that same supreme law, but there is this floor the people can't go under no matter what they vote for. (For contrast, the mirror image is Germany, powerful constitutional court, essentially no federal referendums.)

What I want is the implicit theory, not a summary of what the institutions do. What view of popular sovereignty or constituent power does that package presuppose? Does it require believing the people are the only legitimate source of law and can never be bound by their own earlier constitution, or is that just one reading? Does the double majority presuppose a different demos, i.e. that the political community is a union of cantons rather than one body of equal citizens, and is that consistent with still calling the result a democratic majority? And is the jus cogens floor consistent with the rest or is it an imported foreign element, a bit of natural law smuggled into a system that otherwise denies any higher law above the voters? Tell me which parts the arrangement strictly presupposes and which it merely fits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined, so that two or three different theories would license the exact same institutions. Then tell me what would have to change if you replaced the presupposed view with its main rival (say the constitution as higher law that binds even the people who made it, or the court as guardian of that higher law) — would the pieces come apart, or could you keep them and just reinterpret them?

One thing: separate whats actually settled from whats genuinely contested among people who know Swiss constitutional law and democratic theory, and dont hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. You dont need to cite anyone and please dont invent readings of named authors, just do the reconstruction.
