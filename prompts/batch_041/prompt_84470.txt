I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a chapter on expressive theories of art, and I want to use R. G. Collingwood as a test case for a methodological question: how far a theory written before generative systems existed can be extended to them without our putting words in its author's mouth. I'm working from the account in The Principles of Art, which I'll summarize here so that we work from the same materials rather than from a textbook gloss of them: art proper is the expression of emotion; expression is not the outward publication of a feeling that already exists in finished form but the process by which the feeling becomes determinate, so the artist characteristically does not know what he is expressing until he has expressed it; art is not a craft, and the technical theory that treats it as one errs in supposing the end to be specified in advance and the activity to consist in means and skill; and the pseudo-forms commonly called art — amusement art, magic art, art as representation — are to be distinguished from art proper. I also read him as holding that the work of art is not the physical artifact but an activity of imagination, and that the audience is a party to that activity rather than a consumer of an object. Tell me if that last reading is contentious.

Now put Collingwood, alive today, in front of prompt-based image generation. I don't need the verdict on whether the outputs count as art; that is the easy case and I can guess it. What I want is a doctrine-by-doctrine reconstruction of what his framework actually commits him to, and a clear-eyed account of where it goes silent.

1. The prompter. Craft or art? The technical theory's criteria look satisfied — plan then execute, means toward a preconceived end, a hierarchy of means — but the prompter's own report is frequently the opposite of a preconceived end: she describes receiving an image that was not what she intended and then recognizing it as what she had been after. If we take that report at face value, does it move her activity toward Collingwoodian expression, and if so, whose expression is it?

2. The machine. Nothing in his account of expression looks obviously tied to a biological subject, but the account does seem to require a mind in which something becomes clarified. Is the requirement of a subject load-bearing, or dispensable? And what does he make of a generator whose outputs are unpredictable to its own operators — does that unpredictability mimic the unplanned character of expression, or does it miss the point that expression is something the artist does rather than something that happens to her?

3. The audience. On my reading, the audience completes the work. If so, does a curated generated image become art in the viewing, the spectator supplying the expressive activity — and would Collingwood accept that consequence, or reject the reading in order to avoid it?

4. The moral register. He treats dishonest expression as a failure with more than aesthetic stakes, something closer to a corruption of consciousness than to a merely bad result. Would he locate the industry around generative images there, or is that a projection of ours onto him?

5. The reverse pressure. The same craft criteria would seem to disqualify work he would not want to disqualify — commissioned portraiture, architecture, film, most professional music-making. Is the art/craft line as he draws it stable under that pressure, or is AI art merely the case that makes an old line-drawing problem visible?

For each question, give the reading his texts best support, mark sharply where you are extrapolating, and close with the two or three claims I could not responsibly attribute to him in print.
