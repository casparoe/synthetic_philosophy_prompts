A buddy from my reading group sent me this and I can't tell if it's a real proof or a card trick, so I'm outsourcing it. Setup: A, B, C are just propositions; P is a probability function; P(B|A) is the conditional probability of B given A; A→B is plain "if A then B".

(1) P(A→B) = P(B|A), whenever P(A) > 0. This is the thing being tested and it's the one everybody likes.
(2) A→B is a proposition like any other, so it has a probability and you can condition on other stuff and then ask about it. Also (1) isn't a fluke of one particular probability function; it's supposed to be true of probability as such, so it's true of any function you get by conditionalizing, e.g. P(·|B).
(3) Standard bookkeeping: P(X) = P(X|Y)P(Y) + P(X|¬Y)P(¬Y).

Then:
(4) Take P(·|B) and apply (1) to it: P(A→C|B) = P(C|A∧B).
(5) Same with ¬B: P(A→C|¬B) = P(C|A∧¬B).
(6) Expand P(A→C) with (3) and plug in (4) and (5): P(A→C) = P(C|A∧B)P(B) + P(C|A∧¬B)P(¬B).
(7) But (1) applied to P itself says P(A→C) = P(C|A) = P(C|A∧B)P(B|A) + P(C|A∧¬B)P(¬B|A).
(8) Those two have to agree, and rearranging gives (P(C|A∧B) − P(C|A∧¬B)) × (P(B) − P(B|A)) = 0.
(9) So for every triple A, B, C: either B makes no difference to C once you know A, or B is independent of A.
(10) Take A = "the die came up 6", B = "the die came up even", C = "the die came up 6". The first factor is 1, not 0, so we're stuck with P(even) = P(even|6) = 1. That's obviously false.
(11) So (1) is false.
