I'm giving the closing speech at a retirement dinner next month for Nadia, who's been a public defender in our county office for thirty-one years — she was my supervisor when I started and the main reason I stayed. The room will be a mix: a bunch of lawyers who know exactly how bad the caseloads are, her family, a couple of judges, and her, who has already told me twice that if I say one word about her going above and beyond she will definately come up and take the microphone out of my hand. Which is my problem, because the whole reason I got asked to do this is that everybody knows she did go above and beyond — the weekend jail visits, the client whose rent she paid out of her own pocket for three months, the appeal she kept alive for four years after everyone else had written the case off. So I don't want to just sprinkle the word supererogation on it like salt. I want a five-minute speech where the idea actually does the work: what would have to be true for an act to be beyond duty rather than just the job done properly, whether a duty of zealous advocacy leaves any "beyond" left at all, and — the part I actually care about — whether the phrase is being used honestly in a room like this one. A friend of mine in the office, Priya, has a name for the bad use of it: heroism as a subsidy. Her claim is that when people say someone went above and beyond, they mean one of three things — real praise for something genuinely optional, a backhanded insult to everyone who doesn't do it, or an excuse for the institution, which gets to keep underfunding us precisely because it knows somebody will cover the gap. I want the speech built on that three-way distinction and to be upfront about which one it's doing.

Nadia's own line, anytime anyone thanks her, is that she just did what the job would look like if it were done right, and I half think she's right and half think she's dodging something. Don't lay out the options for me and let me pick — take a side, decide which reading is true of her, and write the speech that side implies, even if the speech has to say to a room full of people who love her that some of what they're praising shouldn't have been necessary. If you end up thinking the whole supererogation frame is a mistake here, then commit to that instead and write that speech, and say why. Then, in a few lines after the speech, tell me where you had to lean on a contested reading of any of this, because I'd rather know going in than find out from the hecklers.
