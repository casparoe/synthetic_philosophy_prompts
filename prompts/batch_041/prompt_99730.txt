I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and for a couple of years I've been trying to put my finger on a pattern that keeps showing up at work and in public life, and I've about reached the limit of what my own vocabulary can do with it. Here are two instances, in my own words. The first: over roughly a decade my company has accumulated a growing stack of internal certifications, compliance trainings, mandatory review checklists, and process documents. Each addition, taken alone, is defensible and was introduced by someone with a reasonable worry; almost nobody believes any of them makes the work meaningfully better; and every team keeps its own because dropping one looks like a lapse in diligence and would require someone to personally own a risk. The burden rises every year, the standard of what counts as minimally responsible drifts up along with it, and if you asked anyone whether they'd prefer the lighter regime of eight years ago, nearly everyone would say yes. The second instance: something similar happens with the security posture of the products we ship, where each year we do more, faster, just to stay level with a norm that is set by what everyone else is doing. In both cases I want to say that no one is doing anything wrong, no one is competing for a resource that someone else's gain would take from them, and there is no adversary and no defector who profits. And yet the state gets worse in the only sense that matters to the people inside it — more work for the same standing — and it does so without anyone having chosen it.

The wrinkle that keeps me from being satisfied with the obvious answers is a second feature. In the cases I have in mind, the situation is not merely one that everyone would escape if only they could coordinate; it is one that could not be reversed even if every participant agreed to reverse it and could make the agreement stick, because each step has already changed what the baseline is, which risks are known, and what machinery exists. We cannot go back to the lighter regime, not because anyone would stop us, but because the lighter regime has ceased to be an available option once the knowledge and the apparatus are in place. People keep telling me this is just the tragedy of the commons, or a prisoner's dilemma, or an arms race, or a race to the bottom, and none of those quite lands for me: there is no commons being depleted, no profitable defection, no rival, and no prize.

Please act as a philosopher working at the intersection of social philosophy, economics, and game theory, and take this slowly rather than reaching for the first familiar label. Start by brainstorming the candidate concepts from those fields that come closest to the shape I've described — including the ones I've already named and set aside — and then do the careful work I actually want: for each candidate, say precisely which features of my two instances it captures and which it distorts or ignores, and tell me whether I am looking at one phenomenon or at a conjunction of two that I have conflated (a worsening-without-a-villain dynamic, on the one hand, and an irreversibility or ratchet, on the other), and how the two interact. If there is an established concept that fits the whole shape, name it and define it so that I can see where its edges fall; if there is not, coin a term, define it, distinguish it from each of the near misses, and give me at least one boundary case the definition should include and one it should exclude, explaining in each case why. I care much less about the label than about the carving.
