I'm on the discipline committee at a mid-sized boarding school -- grades 9 through 12, about 400 kids. For the last three years we've run what we call the accountability circle: when a student is found to have done something serious (cheating, stealing, bullying, drinking on a trip), they don't get a detention. Instead they stand up at the Wednesday all-school meeting, describe in their own words what they did, a few students and one faculty member respond, and at the end the kid says something about how they'll make it right. The theory, which our head of student life brought back from some restorative-justice workshop, is that this produces real remorse -- the kid feels bad about the act and wants to repair it -- rather than just getting caught and punished. It works for some kids. But last month a 10th grader who plagiarized a paper stood up there, got about four sentences in, and fell apart; by the end she was sobbing, one of the seniors had said something pretty harsh, and now she eats lunch alone and two teachers say she's stopped talking in class. Meanwhile a kid who cheated on the same assignment two weeks earlier seemed to come out of it fine, actually better than before.

So the committee is split. Half of us think the circle is doing exactly what it should -- feeling bad is the point, and if some kids feel it harder that's not a bug. The other half thinks we're running a public shaming ritual with a nicer name and calling it moral education. Our school psychologist made a point that's stuck with me: she says you can't make someone feel guilt, you can only make them afraid of being exposed, and those are two totally differnt emotions with two totally different afterlives -- guilt makes you want to fix the thing, exposure makes you want to hide or get even -- so we aren't actually choosing guilt over shame, we're just not noticing which one we're getting. The dean disagrees and says of course a school can choose: cultures decide all the time which feelings are appropriate to have and which are appropriate to show, and shaping that is exactly what we're doing here.

I need to draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Thursdays meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- keep the circle as is, redesign it, or scrap it for something else -- with a timeline, and it should engage the strongest thing the losing side has going for it rather than just asserting our side. Please don't just tell me shame is bad; the interesting question is whether a place like ours can deliberately cultivate one emotion rather than the other, or whether that's wishful thinking, and if it can, what specifically about the circle would have to change. One more thing: our head of student life keeps invoking an idea from criminology that shame only does any good when it ends by pulling the person back into the group instead of marking them as an outsider for good, and I'd like the memo to say whether that distinction is real and, if so, how you'd tell which one our circle is actually producing before another kid falls apart.
