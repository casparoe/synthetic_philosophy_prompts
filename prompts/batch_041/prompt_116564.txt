I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend about drug policy, and we kept failing to disagree about the same thing. She works in public health and treats the Portuguese model as the benchmark; I am more concerned with civil liberties and the violence of black markets. We agreed that prohibition creates a violent black market and that criminal penalties for personal possession are disproportionate, but then everything splintered: whether the wrong in drug use is the use itself, the purchase from a violent market, the sale to minors, or the state's coercive response; whether the proper aim is reducing prevalence or reducing total harm; whether use is a liberty interest, a vice to be discouraged, or a disease to be treated; whether decriminalization is a matter of statute or police discretion, and whether it covers only possession or also tolerates small-scale sale; and whether legalization should allow branded, profit-seeking retail or only home cultivation, co-ops, medical access, or state stores, with or without expungement and licensing caps. By the end I could not tell whether we were prohibitionists, decriminalizers, or legalizers in different clothes.

I would like you to write me a decision tree that takes someone from those starting commitments to a named position. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: first the questions that actually separate the views, then the positions located in that space, including any combinations that no one occupies. Each branch node should be phrased as a concrete commitment a person could answer without already knowing which named position it leads to, so no node should simply ask whether one is for legalization. The tree should be fine-grained enough to distinguish, if they are indeed distinct, a Portugal-style public-health decriminalizer, a harm-reductionist who is indifferent between decriminalization and legalization, a libertarian legalizer, a commercial legalizer, a medicalizer, a state-monopoly legalizer, and a social-justice legalizer. Include at least eight and no more than twelve leaves.

For each leaf, give the position a name or mark it unnamed, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and the branch answers that lead there. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether the reason is that the views are extensionally equivalent, that the separating question is empirical rather than conceptual, or that the distinction collapses under pressure. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; assume no internet access and write from the logic of the positions. If you mention a real jurisdiction, use it only as a one-line illustration and do not lean on specific dates or statutes. The audience is an undergraduate policy seminar, so keep the prose clean, but do not simplify away the distinctions that made the argument interesting.
