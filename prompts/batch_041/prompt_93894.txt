I am on a working group drafting the standing rules for how a professional association's ethics committee reaches decisions. The committee rules on individual cases but also, in the same sitting, affirms the general principles it applies, so the agenda is logically connected: the principles entail verdicts on the cases, and a member's verdicts constrain which principles she can consistently affirm. Before we argue over rival procedures, I want the specification. Give me the desiderata any aggregation method must meet to count as adequate for us: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for instance, between aggregating members' final verdicts and aggregating the reasons behind them), and the results it must not contradict, each with the consideration that motivates it. Say which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp, and whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable; that last point interests me more than any scorecard of particular procedures. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to rule out otherwise attractive methods, and why. Write it as continuous prose in American English, the way you would write an email — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
