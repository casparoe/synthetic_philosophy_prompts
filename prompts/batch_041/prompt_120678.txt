ok so I want a parody. A deadpan product listing slash assembly leaflet for a home appliance that does the Banach–Tarski thing — you put a ball in, it gets cut into finitely many pieces, the pieces get moved around, and out come two balls the same size as the original. Invent the brand and the company, dont invent any maths. Around 600 words, laid out like an IKEA-style instruction sheet crossed with a warranty page: numbered steps, bold warnings, a small FAQ at the bottom, maybe a pull-quote from a customer.

The jokes have to come from the real content, so a set theorist laughs rather than winces:

- The pieces have no volume. They aren't Lebesgue measurable, so the leaflet cannot quote a weight, cannot price anything by volume, and the shipping and insurance sections have to squirm around it. Get the emphasis right: the point is not that a conservation law was broken, it's that the pieces don't have volumes at all, so the volume accounting just doesn't apply to them. Don't let the copywriter say matter got duplicated or created.
- Only rotations and translations are involved — no stretching, squashing or scaling — so "no tools required" is technically true, and you can write the step where the customer rotates a piece completely literally.
- It only works in three dimensions. On the plane and on the line you can't do it with finitely many pieces (there's a finitely additive invariant measure sitting around in low dimensions, and the group of motions there is too tame). So include the section on why there's no flat-pack version, and a warning against trying it on a pancake.
- The standard construction gets it done with as few as five pieces, which is a good number to put in the parts list.
- Nothing tells you which point goes where. The cut is non-constructive: the company cannot hand you a rule for it, cannot describe the pieces, cannot tell you which piece you're holding. Lean hard on that — "contents may vary", "we cannot tell you which one you'll recieve", a returns policy that can't identify the item being returned.
- It needs the full axiom of choice. Offer a budget model that only assumes dependent choice: it does everything else in the catalogue perfectly well and then quietly can't do this one job. If you know the sort of model in which every set of reals is measurable, use it as the warranty exclusion — void where all sets are measurable.
- Socks and shoes: picking one sock from each of infinitely many pairs needs the axiom of choice, doing it with shoes doesn't because you can always take the left one. Good material for an accessories section.
- Optional extras if you want them: an upsell based on the fact that any two bounded solids with nonempty interiors can be cut into the same finite number of congruent pieces (that's where the pea-and-Sun joke comes from), and a well-ordering attachment that puts any collection in order but can't tell you what the order is.

One short quote from a "concerned" employee: the compliance officer who only signs off on constructions that come with an actual rule. Keep him consistent — he isn't anti-choice, he just wants a definition before he puts his name on anything.

What's being sent up is the gap between "there exists one" and "here's how to build one" — the habit of treating the axiom of choice as a construction kit, and the way people use it all day without noticing and then act scandalised when it doubles a ball. No winking at the reader, no "obviously this is impossible", no physics jokes about mass appearing from nowhere, and don't stop to explain what the axiom of choice is — the leaflet's author wouldn't, and neither would I need it.
