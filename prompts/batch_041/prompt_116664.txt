For about three years now I have been caught in a recurring argument — it began in a reading group that a colleague and I run after work and has since migrated into dinners and group chats — over whether a nation can be guilty of a wrong committed generations ago, and whether the people alive now, who did none of it and in most cases were not yet born, can be said to bear that guilt. We are all engineers and scientists with no philosophical training, but the argument is not uninformed: each of us can state the other side's position in a way its holder recognizes as fair. Nothing has moved. One side holds that guilt is a predicate of persons: it attaches to an agent who performed an act, who intended or at least foresaw what it would do, and who could have done otherwise; it cannot attach to a nation, which is not an agent in the required sense, nor to a generation that never acted at all. On that view, saying that today's citizens are guilty is not a harsh claim but a category mistake, and whatever they may owe, if anything, is a debt or a duty of repair rather than guilt. The other side treats this as a dodge. The wrong, it says, is not over: its beneficiaries and its victims are both still here, and "guilt" in this context names a standing relation to an unjust order that one is part of and profits from, not a verdict on a past deed. On that view the demand to produce a culpable agent is a way of changing the subject, and the real question is not who did it but who is answerable now.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that we are running two arguments under a single word, and that the second is the one actually doing the work. The first is metaphysical: what kind of thing is a nation, does it persist through the complete replacement of its members, and is it an agent or merely a collection of them? I keep thinking of the company I work for — every employee and every shareholder may turn over, yet its contracts and debts survive and we all treat it as one continuing party — and I cannot tell whether the state is like that in the relevant respect or whether the analogy breaks somewhere that matters. The second argument is normative: what makes a person answerable for an arrangement she did not create, and whether benefit, membership, or mere participation is the ground of that answerability. My suspicion is that we have been treating a disagreement about the second as though it were a disagreement about the first, which is why the same exchanges keep recurring with no progress.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "guilt," a substantive disagreement about collective agency and identity over time, a substantive disagreement about the grounds of blame, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? Which of those is really doing the work here, and what in each side's position shows it? Is there a hidden question — a conceptual one about what must be true for a collective to act and to persist, or an empirical one about whether contemporary citizens really are the beneficiaries of the old wrong — that could be settled on its own and would then decide the rest? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress between us: a distinction, a reformulation of the question, a shift in who carries the burden of proof, or nothing at all because the question as posed is ill-formed? If it is ill-formed, tell me what we should be asking instead.
