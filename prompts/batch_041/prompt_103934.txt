I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of three years on a book whose entire armature is a single question: can one person ever know what is happening in another's mind, or can she only infer it? My protagonist is a hospital chaplain who must decide, without any test she trusts, whether the unresponsive patients she keeps vigil over are still 'in there' — and the book cannot end honestly until I know which side of the problem of other minds is true. Reading the literature, I find two camps talking past each other, and I want you to write the document they should have produced instead.

The two camps are these. First, the perceptualists: those who hold that in favorable cases we literally perceive, or quasi-perceive, another's mental state, so that our knowledge of other minds is not an inference at all. Second, the inferentialists: those who hold that whatever we know of another's mind we reach by inference — from analogy, from the best explanation of behavior, or by simulating the other within ourselves.

Draft their joint adversarial-collaboration statement. I want the claims both sides can sign — about what the dispute is, about what would count as a case of knowing, and about what neither side is committed to. I want the residual disagreements set out one by one, each phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording of its own view. I want the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and I expect one of them to be whether perception is being used literally or as a term of art, and another to be what an inner state that is behaviorally indistinguishable from a genuine one yet different would show. For each crux, give me a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. And tell me which parts of their quarrel are merely verbal.

Two cases from my own drafting, which I suspect both camps would accept as probative, so use them. The first is the consummate performer: a person who produces every behavioral and physiological signature of grief, flawlessly, with no grief — the understudy who has rehearsed a soul. The second is the inexpressive griever: a person who feels the loss fully and shows nothing of it. If the perceptualist can know the second and not the first, and the inferentialist can know neither, I want that difference written down in their words, not mine. A third case, from my trade rather than theirs, if it helps: when I set a character's interior on the page, my reader comes to know that mind more surely than she knows any living person's. Does that vindicate the perceptualist about fiction while leaving life an inference — or does it show that what we call knowing a mind is always a construction? Take a position on what it shows only if both camps could sign it.

Format the result as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table with a row per disagreement and a final row for the merely verbal ones.
