I sit on the algorithmic review committee of a county health system, and on Thursday we vote on whether to approve a vendor's tool that scores patients for referral into our intensive care-management program: 400 slots, twelve months of nurse-led follow-up, and roughly four times as many patients who stand to benefit as we can enroll. The score is calibrated within each of our two largest patient populations — a 0.30 carries the same meaning in both, a 30% chance of avoidable hospitalization within the year — and at our proposed threshold of 0.30, 45% of the patients we would flag in each population do in fact end up hospitalized. Our community advisory council has looked at the same numbers and wants the tool rejected: because baseline risk is about twice as high among our Black patients (22% against 11%), the false-positive rate at any single threshold is about twice as high for them, and the council will not accept a tool whose errors land that way. Our analyst has told the committee that both demands cannot be met — when base rates differ, a score that is calibrated within each group and error rates that are equal across groups cannot coexist — so what presents itself as a defect to be patched is a choice we have to make and defend. One member wants to split the difference with a lottery: flag at a lower threshold and draw the 400 slots by lot. The vendor, for its part, has offered to re-fit the score to predict who benefits from the program rather than who is at risk, if we think that helps.

Draft the opinion I will circulate before the vote: a recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with the threshold and the review date I should put to the committee, the reasoning, and the strongest version of the case I am giving up. We vote Thursday, so 'collect more data first' only counts if you also say what we run in the meantime. No citations needed; argue from the case in front of us.
