I need a thought experiment built to order, for an argument I am making about how the safety-versus-speed race in technology development is usually explained.

The standard explanation runs roughly as follows. Building safety into a system — extra review, monitoring, restricted deployment, capability held back in the name of caution — imposes a cost. Call that cost the alignment tax. Because developers compete with one another, whoever pays a larger tax than its rivals loses ground, so competitive pressure grinds the tax down to whatever the market will bear. Call that pressure the competitiveness constraint. My suspicion is that this explanation gives the tax a job that the competitive structure is actually doing. I want a case that separates the two, so that I can see which one is load-bearing.

Specifications:

1. Setting: realistic and contemporary. Two or more actors developing or deploying the same class of technology — competing firms, a firm against an open-source collective, two state-backed programmes, two hospital systems adopting the same diagnostic tool. No superintelligence, no rogue system, no exotic physics, and nothing that depends on a technology that does not exist. If an analogue from pharmaceuticals, nuclear power, or platform software separates the variables more cleanly than an AI example, use it.
2. No villains. Every actor is competent, informed, and would prefer the safe outcome if it could have that outcome without losing the race. No one acts out of recklessness, ideology, ignorance, or malice.
3. The safety practice must be free to the actor that adopts it, in every engineering sense: no loss of capability, no extra money, no extra time, no added complexity. Adopting it must leave the adopter exactly as fast, cheap, and capable as an actor that skips it.
4. Despite (3), the race must still select against adoption, and the outcome must be worse along the dimension the actors themselves care about. They end up worse off by their own lights, not merely by mine.
5. The mechanism must be structural: a feature of how the competition is set up, not a mistake, not a coordination failure that any single actor could fix unilaterally, and not something a slightly cleverer actor would see through. Each actor's choice must be rational given what it can know and what the others are doing.
6. One paragraph of scenario. Then the analysis.

The analysis should contain:

- The causal chain: which actor does what, under which constraint, and at which link the free safety practice gets dropped. Show explicitly that no link in the chain depends on the tax being positive.
- A name for the single feature that does the work.
- Two ablations. (a) Remove that feature, holding everything else fixed: does the race survive, or does the case collapse into an ordinary coordination problem with an obvious solution? (b) Restore the feature, but now make the safety practice genuinely costly in capability, money, or time: does the outcome change in kind or only in degree? I want to know whether the tax and the structure are substitutes, complements, or whether the tax is idle in the explanation.
- An honest note on the obvious counter-move: my distinction may be merely verbal, since any disadvantage a cautious actor suffers — including a market it forgoes — might just be a tax, in which case the case shows nothing. Say whether that response rescues the tax-centred explanation or concedes that the tax is not a technical fact about safety but a fact about the market. Also say which feature of my construction is doing work I did not intend, and whether the case genuinely separates the two explanations or only appears to.
- If the specifications cannot all be satisfied at once, name the two that conflict and why, rather than quietly relaxing one.

This is for an argument that the tax framing mislocates the problem, so a case that reaches the desired conclusion by stipulating that safety is costly, or by giving some actor a reason to be reckless, does not count as answering me.

End with a final line of the form Verdict: <short phrase>, naming the feature that does the work, in no more than ten words.
