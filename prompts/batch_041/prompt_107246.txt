So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got a character in my current book who's one of those political-theory types — the kind who thinks the whole problem with democracy is that it counts your vote the same as everyone else's no matter how little you actually know. I wanted to hand her a real argument, something that sounds airtight when she says it at a dinner party, and I ended up building one myself and now it's gotten away from me, because it seems to prove way more than she'd ever sign her name to. Here it is, cleaned up a little:

(1) A political decision should be made by the people who are most likely to get it right.
(2) Getting a decision right means tracking the facts and the values that bear on it.
(3) On any given issue, some people track those things more reliably than others.
(4) So on any given issue, the people who track it most reliably should have the greater say.
(5) But the people who are most reliable on, say, monetary policy aren't the same people who are most reliable on criminal sentencing.
(6) So the weight each person's voice carries should be re-set issue by issue.
(7) Now the rule for setting those weights — the meta-rule — is itself a decision, and some people are more reliable about that than others.
(8) So the meta-rule should be set by whoever is most reliable about meta-rules.
(9) But being reliable about meta-rules is just another kind of competence, and presumably there's a fact of the matter about who's most competent overall, across the board.
(10) So there's a group whose say ought to be greatest on every issue — the globally competent.
(11) And if the gap is big enough, everyone else's say should be zero.
(12) Which gives you a permanent ruling class, picked by a test nobody consented to — exactly what my character swears she's against.
(13) So (1) is false.

I can't tell whether I've smuggled something in along the way or whether the thing is actually valid and one of the early steps just has to go. My gut says defend (1) and (3) to the death — they feel almost definitional to me — and sacrifice whatever else it takes. So: where does it break, and if I give up the step you name, what am I actually paying for it? Walk me through the alternatives and their price tags. No reading list please, I just want the argument taken apart.
