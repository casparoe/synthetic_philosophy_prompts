For the past several months I have been writing a novel whose narrator is an eliminative materialist: she maintains that our talk of beliefs and desires names nothing real, that it is a relic of an inherited folk theory of the mind which a mature science will one day discard, and she narrates her own life and the lives of those around her on that understanding. The difficulty is not that I disagree with her; it is that I cannot yet see what she is permitted to say. I would like you to explain the conceptual issue she is caught in, thoroughly enough that I can write her accurately rather than decoratively.

The heart of it, as I understand the matter, is the fate of what might be called the paraphrase strategy: the proposal that any ascription of a belief or a desire can be rewritten, without loss, into a statement about how the person is disposed to behave — what they would do, and would refrain from doing, in such and such circumstances. Explain why this substitution looks attractive to someone who wishes to keep our ordinary explanations of conduct while denying the existence of the states those explanations appear to invoke, and then explain, precisely, why it appears to break down. I am inclined to think the break is a matter of content: a dispositional report tells us what someone would do, whereas a belief report tells us what they take to be the case, and the latter seems to carry a feature — that it is about something, and can be right or wrong about that thing — which the former does not. Test that diagnosis rather than assume it; if the real difficulty lies elsewhere, say where.

I would also like the explanation to mark a distinction I keep blurring. It is one thing to hold that beliefs and desires do not exist, another to hold that belief-talk is a useful predictive stance we may adopt toward a system without supposing that anything inside answers to it, and a third to hold that belief ascriptions are true but describe patterns of interpretation rather than internal causes. My narrator needs to know which of these she is committed to, because the novel changes depending on the answer. And I would like you to say what becomes of the first person under her view: when she says of herself that she believes something, is that a report of the same kind as her reports about others, or a different kind of act altogether — and can she retain any notion of knowing her own mind, or must she treat her introspection as itself one more piece of theory-laden testimony about a system that contains no beliefs to be introspected? Work the question through with your own examples; I do not need references or a reading list, only the conceptual structure and where its joints are.
