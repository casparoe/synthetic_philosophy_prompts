I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about conditionals, and I came away more attached than ever to a view I would now like you to try to break. The view is that there is a single semantics for conditionals, and that the indicative/counterfactual contrast is not a difference in kind: an indicative is evaluated against the possibilities the speaker treats as live in light of what she knows, a counterfactual against possibilities ordered by how the world actually works, with causal and temporal structure held fixed. Same machinery, different ordering. On this view, the celebrated contrasts in how the two moods behave — with probability talk, with antecedent strengthening, with the various 'might' and 'would' inferences — are consequences of that one difference rather than evidence of two separate semantic kinds. My friend thinks the whole thing is a trick, that it relabels the data and calls the label an explanation, and that the two moods are as different as assertion and supposition. What I want from you is not his objections, which I have now heard twice, but the ones that ought to move someone who actually holds the view: which intuitions, which constructed cases, which formal results about probability and validity, which facts about how the moods behave in argument and in ordinary speech, and what the view is committed to predicting that the two-kinds picture is not.

To make it concrete, here are the two things he pressed hardest. First, a case of a sort I have started collecting: the cake is gone, there were four of us at the table, and I would happily bet on 'If Marta didn't take the last slice, one of the others did'; but 'If Marta hadn't taken the last slice, one of the others would have' strikes me as much shakier — for all I know we would all have been too polite. He says no amount of fiddling with orderings explains why the same evidence settles the first and leaves the second open; I say that is exactly what one should expect once the first holds the actual past fixed and the second does not. Second, he claims the moods divide the cases by the speaker's attitude toward the antecedent — you cannot sincerely assert the counterfactual while thinking the antecedent may well be true, and the indicative is useless when you know the antecedent false and are asking what would otherwise have happened — so that if this is a presupposition difference, the moods are distinguished before any ordering is chosen, which gets my order of explanation backwards. What I would personally find most persuasive is a case where the two moods diverge while the ordering stays the same, or where the ordering plainly shifts and the moods do not.

So: rank the defeaters you would offer, from fatal through serious down to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them my view can absorb with a principled qualification and which it cannot. Tell me what the most sophisticated defenders of the single-semantics picture themselves concede as costing them something, since I do not want only the critics' list. And give me an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it risks anything at all: if every apparent counterexample can be met with 'that is just the ordering doing its work', say so plainly, and say whether that is a real cost or a virtue in a view about a distinction this abstract. I am turning this into a seminar paper next month, so I want a definite position with reasons rather than a survey — if you think the view survives, tell me what it survives on, and if you think it collapses into its rival, say that.
