**Fog, Not Dice: why i think consequentialism is clueless, and my fix**

Consequentialism is the view that whether an action is right depends only on its consequences. Utilitarianism is the most famous version of it, and honestly i think people use the two words for the same thing most of the time, with utilitarianism just being the version where good consequences means happiness.

The view sounds clean until you try to actually do it. Heres my example, which i call the Smoothie Problem. I have six dollars. Option A: buy a smoothie. Option B: donate it. A consequentialist says compare the consequences of A and B and pick the better one. Fine. But the consequences of buying a smoothie dont stop at me being happy for twenty minutes. The shop gets six dollars, so the owners month changes slightly, so maybe she orders one more crate of mangoes, so a truck takes a different route, so someone misses a bus. And i talk to my friend differently because im in a good mood, so her day changes, so her exam goes differently, so her whole life goes differently. And every one of those changes has its own downstream effects, forever. The butterfly effect means the consequences of anything are basically infinite. So both options have infinite consequences, and if you add up infinite good and infinite bad on both sides you get infinity on both sides, which means the two options tie. If every option ties, consequentialism cant tell me anything. That seems like a real problem and i dont think its just me being dumb about math.

But wait — i dont think thats the interesting version of the problem. So i came up with a distinction. There are two ways to not know what you're doing.

The first is like dice. You know the possible outcomes and their odds, you just dont know how the roll went. Thats ordinary uncertainty and decision theory handles it: multiply each outcome by its probability and add.

The second is like fog. You dont even know the shape of the outcome space. You dont know how many sides the die has, or whether the thing in front of you is a die at all. I think this is what people mean when they say consequentialism is clueless — i believe the word comes from a paper by James Lenman in Philosophy and Public Affairs around 2000, though i might be getting the details wrong.

Then i split the fog in two. Shallow fog is when you cant trace the local ripple but the ripples are small and random, so they mostly cancel out. My mangoes-and-buses stuff is shallow fog — its fine, it washes out, thats basically the law of averages. Deep fog is when the missing knowledge is structural and doesnt wash out: does my choice make the long-run future better or worse? Does buying this smoothie make some dangerous technology arrive three days earlier or three days later? Those arent small random ripples, theyre a coin flip where i cant even see the coin.

Deep fog has two sub-problems that i made up names for.

The sign problem: even if i could guess the size of the far-future effect, i usually cant guess its sign. Is it good or bad that the technology arrived sooner? Alot of the time the honest answer is no idea, and no idea isnt a number.

The mirror problem: this is the one i think is underexplored. If my action really does change the far future a lot, then it changes which people get born. So comparing my action to not-my-action isnt comparing two outcomes for the same people, its comparing two different sets of people. On a person-affecting view (where only actual peoples interests count), all those different people dont weigh anything against each other, so the comparison kind of collapses. So deep fog and the non-identity stuff are the same problem wearing two hats, or at least theyre friends.

My fix, which i call Robustness First: dont maximize expected value, because there isnt one. Instead, describe the scenarios you can actually articulate, and pick the action whose worst plausible outcome is the least bad. On top of that, use an epistemic humility discount: weight well-understood near-term consequences more heavily than far-off ones — not because far-off people matter less, but because my knowledge of them is much worse, so the same dollar of expected good is less trustworthy there.

I know the objection: isnt this just rule consequentialism, or satisficing, or being a deontologist with extra steps? My answer is that my rules arent fixed, they get re-derived each time, so its not a rule-based view.

Conclusion: a teacher told me maybe consequentialism is a standard of rightness and not a decision procedure, so its fine that it doesnt tell me what to do. But if the standard cant be applied even in principle, im not sure what its doing. Thats the part i want help with.
