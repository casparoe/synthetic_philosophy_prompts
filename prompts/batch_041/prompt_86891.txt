I am a teaching assistant in an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am marking the final examination. The examination consisted of a single essay question worth 100 points, divided as indicated. The question and one student's answer are reproduced below. Please grade the answer.

Examination question

In this course I have argued that the problem raised in Benacerraf's "What Numbers Could Not Be" and the dilemma posed in "Mathematical Truth" are two faces of one problem, so that a satisfactory treatment of either would automatically be a satisfactory treatment of the other. Assess that claim.

(a) (30 points) Reconstruct the dilemma as a conflict between two constraints on an adequate account of mathematical truth. Identify each constraint, say what motivates it, and say what is at stake in abandoning it.

(b) (30 points) Set out the argument of the earlier paper and the conclusion drawn from it, making explicit the role played by competing set-theoretic constructions of the natural numbers.

(c) (40 points) Test my claim that the two problems are one against the structuralist reading of the earlier argument. If numbers are positions in structures rather than objects, does the pressure of the dilemma ease? Does the claim survive?

Student's answer

(a) The dilemma arises because we want two things from an account of mathematics that turn out to pull against each other. The first is a semantic constraint. Mathematical sentences such as "3 is prime" are true or false, and on the standard Tarskian treatment a sentence of that form is true just in case the object denoted by "3" has the property expressed by the predicate. So if mathematical sentences are to have the truth values we take them to have, their singular terms must genuinely refer, which requires abstract objects for them to refer to. The second is an epistemic constraint. An account of mathematical knowledge must fit our best general epistemology, and knowledge of an object requires some suitable connection between the knower and the object known. Abstract objects are by definition outside space and time and causally inert, so no such connection is available. Neither constraint is a free-floating preference: the semantic one is forced on us by the fact that we reason with mathematical sentences as true, and the epistemic one is forced on us by the fact that mathematics is a paradigm of knowledge and cannot be treated as a lucky guess. Since the causal requirement is a condition on knowledge as such and not something we are at liberty to relax, and since we cannot give up the truth of mathematics, the only way out is to deny that numbers are the abstract objects the semantics appears to require. The dilemma is therefore not a tension we can simply live with: it shows that platonism cannot be right, and it is what leads Benacerraf to the structuralist conclusion of the earlier paper.

(b) In the earlier paper Benacerraf observes that arithmetic can be reconstructed in set theory in more than one way. We may take 0 to be the empty set and let the successor of n be {n}, giving the Zermelo numerals, or we may let the successor of n be n ∪ {n}, giving the von Neumann numerals. Both constructions satisfy the Peano axioms and yield all of arithmetic, but the two are not isomorphic to one another: in the von Neumann construction every number is the set of its predecessors, and in the Zermelo construction it is not. They also give different answers to questions about the numbers themselves, for instance whether 3 is a member of 17, while agreeing that 17 is a set with seventeen members. Since arithmetic itself contains nothing that could favour one construction over the other, there is no fact of the matter as to which sets the numbers are. Benacerraf concludes that numbers are not sets at all, and indeed not objects at all: they are positions in structures, and everything true of them follows from their place in the progression. What arithmetic studies is the structure, not the things that occupy its places.

(c) If the structuralist reading of the earlier paper is right, then the two problems really are one, and the course's claim is vindicated. The dilemma depended on numbers being objects of a distinctive kind: abstract items that our terms must refer to and that are nevertheless causally insulated from us. But if numbers are positions in structures, there is no realm of objects standing behind the numerals for our terms to latch onto, and no causal gap to cross. To know that 2 + 2 = 4 is to know a fact about the natural-number structure, and we grasp that structure by grasping the relations that constitute it, not by being causally affected by 2 or by 4. The semantic constraint and the epistemic constraint were both constraints on our relation to objects; once the objects are replaced by positions, the two constraints collapse into one another, and there is nothing left for the dilemma to be a dilemma about. So the claim survives: a solution to the earlier problem is automatically a solution to the later one.
