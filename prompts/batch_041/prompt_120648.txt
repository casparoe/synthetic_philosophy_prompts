I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-five years in industrial corrosion testing, mostly pipelines and heat exchangers — and since I packed it in I've been working my way through the classics of philosphy of science, which is slow going because I never read any of this at university. I've got stuck on a bit of Bacon's New Organon, the second book, the long catalogue where he sorts experiments into all these special kinds he calls prerogative instances. One of the kinds he likens to a signpost at a fork in the road; the translation I've got calls them instances of the fingerpost, and I think I've seen crossroads instances in another version. I'll give you the gist in my own words rather than risk mangling his actual sentences, because the prose is four hundred years old and I don't trust myself to paraphrase it cleanly: the idea seems to be that some experiments are set up so that when you're torn between two ways of explaining the same phenomenon, the result points you down one road or the other — like a fingerpost, it settles which way to go rather than just adding another data point. What I can't work out is what exactly he thinks this settles. A real signpost at a real crossroads doesn't establish which town lies where; it only helps you if you already know where you're trying to get to and you're merely unsure of the route. So if the experiment is doing the pointing, where does the destination come from? And I have a feeling he says somewhere that you only get to use these instances late, once the rest of the inquiry is more or less finished, not at the start to find the road at all — is that right, or am I reading Popper back into him? Popper is the only one of these authors I'd actually heard of before I started, so I'm probably pattern-matching.

Here's why it's bugging me rather than just being an antique curiosity. For most of my career the interesting question was never whether an experiment gave a clean yes or no, it was whether you could trust the rig, and my own field is full of cases where two rival mechanisms — say, a purely electrochemical cracking route versus a hydrogen-embrittlement route — predict the same crack-growth signature in the standard test and only diverge under some odd slow-strain-rate condition nobody runs by default. When we finally ran that odd condition and the two predictions came apart, I'd have called it a decisive test; but a colleague of mine always insisted it wasn't decisive at all, because you could always blame the specimen prep, or the environment control, or some assumption in how you convert crack length into stress intensity, and keep whichever mechanism you liked. I gather philosophers have made a whole meal out of that objection. So what I want from you is help actually understanding Bacon here, not a reading list: what the fingerpost instances are supposed to do that ordinary experiments don't, whether he really reserves them for the end of an inquiry, whether his signpost is the same animal as the later notion of a crucial experiment, and whether the thing survives the objection that a bad result can always be absorbed by blaming a background assumption. If the honest answer is that Bacon means something much weaker than the nineteenth-century phrase suggests, I'd rather hear that than a tidy synthesis.
