Ok so I've been going back and forth on this for a week and I've given up trying to settle it myself. Bit of context, I'm doing an MA in politics part time while working at a housing charity, so I'm perma-tired, and after the constitutional law seminar a few of us went for a drink. Mira is a PhD student working on the royal prerogative and thinks the crown is a genuinely useful piece of machinery; Tom works for a think tank that campaigns to abolish the monarchy, so you can imagine the vibe. What I can't get past is that they were both making real points and I couldn't tell who was winning. Roughly how it went:

(1) Mira: the whole point of a hereditary monarch is that it's the one office in the system with no programme and no voters to court, so it's the only actor who can make the call nobody else can make without looking self-interested — a hung parliament, a PM who's lost the House but won't go. And the fact that she's never actually had to make that call is a sign the thing is working, like an unused fire exit. (2) Tom: an unused fire exit is indistinguishable from a painted-over door, and it's not unused anyway — 1975, an unelected Governor-General sacked an elected PM who still commanded the House of Representatives, because the Senate blocked supply. No appeal, no court, no remedy; the only comeback was an election weeks later. Mira said that IS the remedy and it worked, the system bent but didn't break. Tom said that just means the voters clean up afterwards, and for those weeks the country was run by a man nobody elected, doing a favour for the man who then won the election. (3) Mira: compare the alternatives. A directly elected president has a mandate of his own and can deadlock with the legislature for years; the ceremonial version is safer. Tom: then heredity is doing no work at all, because everything you've said is satisfied by a president elected by a big supermajority of the parliament, like Germany or Italy, and you'd lose the dynasty, the money and the bowing. Mira: no, an elected president is a politician who owes somebody something, and the point of the crown is that the holder can't have campaigned for the job. Tom: that's an argument for choosing the referee by lottery, not by birth — on your own reasoning a lottery is better, it removes the campaign and the dynasty. (4) Mira: what birth buys you that a lottery doesn't is forty years of training and no incentive to build anything, because there's nothing to build; the job ends when you die and your kid inherits the furniture. You can't run a constitutional backstop on a dice roll. Tom: then the value of the institution rests on the character and judgement of the person holding it, which is the same move people make when they say the king saved democracy in Norway in 1940 by refusing to appoint Quisling's government and saying he'd abdicate instead — and if it all depends on getting a brave, sensible person, it's a lottery anyway, just a hereditary one, which is worse. Mira: that's not a lucky draw, that's the convention working. The cabinet was minded to cave to the German ultimatum, the King said he'd abdicate rather than appoint Quisling, and the cabinet backed down — the elected government changed its own mind, nobody was overruled, the crown just forced a pause. Tom: a pause backed by the threat of abdication is exactly the personal discretion you said wasn't there. (5) Tom: and when the government won't change its mind you get the Belgian business in 1990 — the king couldn't sign the abortion law, so the government declared him unable to reign, took his powers for a day, signed the law in the name of the people, and put him back on the throne the next day. Everyone pretended nothing had happened. If the office is as harmless as you say, why does the constitution have to lie to protect it? Mira: that's not a lie, it's a valve — the law went through, the king didn't have to act against his conscience, and the fiction is the price of having a head of state whose conscience is allowed to matter at all. Tom: the price is that one citizen's conscience gets a procedural veto that nobody else's does, and it gets accommodated by declaring him too ill to do his job; I'd rather have a head of state with no conscience to protect. (6) Mira, last word: look at 2019, when Johnson prorogued parliament for five weeks and the Supreme Court said it was unlawful and void. The Queen did nothing wrong, she took advice, and the blame landed entirely on the PM — she's the one actor who can be used without being blamed. In a republic that fight is between two elected people and it gets much uglier. Tom: the blame landing on the adviser is the problem, not the point. The crown got used as an instrument, a court then said the instrument shouldn't have been used, and the crown walked away with clean hands. That's not a safeguard, it's a way of laundering the exercise of power through an office nobody's allowed to criticise.

I wasn't on either side, I really couldn't referee it, and I'd rather not just be told to read a load of books — I can find the literature myself. What I want is for you to go through it move by move and say who's right about each one, rating every move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, then say who has the better of the argument overall, and finally what both of them are missing — I have a nagging feeling there's a distinction neither of them has that would clear up about half of this, but I can't name it. Please don't be diplomatic about it; if one of them is just wrong I'd rather know.
