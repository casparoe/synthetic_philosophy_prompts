I'm putting together a few poems to close out a grad seminar on Victorian agnosticism, and I want one on Henry Sidgwick. Here's the ask: a short terza rima, say 18 to 21 lines, iambic pentameter, the aba bcb cdc chain, spoken in Sidgwick's own voice at a seance sometime in the 1880s. He's the guy who resigned his Trinity fellowship in 1869 because he couldn't in good conscience keep declaring himself a member of the Church of England, and then spent the last stretch of his life as the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, hunting for evidence that the dead survive. I want the poem sitting right inside that contradiction — the same honesty that cost him the fellowship is what makes him a terrible witness at a seance, since he can't stop auditing every knock and table-tip, and each scrap of evidence he gets only commits him to needing the next one.

The form has to carry that, not just the words. Terza rima is the whole point: it's rhyme scheme never lets a rhyme rest — each stanza's middle sound hands the obligation to the next stanza — so the poem should feel like a chain of debts, one proof forever owing the next. Then when you close it off (a couplet, or just a single line, your call), it should either land the proof or fail to, and I want a reader who knows nothing about him to feel which one it is. Don't let him buy a neat little irony about it either; he seems to have been pretty miserable about the whole business.

Accuracy matters, a Victorianist in the room shouldn't wince. No fake quotations from real people and no invented dates.
