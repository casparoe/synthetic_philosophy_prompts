I'm giving a paper at a two-day workshop on the law and ethics of armed conflict next month -- 25 minute talk, 25 minutes of Q&A, and it's the Q&A I'm worred about. The room is mostly international humanitarian law people: a few academic IHL scholars, a couple of former military legal advisors (JAG types), one historian of the laws of war, two philosophers who are both basically revisionists in the McMahan mold, and someone from a humanitarian NGO who will probably be the sharpest person there. Nobody in that room needs me to explain Walzer vs. the revisionists; they've all heard it and most of them are sick of it.

Here's what I'm defending. Moral equality of combatants, but not Walzer's version and not the deflated "they're all excused" version either. I want to say combatants on both sides have an actual permission -- a liberty-right, not merely an excuse -- to use lethal force against each other, and that the permission is symmetric even when one side's war is plainly unjust. The ground of it is what I've been calling reciprocal vulnerability, which has three parts: (1) each side's fighters are exposed to the other side's lethal force; (2) neither side's fighters have reliable access to the facts that would fix the justice of the other side's cause, or often of their own; (3) both are under the same kind of discipline and coercion that makes walking away effectively impossible. Where those three hold symmetrically, I say the permission is symmetric, and asymmetries in jus ad bellum don't reach down to the tactical level.

I do keep permission and impunity apart: I'm not saying aggressor soldiers can't be prosecuted, and I lean on the fact that aggression is generally treated in international law as a leadership crime anyway.

I'm dimly aware that condition (2) is doing alot of work in the argument, and that if it's doing all of it, I've reinvented the excused-culpability view with extra steps. But I don't know whether that's the objection that will actually land in this room.

Order them by how much damage they do, say who's likely to raise each, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, give me a two-minute reply for each, and flag where each reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks. Also tell me which objection, if any, I should preempt in the talk itself rather than wait for.

Skip the citations and reading lists -- everyone in that room knows the literature better than I do, and a list of names would just be padding. American English please.
