A colleague in my political philosophy seminar has been pressing an argument I find slippery, and I would rather respond to it at the level of its central analogy than trade intuitions with him.

His argument is this. The interminable dispute about whether a nation can be guilty, and whether guilt can be inherited, is muddled because it conflates two things that the law of estates keeps cleanly apart: culpability, which is personal, non-transferable, and dies with the wrongdoer, and debt, which attaches to an estate and is serviced by whoever comes into possession of it. What a state does to a people should be modeled as the second of these: an obligation that the political community incurred, that survives the deaths of the individuals who incurred it, and that the present generation takes on along with the territory, the institutions, and the accumulated benefits. On this picture, the question whether today's citizens are guilty is simply the wrong question, and the answer to the right one, concerning what is owed and by whom, does not depend on their conduct or their attitudes at all. He draws a practical conclusion from this: that material reparations are owed by whichever political community now holds the assets, that the obligation is untouched by whether the present citizens disapprove of what was done, and that the whole vocabulary of guilt and inherited shame should be dropped as a distraction.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than an endorsement or rejection of the conclusion, in three parts.

First, the mapping. Give me a table of correspondences in which every element of the source — creditor, debtor, principal, interest, the estate, its executor, the assets, the transfer of assets, default, limitation periods, discharge, and the assignment of the claim to a third party — is paired with whatever the argument needs it to be, and flag any element that has no plausible counterpart in the target at all.

Second, the disanalogies, separated into those that bear on his intended use and those that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the debt picture quietly imports: a determinate sum, a single currency, alienability, discharge by payment, a bearer whose identity is not itself in dispute, and a ledger already settled as to who owes what to whom. Where the target lacks these features, does the conclusion suffer, or only the rhetoric?

Third, the refinements the analogy invites, which I would like considered individually rather than waved at: liability without fault, and the doctrines under which a successor entity does or does not take on its predecessor's obligations; tracing and knowing receipt in the law of restitution, where the claim runs against the asset rather than the person, is capped at the value received, and can be defeated by a change of position or by purchase in good faith; the doctrine permitting a successor state to repudiate debts incurred by a predecessor regime without the people's consent and not for their benefit; and the fact that guilt has no analogue of limitation, assignment, or bankruptcy. Say for each whether it strengthens his case, embarrasses it, or cuts both ways.

Close by telling me whether the practical conclusion survives, and if it survives only in weakened form, exactly what has been weakened — the amount owed, the reason it is owed, the identity of those who owe it, or the claim that the debt idiom can dispense altogether with any notion of responsibility for what one did not do. If a better analogy is available, name it and say what it buys and what it costs. I would also like to know whether the debt idiom genuinely bypasses Jaspers's fourfold distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, or whether it merely relocates one of those categories under a new name.
