I've been reading, mostly out of a slightly uncomfortable self-recognition, about akrasia and hyperbolic discounting, and what I actually want is a poem rather than an explanation, because the thing I keep failing to explain is precisely how my own resolves behave once time starts moving through them. My understanding is that a hyperbolic discounter is not merely weak: when the smaller-sooner and the larger-later reward are both still far off, I genuinely prefer the larger, and then as the smaller-sooner approaches, the preference reverses, and the reversal is not a change of mind I can locate or defend but a sliding of the whole curve beneath my feet. And akrasia, as I take it, is not just losing to temptation but acting against my own all-things-considered judgment about what is best, so the interesting failure is the one where I know better at the exact moment I do worse. The only mental model I have for this is a state machine that keeps re-entering the same state, and I'd like the poem to make a reader feel that structure rather than be told about it.

Could you write it as a pantoum? The form is the reason I'm asking: I want the repeating lines to come back in new positions and mean something different each time they return, so that a line which is a vow in the first stanza has become a rueful quotation of itself by the third, and by the last stanza the resolve has been recycled into the very thing it was supposed to prevent. The pantoum's convention of folding the opening line back in as the final line matters to me here — I want the poem to end where it began, so that it is literally self-defeating, the way the loop is. Please keep it honest about two things someone who knows this literature would check. First, the reversal has to read as an actual reversal of preference over time and not merely a failure of nerve or a sudden craving. Second, I'd rather the poem not resolve into the tidy moral that precommitment fixes everything, since the decision to bind myself is made by the very self whose preferences are sliding; I'd rather it end still caught in the loop than hand over a remedy.

Four or five quatrains should be enough. It should be a real poem first and a lesson second — good enough that someone who has never heard of time-inconsistent preferences still finds it true, and precise enough that someone who has doesn't wince at what the form is being made to carry.
