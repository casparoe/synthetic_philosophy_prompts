Next month I am giving a forty-minute talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on culture, coordination, and compliance, and I want to walk into the question period already knowing what is coming. The room will hold roughly the following people, and I know most of them by reputation.

Two cultural psychologists whose work is on measuring how strictly different societies enforce their norms. They have spent years defending their instruments against the charge that they mostly track wealth, threat, and state capacity, and they are protective of the construct as a whole. One anthropologist who has done long fieldwork on everyday rule-following in a place where several of my examples are simply the local customs, and who has no patience for armchair taxonomy. Three legal and political theorists who care about legitimacy, jurisdiction, and the gap between a norm being real and its being enforceable. And one institutional economist who suspects that most questions about norms are really questions about monitoring and enforcement costs.

My thesis, in outline. The familiar framing of tight versus loose cultures treats a society's willingness to enforce its norms as a single dial, turned up or down, and I think that picture is a mistake. Societies are not more or less demanding; they are differently sorted. The sorting I want to defend divides conventions into two kinds. Load-bearing conventions are those whose entire value lies in everyone doing the same thing: several arrangements would work equally well, and what the convention delivers is predictability and mutual expectation. With those, a society may demand strict compliance, may enforce against visitors, and may treat deviation as a genuine fault, because the harm deviation does is to erode the very thing the convention exists to supply. Expressive conventions are those whose value lies not in the sameness but in what compliance says about who one is, where one belongs, and what one owes; there a society may at most invite and never demand. The test for sorting them is counterfactual. Imagine a different convention universally adopted: if everyone's capacity to act and to predict is unaffected, the convention is load-bearing; if the loss appears only when some people do it differently, so that the convention's work is done by the fact of who complies rather than by the fact of compliance, it is expressive. Queue order and the direction of foot traffic come out load-bearing. Tipping, dress, and forms of address do not. Jaywalking is the case I find hardest, which is why I use it. I am aware that the counterfactual test is the weak joint of the argument, and I would rather learn that now than from the floor.

Prepare me a Q&A sheet. I want the six or seven objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a philosophy seminar, ordered by how much damage each would do if it landed, with a note on how likely it is. Phrase each one the way that particular audience member would phrase it, in their own disciplinary idiom: I want to hear the construct-validity version, the fieldwork version, the legitimacy version, and the enforcement-cost version, and to see which of them are really one objection wearing different clothes. For each, tell me what to concede up front, give me a reply that fits in two minutes, and add an honest note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the session, and give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations or reading lists; argue from the positions as I have described them, and where an objection depends on a claim I have not supplied, state the claim rather than attributing it to a literature.
