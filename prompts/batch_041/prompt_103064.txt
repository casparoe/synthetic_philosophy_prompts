Write an essay on what the lamp puzzle establishes about supertasks — tasks consisting of infinitely many steps completed in a finite span of time — and defend a verdict.

The case to work with: a lamp is off at t = 0 and is flipped once in each of the intervals (1 − 2^−n, 1 − 2^−(n+1)), so that it is switched on at t = 1/2, off at t = 3/4, on at t = 7/8, and so on, with infinitely many flips completed before t = 1. Ask what the lamp's state is at t = 1.

Do not spend the essay rehearsing the familiar standoff between the demand that the lamp be either on or off and the reply that the description fits both states equally well, so that there is no fact of the matter. Instead, organize the essay around three variants and use them to decide what the original case shows.

(i) The binary lamp as stated: the only thing fixed is that the switch's position differs after each flip. Nothing is said about what carries the switch from one position to the other, about its position between flips, or about what happens at t = 1 itself.

(ii) A dial whose reading is a real-valued angle replaces the switch. Starting at 0 degrees at t = 0, it turns at a constant rate of half a turn per unit time, so that at t = 1 − 2^−n its reading is 180(1 − 2^−n) degrees. The same times are marked, but nothing happens at them. The question about the dial's reading at t = 1 has an answer that continuity settles, and any on/off question defined by a threshold other than one exactly equal to 180 degrees inherits that answer.

(iii) A dial whose successive increments are 180, −90, +45, −22.5, ... degrees, the direction reversing at every step and the total converging to 120 degrees.

Among the diagnoses to weigh: that the original case shows only that a specification fixing the states at the flip times does not fix the limiting state, so that indeterminacy here is a property of an incomplete specification rather than of the world; that it shows supertasks to be physically impossible, since no mechanism can execute infinitely many discontinuous transitions in finite time; and that it isolates a fact about state spaces — a two-valued variable driven by non-vanishing increments cannot have a limit, whereas a continuous variable can, so the difficulty lies in the discontinuity rather than in the infinity. A ball bouncing with geometrically decreasing heights, which comes to rest after infinitely many bounces, may help you.

The essay should say what it takes a determinate outcome to be — a fact of the matter about the lamp, a value fixed by the physics, or a value fixed by a complete description — and should say whether the puzzle's indeterminacy survives if the process is described from t = 1 backwards. Reach a verdict on what the lamp shows, and give the strongest reading of the case against your verdict a fair hearing before rejecting it.
