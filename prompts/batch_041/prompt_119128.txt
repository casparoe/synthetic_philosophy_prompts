I've been trying to figure out what I actually beleive about discounting the future, going by my gut on cases, and I keep landing in contradictions. Here's my list.

- I'd rather have a good dinner tonight than a slightly better one a year from now, and I don't think that's a moral failing, just a fact about me.
- But I think I'm being irrational when I put off the dentist because the appointment is unpleasant. Same structure -- small thing now over bigger thing later -- and I want to call that one a failure of will.
- If I could sign something today that locked in a small monthly payment for a seawall whose payoff lands in 80 years, I'd sign it. If the payoff landed in 800 years I don't think I'd bother, and I can't tell whether that's realism or cowardice.
- I'd rather save for my own retirement than for a stranger's, same year. That's just partiality, fine. But I'd also rather save for my retirement than for my great-grandchildren's college fund, and I'll never meet either of them. So my concern seems to be decaying with time, not with whether I'll be around to see it.
- I think it's wrong for a government to value a life saved in 50 years at a quarter of a life saved now if the reason is just that it's later. But the same government using a high rate on a bridge that might not get built, or on money it could otherwise invest, seems fine to me.
- I'd pay a lot to make sure a promise I made to a dying friend gets kept, even if it only comes due in 40 years and I'll never learn whether it was.
- I feel the sting of a past wrong done to me more than the prospect of an equal future one, even though only the future one is something I could still avoid. If I discounted the future at a steady rate, shouldn't that come out backwards?
- And on the spatial side: I think I should care about a stranger 5,000 miles away about as much as my neighbor. If that's right, why do I get to care less about someone 5,000 years away?

My half-formed principle is that a discount is fine when it tracks something real -- the later thing is less likely, or costs me something to reach, or isn't anyone's good in particular -- and wrong when it tracks nothing but the calendar. I've been calling the first kind a grip discount and the second a clock discount. But that doesn't sort my own cases: the dinner and the dentist have the same structure and I give them opposite verdicts, and I can't find any grip story for the great-grandchildren case.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of them, that gets most of this? State it precisely, tell me which case is doing the work for each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't thought about -- a friend choosing between a career helping people now and one running a seed vault for people 150 years out. I don't want a survey of what other people have written about this; I want the principle. American English please.
