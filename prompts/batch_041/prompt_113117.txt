Six months ago I would have described myself as a person of settled character, and I would have said so with the untested confidence of someone who had never had that confidence put under load. I am a psychometrician by trade: I design and validate the instruments that organizations use to measure conscientiousness, integrity, and what they insist on calling leadership potential, and my working life is spent among latent variables, factor loadings, item-response curves, and the long quarrel over whether a person's conduct is better predicted by the trait you assign them or by the situation you put them in. A recent episode at my own workplace, in which I watched myself and several colleagues behave in ways none of us would have predicted from our own self-descriptions, has sent me back to the virtue-ethics literature and the situationist critique of it, reading them now with a professional's eye rather than a student's.

What I want from you is a translation exercise, performed with the rigor I would demand of an analyst on my own team. Take the core of virtue ethics — that the primary object of ethical assessment is the agent and their stable dispositions rather than the isolated act, that virtues are acquired through habituation, that practical wisdom is internal to virtue and the virtues form something like a unity, and that the good life is assessed as a whole rather than episode by episode — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: latent traits and their indicators, cross-situational consistency and aggregation, internal consistency and construct validity, bandwidth-fidelity trade-offs, situation strength and trait activation, base rates and incremental validity over situational predictors. Then do the same for the situationist critique: its empirical claim about the predictive power of situations over traits, its methodological claim about aggregation and the person-situation debate, and its philosophical claim that the global character traits virtue ethics requires are not the sort of thing that exists.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims of virtue ethics survive the translation cleanly, which survive only in distorted form, and which cannot be expressed in psychometric terms at all? For each item of residue, tell me whether the loss reveals a defect in my vocabulary or a place where virtue ethics was less precise than it appeared. I am suspicious of three things in particular and would like them addressed head-on: first, whether phronesis, which seems to be a normative competence rather than a descriptive one, has any counterpart in a framework built to measure what is rather than what ought to be; second, whether the unity of the virtues was ever anything more than a claim that certain indicators load on a common factor, and if so whether the virtue ethicist needs it; and third, whether the situationist's local traits are simply facets, or traits of narrow bandwidth, under another name — in which case the dispute may be less about what exists than about what granularity of description earns the name of character. Be explicit about what you had to fudge. If the translation cannot be carried out without smuggling in a psychometric assumption — say, that a virtue must be a stable dispositional cause of behavior that manifests as cross-situational consistency — flag it rather than conceal it, because a smuggled assumption is precisely the error I spend my working life catching in other people's validation studies.
