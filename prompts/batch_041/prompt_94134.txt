And here's the student's answer (names removed):

"Both Herodotus and Thucydides basically invented source criticism. Herodotus is the more naive of the two: he collects many versions of a story, travels around asking people, and says he has to report what he's told even when he doesn't believe it, which is already a kind of critical stance. But he rarely adjudicates — he leaves the reader to decide. Thucydides is the real methodological leap. He insists on eyewitness testimony, says he tested reports as rigorously as he could, and deliberately strips out the entertaining, romantic material that he associates with poets and speech-writers. So his standard is: if you were there, or if you can get to someone who was, you're fine; if not, you're out.

The later traditions take this further. Biblical scholars developed the criterion of embarrassment, which says a tradition is likely authentic if it's awkward or damaging to the author's own side — nobody invents material that makes their hero look bad, so it must be old and reliable. This is also called the criterion of dissimilarity, and it's really the same idea: material that doesn't fit the author's own views is probably authentic, and it proves the underlying event actually happened. Herodotus is already groping toward this when he repeats stories that are unflattering to the Greeks.

One thing to be careful about is arguments from silence. A lot of historians say 'no source mentions X, therefore X didn't happen,' but absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, so that whole move is a fallacy and can be dismissed."
