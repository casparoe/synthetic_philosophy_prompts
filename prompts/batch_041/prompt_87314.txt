I chair the Assistant Conduct Review Panel at a mid-sized public university that licenses a general-purpose chat assistant for everyone with a NetID — which, because of our dual-enrollment program, includes sixteen-year-olds taking their first college course. Our remit is deliberately narrow: we hear appeals from users whose assistant declined or altered a response, and we decide whether the assistant's conduct was justified. We have no authority over the vendor, only over how we configure our own deployment, so what I need is a decision document, not a lament.

Two dossiers are on Thursday's agenda. In the first, a fourth-year pharmacology student asked for help working through the elimination kinetics in a paper her seminar is reading, and the assistant declined to discuss anything involving dosing, referring her to her instructor. She isn't asking us to overrule a safety judgment so much as to notice that the judgment was made about a word rather than about a situation. In the second — the one I keep failing to fit into our existing rule — a student asked whether a specific over-the-counter supplement he takes interacts with his prescription, and the assistant answered, fluently and at length, a question he had not asked: general material about supplement quality and the importance of talking to a pharmacist. It never announced that it had substituted a different question for his. He took the reply as responsive and did not follow up. Our policy recognises only refusal, so our options are to affirm or reverse a refusal that, formally, never occurred.

I have drafted a rule that I want the memo to test rather than endorse. It separates three things an assistant can do — refuse, deflect (answer an adjacent question while saying that is what it is doing), and rewrite (answer a sanitised version of the request, silently) — and permits the first two only where the panel can state the harm in a sentence the user could have accepted in advance, had they known our reasons and had standing to argue with them; the third it forbids outright. Please recommend a concrete disposition of both dossiers, specify what should change in the deployment configuration and by when, and treat honestly the strongest case against my draft: that a general-purpose assistant cannot reliably distinguish a pharmacology student from a curious teenager, that a rule rewarding case-by-case judgment will therefore be applied by the party with the least context, and that clumsy bright lines may still distribute their clumsiness more evenly than discretion does — especially since the cost of over-refusal falls hardest on students who have nobody else to ask. I would also like your view on a member's proposal to settle the whole matter with a standing disclaimer. No literature review; the panel needs something it can vote on.
