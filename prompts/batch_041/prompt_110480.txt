i wrote up some notes for a short essay on moral compromise and i want comments before i write the real thing, mostly on whether the central claim holds up.

The essay is meant to argue that moral disagreement is over-diagnosed. Most of the time two people who disagree about values aren't in a zero-sum fight, because they care about different things with different intensities, which means there are usually packages they both prefer to whatever's happening now. That's just gains from trade. Two people who both hate the current animal-testing regime for different reasons -- one because suffering matters, one because the science is bad -- can trade: the first accepts a delay in the phase-out the second wants, in exchange for the second supporting a funding shift the first wants. Neither gets what they'd have picked alone. Both get something they rank above the status quo. Coase with moral vocabulary.

The claim i actually want to defend is this: compromise of that kind fails only when someone holds a lexicographic value -- something they won't trade at any price, no matter what's on the other side. And i think real lexicographic values are much rarer than stated ones. People announce them as bargaining positions ('i will never vote for X') and then trade them anyway when the price is right, which suggests the announcement was a commitment device rather than a preference.

Then the part i'm least sure of. I want to seperate two things people call compromise. Bundling: i give up something on issue 1, you give up something on issue 2, and both of us end up above the status quo -- that's a genuine gain from trade. Splitting: we divide a single thing in half, or write a sentence we can both live with. I want to say splitting has no gains from trade in it at all, its just a loss for whoever cared more -- unless the thing being split is a divisible resource, in which case it collapses back into bundling. And the thing that actually blocks compromise, even when neither side is lexicographic, might be signalling: if i trade away a value, everyone watching learns my value was weaker than i said, so the trade costs me credibility on every future issue. Under that view the obstacle isn't disagreement at all, it's that values are partly made of your willingness to trade them away.

So: (1) is the lexicographic claim true, or can trade be blocked without it -- say when the two sides' values aren't comparable at all, so there's no ratio to trade at? (2) does the bundling/splitting distinction do any work, or is it just relabelling? i keep going back and forth on whether splitting a resource is a different phenomenon from trading across issues, adn if it isn't i lose a chunk of the essay. (3) the signalling bit -- is that a real mechanism or am i just describing reputation with extra steps? And tell me which paragraph is weakest; i suspect it's the third. If the lexicographic claim turns out false, tell me what should replace it, because i don't want to just retreat to 'compromise is complicated'.
