You are an expert in the philosophy of the social sciences, with a particular focus on how nature-and-nurture claims get tested and how social measurements get constructed. I am a high school student preparing for a class debate, and I would rather be careful than confident.

There is a pattern I keep running into. In many of the world's richest countries, which also tend to rank near the top on measures of gender equality, women choose engineering, physics, and computer science less often than women do in poorer countries that rank much lower on those same measures. Commentators on one side say this shows that once material and legal constraints are lifted, innate differences in interest are free to express themselves. Commentators on the other side say the pattern is a measurement artifact, or that equality in law has not touched the messages people absorb about what boys and girls are good at. I do not want you to settle the debate for me yet. I want the specification first.

Please do the following, in order.

First, spend some time brainstorming before you commit to a list. Survey the kinds of explanations that are on the table — biological, developmental, cultural, institutional, and measurement-based — and let that survey shape what you decide to demand of any one of them.

Second, write the desiderata. Give me the requirements that any adequate explanation of this pattern must meet to be worth taking seriously. For each requirement, state it in a sentence, give the case, finding, or possibility that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Include at least these, and add whatever else you think belongs:

- it must handle the reverse cases as well, meaning egalitarian countries or regions where the gap is small and less egalitarian ones where it is large;
- it must not slide between claims about groups and claims about individuals;
- it must say what it predicts about men's choices, not only about women's;
- it must distinguish a widening gap caused by women moving away from male-typed fields from one caused by men moving further toward them, and say what evidence would separate the two;
- it must be honest about what happens when the equality ranking and the outcome measure are both built out of the same underlying data;
- it must separate what it explains about the causes of the pattern from what it licenses about policy, since those are not the same question;
- it must say what it forbids, that is, which observation would count against it.

Third, test the list against a stipulation of mine. Suppose a country passes a thorough package of equality reforms and, within a single generation, reaches parity in pay, parental leave, and legal rights, while occupational choices stay exactly as they are for thirty years. Suppose further that during those thirty years surveys show young women's stated preferences shifting steadily toward engineering while their actual course enrollments do not move at all. What must your requirements force a theory to say about that case, and does any requirement become impossible to satisfy there?

Fourth, flag the collisions. Tell me which pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be met at once, and which single requirement does the most to rule out the explanations people reach for most often.

Finish with a short table scoring three families — innate-preference explanations, socialization explanations, and measurement-artifact explanations — against your requirements, followed by a couple of sentences on what I should be most suspicious of in my own reasoning.

Please write in American English. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning itself.
