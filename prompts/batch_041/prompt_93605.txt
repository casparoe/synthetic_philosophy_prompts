I have been keeping a record of my own choices under risk, and I want to know what decision rule, if any, they amount to. I am not asking which theory I have read about. I am asking what principle would reproduce the verdicts below, what that principle implies about cases I have not yet considered, and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Please state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case and derive a verdict.

My verdicts:

1. A certainty premium on gains. Offered a sure $500 or a coin flip that pays $1,200 on heads and nothing on tails, I take the $500. I still take the $500 when the flip's expected value only ties the sure thing (heads pays $1,000). Heads would have to pay about $1,300 before I would rather flip.

2. Risk seeking on losses. Offered a sure loss of $500 or a coin flip that loses $1,200 on tails and nothing on heads, I take the coin flip. The sure loss would have to shrink to about $250 before I would prefer it to the flip.

3. A pair I cannot square. Between a sure $1,000,000 and a lottery that pays $1,000,000 with probability 89%, $5,000,000 with probability 10%, and nothing with probability 1%, I take the sure $1,000,000. Between an 11% chance at $1,000,000 and a 10% chance at $5,000,000, I take the 10% chance at $5,000,000. The two pairs differ only in an 89% chance at $1,000,000 that is common to both, and I am told the combination is inconsistent with any assignment of utilities to dollar amounts; the verdicts are nonetheless the ones I would give.

4. Overpaying at both tails. I buy lottery tickets and would pay up to about $5 for a one-in-a-million chance at $2,000,000, well above its expected value. I also pay a $400 premium to insure against a one-in-five-hundred chance of a $150,000 loss, again above its expected value. I overpay for a tiny chance at a gain and to escape a tiny chance of a loss.

5. Small bets and their bundles. I decline a single coin flip that wins $110 on heads and loses $100 on tails. I accept a package of one hundred independent flips of that same bet. I also accept a single flip at a hundred times the stakes, winning $11,000 or losing $10,000, even though the chance of losing is the same as in the small bet I decline.

6. Choosing for others. With my own money the verdicts above hold. Choosing for a charity with a $10,000,000 budget and a mandate to do as much good as possible, I would accept almost any positive-expected-value bet that does not put the whole budget at risk; I would refuse a bet carrying a one-in-twenty chance of losing the entire $10,000,000, however large its expected gain.

7. The case I cannot settle. I cannot tell whether I prefer a 1% chance at $1,000 or a 10% chance at $100, which have the same expected value, and I would like the principle to tell me which of the two I am committed to.

The only two stories I can tell myself are these. The first is that money has decreasing marginal value, so I buy certainty and avoid risk and the rest is noise. The second is that I misperceive chances, inflating small ones and sharpening near-certainties, and that gains and losses are measured from wherever I happen to be standing. The first story cannot be right, because of verdicts 2, 4, and 5. The second I cannot state precisely enough to compute anything with, and I do not know whether it is one principle or two; I would like it tested against the verdicts and repaired where it fails.

What I want is: (a) the principle, or the smallest set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to generate a verdict on a case neither of us has yet described; (b) for each clause of it, the verdict above that forces that clause; (c) its verdicts on cases I have not listed, in particular on a choice between a bet with a small chance of an outcome below any floor I have named and a sure but smaller loss, and on the same distribution presented once as a single gamble and once as a long series of smaller steps; and (d) an honest account of which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the best repair is to drop that verdict, to add a parameter, or to split the principle into one for choices I make for myself and one for choices I make for others.

Please answer in American English.
