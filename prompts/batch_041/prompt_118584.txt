You are a philosopher specializing in Kant's ethics and in the theory of practical reasoning, and I'd like you to work on the following problem as a piece of constructive philosophy rather than a literature review. Take your time: begin by brainstorming several candidate formulations of a universalizability test and stress-testing each against the cases below before you commit to one, then write up the best version you can. Precision matters more than brevity.

I have been applying the first formulation of the categorical imperative — act only on maxims you could at the same time will as universal laws — to my own decisions, and I've written down what I think about a series of maxims. For each case I record two things: my moral verdict on acting on the maxim, and what I take the universalization test itself to say. I care much less about whether I have Kant's text right than about whether a single principle can generate my moral verdicts, and what that principle implies for cases I haven't yet considered. I should add that I keep running into a distinction between maxims whose universalization supposedly cannot even be thought, and maxims that can be thought as universal law but cannot be willed. I don't know whether that distinction is doing honest work or is an ad hoc patch, and I'd like it either justified or dropped.

1. The overdue deposit. I take a client's deposit for a piece of work and promise delivery in three weeks, knowing that my supplier's delay makes this impossible and that I need the money for rent regardless. My verdict: wrong. Universalized, the practice of taking such promises at face value collapses, and my action destroys the very thing that made it useful. The test agrees with me.

2. The padded résumé. On a job application I write that I "managed a team of five," when in fact I supervised two interns for one summer. My verdict: sleazy, but not wrong. Universalized, however, employers who know that everyone pads will discount résumé claims, and the informativeness that makes my padding worth anything disappears — the same self-undermining structure as case 1. The test says wrong; I don't. Note that 1 and 2 look structurally identical to me and I give them opposite verdicts.

3. The refusal that costs nothing. A passerby is drowning in a shallow canal; I can pull them out at no risk to myself; I decline, because I don't want to get wet. My verdict: clearly wrong. Universalized, a world in which no one ever helps anyone is perfectly consistent — no practice collapses, no one is deceived, nothing is self-undermining. If the test asks only whether the maxim can be thought as a universal law, it lets this through.

4. The same refusal, with a rider. Now suppose my maxim has a second clause: I will never help anyone in distress, and I will never accept help from anyone either. Universalization seems to go through twice over, since I no longer even rely on a world in which I would be helped. My verdict: still wrong, and not one bit less wrong than 3. Whatever condemns 3 must condemn 4.

5. The person who never asks. My maxim: I will give help freely whenever I notice a need, but I will never ask anyone for help, no matter what. My verdict: not wrong — proud and self-defeating, perhaps, but not immoral. Universalized, though, nobody ever asks, and if I never ask then in the situations where help depends on asking I will not be helped, which appears to contradict my own will to be helped in need. The test says wrong; I don't. I can't tell whether the difference between 3 and 5 is the helping or the willing.

6. The boat. My maxim: I will spend my savings on a sailboat rather than send the money to famine relief. My verdict: not wrong, though not admirable. Universalized, nothing collapses; everyone simply keeps their money. Test and verdict agree. I include this case because it shows how much the test lets through, and I want to know whether the principle you give me is meant as a full account of right and wrong or only of one family of duties.

7. The emergency shift. I promised to help my brother move on Saturday, then I am called in for an emergency shift as a hospital anaesthetist, and I break the promise. My verdict: not wrong. But the very same action — breaking a promise to help my brother move — is wrong if my reason is that a concert I want to attend has just gone on sale. So the verdict seems to depend entirely on how the maxim is described, and I can get whichever answer I like by describing it thinly or thickly. I want the principle to tell me how maxims are to be specified, not to leave that to me.

8. The last slice. My maxim: I will take the last slice of cake at a shared table without offering it around. My verdict: rude, not wrong. Universalized, nothing collapses. Test and verdict agree.

Here is the tentative principle I have been working with, which I suspect is what the self-undermining talk in case 1 really amounts to: an action is wrong just when universalizing its maxim would make the action self-defeating — would destroy the practice, convention, or expectation that the action depends on in order to work. That principle handles case 1 and the thin reading of case 7. It seems to me to fail case 2 (which it wrongly condemns), cases 3 and 4 (which it wrongly permits), and case 5 (which it wrongly condemns, if the contradiction-in-will argument is allowed to count as self-defeat).

What I would like from you:

(a) A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it.

(b) An honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and why I should give that verdict up rather than complicate the principle further.

(c) Your verdicts, under that principle, on two cases I have not listed. First: my maxim is that I will torment animals for my own amusement, on the assumption that I will never be an animal. My verdict is that this is wrong; the test appears to let it through. Second: my maxim is that I will never accept a favour I cannot repay and never grant one to someone who cannot repay me — strict reciprocity, no exceptions. My verdict is that this is not wrong, merely cold; the test appears to condemn it through the will-contradiction.

(d) Your view on case 7: is maxim specification a genuine part of the test, or is it the place where the test smuggles in an answer it did not earn?

Please don't summarize what Kant or his commentators say about the first formulation; I want your own best reconstruction of a test that actually delivers the pattern above. If the pattern turns out to be incoherent, I would rather hear that plainly than receive a tidy synthesis.
