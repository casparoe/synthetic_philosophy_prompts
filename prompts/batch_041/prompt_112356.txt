I'm in a small reading group that's been working through the pragmatists and we've got a fight on our hands. We did Peirce for three weeks, then jumped to James, and now half the group thinks they were basically saying the same thing and half thinks they weren't even answering the same question. The fight started over something dumb: my great-grandmother ran the family shop for about forty years while her two brothers went off to university, and in every letter and diary we have from her she's unfailingly cheerful about it, never one sour line. She died in 1974, the shop is a phone repair place now, and there is nothing left to find. I said I think there's a fact of the matter about whether she was quietly bitter about how that went, and my friend Priya said that's just a story the living are telling about a dead woman. So write me the dialogue Priya and I should have had, with her taking the Peircean line — truth is what the community of inquirers would be fated to agree on if inquiry could go on indefinitely, so the answer about my great-grandmother is already settled one way or the other even if nobody will ever know it — and me taking the Jamesian line — an idea becomes true as it gets verified in experience, and what verifies it is what it's good for us to believe in the way of practice, so the real question is what believing one way or the other does for the family.

Two things I especially want out of it. First, have each of them turn the other's own principle against them: Priya should press me on how "true" differs from "comforting" on my view, and whether "true for me" is even a coherent thing to say, and I should press her on what the cash value of "the ideal limit of inquiry" is supposed to be, given that nobody will ever cash it. Second, I want at least one exchange where the usual roles flip — where I'm the one saying no, you don't get to just believe that, theres nothing there to be true, and she's the one insisting there's a truth here whether or not any of us can ever touch it.

Neither of them should be a strawman — no cheap "so you think truth is whatever's convenient" from her side, no "so you think the future decides everything" from mine — and they should actually answer each other's points rather than deliver set speeches. Dont lean on the famous examples (the squirrel, the religious belief stuff) and dont quote anybody; I want their arguement, not their greatest hits. End with them still disagreeing, and have each say what they'd have to give up to accept the other's position. If it turns out the whole thing is really a dispute about what the word "true" is doing rather than about my great-grandmother, that's fine, but then make them actually argue that out instead of just announcing it.
