A friend of mine who works on fair division keeps telling me that Rawls's original position is nothing more than the cake rule every child knows: one person cuts the cake and the other chooses a piece first. On his reading, the veil of ignorance is simply the cutter's ignorance of which piece will be his; the parties in the original position are the two children; and the difference principle is nothing but the maximin strategy the rule forces on the cutter, who must cut as evenly as he can because he may be left with the smaller piece. He takes this as a vindication of the difference principle — an impartial procedure, a maximin outcome — and concludes that the whole apparatus of the social contract, the parties' deliberation about primary goods, and their concern with the strains of commitment are decoration around a result that any fair division procedure would have delivered anyway. I am not convinced, but I cannot say exactly where the argument goes wrong. Give me an audit rather than a verdict. Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what plays the role of the cutter, the chooser, the knife, and the cake; distinguish the features of the cake case that are doing real work from those that are idle or actively misleading; separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that do not; and then say whether the conclusion survives. Two points in particular interest me. First, the cake rule appears to yield an equal split, whereas the difference principle permits inequalities; does the analogy support equality, or the difference principle, and does it matter whether the size of the cake is fixed in advance or is itself a consequence of the division rule the cutter selects? Second, is the cutter's maximin behaviour forced by the rule as such, or by the assumption that the other child is an adversary who will take the larger piece? If it is the latter, say what the analogy thereby smuggles in about how the worst case is to be weighed, given that behind the veil nobody is choosing against you — and whether anything in the original position plays the adversary's role at all. If there is a better analogy for what the original position is doing, name it and say what it captures that the cake rule misses.
