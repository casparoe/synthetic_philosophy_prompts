I'm enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and we've spent the past two weeks on a question that keeps splitting the room: when a long-run prediction fails on its own schedule but the predicted outcome eventually shows up decades later, has the underlying theory been vindicated, or merely rescued? The paradigm case is Malthus. His principle of population—roughly, that population tends to grow geometrically while the food supply can grow at best arithmetically, so that any gain in living standards is eventually swallowed by more people—is commonly said to have failed, since the mass starvation it seemed to portend for industrialized Europe never arrived. Yet it is just as commonly said to have been "right, only early," with fingers pointed at later famines, resource bottlenecks, and the present worry that climate change and depletion are at last delivering the Malthusian outcome. I'd like you to write an essay that takes a stand on whether a theory can legitimately be "right but early," and that lands on a verdict rather than straddling the fence.

To sharpen the question, here is a criterion a classmate floated, which I want you to engage with directly rather than set aside: a late-arriving outcome counts as vindication of a prediction only if the mechanism that actually produced the outcome is the same mechanism the original prediction named. If the result shows up for reasons the theory never specified, the theory was rescued, not confirmed. Call this the mechanism-match criterion. Weigh it against the obvious rival—that what matters is simply whether the predicted outcome occurred, mechanism be damned—and test both against the Malthus case and at least one other genre of long-run forecast (say, a resource-exhaustion projection, a technological-singularity projection, or an epidemiological one). In doing so, consider whether the mechanism-match criterion proves too much: whether it would disqualify predictions we intuitively want to count as successful, and whether it can be applied without the benefit of hindsight. Reach a definite conclusion about when, if ever, "right but early" is a legitimate verdict.

Please write this as a polished, self-contained essay, reasoning from principles and the cases rather than from any literature. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states your conclusion in a single sentence.
