Right, so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've drifted into a view I can't shake and I'd like you to have a proper go at knocking it down. Here it is. When I write a character, the character's self isn't anywhere on the page — there's no tiny person hiding in the sentences. It's more like the centre of gravity of a body: you can't point at it, it isn't made of anything, but it isn't nothing either, because the whole mass of the story organises itself around it, and once you've got it you can predict what the character does next. Lately I've come to think my own self is that same kind of thing. Not an object in my skull, but the centre of gravity of a running story I keep telling about myself — real the way an abstraction can be real, load-bearing even, but not a piece of the world's furniture the way a neuron or a liver is.

So: what would count against that? I want the actual evidence and arguments, sorted by how hard they hit — from the ones that should make me give the view up to the ones that are merely awkward. I'm thinking of things like people whose two halves seem to want different things, hypnotised and dissociated states, radical memory loss, the fact that babies and animals seem to have some kind of self going before anyone could tell a story, and the people who insist — and I half beleive them — that they don't experience their lives as a narrative at all, just one damn moment after another, and yet they seem perfectly whole. Is there a bare felt sense of being alive that isn't narrative, and if there is, does that sink me or just narrow my claim? Also, be honest about whether the view can be falsified at all, or whether people like me just keep qualifying it until nothing could count against it — and if that's whats happened, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of the abstraction being useful.

I'd also like to know what someone who holds this would themselves admit counts against them, not just what the critics yell. And for what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where the story and the self come apart — where someone's narrative says one thing and they clearly are something else — so tell me if any of these are really that, or if I'm fooling myself. I don't need a reading list, just the arguments and the cases.
