For years I have been the resident skeptic among my friends, and over the last twelve months I have been writing down the judgments I actually make when conspiracy talk comes up, because I have started to suspect that they do not all issue from the same source. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, they express, and to tell me where I have to give one of them up.

Here is the list. (1) A nurse I know believes that her hospital's administrators quietly reclassify deaths so that the published mortality figures stay low; she has seen two internal spreadsheets that do not match the public dashboard, and a memo telling staff not to discuss the discrepancy. I take her belief to be well grounded. (2) A man I met at a party believes the same thing about the same hospital, on the ground that administrators always hide something. I take his belief to be ungrounded, even though the claim is identical and may be true. (3) A friend who reads engineering reports believes that the official account of a local bridge collapse is wrong — he says the reported sequence of failures does not fit the photographs or the inspection timeline — and he declines to say what did happen. I think that is a defensible position. (4) The same friend then tells me the collapse was a demolition arranged by a contractor facing penalties. On the evidence he has described, I do not think he is entitled to that. (5) An acquaintance believes a conspiracy theory, and when I show him documents that contradict it he says the documents are part of the cover-up; I think he is being unreasonable. (6) A different man held a conspiracy theory with exactly that unshakable confidence, and the theory turned out to be true when an archive was opened ten years later. I still want to say he was unreasonable throughout, and I am not sure I am permitted to. (7) A woman believes that her own union is burying an internal scandal; she could ask three officers who would know, and she has not, because she assumes they would lie. I think she is being unreasonable. (8) A colleague believes that two or three people quietly agreed to bury a mistake at work. I find that entirely credible and think no worse of him for it. (9) A relative believes that a cabal spanning several governments has sustained a deception for fifty years. I find that much harder to credit, though I cannot tell whether I am simply applying base rates to the number of people who would have to keep quiet. (10) A bereaved father believes a conspiracy about his son's death; I think he is mistaken, but the belief seems to be doing necessary work for him, and I cannot bring myself to call him irrational. (11) And the one I keep returning to: a man is very confident that the official story is false, offers no positive alternative, and insists he is under no obligation to supply one.

I can see at least three patterns here and they pull apart. One is about evidence and formation — how the person came to the belief, and whether contrary evidence would move them. That pattern makes (1) good and (2) bad, keeps (6) bad, and is what is doing the work in (5). A second is about the content of the belief — its scale, whether it is self-sealing, whether it posits a mechanism that would explain the very absence of evidence against it. That pattern is what makes (4) and (9) bad and (3) and (8) acceptable. A third is about the believer rather than the belief — whether they are being negligent, or evasive, or simply damaged — and that is what seems to drive (7) and (10). Please tell me whether a single principle can be stated that yields all eleven verdicts, or whether I must choose; if I must choose, say which verdict each candidate principle forces me to abandon and why that is the cheapest one to lose. State the principle, or the small set of candidates, precisely enough that I can see what each clause is for and which case motivates it; give me their verdicts on cases I have not listed — including a person who believes a conspiracy theory solely on the word of a friend who says she was in the room, and a person who believes it because every rival explanation he has heard has been debunked; and tell me honestly which of my judgments the best principle cannot accommodate.
