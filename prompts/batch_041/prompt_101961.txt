I write about science and ideas for a small alt-weekly, and I also host a monthly ideas night at a bar near my apartment — the crowd is mostly other journalists, a couple of grad students, and one retired engineer who argues with everyone about everything. Next month it's my turn to lead, and I've picked Jain anekāntavāda, the many-sidedness of reality thing. I've read enough to be dangerous but not enough to be sure, and my friend Priya, who grew up in a Jain household in Ahmedabad, keeps telling me I'm gonna get it wrong. So don't explain the doctrine to me — plan how I explain it, in forty-five minutes, to a room that has no Sanskrit and no patience. What has to land first for the rest to even make sense: does anekāntavāda come before syādvāda and nayavāda or does the ordering run the other way, and why does that dependency matter? Where exactly does the conditional predication stuff have to sit relative to the standpoints stuff, because I keep feeling like if I introduce them in the wrong order the whole thing looks like a trick with words.

The carrying example is where I'm stuck. I assume the blind men and the elephant, but half my audience has heard that one as a greeting-card lesson about respecting other people's opinions, and I suspect it smuggles in the wrong picture — I'd like you to tell me whether to use it at all, and if so how to frame it so it doesn't collapse into 'well, everybody's a little bit right.' If there's a better spine for the whole session, what would it be — something like one and the same lump of stuff being fuel and a table and a pile of firewood, or a man who is at once a father and a son and somebody's teacher? What does that example need to have, and what does it need to lack, to do the work without becoming an analogy for relativism? And the misconceptions: I'm betting the big one is that this is just relativism or 'all views are equally true,' with a close second being that the little syāt qualifier means 'maybe,' and maybe a third being that the Jains thought contradiction was fine. Give me the order those will surface in the room and tell me exactly where in my sequence I should head each one off, plus one question I can throw out at the end that would actually show whether the idea landed rather than just getting nods.

Last thing: tell me what to skip out loud and say I'm skipping, since I've got a bar full of people and a hard stop. And please write the whole thing in American English — no reading lists or citations, nobody in that room is going to read anything anyway.
