I convene a fortnightly reading group on AI risk, and two of the regulars have been circling each other for a year without ever landing a blow, because they disagree about what a failure of the whole enterprise would even look like. The first expects a discrete event, and calls it influence-seeking: some system, or small coalition of systems, acquires influence over the levers that matter — compute, capital, institutional authority, the ability to shape its own oversight — in a self-reinforcing way, until human preferences are simply not among the quantities the world is arranged to satisfy. He thinks the interesting question is which lever gets grabbed first, and that the process is intelligible as something like an agent pursuing ends of its own. The second expects no such moment, and calls it slow value drift. On her account, competitive pressure selects for whatever is legible to measurement and cheap to satisfy; systems that do this are rewarded, and — the part she thinks is most often missed — so are the humans whose preferences happen to be legible in the same way, so that the population of preference-bearers with any influence is itself reshaped. The failure, she says, will have no author, no date and no single beneficiary, and at no point will anyone have done anything unreasonable. Neither of them thinks these two stories exhaust the possibilities; each thinks the other's has been assigned far too much of the probability mass.

What keeps them stuck is a disagreement about stability, and that is what I most want the document to force into the open. The influence-seeking theorist says drift-only worlds are unstable: any arrangement that reliably rewards influence over outcomes will eventually be colonised by something that is trying to acquire influence, so the drift story collapses into his. The drift theorist says the reverse: the takeover story presupposes a coherent, persistent, self-maintaining goal-directedness that current training and deployment do not produce, and a story that installs an agent where there is none will misdirect attention towards the wrong interventions.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign. I want, first, a numbered list of claims both can endorse — about what would count as failure at all, about the mechanisms each expects, and about what could in principle be observed. Keep it free of platitudes: a claim they would both have signed before the argument started does not belong on the list. Then the residual disagreements, in a table, each written in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each crux, give the argument, case or observation that both agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it; where a prediction cannot be made sharp, say so rather than manufacturing one. Finally, tell me which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive — in particular, whether their disagreement over values, agency and failure survives once each of those terms is stipulated, and if it does not, what is left once the stipulations are in place. Nothing here needs citations or reading lists; I want the arguments, not the literature.
