My great-aunt died in March and left me the family papers, which is how I ended up chairing the commitee that's trying to get a plaque put up in the town my grandmother's family came from — the Jewish community there was rounded up and shot over two days in 1942, and the town has never marked it. The wording is now a full-blown fight. The historical society wants "massacre" and won't use the word genocide, on the grounds that nothing survives that orders it and that one village isn't a plan to destroy a people; my cousins say that if we can't call it what it was then the whole plaque is a lie. I have to give the dedication remarks with all of them standing there, plus the mayor, a rabbi, local press, and a lot of relatives who will tune out anything that sounds like a lecture.

Can you write me the speech? I want it to take the fight seriously instead of dissolving it into "we remember." Some things I'd like it to work with: Lemkin's original idea of genocide was much wider than killing — language, leadership, culture, economic life all counted, and the crime was replacing one people's pattern of life with another's; the 1948 convention cut that down to a short list of physical acts plus intent to destroy a group in whole or in part, and it dropped political groups and cultural destruction on purpose. So the historical society's objection isn't stupid. But I want the speech to also say that the crime wasn't waiting on the word, that a legal definition is a tool for courts and not a verdict on what a town owes its dead, and to be straight about the uncomfortable part: the line between genocide and not-genocide got drawn partly by governments protecting themselves. (I also like that the word itself is a mongrel — half Greek, half Latin — and that he knew it.)

Five minutes, please. No citations, no quoting treaties at people. It has to land for the two lawyers and the retired history teacher in the room without losing my aunts.
