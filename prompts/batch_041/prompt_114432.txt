I've been volunteering for a few months at a restorative-justice project attached to a local prison, and it has pushed a question I used to find merely academic right to the front of my mind. The volunteers split, fairly cleanly, into two camps, and neither will give an inch. One camp says that if a man did the thing, and nothing about him excuses it in the ordinary way, then resentment, indignation and the demand for an apology are simply the appropriate responses — that is what it is to treat him as a participant in our moral life rather than as a problem to be managed. The other camp says that once you understand how little the man's history let him do otherwise — the neglect, the acquired brain injury, the years in care — the reactive attitudes are at best a self-indulgence and at worst a category error, and the honest response is something more like the clinical or the managerial: predict, contain, rehabilitate. What strikes me is that both camps agree about the facts of his history and about what he did; nobody is disputing the neurology or the sociology. And yet the argument in our debriefs, and apparently in the philosophical literature too, has run for decades without either side gaining any ground. I have a hypothesis I would like you to test: that the two camps are not really disagreeing about what he did or about how much his history constrained him, but about what has to be true for an attitude to count as 'appropriate' at all — one side treating appropriateness as a matter of what the agent's conduct actually expressed, the other treating it as a matter of whether holding him to account could serve any point. But I may be flattering myself that the issue is that tidy, and I want a proper diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right.

Please diagnose the dispute: is it a substantive disagreement about the moral facts, a verbal one turning on different senses of words like 'responsible' and 'appropriate', or two parties answering different questions — one about whether he is blameworthy, the other about whether blaming is worthwhile — under a single heading? Consider seriously the possibility that the impasse is not a defect but a sign that the question is ill-posed, and if so, say why. Where the disagreement rests on something each side treats as bedrock — a starting intuition neither can argue for — identify it and say whether it could be argued for at all. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress: a distinction that dissolves a pseudo-dispute, a case that both sides would have to accommodate, an empirical finding, or an argument that we should expect no progress and why. Be concrete about what each side actually says in the kind of case I have described, rather than retreating into a general survey of the free-will literature. Please write your answer in British English.
