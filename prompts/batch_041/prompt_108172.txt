I am a third-year student in a political philosophy course, and we have just finished the unit on political obligation and civil disobedience; the essay is due at the end of term and I would like to spend my preparation on the framework rather than on another march through the familiar positions. What I want from you is the specification first: the full list of requirements any adequate theory of political obligation has to satisfy — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts about states and citizens it must remain consistent with — each one accompanied by the consideration or example that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. Among the distinctions I already suspect matter: between being under an obligation and merely being liable to sanctions; between a state's being legitimate or justified and my being bound to obey it; between an obligation that is general (owed to any decent state that benefits me) and one that is particular (owed to this state, which is where consent, fair play and gratitude all seem to struggle); between obligations that are content-independent and those that attach to what is commanded because it is just; and between what could be owed by a founding generation and what could be owed by those merely born into the arrangement. The cases I would want the list to cover include at least the following: someone who receives substantial benefits from a state that denies them citizenship and any political standing; a population whose constitution was replaced without its consent, so that the question arises whether the old obligations transfer to the successor; and a person who could in principle emigrate but only at a cost that makes exit not a real option. I would also add as a constraint on the theory's inputs, not as a conclusion it may help itself to, the fact that states themselves rarely ground their claim in the consent of the governed — they claim jurisdiction over whoever is born on the territory, and they assert it against rival states at least as much as against the individual. Please mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and say plainly if some pair of them cannot be satisfied together; I would rather learn now that the list is jointly unsatisfiable than discover it in my conclusion.

The second thing I want is the interaction with civil disobedience, since that is where my study group keeps getting stuck. If the theory establishes a genuine obligation to obey, disobedience begins to look like simple wrongdoing; if it is weakened enough to license disobedience whenever a law is unjust, it stops doing any work and the whole apparatus becomes decorative. So I would like you to say what the requirements must look like in order for both of the following to hold at once: the disobedient act is a real breach of a real obligation, and it can nonetheless be justified — and, if you think it can, required — while the state's punishment of it can still be legitimate. Does that force a particular answer to whether civil disobedience must be public, non-violent, and undertaken with a willingness to accept the penalty, or are those contingent features of one theory rather than requirements on any? And if the two demands really are unsatisfiable together, tell me which one I should surrender and what the cost of surrendering it is.

A brief scoring of the main contenders — consent, fair play, natural duty, and associative or role-based accounts — against your list would be useful at the end, but keep it short; the list and the joint-satisfiability question are what I actually need.
