For an internal training program at a company that builds rating, ranking, and recommendation systems, I have to run a single session on the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem for a group of engineers who aggregate user preferences for a living but have never seen social choice theory. Don't explain the theorem to me; plan the session.

Set out the order in which the components must be introduced so that the conclusion is earned rather than announced: which definitions have to be in place before which, what each of the theorem's assumptions actually buys, and the exact point at which the dictatorship conclusion can first be stated without its seeming to arrive from nowhere. Nominate the one concrete example that should carry the whole session and justify the choice; it has to be a disagreement these engineers already have, it has to involve at least three alternatives (otherwise the result is vacuous), and it should make the incentive to misreport one's preferences vivid without presupposing the theorem.

Give me the misconceptions the audience will predictably arrive with or form, and say precisely where in the sequence each should be confronted and how. I expect them to conflate the result with Arrow's theorem, to assume that some cleverly designed scoring or approval rule escapes it, and to read it as a claim about voters' selfishness rather than about the structure of the aggregation rule; add whatever else you think they will get wrong. Tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so explicitly.

Finally, give me one closing question or exercise that will reveal whether they have understood the theorem's scope rather than merely memorized its conclusion: in particular, whether they now see why the impossibility bites only once there are three or more alternatives, and why an appeal to randomization buys a lottery over dictators rather than an escape.

Write the plan in American English.
