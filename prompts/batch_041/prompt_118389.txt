ok so I've been slowly working through Plato's Euthyphro with my coffee in the morning and I've hit a wall at the bit near the middle (10a in the Stephanus numbers I think — the one everyone calls the Euthyphro dilemma) and I'm hoping someone here can walk me through what Socrates is actually doing, because the version I keep seeing quoted online doesn't match what's on the page.

The part that's tripping me up is where Socrates gets Euthyphro to agree that piety is what all the gods love, and then starts poking at the order of explanation. Jowett's introduction sums the move up like this: "He shows that in other cases the act precedes the state; e.g. the act of being carried, loved, etc. precedes the state of being carried, loved, etc., and therefore that which is dear to the gods is dear to the gods because it is first loved of them, not loved of them because it is dear to them. But the pious or holy is loved by the gods because it is pious or holy, which is equivalent to saying, that it is loved by them because it is dear to them." Which seems to say both that the gods love the holy because it's holy and that this is the same as saying they love it because they love it? I genuinly can't tell if that's Jowett being sloppy or if the argument is supposed to land there.

What I want help with:

(1) What is the actual logical structure of the 10a-11b argument, stated cleanly, without the Daedalus "walking away" imagery? Is it a claim about causation, about explanatory priority, about definitional order, or are all three slid together? And where exactly does the contradiction Socrates claims to find come from?

(2) Is the dilemma as it's usualy stated today — either God commands things because they're good, in which case goodness is independent of God and God is redundant, or things are good because God commands them, in which case morality is arbitrary — actually the same argument as the one in the dialogue? Because the dialogue says "loved," not "commanded"; it's about piety/holiness rather than goodness; and the gods are plural for most of the exchange. I have a suspicion the modern version is a retrofit and that the original is more about the logic of "because" than about divine command theory, but I can't tell if that's a real point or just me being clever.

(3) The thing I actually care about: is the dilemma exhaustive? The third-way move I keep hearing is "God's nature is the standard, so it's neither arbitrary nor independent" — but I can't see how that isn't just horn one with extra steps, or horn two with extra steps. Is there a version of that move that genuinely works, and if so, what is it doing that the naive dilemma rules out in advance? Relatedly, my friend who works on response-dependence says the exact same shape shows up for "funny" (is a joke funny because we laugh, or do we laugh because it's funny?) and for secondary qualities, and that in those cases the dispositional analysis is treated as a real solution rather than a dodge. If that's right, does the analogy help here, or does it break because God is supposed to be an agent making a choice rather than a disposition? I want to know whether the third-way moves in the divine-command literature are doing the dispositionalist thing or something else entirely.

(4) Just so I don't walk away with the wrong impression: the dialogue ends in aporia, Euthyphro bails, Socrates says he was hoping to learn what piety is before his trial. Is the point that the definition project fails, or that Euthyphro's confidence is the real target? I keep seeing people read the ending as a knockout blow against divine command theory and I don't think the text supports that, but I might be reading my own priors into it.
