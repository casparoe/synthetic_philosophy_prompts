I recently convened a small closed-door adversarial-collaboration workshop between two camps who have been arguing past each other about Bertrand's paradox for years, and I am now the one who has to produce the document they should have written together. On one side are what I have been calling the proceduralists: they hold that a probability question about a continuum is well-posed only relative to a specified chance setup; that the classic chord puzzle simply demonstrates that the phrase 'chosen at random' names an outcome space rather than a mechanism; and that the principle of indifference is accordingly not a principle of rationality at all, but at best a heuristic for guessing at a mechanism. On the other side are the invariantists, working in the maximum-entropy and group-invariance tradition: they hold that indifference survives, disciplined, as invariance; that a paradox arises only when one tacitly assumes a symmetry the problem does not possess; and that wherever a transitive group acts on the space of possibilities the prior is uniquely pinned down — a randomly marked great circle on a sphere, they insist, is perfectly determinate, even though a random chord is not. Neither group is a crank, both are competent, and I want a text that both could actually put their names to, so that the residual disagreement is made exact rather than papered over.

The document has to do real work on four stipulated items, which I want handled explicitly and in this order. First, a great circle marked on a sphere, with nothing said about how it was marked: is the question determinate, and if so what makes it so? Second, a single real number said to have been drawn at random from the whole line, with no scale information supplied. Third, a bent coin taken from a factory whose process is symmetric between the two faces, so that a coin of bias p and a coin of bias 1−p are equally likely to be drawn, with nothing else known about the process: what credence should a rational agent have that the first toss lands heads? Fourth, a candidate principle of my own devising, which I want the two camps to negotiate rather than simply endorse or reject — the Specification Requirement: no probability assignment to a continuum-valued event is well-posed unless either the chance setup that produces the event is specified, or a transitive group of transformations is specified under which the question is invariant; and where these two ever conflict, the chance setup takes precedence. For each of the first three items I want it recorded, in advance and without hedging, what each camp predicts the case shows and what would count as a surprise for it; for the fourth, whether both can sign it as it stands, and if not, the exact amendment each side would require.

Format it as follows: a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about the formalism, about what a solution would even have to look like, and about which of the two camps is making a claim about the world rather than about norms of belief; then a disagreements table giving each remaining dispute in wording that the side holding it would accept as a fair statement of its own view; then a crux table listing the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, and for each crux the case or argument both camps agree bears on it, with a prediction column for each; and finally a short closing note on which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal — in particular, whether they mean the same thing by well-posed, uninformative, and the correct prior — and on what would genuinely move each side. Around 1,000 to 1,500 words. No literature survey and no reading list; both camps know the terrain, and what I need is the substance.
