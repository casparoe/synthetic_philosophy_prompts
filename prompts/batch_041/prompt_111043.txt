Consider a legal order whose entire rulebook is one rule, R: "Any rule, including this one, may be amended by a two-thirds majority of the assembly, provided the amendment is adopted in accordance with the rules in force at the moment of the vote." At t1 the assembly passes, by a bare majority, an amendment M reading: "R is repealed and replaced by: rules may be amended by a simple majority." Supporters say the vote satisfied the rules in force once M took effect; opponents say it had to satisfy R, which it did not. Call this the bootstrap amendment.

I want your credence in a general proposition: that for a system like this, the validity of any given amendment is a determinate matter of fact rather than something that dissolves into whatever the relevant officials happen to treat as valid. Decompose it: (i) whether self-referential amendment rules produce genuine antinomies or are benign, like a program that rewrites its own code; (ii) whether validity can be grounded in a rule that authorizes its own amendment without vicious circularity, or whether some unamendable anchor -- a rule of recognition, a basic norm, brute social acceptance -- must sit at the bottom; (iii) whether "determinate fact of the matter" is even the right notion for legal validity, or whether it is constitutively constituted by official practice. Give a number for each term with one line of justification, show how they combine, and say which term you are least confident in and what would move it most. End with a single final line of the form \answer{0.xx}.
