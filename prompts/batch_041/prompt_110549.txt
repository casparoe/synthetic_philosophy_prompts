You are an epistemologist working on evidence, defeat, and the limits of closure — someone with a good nose for when a paradox dissolves and when it is a genuine reductio. Think carefully before committing, and open with a brief brainstorm of the candidate diagnoses before you give a number.

A friend and I spent last night on the dogmatism paradox, in the version usually credited to Kripke: I know that p; if p, then any evidence that appears to tell against p is misleading; so I am in a position to know that any such evidence is misleading; and since I know it is misleading, I may rationally disregard it without weighing it. Knowledge thus licenses dogmatism — I may refuse to look. My friend takes this to be a real reductio and thinks something in our epistemic furniture has to go. I think it is an artefact of how the argument slides between what is known and what is knowable, or between the content of the evidence and its misleadingness, or between the knowledge case and a probabilistic one.

Give me your credence that the paradox has a resolution preserving all three of: single-premise closure of knowledge under known entailment; the factivity of the misleadingness verdict (if I know that p, evidence against p really is misleading); and the permissibility of treating a known proposition as settled. A number between 0 and 1, then the decomposition. At minimum separate the probability that the fault lies in the bridge principle from the evidence is misleading to I may disregard it, from the probability that it lies in the step that licenses the second-order knowledge claim, from the probability that the paradox is an artefact of knowledge-talk and vanishes once restated in terms of high credence, from the probability that one of the three principles must be abandoned after all. One line of justification each, then the combination.

One wrinkle I suspect the standard discussion never quite faces: suppose I know p, and I also know that tomorrow a test will be run whose positive result, had I not already known p, would be strong evidence against p. The paradox seems to license pre-committing tonight to ignore tomorrow's result. Is that the same paradox in different clothes or a harder one, and does your verdict move the number? Finish by naming the sub-question your number is most sensitive to, what would shift it most, and whether my friend's 0.2 and my 0.8 most likely differ over a technical sub-question or over what knowledge has to be for the third principle to hold. Argue it out rather than leaning on names.
