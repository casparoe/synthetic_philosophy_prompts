Next week, for a small interview series I run out of a reading group, I'll be talking with a political theorist whose work I've been following closely. Her central claim is that the usual three-way split between negative liberty, positive liberty, and republican liberty is misconceived: she thinks the real contest is between liberty as non-interference and liberty as non-domination, and that non-domination is not a third entry on the list but a rival that absorbs the other two. Two of her moves I especially want to press on. First, she distinguishes domination — another agent's capacity to interfere arbitrarily — from mere vulnerability, which she says is a loss of well-being but not of freedom, so that illness, poverty, or a bad market are not unfreedom unless some agent's arbitrary power lies behind them. Second, she draws a provocative corollary from this: a slave whose master happens never to interfere remains unfree, while a citizen interfered with by a democratic state she has authorized remains free.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for her, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question on the domination/vulnerability distinction, pressing on whether it can hold the line without making a person's freedom turn on the intentions of the powerful; one on the authorized-democratic-state corollary, pressing on whether she can avoid making freedom a matter of procedure or consent rather than of what actually happens to a person; one on the objection that if 'arbitrary' is the key term, her theory is either circular or quietly an account of legitimacy rather than of liberty; and one on whether her relational picture can still attribute freedom to individuals at all, or whether it commits her to treating a person's freedom as a fact about a whole society rather than about her. Nothing about her career or what drew her to the topic.
