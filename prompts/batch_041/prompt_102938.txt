I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years in physical chemistry, and since then I've been sitting in on a philosphy night class, which has been great fun and mildly humbling. Last week we did the old sea-battle puzzle — whether the claim that there'll be a naval battle tomorrow is already true or already false today, and whether that means tomorrow is already settled. What struck me is that the whole thing rests on an analogy my old colleagues reached for constantly and that I now realise I never once inspected: that the future is like a landscape you simply haven't driven through yet. The territory is already out there, the roads are already where they are, you just haven't arrived. In my old field we'd have put it as spacetime being a block, everything equally real, so I'll admit the picture feels natural to me — which is maybe exactly why it bothers me that I can't fault it. In the seminar version it comes out as: if the battle is going to happen, then it's already true that it will, in the same way it's already true that it rained yesterday; both are just facts sitting at their own dates. The bloke next to me used this to conclude that there's no point preparing for anything, since if it's going to happen it'll happen either way. I'm suspicous of that, but I can't yet say precisely where the picture goes wrong, and I refuse to just mutter "free will" and wave my hands.

Could you take the analogy seriously as an argument and audit it? Start with a plain correspondance table — what plays the role of the landscape, the map, the traveller, the act of driving, and the word already — then give me the places where the mapping genuinely breaks. Please sort the breaks into the ones that matter for the conclusion my neighbour drew and the ones that are merely cosmetic, because I keep losing that distinction. Two things I'd especially like you to press on: whether the picture quietly assumes that for a statement about tomorrow to be true today there has to be something present today — some fact, or fact-shaped thing — that makes it true, and whether it slides from "one of the two outcomes will happen" to "nothing I do makes any difference." I have a feeling those are different claims wearing the same coat, but I can't get it crisp on my own.

Then the verdict I actually want: does the so-there's-no-point-doing-anything conclusion survive once the disanalogies are laid out, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the analogy is doing illegitimate work, what's a better picture for whatever is genuinely true in this neighbourhood — one that doesn't smuggle in the answer.
