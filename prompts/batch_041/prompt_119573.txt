I am the methods editor at a journal that publishes experimental work on perception, and roughly fifteen times a month a submission arrives claiming that some cognitive state (a belief, an expectation, a category label, a desire, a stereotype) changed what subjects saw. Before I send these to referees I have to decide what the paper is entitled to claim, and at present I do it by feel. I want a written screening procedure I can run in half an hour, and that an editorial assistant with a master's in psychology and no background in this literature can run on a first pass. I sign the decision letter and the authors will argue, so the procedure has to give me a reason I can put in writing.

Take the following as the working distinctions the procedure must respect, since these are the ones doing the work in this area. An effect on what subjects report is not yet an effect on what they see: reports can shift through memory, through the mapping from percept to response scale, through a moved decision criterion, or through subjects reading a cue in the display rather than the stimulus. An effect counts as penetration only when the cognitive state is doing the causal work, not when the experimenter's setup is doing it; that is the difference between a belief changing the percept and a belief changing where subjects look, how long they look, or what they are willing to say. A top-down effect may be indirect, working through attention, through training, or through task strategy, and it is then a further question whether we want to call it penetration at all. The effect has to be on the perceptual state itself, on its content or its character, and the strong version of the claim requires the same stimulus to be experienced differently, which is precisely what a behavioral measure struggles to establish. Finally, an effect driven by the subject's own standing states is more interesting than one driven by a cue the experimenter supplied, and the two should not be run together.

Deliver an ordered procedure. For each step, state the question being asked, what counts as a sufficient answer, which answers stop the process, and which steps are mechanical as opposed to a judgment I am making and should not dress up as otherwise. The procedure should output a verdict about what the evidence can license rather than a bare pass or fail: candidate penetration, post-perceptual effect, design cannot distinguish, or not a perceptual claim at all. Mark the points at which the decision must go to a person, and say what that person is deciding.

Then run it on three submissions. First, hungry subjects rate photographs of food as larger and more saturated than sated subjects do, with unlimited viewing and a rating scale. Second, subjects who have just read a crime report are more likely to report a gun in a briefly flashed, ambiguous image of a hand. Third, a study in which subjects primed with a category label, viewing a suppressed stimulus in a rivalry setup, report the primed object breaking through sooner and more often; this is a real and striking result and I would like to publish it as penetration, but I suspect the procedure will not let me.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which of the distinctions above you had to flatten to make it usable, and what is lost by flattening. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the procedure.
