My friend and I spent yesterday evening arguing about whether a visitor is morally bound by the everyday conventions of the place she is visiting. The dispute began with tipping: she left nothing at a restaurant where servers are expected to be tipped, and my friend said she had wronged the server, while I said she had only been rude. It then spread to queueing, where a visitor who does not recognize the local queue may cut in front without intending any insult, and to jaywalking, where a local who crosses against the light is treated as a minor lawbreaker but a visitor who does the same is often excused as ignorant. What struck me is a pattern I cannot name. These norms are local, variable, and not derivable from any universal moral principle; insiders experience them as binding and resent violations; yet we do not ordinarily think a visitor is morally obligated to know or follow them, even though we may think she is obligated to make a good-faith effort once she is told. The nearest terms I know -- convention, social norm, etiquette, custom -- each seem to miss something: convention suggests mere coordination, etiquette suggests mere politeness, and social norm suggests a descriptive regularity rather than a felt obligation. Is there an established name for this class of norms -- local, not grounded in any consent the visitor gave, backed by resentment, but not universally morally binding -- and, if not, can you coin one and define it, saying exactly how it differs from convention, etiquette, and moral obligation, and where the boundary lies between a visitor's ignorance, her rudeness, and her wrongdoing? I care less about the label than about the carving.
