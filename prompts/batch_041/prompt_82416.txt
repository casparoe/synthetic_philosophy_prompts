I'm a postdoc in philosophy of biology, and in ten days I have to run a single ninety-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate reading group — mostly philosophers, two molecular biologists, and an economist who has wandered in and stayed — on the question of whether DNA is really a code and whether talk of genetic information is doing any work beyond metaphor. I don't want the lecture; I want the plan. Take the role of someone who has taught this material and knows both the biology and the philosophy of scientific metaphor, and begin by thinking through, before you write anything, what actually makes this question hard rather than merely terminological — where the real disagreement lives once you strip out the verbal dispute. Then give me the order in which the ideas must arrive for the question even to become sharp, with the dependency structure made explicit: which piece has to be in place before the next one can be understood at all, and why the obvious alternative ordering fails. I need the one example that carries the whole ninety minutes, the misconceptions the group will walk in with, the exact point in the sequence where each has to be headed off and how, the single question I should put to them at the end that would show me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been nodded at, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to settle at this level and tell them I am leaving out.

Two things I want you to argue with me about rather than simply accept. First, I had planned to make the standard codon table the carrying example, but I now suspect it is precisely the wrong choice: a table is a dictionary, and a dictionary is the picture I am trying to complicate. Settle this for me either way, and if you replace it, specify what the replacement must have and must lack — these people have no biochemistry beyond the central dogma, so it has to be concrete and followable, and it has to break in the right place. Second, I want to end somewhere less familiar than the usual back-and-forth over whether the metaphor is harmless. The line I am drawn to is that the interpretation of the genome is not itself written in the genome: whatever makes a given codon mean a given amino acid is the charging machinery, which is made of protein, which is itself the product of translation — so the code looks less like a property of the DNA molecule than like a convention sustained by an entire apparatus, and the DNA is not a self-interpreting text. I also want to press the fact that the mapping is not context-free and not universal: at least one of the ordinary stop codons can be read as an amino acid when a particular element downstream instructs the ribosome to do so, and the assignments differ between lineages and even between compartments of the same cell. Work out how to get them to that conclusion without lecturing at them, and tell me where the standard framing will have led them astray before I get there.

On misconceptions, I already expect at least these four: that information means Shannon information, so that a high-information signal is assumed to be a meaningful one; that if the code is arbitrary then it is arbitrary, i.e., that convention implies anything goes and nothing constrains the mapping; that genes code for traits rather than for molecules; and that because the code is nearly universal it is therefore necessary rather than contingent. Rank them by how much damage each does to the session, tell me which one has to be dismantled before I can even state the question, and design the closing check so that it uses a case they have not seen — ideally one where they have to say what would have to be true for something to count as coding at all. Don't give me a reading list.
