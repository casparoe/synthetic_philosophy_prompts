I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — since the sequences were still going up — and I'm about to publish a post on my own blog, where most of the readership comes from that same corner of the internet and will notice if I fudge a distinction. The post is about the color phi phenomenon and Dennett's Orwellian/Stalinesque fork. Here's my draft.

The phenomenon: two dots are flashed in succession at different positions on a screen, close enough in time to produce apparent motion, and in different colors — say green first, red second. Subjects report a single dot travelling from the first position to the second and changing color partway along the path. The report has a temporal oddity built into it: the change is experienced as happening at the midpoint of the trajectory, which is before the second flash could have been seen at all. (The experiment, incidentally, was prompted by a question Nelson Goodman put to Kolers about what a color change would do to the phi effect.)

Dennett's fork: either the green is registered, and when the red arrives the record of the green is quietly rewritten so that the subject ends up remembering a color change at the midpoint — the Orwellian hypothesis, the past edited to fit the present — or the brain holds off until both flashes are in and then presents a fully formed story with a falsified timestamp, the Stalinesque hypothesis, the show trial whose verdict was decided before the trial began. Dennett's claim, in the multiple drafts chapter of Consciousness Explained, as I read him, is that these are not rivals about the brain: they agree on the content, the pathway, and the timing, and disagree only about whether to call the point where that content gets fixed pre-experiential or post-experiential. That disagreement presupposes a finish line in the brain where order of arrival becomes order of presentation, and there is no such finish line, so there is no principled basis for choosing between the two.

My objection. Undecidability by the subject's report is not undecidability in principle. Two hypotheses about a mechanism differ only where they predict differently — call that the counterfactual profile. So the question should be whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque architectures have different counterfactual profiles: what happens if the second flash comes earlier, or later; if a third flash follows; if the subject is asked to respond during the interval; if a distractor is interposed. If they diverge under some perturbation, the distinction is empirical after all, and Dennett's argument shows only that the subject's report cannot settle it, not that nothing can. If they never diverge, then the difference between them is a difference in our description rather than in the world — which is Dennett's conclusion, but reached by a route that never mentions the Cartesian theater.

Borrowed idea, which may or may not be doing work. This looks structurally like the problem of ordering events in a system with no global clock: in a distributed system, which of two concurrent events happened first has no answer, and the resolution is not to hunt for the hidden clock but to notice that only the causal relations are real. Dennett's move is that only causal structure is real, and that before-or-after-in-experience is a request for a global timestamp the system never issues. But the analogy worries me in the other direction as well: if coinciding outputs are enough to make two mechanisms one mechanism, the reasoning dissolves distinctions I want to keep.
