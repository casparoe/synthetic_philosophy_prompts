I'm an undergraduate in a political philosophy seminar on historical injustice, and we've spent the past two weeks reading Jaspers on German guilt — which I read as making the point that guilt is not one thing but several, and that a lot of confusion comes from sliding between them. The move that has been doing the most work in our discussions, raised by a classmate and left standing by a professor who clearly found it uncomfortable, is an analogy. A nation's guilt across generations, the argument goes, is like an inherited debt. A state borrows money, the people who authorized the borrowing die, and the grandchildren who never saw a penny still owe it; the debt outlives the debtors because the state, unlike the individuals inside it, keeps going. So, by the same logic, a nation that committed an atrocity can still be guilty long after everyone who did anything is dead, because the guilty party was never really the individuals — it was the nation, and the nation is still here.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on the underlying question. Please lay the correspondence out explicitly — a table would be ideal — saying what is supposed to play the role of the debtor, the creditor, the sum owed, the mechanism by which the obligation passes to heirs, and the entity that persists across a complete turnover of persons. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be careful to separate the disanalogies that matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do from the ones that are merely decorative.

I suspect two of the breaks are not decoration. First, the analogy seems to require that the nation be a single persisting agent that can hold an obligation the way a person can — which is precisely the claim the analogy was introduced to establish, so I want to know whether it is quietly assuming its own conclusion. Second, debts and guilt seem to part company over agency and blame: one can owe money one never borrowed, but it is not obvious that one can be blameworthy for a wrong one never committed, and I want to know whether the argument is trading on guilt in the sense of owing something (reparations, say) or guilt in the sense of deserving reproach. Those come apart, and much of the argument's force seems to depend on sliding between them. A version of the argument spells out the persistence by likening the nation to a corporation or other legal person whose members turn over entirely while the entity remains; if that version is doing work, test it too, because I have a nagging suspicion that the corporate analogy cuts the other way on the question of who is actually liable.

Finally, tell me which features of financial obligation the analogy imports into morality without announcing the import, and say honestly whether the conclusion it was used to support — that later generations can be guilty — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If it supports only a weaker claim than the one drawn, state the weaker claim plainly. And if there is a better analogy for whatever real point was trying to be made here — something about obligations or institutions outliving the people inside them — sketch it and say what it gets right that the debt picture gets wrong.
