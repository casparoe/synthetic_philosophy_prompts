And here's one student's answer:

"(a) The Humean says the world is just a giant mosaic of particular facts — no necessary connections between distinct events. So laws can't be anything over and above the regularities. For Humeans like Lewis, a law is just a true universal generalization: 'all Fs are G' is a law when it's true and non-accidental, and that's all there is to it. The anti-Humean (the necessitarian, e.g. Armstrong) says this leaves out what makes a law lawlike: laws are relations of necessitation between universals. So for Armstrong it isn't just that every F is a G — the property of being F necessitates being G. The whole fight is whether laws add anything to the patterns.

(b) The objection is that the Humean can't use a law to explain its instances, because the law and the regularity are the same fact, so it's circular. The Humean replies that the objection assumes explanation has to run through necessitation, which is exactly what's in dispute, so it begs the question. On the best-system view the law isn't simply 'the regularity' — it's an axiom of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength. And the axioms do explanatory work: we derive the particular regularity from the axioms, and a derivation like that is what explanation is, so the circle is broken.

(c) Here the Humean has to bite a bullet. If laws are just regularities about what actually happens, there's no necessity in them, so they can't tell you what would have happened in counterfactual situations — they only cover the actual world. So the Humean has to deny that laws support counterfactuals, and this is one of the main costs of the view."
