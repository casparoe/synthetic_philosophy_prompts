The question: Distinguish indicative from counterfactual conditionals. Illustrate the distinction with a single pair of conditionals sharing the same antecedent and consequent, explain what the difference between them consists in, and say whether the two kinds require different semantic treatments. (10 pts)

The student's answer:

"The standard illustration is the Oswald pair. 'If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, somebody else did' is the indicative one, and it's true — Kennedy was shot, so if it wasn't Oswald then it was somebody else. 'If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, somebody else would have' is the counterfactual, and it's false — Oswald acted alone, as far as anyone can tell, so there was no second shooter waiting in the wings. Same antecedent, same consequent, opposite verdicts, so the difference has to be semantic rather than merely pragmatic.

What's going on, on the usual story, is that you evaluate the indicative with a Ramsey-style test: drop the antecedent into your beliefs and see whether the consequent then comes out probable. You can't do that with the counterfactual, because its antecedent is false and you can't just add a falsehood to what you believe. Instead you need the Lewis/Stalnaker machinery — a similarity ordering over worlds where the antecedent holds, with the conditional true iff the consequent holds all the way through the nearest of those worlds. That's basically the Adams line: counterfactuals don't have truth conditions, they just register a conditional probability, which is why Lewis went to the trouble of building them a semantics in the first place.

The upshot is that the material conditional is fine for indicatives and a dead end for counterfactuals."
