I have been keeping a private ledger of my verdicts about when a decision made by an earlier self ought to bind the person that self later becomes, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. A man signs a contract at a rehabilitation clinic authorising ninety days of confinement; on the twentieth day, sober and articulate, he demands release: I think we hold him, and I do not think this is merely a matter of contract law. A woman signs a living will while healthy, refusing ventilation and CPR should she ever develop advanced dementia; years later she has advanced dementia and pneumonia, and in her confusion she greets her grandchildren and sometimes says she wants to get better: I think we honour the will and let the pneumonia take its course. A man in the early stages of dementia, still plainly competent, states that once he is far gone he wants no antibiotics for pneumonia; in the far-gone stage he says, cheerfully and repeatedly, that he wants to live: I follow the earlier statement. A woman in a lucid interval revokes her do-not-resuscitate order, then lapses and contracts pneumonia: I honour the revocation, and I feel no conflict with the previous case. A competent adult refuses a blood transfusion on religious grounds: I honour the refusal and would not let a hospital override it. A man signs a directive refusing all future life support while in the grip of a severe depressive episode: I do not honour it. A woman signs a directive refusing ventilation, later joins a coercive sect, and in that state demands every possible intervention: I do not honour the demand. A man signs a directive refusing a leg amputation; years later, still fully competent and now a father, he wants the amputation: I honour the later wish.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the rehabilitation contract against the last two: in the first I bind a competent, contemporaneous self to an earlier choice, while in the amputation and revocation cases I let a competent, contemporaneous self override an earlier choice, and the only difference I can point to is that in the rehabilitation case the present preference is itself the very thing the earlier self was arming himself against. My tentative principle is that the most recent competent self always wins, and where the present self is not competent, the last competent self wins; that saves almost everything, but it fails the rehabilitation case, and I cannot tell whether adding a precommitment clause is a repair or a patch. I am also uneasy that I think a ninety-day commitment binds but a two-year commitment would not, which suggests duration is doing work my principle cannot express.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to cases I have not listed — someone who signs a directive and then suffers a personality-altering brain injury while remaining legally competent; a directive drawn up jointly with a spouse whom the person has since divorced; a patient who asks a physician to enforce a directive over her family's objections — together with an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should therefore give up. I would also like your view on two devices a friend has proposed: a sunset clause, under which a directive lapses after five years unless re-signed, and a two-key rule, under which a revocation counts only if a designated witness co-signs it. Are these principled refinements of whatever principle you land on, or ad hoc machinery that would not be needed if the principle were right?
