Two years ago I retired from a geology department after thirty-eight years of teaching and laboratory work — most of my career was spent measuring the chemistry of igneous rocks — and since then I have been trying to teach myself philosophy, chiefly by auditing graduate seminars at the same university. I can now follow an argument from premise to conclusion, but I have not acquired the ability to referee one, which is why I am writing. Last Tuesday the seminar on mind and language ran forty minutes past its hour because of an exchange between two of its members, and I have been turning it over ever since. The two were Nadia, a computational linguist who builds and evaluates language models, and Peter, a philosopher who has evidently spent years thinking about representation. The argument started from a case I had mentioned myself, over coffee before the session: a text-only system trained on a large archive of geological writing — decades of field notebooks, journal articles, and laboratory records — which could answer questions about igneous rocks more accurately than most of the graduate students in my old department. I said, half joking, that it had read more basalt than I had ever held in my hand. That remark set them going, and what follows is as faithful a record as my notes allow.

(1) Nadia said the system's use of the word basalt is grounded, if grounding means what it ought to mean: the term is answerable to something outside the system. Text is not a closed loop; it is a channel, because the people who wrote the archive were in contact with rocks, and a system that learns the regularities of what competent observers say when they are standing in front of basalt is thereby sensitive to basalt — at one remove, which is precisely the remove at which you and I know the composition of the Moon. (2) Peter said that conflates a channel of information with a relation of reference. A thermostat has a channel to temperature and refers to nothing; a barometer calibrated to print the word rain is not thereby a meteorologist. What fixes reference, he said, is the system's practical commerce with the thing — its capacity to be corrected by the thing itself rather than by further words about it. Define every word in a dictionary by other words and you never leave the page; the model has all the definitions and none of the definienda. It borrows whatever anchors the archive's authors supplied and has none of its own. (3) Nadia replied that he owed an account of what "its own" amounts to which does not simply take the human case as the standard. If the criterion is causal history, then most of his own beliefs fail it, since they arrive from textbooks and colleagues he has never verified. If the criterion is a capacity — to predict what will happen, to act on the term, to be corrected — then it is an empirical question whether the system has it, and the answer is not obviously no: a model that can tell you how a basalt flow will weather, and is right, is doing something very like what my field geologists did with the word, and its advantage or disadvantage is one of data, not of kind. (4) Peter conceded the empirical point and denied that it mattered. Being right is not meaning; the calibrated barometer is right about rain. The crux, he said, is the error signal. A grounded system can be contradicted by the thing itself and registers that contradiction as the world's doing; the model can be contradicted only by more text — by a document, never by a rock. Inheritance will not help, because what is inherited is the word and not the relation: relations of reference are not transferable by description, or the dictionary would have been enough. (5) Nadia then raised what she took to be a fatal counterexample, and it happened to be my own trade. I spent forty years reading numbers off instruments; the rock never spoke to me directly, only through printed outputs, calibration curves, reference standards, and other laboratories' reports. If a chain of symbols cannot terminate in contact with the world, then nothing I believed about any sample I ever analyzed was grounded, which is absurd. If a chain can so terminate, then the archive's chain terminates in the world too, at the hands and eyes of the people who wrote it. (6) Peter said the chain terminated in the sample he had placed in the machine, and the sample was not a symbol; the archive contains no sample, only sentences about samples. He added that the difference is not the length of the chain but who can shorten it: he could, in principle, have walked to the standard and handled it himself, whereas the model can never bring its chain to an end.

I have no stake in the outcome and no idea how to weigh these replies. Please adjudicate the exchange as it was actually made, move by move: for each of the six, say where it falls between clearly mistaken and clearly right, and justify the rating in a sentence or two. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and who is closer to the truth overall. I am especially unsure about Peter's final move, which seemed to turn on a distinction between a chain that the system itself can shorten and a chain that could in principle be shortened by someone else; I could not tell whether that distinction does real work or merely restates his conclusion in a new form.
