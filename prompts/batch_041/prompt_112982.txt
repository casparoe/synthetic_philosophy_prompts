I've been working through the arguments about how we should reason about our own place in the universe, the ones that come up in discussions of the Doomsday argument and the idea that cosmology might be settled from the armchair. I don't have any training in this area — I studied accounting and I've mostly been reading on my own, plus arguing about it with a friend who's a bit more mathematically inclined than I am — so I'm aware I might be missing something that's obvious to people who work on this. A popular book I read recently has a chapter on it, and I found its presentation so clean and memorable that I've been repeating it to people, which is exactly why I want to check it before I keep doing that. Here is how the chapter put it, as best I can reconstruct it: whenever you reason about your own case, you have to assume something about how you were selected, and there are two candidate assumptions. The self-sampling assumption says you should treat yourself as a random observer drawn from all the observers who could possibly exist, so a theory that implies a hundred trillion observers gets a boost over one that implies a hundred billion, because there are more positions you could have occupied. The self-indication assumption says you should treat yourself as a random observer drawn from the observers who actually exist under the theory you're evaluating, so your existence tells you nothing about how many observers there are. The Doomsday argument, on this telling, runs on the self-indication assumption — you find yourself somewhere in the middle of the human sequence, so the total number of humans probably isn't vastly larger than the number born so far, meaning we are almost certainly near the end — while the self-sampling assumption dissolves it. And the Presumptuous Philosopher is the reductio that kills the self-indication assumption: he walks out of his study and announces that he has refuted the small-universe cosmological theory by pure thought, because the big-universe theory contains far more observers and therefore makes his own existence more likely. The chapter's conclusion was that once you see this, the whole puzzle is less alarming than it looks.

Something nags at me about this, though I can't put my finger on it. I have a vague sense that the Doomsday argument is supposed to be the worrying one, and that the book has made it come out reassuring instead, which seems backwards.
