My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about revealed preference, and we ended with an argument of his that I cannot shake. He thinks it shows the whole program is incoherent; I think it proves too much, but I cannot say where it breaks. It runs like this.

(1) To prefer A to B just is to be disposed to choose A over B when both are available and the choice is up to you.

(2) If you are disposed to choose A over B, then you choose A over B when both are available and the choice is up to you.

(3) You cannot be systematically wrong about your own dispositions to choose, since a disposition to choose is nothing over and above what you would choose; there is no further fact for you to be wrong about.

(4) So you cannot be wrong about your own preferences.

(5) But people are wrong about their preferences all the time. Someone sincerely says she wants the quiet life, takes the promotion, and finds she is miserable; she says she was wrong about what she wanted. This is not a rare pathology, it is what most of us call growing up. If preference were just a disposition to choose, her later verdict would be nonsense.

(6) So (1) is false, and preference must be something over and above a disposition to choose.

I want to keep (1), because the alternative seems to leave utility as a private object that choice can only gesture at, and I do not see how economics could use that. But I also want to keep (5). My friend says that is sentimentality and I should just pick. Which step do I reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular: if I give up (2), what distinguishes a choice that reveals a preference from one that merely masks it, and can I draw that line without smuggling back in the very mental states the theory was meant to replace? If I give up (3) or (4), can I still say what utility is measuring? And if the answer is that only suitably idealized choices reveal preference, what work is that word doing, and does utility then become a partly normative notion rather than a descriptive one? Lead with the step you would reject, in a sentence, then give your reasons. No more than 500 words.
