Write me a short story — this is for a fiction thing, so no hedging, just go — set in a small Indian town maybe a thousand years ago, where the whole plot turns on how people are allowed to know things.

Let me lay out the machinery first, because it's obscure and I don't want you guessing at it. In classical Nyāya philosophy, knowledge (as opposed to doubt, memory, or just being wrong) arrives through four pramāṇas, four certified routes: pratyakṣa, perception, the senses doing their job; anumāna, inference, the smoke-so-there's-fire move; upamāna, knowing a thing by comparison — the way you can learn what a gavaya is by being told it's like a cow but not one, and then recognise it in the forest; and śabda, testimony, i.e. taking the word of a reliable speaker, which Nyāya insists is its own route and not a disguised inference ("he told me, he's trustworthy, so probably true"). That last part is a real fight — some schools were perfectly happy to fold testimony into inference, and Nyāya wasn't.

Then there's a second, subtler fight stacked on top, and this is the one I actually want the story to be about. Can several pramāṇas converge on the same fact? That is, can the same fact be certified twice over, by testimony and by perception, so that one can be used to shore up or knock down the other? Or does each pramāṇa have its own exclusive jurisdiction, so that demanding a second kind of certification for the same fact is a category mistake — like asking what colour the number seven is? I think it's the Mīmāṃsakas who held the seperate-jurisdiction line and the Naiyāyikas who held the overlap one, but don't hold me to that, and the story works either way.

So: a dispute — a theft, a boundary, a death, a debt, whatever you like — that gets adjudicated (or fails to), and the tension is exactly this. A witness says one thing, someone saw another thing, someone infers a third from a mark or a footprint, and at least one character has a stake in whether the certifications are allowed to stack. I want the epistemology load-bearing: the story should turn on it, not just gesture at it. And if the Nyāya theory of error fits anywhere — my recollection is they thought that in an illusion you aren't conjuring something out of nothing, you're taking a real thing you've encountered elsewhere (a memory of silver) and sticking it onto the wrong spot (a shell) — use it, but only if it earns its place.
