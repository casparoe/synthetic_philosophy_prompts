I retired from the bench a few years back — physical chemistry, thirty-odd years of it — and since then I've been working my way through philosophy on my own, mostly the philosophy of mind and the AI stuff. Which has somehow landed me in the position of giving a one-hour session at our local adult-ed place next month, on the frame problem. Twenty or so people, mostly retired professionals, a couple of ex-engineers, nobody who has done any formal logic, and they'll all be walking in assuming it's going to be about robots taking over.

I don't want you to explain it to me, I want a plan for how I explain it to them. So: what has to be in place before the problem can even be stated as a problem? I've got a hunch you can't say what the problem is until they've already accepted that the machine's knowledge of the world has to be written down somewhere in the first place, and that in a setup like that nothing is presumed to stay put unless you say so. Tell me whether that's the right first move or whether something else has to come before it, and why the pieces have to go in that order rather than the one that seems natural to me.

The carrying example is the part I'm least sure about. The usual one seems to be blocks on a table, and I suspect that one is so well-worn that it does half its work through familiarity and none of it by making anybody think. My own instinct is to use something from my old line of work — you write down rate equations for the species you care about, and every species you don't mention is silently assumed inert. Nobody ever pays for that assumption; it's free. Will that carry it, or does it smuggle in exactly the thing that needs to be made strange? And if it won't carry it, what would — what does the example have to have, and what does it have to lack? (I'd also like a verdict on whether the one with the turkey and the gun is worth ten minutes or is just too cute.)

Then the misconceptions, which I'd like mapped rather than listed. My guesses: that its just a speed problem, solved by faster chips; that it's the same thing as the qualification problem (I only half understand the difference myself, so correct me); that frame here means the Minsky sort of frame, because of the word; that the logicians had it tidied up by the end of the eighties, so there's nothing left worth caring about; and in the other direction, that it proves machines can never have common sense. Where in the sequence should each of those be headed off — and which of them am I simply wrong to expect at all?

Last thing. I want the back half to be about the difference between having to write down everything that doesn't change and having to know what's worth thinking about, because my sense is that that's where the real weight is, and I'd like the first half built so the distinction arrives as a relief rather than a jolt. So tell me the order, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and give me one question to put to them at the end that would show me whether they've actually got it or are just nodding along.
