I cover science and ideas, and I am deep into a long feature on the fight over what an emotion actually is. The trouble is that every camp has rigged the question. The researchers who favor evolved basic emotions define the target by its facial expressions and bodily signatures; the appraisal theorists define it by evaluations of what matters to us; the constructionists define it by learned concepts and the body's predictions. Each camp's criteria turn out to be precisely the ones its own answer happens to satisfy. Before I can referee any of this in print, I want the neutral specification: the desiderata that any adequate theory of emotion has to meet, whichever camp it comes from.

Give me that list. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that puts it there; say which distinctions the theory has to respect (between an emotion and a mood, between having an emotion and feeling one, between a component of an emotion and the whole episode); and be explicit about which requirements are genuine constraints and which are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement. I am thinking especially of the demands that an emotion be consciously felt, that it have an object, and that the same emotions be recognizable across cultures, but you should decide which of those survive.

Here are the awkward cases I have been collecting while reporting. Treat them as constraints the theory has to accommodate, not as puzzles to solve one at a time:

- Emotions that come apart from their usual parts: people who show the entire behavioral and physiological signature of fear, the flinch and the freeze and the stress hormones, while reporting that they feel nothing, and the mirror case of people who describe overwhelming fear while every bodily measure stays flat.
- Emotions with no object: the chills that music produces, or a dread that fastens onto nothing in particular.
- Collective emotion: the dread that moves through a full stadium, or a country's grief after a disaster. I cannot tell whether there is a fact of the matter about the group's emotion over and above the emotions of its members.
- An emotion with no name and no precedent: someone describing a feeling that matches none of the recognized emotion words, that they have never felt before and have no script for.
- Emotion in a creature we are not sure has any inner life at all: a fish or an insect whose avoidance and aggression look like fear and anger but may be nothing of the kind.

Two things I want from the list in particular. First, flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. If the specification turns out to be inconsistent, that is the story, and I would rather find it now than after four thousand words. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule theories out. You can skip the full scorecard of which camp wins; I will do that in the piece. Keep the reasons attached to each requirement, since the reasoning is what I am after.
