I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or pure mathematics, and for the last year I've been running a monthly lunchtime discussion session at my company — a dozen or so working programmers, a couple of whom have maths degrees, most of whom have not thought about sets since school. Next month's topic is infinity, and I've committed to getting the room from "infinity is just a really big number" to a working grip on the difference between a potential infinity and an actual one, including why anyone would refuse to accept the latter. I don't want you to explain infinity to me; I want you to plan how I should explain it. Please take the role of someone who has taught this material many times to mathematically competent but philosophically innocent adults, think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the candidate routes through the session and telling me which ones you are rejecting and why, before you give me the plan itself. I have forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no internet in the room, so please don't give me a reading list or citations.

Concretely, I need five things. First, the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependency made explicit: what has to be in place before the next thing can even be stated, and what can safely be postponed or dropped altogether. Second, the one example or case that carries the whole session and why it should be that one. My instinct is Hilbert's hotel, but I'm worried it has become a party trick that half the room has already seen in a YouTube video and that it makes the conclusion look like a conjuring result rather than something argued for; my other instinct is the diagonal argument, because the same shape turns up in the halting-problem proof that every programmer in the room has met, and I am tempted to make that parallel the spine of the session. Tell me honestly whether either of those should carry it, and if not, what the carrying example must have and must not have — for instance, it must not quietly presuppose the answer to the question it is supposed to make vivid — and give me the example you would use instead. Third, the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or form, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I am already predicting that part of the room will think the pairing argument is a cheat ("sure, you can line them up, but that doesn't show they're the same size"), that another part will have absorbed from functional programming the idea that an infinity is just a computation that never finishes and will therefore hear "actual infinity" as a category error rather than a thesis, and that finitism will be treated as either a joke or a religious position; I want to know what I am not predicting. Fourth, one question I can put to the room at the end that would genuinely tell me whether the potential/actual distinction has landed, as opposed to whether they can recite that the rational numbers are countable. Fifth, what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level and what I should say out loud about why — I have a colleague who will certainly ask about the axiom of choice and about whether the physical universe contains an actual infinity, and I would rather decline gracefully than bluff or shut the question down.
