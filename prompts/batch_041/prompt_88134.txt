I am a retired astrophysicist — forty years of observational work, much of it spectroscopy — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy and sitting in on a small reading group that has spent the last few months on what is now generally called the dark forest hypothesis, the argument that came out of a widely read science fiction novel and has since been picked up as a serious proposal about galactic civilisation. The argument is almost always presented to me through a comparison rather than on its own footing. One member of the group works on international relations, and he puts it this way: the galaxy is simply Hobbes's state of nature written large — many independent actors, no common power over any of them, none able to know the intentions of the others, each therefore driven to anticipate the others' moves, and the upshot being a war of every party against every other in which the only rational policies are to hide and, when hiding fails, to strike first. He treats this comparison as licensing the entire conclusion: universal silence, no contact, pre-emptive destruction of anything detected. Once the mapping is in place, he thinks, there is nothing left to argue about.

I would like you to audit the comparison rather than the fiction. Concretely, give me the correspondence table item by item: on the Hobbesian side, what plays the role of the parties, of their rough equality of ability, of the absence of a common power, of the fear and anticipation that follows, and of the covenant or sovereign that would end the condition; on the dark forest side, what plays the role of the chain of suspicion, of the technological explosion, of the finitude of resources, and of detection and concealment. Then tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion he wants and which are merely picturesque.

I am particularly interested in three things I suspect he has not considered. First, in the international-relations literature the severity of the security dilemma is supposed to depend on the offence-defence balance, and I want to know what corresponds to that variable in the galactic case and whether the dark forest argument contains any independent premise that fixes it. Second, actual states are in a repeated game — they trade, negotiate, keep and break treaties, and meet one another again — whereas two civilisations that detect each other may interact exactly once; I cannot tell whether this one-shot structure strengthens his conclusion or instead destroys the analogy to any practice that international-relations theorists have actually studied. Third, the remedies those theorists draw from the security dilemma are signalling, institutions, deterrence, and shrinking the offence advantage, not first strikes, so I want to know whether the analogy taken at face value licenses his conclusion or the opposite one.

Finally, does the conclusion survive the audit? Commit to an answer rather than surveying the options. If the dark forest can be supported only by premises that the Hobbesian comparison does not supply, identify those premises and say whether they are independently plausible. If a better comparison is available — one that carries more of the argument and fewer of the false entailments — supply it and say precisely what it drops. I would rather have a definite verdict with its reasons laid out; where the verdict is close, say so and say what would move you. I am not asking for a literature review.
