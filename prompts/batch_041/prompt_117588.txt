I'm a science and ideas writer, and for the past three years I've been the reporter who actually sits through the meetings of our national biosafety panel — the body that decides whether anyone gets to put an engineered organism, or a new chemical, out into the world. I'm filing a piece on next month's agenda item and I definately keep flip-flopping, so I want your read on what they'll do.

Their record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- Approved a blight-resistant engineered chestnut trial, on the conditions that it couldn't flower for a decade and that they could bulldoze the plot. Nobody showed it was safe. They approved anyway.
- Blocked dumping a chemical in a lake to kill an invasive fish, though nobody showed it would hurt anything else. The stuff would persist for decades, and the panel wasn't willing to treat we-have-no-evidence-of-harm as we-know-it's-harmless.
- Approved a livestock antibiotic that some modelers said would breed resistance, reasoning that the resistance pathway was speculative and that healthier animals is a real good. So speculation by itself doesn't stop them.
- Rejected a permit to release a gene-edited moth — low estimated likelihood of harm, but if it spread you could never call it back.
- Allowed a wildlife vaccine trial on an island: five years of monitoring, a commitment to stop if anything turned up, and, crucially, everything was undoable.
- Blocked introducing a predator for pest control when the ecological models contradicted each other.
- Approved a drug with a rare but nasty side effect, because the disease it treated usually killed you. The harm of doing nothing was in the ledger.

Next month: a team wants to release a vaccine against a disease that's wiping out a native mammal, and the vaccine is engineered to pass from animal to animal on its own — you inoculate a handful and it keeps going. No evidence it would hurt anything, no evidence it wouldn't, and nobody's ever done it at this scale. If it goes somewhere you don't like, there's no recall. If it works, the species probably survives.

So: what does the panel do? I want the rule, not a coin flip. My own hunch is that everyone in that room is some kind of precautionary thinker, but the word is doing at least three different jobs — a rule about who has to prove what, a rule about which kinds of harm count, and a tiebreaker that just tips toward the status quo when the science is thin. Which of those actually fits their record, what does each predict here, and which of their past calls is the one that really settles it? If they go the other way, what does that tell me they'd been assuming all along? And what would you put in front of them to make them show their hand?
