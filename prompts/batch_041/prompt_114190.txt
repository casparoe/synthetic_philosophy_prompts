In central-bank and finance-ministry policy analysis, one practice has become close to mandatory: any statement about the economy as a whole must be derived from the choices of individual households and firms, and a model in which aggregate quantities are taken to obey laws of their own — consumption growing at some rate, investment responding to an accelerator — is regarded as unrigorous unless those aggregate relations can be shown to fall out of individual optimizing behavior. The standard vehicle is a model in which the entire economy is a single infinitely-lived household that owns the capital, supplies the labor, and saves, so that the aggregate is that household writ large. The same institutions do something that looks like the opposite: in public analysis they attribute beliefs, expectations, and moods to the market as such, treat business confidence as a causal factor in its own right, and describe the economy as recovering, overheating, or losing momentum, as though the aggregate had states and dispositions of its own rather than being a summary of what individuals do. And when the question turns to distribution, the single household quietly disappears, with the total change in consumption treated as the thing to be maximized, as if the identity of gainers and losers did not enter.

What does this combination of practices presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, with the following worked through. First, which view of social explanation is actually built into the modeling practice: the thesis that social facts must be explained in terms of individuals and their actions, or something weaker — that only individuals exist, while aggregate-level regularities may still do explanatory work? Second, is the single-household device a commitment to the existence of an aggregate agent, or a computational convenience that is in principle compatible with thoroughgoing individualism? Defend a reading rather than listing options. Third, does the practice strictly require the presupposition, or would it survive under either the individualist or the holist reading, so that it is philosophically underdetermined — and if the latter, what in the practice is doing the work that the metaphysics is credited with? Fourth, is the package internally consistent, or does the austere individualist construction sit in genuine contradiction with the holist-sounding public analysis and with the aggregation in the welfare step? Fifth, consider a proposal someone might make: call it asymmetric individualism — individual actions are the only causes, yet aggregate-level predicates are irreducible in explanation, because the map from the distribution of individual characteristics to aggregate outcomes fails to preserve counterfactuals, so that a small change in the distribution can produce a large change in the aggregate with no counterpart at the level of any individual. Is that coherent, or is it holism under another name, and would the practice need it? Sixth, what would have to change — in the models, in the welfare analysis, and in what gets said in public — if the presupposed view were replaced by its individualist or its holist rival, and what would be lost in each case?

Give me a verdict rather than a survey: say which reading you think is correct, how confident you are, and where you are guessing. Tell me which of the questions above is doing the most work in your answer. If the inconsistency I am pointing at is not really an inconsistency — if it is just the ordinary division of labor between a tractable model and a persuasive speech act — then say so plainly rather than manufacturing a reconciliation. I would rather be told that my framing is confused than receive a tidy answer to a badly posed question.
