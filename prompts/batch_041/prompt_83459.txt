I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and in two weeks I'll be interviewing, for our department's graduate podcast, a decision theorist who has spent a decade arguing that Good's theorem -- the result that acquiring costless information cannot lower a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer's expected utility -- is more than a bookkeeping artifact. My listeners are graduate students in the field, so I don't want her simply restating the theorem; I want questions that locate where it does real work and where it quietly does none. Draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, covering: whether the theorem is substantive or close to analytic given how the value of information is defined; what becomes of it when a signal does not merely refine an existing partition but adds states or options the agent had not conceived of; the counterexamples that are said to arise for agents who do not maximize expected utility (maximin or ambiguity-averse preferences) and for decision theories on which one's act is itself evidence about the state; and the gap between the ex ante guarantee and any particular one-shot decision, including whether the theorem really supports the principle of total evidence once thinking is costly. Where a question of mine presupposes something contested -- especially if I have mis-described one of those counterexamples -- flag it, and tell me how confident you are in your characterizations of the theorem and its failure conditions, singling out the point you are least sure of.
