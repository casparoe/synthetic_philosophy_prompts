I took early retirement from soil microbiology about three years ago and have been making up for lost time on philosophy — I'm most of the way through an online intro-to-logic course, and I've gotten myself throughly stuck on the one slide the lecturer waved past, saying Skolem's paradox isn't really a paradox and moving on. A friend from my reading group and I spent an entire evening building out what we think the paradox really shows, and I want someone to tell me where it breaks, because the conclusion we reached can't be right and neither of us can find the seam.

Here's our chain. The set-up: ZFC is a first-order theory, and downward Löwenheim–Skolem says any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable one; and ZFC proves there are uncountable sets, which is Cantor. (1) So if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model M. (2) Since ZFC proves that an uncountable set exists, and M satisfies ZFC, M satisfies that an uncountable set exists. (3) Every set in M is a subset of M's domain, and M's domain is countable. (4) Every subset of a countable set is countable. (5) So the very set M calls uncountable is countable. (6) So that set is both countable and uncountable; countability just isn't an absolute property, it's relative to the model you're standing in. (7) So nothing is uncountable in itself — Cantor's theorem tells us what some model says, not what's actually true. (8) So the reals aren't really bigger than the naturals, and set theory isn't describing a determinate world at all.

I can live with (6) — the relativity is honestly the part I find beautiful, and it matches my instinct from science that alot depends on your frame. But (7) and (8) seem to prove far too much: if there are no uncountable sets, then Löwenheim–Skolem itself and the whole business of comparing sizes stop meaning anything, and I refuse to believe mathematics is just a bag of fictions. My friend's fix is to give up (1) and stop talking about models of ZFC at all, which feels like burning the house down to fix the wiring. So: which step actually breaks, why did it look fine when we wrote it down, and what do I have to pay if I won't give it up? If the honest answer is that I have to choose between keeping (5) and keeping any absolute notion of size, say so, and tell me what that leaves me standing on.

Please finish with a last line that begins "Verdict:" and then gives the number of the step you'd reject (or the smallest range, e.g. "6-7") followed by a label of five words or fewer for the error.
