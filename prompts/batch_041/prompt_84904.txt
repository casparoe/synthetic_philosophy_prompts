For about a year I have been in a reading group on moral worth and on acting for the right reasons, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of why the dispute will not settle, not another survey of the positions.

The landscape as we have it. One camp holds that an act has moral worth only if the agent does the right thing because it is right: the operative motive must be duty, or the agent's recognition of the act's rightness, so that the familiar case of the boy who helps an escaped slave while believing he is doing something wicked is, at best, a puzzle about praiseworthiness rather than an instance of worth. A second camp holds that worth attaches to being moved by the very considerations that in fact make the act right, whether or not the agent conceives of them under that description; on this view a morally mistaken but loving agent can act with full worth, and the first camp has fetishized the agent's self-conception. A third position insists that the debate conflates the worth of an act with the credit due an agent, and that the two come apart. Everyone in the group accepts the same cases and the same intuitions — including about the shopkeeper who is honest from prudence — and each side has a standing reply to each of the other's moves.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that the camps disagree about no case at all, but about what the concept is for. One side wants moral worth to mark the distinctive value of acting from the recognition of duty, and so to explain why morality can require that we act from duty and not merely in conformity with it; the other wants it to mark the goodness of the will, and so to ground praise and blame and to be available to agents who are morally mistaken. If that is right, the stalemate is two inquiries sharing one heading, and its persistence is exactly what we should expect.

Here is a case I have not seen discussed, and which I think pressures both camps. Mira reliably does the right thing, but only by deference: a mentor she trusts tells her what to do, she does it, and she has no grasp of why it is right; told otherwise, she would act otherwise. Her act is right, and its rightness is not accidental — the mentor is reliable — but the right-making features never enter her will, only the causal history of her act. The duty camp must say she acts from deference rather than duty, and so without worth. The right-reasons camp must either say she does act for the right reasons, because the right features reliably guide her, or that she does not, because nothing in her will tracks them; each answer costs it something.

So: diagnose the impasse and say what kind of disagreement it is — about cases, about the function of a concept, about whether normative accuracy is required of a worthy agent, or about nothing beneath the words; tell me whether my hypothesis survives contact with Mira; and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a case, a change of question — or why none should be expected.
