I am preparing a seminar on the drinking sections of Plato's Laws — the Athenian's long and deliberately perverse defence of the symposium in Books I and II — and I want Edward Albee's Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1962) to serve as the case study, because the play is very nearly the institution Plato is theorising, with every safety rail removed.

Work from the following account of the doctrine rather than from whatever you recall about Plato and wine; the Symposium is not the relevant text. The Athenian rejects the ordinary verdict that drunkenness is simply a vice, and defends the drinking party as an instrument of moral education. Education, for him, is training in pleasure and pain; the gods gave human beings festivals, with the Muses and Dionysus as companions in the dance, as a respite from human misery. Wine is Dionysus's gift, and its characteristic effect is to fill a man with a sense of his own power and to make him audacious, so that he loses all scruple about what he says and does. That is exactly what makes the symposium useful: it is a cheap and safe "playful method of inspection" that shows what a man actually is, unlike the alternatives — testing a man's continence by entrusting him with your wife and children, say — which are costly and damaging. Wine is also a medicine, "potent against the crabbedness of old age": the young are to be kept from intoxication, while a man of forty may join the revel, so that through forgetfulness of care the temper of his soul may lose its hardness and become softer and more ductile, "even as iron when it has been forged in the fire" — a softened soul being easier to shape, and more willing to sing the chants the lawgiver wants sung. But because wine dissolves shame, the institution requires a governor: there must be "sober and sedate men to act as commanders over the un-sober," and the noble fear of shame must be brought in against the ignoble audacity that drink produces. A symposium with no sober ruler is not the institution at all. (The quoted phrases are from Bury's translation.)

Now read the play as a symposium held under precisely the conditions Plato forbids. Its three acts are titled Fun and Games, Walpurgisnacht and The Exorcism; the evening is organised as named games, George's being Get the Guests; the hosts set the pace at the bar; and the one man present who might have kept order is drunk, out of his depth, and being played with. Plato promises an inspection of the soul that costs nothing. Albee's inspection costs a career, a marriage, a pregnancy that never happened, and a son who never existed.

Where does the play bear Plato out, and where does it break him? The hardest question I want answered is this. Plato's inspection presupposes a determinate soul behind the mask, which wine exposes and then softens so that it can be educated. In the play, what the night finally strips away is not a hidden self but the story that the self was propping itself up with. If what is revealed is a fiction, does the Platonic model of the drinking party as self-revelation still apply, or has Albee produced a case the Laws cannot describe? And if a softened soul needs a chant to be shaped by, what becomes of the institution when the only chant on offer is a taunt?

I also want the honest caveat. Albee is not writing about drink, and a critic who treats the whole play as a dramatisation of the Laws is doing it some violence. Say where the violence is worst, and which scenes the lens genuinely illuminates — I would nominate Get the Guests, the seduction Martha stages for George's benefit, and the last act's killing-off of the son, but tell me if I have picked wrong.

Close by saying what the play contributes to the question the Laws cannot settle by itself: whether the drinking party is an education at all, or only a way of discovering what a person is — and how that question changes when the party is a marriage. No references needed; I want the reading, not the bibliography.
