I am a retired soil scientist who has spent the last several years reading in the philosophy of science, and I have a request of an unusual kind. I would like you to write a joke about Simpson's paradox — a joke that a statistically literate audience would find genuinely funny because the punchline turns on the actual content of the phenomenon rather than merely gesturing at it. To keep it from collapsing into the stock example everyone has already heard, please build it around the following two-part observation, which I arrived at through my own field's data.

First, imagine a trial comparing a fertilizer against a control on crop yield, with fields classified by soil type. Pooled, the fertilizer looks worse; within every soil type it looks better. Here the pooled comparison is the misleading one, because soil type is a common cause of both which treatment a field received and how well it yielded.

Second, imagine an arrangement with identical arithmetic in which soil type is instead a common effect of the fertilizer and the yield — the fertilizer alters soil chemistry, and a high yield alters it too, so that certain soil types arise only when both are present. Stratifying by soil type now manufactures an association that is not there, and the pooled comparison is the defensible one.

I would like the joke to turn on the fact that these two situations present the same numbers and opposite verdicts, and that nothing in the data themselves tells you which situation you are in. If it helps, you might let the characters include a statistician who insists the numbers are simply ambiguous and a colleague who demands to know what kind of variable soil type is; and, if you can work it in without overloading the joke, let someone observe that for certain measures — an odds ratio, say — a reversal of this sort can arise from the arithmetic alone, with no confounder anywhere in sight. The resolution I am after is neither purely statistical nor purely causal but a matter of what question was being asked.
