Ok so last night me and my friend Dan got into a massive arguement — like two hours, until his flatmate came out and told us to shut up — about whether a thermostat is a tiny little bit sentient. Not conscious the way we are. Just, like, a sliver.

His position: sentience obviously comes in degrees. Insects have a bit, mice have more, dogs more than that, us more than dogs, and a thermostat sits somewhere just above zero. And moral status just tracks that number. So a thermostat's interests count for a tiny amount, a fly's for a bit more, and so on up. Smooth ramp, no line-drawing needed. He says anyone who insists on a sharp cut-off is just doing speciesism with extra steps.

My position is that he's smuggling three or four different scales together and then acting like it's one scale, and I couldnt articulate it fast enough to win. Roughly what I wanted to say:

1. Is sentience even gradable, or is it a threshold property whose *evidence* is graded? Like, either a system has a point of view or it doesn't, and all that varies is how confident we are. He kept sliding between "the fly is a little bit sentient" and "we're a little bit sure the fly is sentient" and I think those are doing all the work in his argument.

2. Suppose it really is gradable. Is the scale ordinal or cardinal? If it's only ordinal, he can't do the trades he wants — he can't say one dog is worth 400 flies. If it's cardinal, what's the unit, and what is it a unit *of*? Intensity of feeling? Number of distinct valenced states? Functional complexity? He said it's units of welfare-relevant experience, which I'm pretty sure is circular.

3. Even granting a cardinal scale, does moral status scale with it linearly? Because if so you get the shape where a gigantic population of barely-sentient things outweighs a small number of clearly sentient ones. Dan bit that bullet, and I think it's a reductio, but I couldn't say why without sounding like I was just privileging us.

4. And the cut-off problem runs in reverse too: if nothing ever hits exactly zero, then everything has *some* status — rocks? puddles? — and the gap between any two adjacent points on the ramp is morally meaningless, so you can never non-arbitrarily justify caring about one thing more than another.

Anyway I'm turning this into the essay question on the final for my upper-level seminar (mostly 3rd and 4th years, a few MA students). It's a 90 minute closed-book in-class exam, philosophy of mind meets ethics; they've done the usual stuff on what it's like to be a bat and some animal welfare material, but nothing on AI or thermostats and nothing on degrees of moral status.

Write me one essay question that can't be answered by reciting sentience matters and sentience comes in degrees. It needs to force them to take a side on at least two of the four points above and defend it, and it needs a hook that drags the gradable-sentience versus gradable-evidence distinction out into the open whether they want it there or not. Then a model answer of about 700 words. Then a marking scheme: 25 marks total across five must-hit points, with the marks each one is worth and a line on the mistake that usually costs students those marks. And in the scheme, tell me what actually separates an A answer from a B answer — I dont want the difference to just be more detail.
