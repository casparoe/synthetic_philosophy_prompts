You are a philosopher of decision theory who has taught money-pump arguments many times and thinks hard about how they actually land. I am leading a ninety-minute session on money pumps and diachronic rationality for a mixed room: economics graduate students fluent in expected utility, plus a few philosophers who are not. Do not explain the argument to me; plan how I should teach it. Work through the dependencies carefully before you answer, and open with the list of conceptual prerequisites you think the session has to respect.

Give me the order the pieces have to go in — what must be established before the pump even looks like an argument, and where the synchronic/diachronic distinction has to enter so that the temporal structure is not mere decoration. Then tell me which single case should carry the session. My candidate is a self-control case: someone who each morning would pay a dollar to keep her afternoon self from being offered a slice of cake, and each afternoon would pay a dollar to get the offer back. Say whether that case can carry the weight, and if it cannot, specify what the carrying example must have and must lack instead. Assume the room has already met the three-good preference cycle and will find it too schematic to do any work.

Then map the misconceptions in the order they will surface. I expect: that the pump only shows intransitive preferences to be irrational; that it is a prediction about what agents will in fact do rather than a claim about rationality; that the victim must be repeatedly fooled, so the argument depends on the exploiter's cleverness; and that since each individual trade is one the agent endorses at the time, she is not really worse off in any sense that matters. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off and how. Close with what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes, and one question to put to the room at the end that would separate someone who has understood the argument from someone who has merely memorized it.
