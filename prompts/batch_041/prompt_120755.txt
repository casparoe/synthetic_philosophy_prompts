Ok so some background: I've been reading rationalist blogs for like fifteen years now (LessWrong back in the day, then SSC/ACX, occasionally the EA forum when I'm feeling brave), and through a chain of bad decisions I'm now one of two people who actually runs moderation for a ~4000 person Discord plus the associated subreddit. It's small time — maybe 5-10 real decisions a week — but they're the kind of decisions where everyone feels like the answer is obvious and everyone disagrees about what it is: should we add a rule against low-effort dunking, does this person get a timeout for something they said in a DM, do we let a popular member back in early. And I've noticed our actual process is "the loudest mod names a principle and we all nod," which is bad, and I keep half-remembering Kant's universalizability test from a phil 101 class and wondering if we could just... use it.

So: take Kant's universalizability test and turn it into a procedure my co-mod and I can actually run on a proposal, in 20 minutes, without either of us having any real philosophy training. Not a summary of the theory — a procedure. I want it to include:

- The steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, and where it just stops with a no.
- A step zero that gets from the thing on the table (a rule, a one-off ban, an exception) to a maxim, since I gather the test applies to maxims and not actions, and that seems to be where all the actual work is. Give me a rule of thumb for how much description goes into the maxim, because I can already see the two ways to cheat — describe it so specifically that nothing ever fails, or so generally that everything does — and a specific trick for catching the too-specific dodge, like restating the maxim with one more excuse clause bolted on and seeing if we'd still accept that version.
- Something about the difference between maxims of doing a thing and maxims of not-doing a thing, because I have a suspicion those come out really differently and nobody ever mentions it.
- The two failure modes (can't even think the universalized version / can think it but couldn't will it) kept distinct, with an honest note on which one is doing most of the work and how soft it is.
- A separate pass with the humanity formula, plus a verdict on whether that's a genuinely different test or the same test wearing a hat.
- A tripwire for when we've drifted into "if everyone did this the consequences would be bad," which I'm pretty sure is just rule-consequentialism and not the test at all.

Then run it on two or three cases — I'd suggest a rule allowing small white lies to spare people's feelings, and a mod breaking a promise of confidentiality to warn someone, and honestly any third one where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like, because if it never does that then it isn't doing anything. Finish with what the procedure can't do and which points are just judgment calls dressed up as steps. No citations or reading lists, please, I'm not going to read them.

One more thing: tell me where you think this is most likely to be bullshit — which step is most likely to be me and my co-mod fooling ourselves — and what would change your mind about the verdict. If there's some consideration that would make you say "no, don't run this test here, use something else," say what it is.

American English, please.
