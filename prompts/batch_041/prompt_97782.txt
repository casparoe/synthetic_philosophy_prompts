I am a novelist, and the book I am now planning turns on the question of who, if anyone, should be held responsible for the First World War. One strand follows a mid-level official in the German foreign ministry through the July Crisis; a second follows a British infantryman who enlists in 1915 believing he is answering an unprovoked aggression; a third is set decades later, in the aftermath of the Fischer controversy, where a family argues over the claim that Germany bore the primary responsibility for the war. I want the book to earn its moral weight rather than gesture at it, which means I need to know which questions in that debate are genuinely load-bearing and which are the ones everyone reaches for merely because they are familiar.

I have perhaps a year of reading before I draft, and only so much of the novel can be given over to staging an argument, so I cannot foreground everything. The open questions I keep circling are these: what the proper unit of responsibility is — state, government, a governing elite, the nation as such, or particular individuals; whether being responsible for a war is the same thing as being guilty of it; how much weight counterfactual claims of the form "this could have been prevented" can actually bear; whether the Fischer thesis is really a claim about the outbreak of the war or about its aims; and how a verdict on 1914 ought to bear on the settlement that followed or on later German history. I have also been working with a three-way distinction of my own — authorship (who set the process in motion), intention (who wanted the war), and liability (who should bear its costs) — and I suspect that much of the confusion in the debate comes from sliding between these without noticing.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Of the questions above, which two or three should I actually foreground in the novel, and in what order? For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others — which questions become easier, or simply moot, once it is settled — how tractable it is for a writer with no archive and no German, and what would change in the book if I got it wrong. Assess my three-way distinction as well: does it do real work, or does it collapse under pressure? Then tell me which of these questions is the one that everyone overrates, the one that feels indispensable but that, on close inspection, would not change what a careful writer puts on the page. Finally, because a novel must take a stance even where the history does not, say where the remaining disagreement is genuine rather than merely unresolved, and what it would take for me to be entitled to a verdict rather than a hedge.
