Two friends of mine, sisters, jointly inherited their parents' cabin on a lake, and they now have to decide together whether to keep it or sell it. One of them, Mara, wants to keep it, and not for the reasons a realtor would give: she says the cabin is the last place the family was whole, and that putting a dollar figure on it is already a kind of betrayal. The other, Ines, wants to sell it and put the money toward causes she believes are urgent; she says attachment to a building is a luxury she can't justify while the money could do real good. They asked me to sit in while they talked, and I mostly stayed quiet, but the argument went well past the cabin and I've been turning it over since. I'd like it taken apart step by step.

Mara said: 'You keep talking as though we can split the difference. But compromise is trade, and trade needs two people who each want what the other has more than what they're giving up. You're asking me to sell part of the cabin to buy your cause. Fine — then say it that way. It's a partial sale, and I'd rather lose outright than be a party to pricing something I don't think has a price.'

Ines said: 'The trade story doesn't need a common yardstick; that's the entire point of trade. You value keeping it more than I do, and I value the money more than you do, so we can each end up better off by our own lights. Refusing that because the thing is "priceless" just means you'd rather we both lose than that your hands stay clean.'

Mara said: 'Then test it. If the cabin really has a price, tell me what yours is, and notice where the number comes from — it comes from the good the money would do. That isn't a price for the cabin, it's a price for your cause. So the exchange rate isn't a fact about my values, it's a fact about yours, and you're asking me to adopt yours so that the trade can go through.'

Ines said: 'By that standard nobody could ever compromise, because everyone's reasons come from somewhere. And you do have a rate: you'd sell if the alternative were something truly awful. So "priceless" just means "very expensive."'

Mara said: 'There's a difference between a limit and a price. What I'd do if the choice were between the cabin and something terrible isn't what I'm willing to do here, with an ordinary alternative on the table. A limit tells you where I'd break; it doesn't tell you what I'd sell for.'

They went around like that for an hour and neither of them moved. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each step, who has the better of it and why, whether the two of them are talking past each other at any point, and what both of them are leaving out — including whether anything turns on facts about this particular cabin rather than on the general question of what compromise between people with different values can amount to.
