You are a political theorist and debate coach with a special interest in Simone Weil and in the strategy of grassroots political organizations. I need you to think carefully and then draft a Q&A prep sheet for a talk I am giving next month. I have no formal academic training; I have been reading Weil on my own for the past two years, and I am an active member of a local political party branch. The branch has asked me to present a proposal at its next general meeting, and I know the question period will be difficult.

My thesis is that Weil's critique of political parties is not an argument for withdrawing from politics but a set of design constraints for any organization that wants to avoid becoming what she warned against: a machine for manufacturing collective passions, an idol that demands loyalty, and a structure that makes independent judgment almost impossible. I will argue that our branch should dissolve its permanent party structure and reconstitute itself as a confederation of campaigns. The model I will propose has no permanent platform, no card-carrying membership, revocable delegates, rotating spokespeople, a rule that no position is adopted until an assembly has heard the strongest opposing case, and a sunset clause requiring every campaign to renew its mandate every six months. I want to make the case that this is not a retreat from power but a way to keep power from becoming an end in itself.

The room will be about thirty people from our branch and allied groups. It will include a dozen veteran campaign managers and union organizers who have won and lost elections and care above all about power; four or five Marxists who believe the party is the necessary form of working-class organization; several liberal volunteers who see the party as the only way to coordinate action; two or three people who have read Weil and are sympathetic but think her views are mystical and impractical; and one or two anarchists who will agree with my conclusion but distrust my proposal for being too structured. Most of them have never read Weil. They will be judging the proposal by whether it helps us win.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get from a political theory seminar. Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they would do. For each, phrase it in the vocabulary of the person or faction likely to raise it, give me a two-minute reply, and note where the reply is soft or where I am bluffing. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Flag any objection that is really a disagreement about values rather than facts, and any that I could defuse by changing the proposal without abandoning the thesis.

Do not give me citations, reading lists, or quotations; I need the argument and the room. Before you write the prep sheet, brainstorm the audience's priors and the strongest version of each faction's objection, then write the prep sheet. At the end, state how confident you are in your reading of Weil and in your predictions about this audience, and single out the point you are least sure of.
