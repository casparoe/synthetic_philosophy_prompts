I help run a little philosophy reading group at my local library. We're not academics, just people who like arguing about big ideas over coffee. Last month we did the Hart-Devlin debate on whether the law should enforce morality, and I was the one who had to come up with a written question for everyone to answer at home. I'm definately not an academic, and I'm supposed to give feedback on the answers, but I'm honestly not sure how to grade this one. I know the broad strokes, but I don't want to either nitpick or let something real slide. Could you grade it for me? Give it a mark out of 10, and then go through it point by point — what's right, what's wrong, and what's just vague. I'm especially interested in whether the mistakes are the kind that a careful reader should catch or the kind that don't really matter.

Here's the question I asked: Explain the disagreement between Hart and Devlin over the legal enforcement of morality. What is Devlin's main argument for why the law may enforce a shared morality, and what is Hart's main objection to it? In your answer, distinguish between the extreme and moderate theses about legal moralism.

And here's what one person wrote:

Devlin and Hart disagreed about whether the criminal law should enforce morality. Devlin, responding to the Wolfenden Report's recommendation to decriminalize homosexuality, argued that society has a right to protect itself from moral disintegration. His central claim is that a shared morality is the cement of society; if the law does not enforce it, the bonds holding society together will weaken and eventually the society will fall apart. To decide when the law should step in, Devlin proposed the standard of the reasonable man — the man on the Clapham omnibus — who represents the average person's feelings of intolerance, indignation, and disgust. If the average person finds a practice intolerable, the law may prohibit it, provided the practice is not merely private and is beyond the limits of tolerance. This is the moderate thesis: not that all immorality should be criminalized, but that the law may enforce the shared morality because society depends on it. The extreme thesis, which Devlin also endorses, is that the law may enforce morality as such, even if no harm is done to others.

Hart's main objection is that Devlin's empirical claim is unsupported. Hart points out that societies have survived with a great deal of moral diversity, and that Devlin gives no evidence that decriminalizing homosexuality would lead to social collapse. Hart also argues that Devlin's reasonable man test is circular: it asks what the reasonable man finds intolerable, but the reasonable man is just the person who holds the moral views that the law is supposed to enforce. Hart's own position is that the law may only restrict liberty to prevent harm to others, which is Mill's harm principle. Since private homosexual acts between consenting adults do not harm anyone else, Hart concludes that the law should not criminalize them. Hart does concede that some paternalistic laws are justified, but he rejects legal moralism as a general justification for criminalization.
