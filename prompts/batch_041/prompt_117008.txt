I am a third-year Politics and Philosophy student currently taking a seminar on democratic theory and institutional design. In three weeks my seminar is convening a mock citizens' assembly on a local housing proposal, and I have been asked to give the opening address to the assembled participants. The assembly will consist of roughly sixty people selected by lot from the university and the surrounding neighborhood. The room will be mixed: my classmates, who have read the standard arguments for and against sortition; neighborhood residents who agreed to take part but are wary of being used as a political science experiment; and two city councillors who have accepted invitations and believe that only election confers democratic legitimacy. I need the text of a five-minute opening speech, written to be delivered aloud.

I want the speech to be built around a tension I have been calling the mirror/deliberation trade-off. One familiar defense of sortition says that a randomly selected body is legitimate because it is a statistical mirror of the public: it looks like the rest of us, so it can speak for the rest of us. Another defense says that the point of sortition is to create a small group that can actually deliberate, revise its initial opinions, and resist the deformations of mass opinion. The trouble is that these defenses pull against each other. The more perfectly the body mirrors the wider public in demographics, attitudes, and levels of political knowledge, the more likely it is to reproduce the public's ignorance, polarization, and deference to the loudest voices. The more it is pushed toward deliberative independence, the less it can claim to be a mirror. I do not want the speech to dissolve this tension by saying that the participants are simply a sample, or simply a mini-public, or that election and lot are merely two routes to the same kind of representation. I want the tension to do real structural work: it should shape how I explain why these particular people were chosen, how I answer the councillor who will ask about accountability, and what I say in the closing charge about the authority they do and do not have.

Please write that speech. It should be intelligible to the residents and the councillors without condescending to my classmates, and it should avoid the usual opening about Athens or the standard quotations that everyone uses. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or a bibliography. The speech itself should run about seven hundred to eight hundred words, with at least one moment where the audience can laugh or at least recognize itself. After the speech, in a separate note to me, tell me what would change your mind about the mirror/deliberation trade-off being the right frame: which argument or consideration would lead you to write a different speech, and where your reasoning in the speech is most likely to fail or to lean on a contested reading of sortition. If you think the trade-off is a false dilemma, say so, explain what you would replace it with, and say what would have to be true for that replacement to be right.
