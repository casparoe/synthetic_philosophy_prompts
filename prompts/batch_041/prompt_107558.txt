Next month I am presenting a paper at a colloquium jointly hosted by a translation studies department and an NLP lab, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a summary of my argument. My thesis is that contemporary neural machine translation can be genuinely context-sensitive without understanding, and that the best diagnosis of its limits is not shallowness in the Hofstadter sense but what I call normative parasitism: the system's outputs count as translations only because they are hooked into a practice sustained by human translators, users, and institutions, yet the system itself is not answerable to the norms it manages to satisfy. I develop this with a thought experiment I call the overheard interpreter: a system that produces a flawless rendering of a conversation it has no idea is a conversation, and then, when one word of the transcript is altered, produces a different flawless rendering, with no stable representation of what was said. I also argue that the standard statistical-shallowness objection is right about understanding but wrong about why, because it treats statistical association as intrinsically shallow when the deeper issue is the absence of normative answerability.

The audience will be about twenty people. Eight or nine are literary translators and translation studies scholars, mostly descriptive and expertise-oriented, deeply suspicious of computational claims, alert to untranslatability, register, and the ethics of translation, and inclined to say that translation is always situated and embodied. Three are NLP engineers: one works on evaluation metrics, one on multilingual embeddings, one on low-resource languages. One is a philosopher of language with Gricean commitments, interested in speaker meaning, implicature, and the semantics-pragmatics interface. One is a cognitive scientist who studies bilingual processing and code-switching. My abstract has been circulated, but no one has read the full paper.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do to the thesis, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and which objection I can safely dismiss. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need the objections and the replies, not sources. Write the prep sheet in American English.
