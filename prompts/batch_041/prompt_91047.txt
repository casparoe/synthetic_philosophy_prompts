A colleague of mine in the law faculty is contributing a chapter to a handbook of moral and political theory, and she has asked me to look over her treatment of Scanlon's contractualism before she submits it. I am not a contractualist, but I have taught the view often enough to feel that her paragraph, though fluent and largely in order, quietly stacks the deck by the end. What I want from you is a careful audit rather than a verdict of my own.

This is what she writes:

'Scanlon's contractualism tells us that an act is wrong when it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. The guiding thought is that morality is not a matter of maximizing some total but of being able to justify what one does to each person affected — which is why the view is often described as a sophisticated restatement of the golden rule. Its most controversial feature is its insistence that complaints be weighed one at a time: when we assess a principle we consider each person's objection on its own, and we are forbidden to add objections together across persons. This is what makes contractualism's well-known trouble with numbers so damaging. In the familiar case where we can save either one person or five, the contractualist cannot say that we ought to save the five, since the one who is passed over has a complaint that is not made any weaker by the fact that five others are helped; the theory therefore returns no answer where an answer is plainly required, and to that extent it fails as an account of right and wrong. Its defenders have in effect conceded the point, rescuing the numbers by appeal to tie-breaking among complaints of equal strength — which is aggregation under another name, and leaves the view looking like rule utilitarianism in Kantian dress. In any case the reasonable-rejection test is simply the hypothetical-contract device applied to principles rather than to institutions, and it inherits the standard difficulty of all such devices: we are asked to be bound by what parties who do not exist would have agreed to, whereas the only parties whose consent could bind us are the actual people affected.'

I would like you to go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a well-informed defender of Scanlon would accept as fair, what she has simplified in a way a defender would want to correct, and what is a genuine misrepresentation. I am particularly interested in two things. First, has she kept apart the general contractualist idea that wrongness consists in unjustifiability to others from the much more contentious claim about whether complaints may be summed across persons, or has she treated the second as though it were simply what the first means? Second, is she entitled to conclude that the numbers problem shows the view to be extensionally inadequate, rather than a live difficulty that its proponents have addressed in ways she does not consider? Her closing suggestion — that the numbers are recovered by a de facto aggregation that reduces contractualism to a rule-utilitarian structure — strikes me as the place where the paragraph does its real work; is that a fair description of how Scanlon and those who have followed him have responded, or does it conflate positions that the literature keeps distinct? And does the hypothetical-contract objection land against Scanlon's version of the view at all, or only against the contractarian tradition he takes pains to distinguish himself from? You need not supply references; I want the substance, not a bibliography.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of, with a word about why.
