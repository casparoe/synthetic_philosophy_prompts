Question: "Scanlon's contractualism is often said to face an 'aggregation problem'. Explain what feature of his view generates the problem, explain how Scanlon responds to it, and assess whether the response succeeds. (700 words max.)"

Answer:

Scanlon's contractualism says that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could rationally reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. What generates the aggregation problem is a further commitment that goes with this test. Scanlon insists that when we ask whether a principle could be rejected, we look at each person's objection one at a time. We are not allowed to add up the complaints of different people and weigh the sum against the complaints of others, the way a utilitarian adds up pleasures and pains. This is what is meant by the individualist restriction. The thought behind it is that a justification has to be addressed to each person separately — you can't answer my objection by telling me that other people would be worse off under the alternative. This is close to the point Rawls makes when he says utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons.

The trouble is that the restriction seems to make the numbers invisible. Suppose I can rescue one person from a serious injury or rescue a hundred people from injuries of exactly the same severity. Each of the hundred has an objection no stronger than the one person's, and I'm not allowed to add the hundred objections together, so it looks as though the two options are on a par — even though any sane person would save the hundred. Worse, consider the case where one person faces a severe harm and a very large number of people face a mild one: the individualist restriction seems to say that the severe harm is decisive no matter how many mild ones there are.

Scanlon's response is that numbers can matter without our having to aggregate them. Where the objections on each side are of the same kind and roughly equal in strength, the fact that more people would bear the loss gives each of them something to say against the principle that does not depend on adding their complaints to anyone else's; the numbers do the work that aggregation was supposed to do, without ever treating a person's objection as a contribution to a total. So the correct way to read him, I think, is that a principle is the right one when it minimises the total weight of reasonable rejection across persons.

I think this is a partial success at best. It works for the first case, where everyone's complaint is the same size, but it is silent about the second, where the complaints are of different kinds. Scanlon must either say that the hundred mild headaches really do not outweigh the one severe harm, which is hard to believe, or that numbers matter even across kinds, which looks like the aggregation he set out to avoid. So the individualist restriction earns contractualism its attractive anti-utilitarian verdicts at the cost of making it unable to explain something we are all confident about.
