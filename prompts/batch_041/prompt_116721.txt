For the past semester I have been in a small reading group on the ethics of procreation, and two of its members have reached a stalemate that I would like to see written up as a proper adversarial collaboration. One of them is an antinatalist of the classical sort: he accepts the four-part asymmetry usually credited to David Benatar — that the presence of pain is bad, that the presence of pleasure is good, that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one to enjoy that good, and that the absence of pleasure is bad only when there is someone who is deprived by it — and he concludes from it that bringing a person into existence is always wrong, since every life contains some pain while the goods of a life count only for the one who has them. The other is a natalist who accepts the first, second, and fourth parts of that table but rejects the third: he holds that value must be value for someone, that a world with no one in it is not better than a populated one in any sense that could give a couple a reason to act, and that once the third row is removed the asymmetry collapses into the ordinary judgment that a good life is a good thing and a bad life a bad thing, which leaves procreation permissible whenever the child's life will go well. They have been arguing past each other for weeks, and I suspect that at least one of their disagreements is about which question is being asked rather than about the answer to it.

Please draft the document their collaboration would produce. I want the claims both of them can sign, including claims about the asymmetry itself and about what the antinatalist's argument would and would not establish if it succeeded; the precise residual disagreements, each phrased in the way its own side would want it phrased; the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and, for each crux, a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I have found it useful to mark off goods whose value does not depend on there being anyone around to enjoy them as existence-independent goods, and I would like to know whether that is the right way to carve the issue and whether each of them would accept the label. For the case that both of them must predict about, use the following rather than the usual examples about lives that are barely worth living: a couple can with certainty bring into existence a person whose life will contain no severe suffering at all, only a long stretch of mild, undistinguished contentment interrupted by occasional mild frustration, or they can bring no one into existence. If part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which part it is and why the appearance of disagreement survives it.

Format the result as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a table of disagreements, with columns for the crux, the case or argument, and each side's prediction.
