I am a retired behavioural biologist. For forty years I read preferences off the choices of animals, in two-choice tests and in demand-curve experiments, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of economics. Here is what I hold:

1. Choice is the measure of preference. If I want to know what an animal or a person prefers, I vary the options and watch what is taken. A preference that no possible choice could reveal is not something I know how to talk about.
2. Utility is bookkeeping. Any consistent ranking can be given numbers, but the numbers are arbitrary up to any increasing transformation, and there is no further fact about how much more someone wants A than B.
3. All the same, demand curves are real measurements. I spent years showing that the price an animal will pay for a reward — presses of a lever, seconds of discomfort tolerated — yields a genuine magnitude of motivation, and that two animals can differ in how much they want the same thing.
4. Welfare is not the same as getting what you want. An animal in a barren cage may stop trying for anything better, and a person adapted to a deprived life may sincerely choose it; both can be worse off than they would otherwise have been.
5. When someone's choices run in a circle — A over B, B over C, C over A — the fault lies with the person, not with the theory.
6. I have no patience with the idea that people know best what is good for them: ask what they do, not what they say.

Some of these plainly belong together. Others, I suspect, do not, though I cannot tell which. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, how serious each genuine tension is, and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me; then say which single revision would clear up the most.
