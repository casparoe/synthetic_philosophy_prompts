I'm teaching the final unit of a philosophy of law course on the justification of punishment, and I want to close with a session on Frank Darabont's The Shawshank Redemption (1994). By then my students will have the usual four justifications — retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation — and I'll also have given them a sketch of comparative penology: the Norwegian model that Halden exemplifies, where the governing ideas are the principle of normality and the import model (prison life is made to resemble life outside, and health, education, and welfare services are brought in from the community), set against the American punitive regime of long determinate sentences, isolation, and mass incarceration. I'd like you to read the film through that material and give me real criticism — not a plot summary, and not the standard tribute to hope, friendship, and the redeeming power of a good man.

That is because I suspect the film is really about the failure of the rehabilitative ideal, and that its most philosophically revealing scenes are the parole hearings rather than the escape. Red learns to recite the language of reform and is refused; he says the same things again and is refused; and only when he abandons the performance and tells the board he is simply an old man with regrets does it let him go. I want you to treat that sequence as a serious claim about what rehabilitation becomes when it is turned into an administrative test, and to work out what the film thinks the board is actually measuring. Bring in Brooks, whose release and suicide are the film's portrait of institutionalization, and the prison library, which offers education as the humane face of reform; and use the old dispute in prison sociology between the deprivation model, on which the prison environment itself manufactures the inmate's character, and the importation model, on which men carry their pre-prison identities and values in with them. Which model does the film assume, and does Andy vindicate the second while Brooks illustrates the first?

Where I want you to land is on the question the film usually gets credit for dodging: does it show that rehabilitation is possible inside an American punitive prison, or that the institution manufactures the very incapacity it claims to cure, so that its ending is an escape from the problem rather than an answer to it? Say what, if anything, the film's implicit penology has to teach the Halden alternative — whether a prison built on normality and imported services could have saved Brooks, or whether the film implies that what he and Red needed was not a better prison but no prison at all. Be concrete about which scenes carry the argument, and be honest where the film resists the reading I've proposed; I'd rather have a criticism that disagrees with me than one that flatters the thesis. A colleague insists the film is only a liberal fantasy of individual escape, and I want to know whether he's right.
