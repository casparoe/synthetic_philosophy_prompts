I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years as a physical chemist — instrument work, mostly, which meant a lot of variance decomposition: how much of the scatter in a measurement is the machine drifting, how much is the sample genuinely being heterogeneous, how much is the day of the week. One thing that was drummed into me early is that a variance component is a property of your study design and your sampling range, not of the world. Sample only one soil type and its contribution vanishes; widen the temperature range and the instrument term shrinks. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy badly but enthusiastically, and I've drifted into a fortnightly discussion group at the local university, where for the third time in about eighteen months we've ended up in the same shouting match about heritability. The behaviour-genetics people say heritability is a perfectly well-defined population statistic, estimable, and that denying it is either innumeracy or bad faith. The other side say that whatever the number means it can't tell you what anyone actually wants to know about why people differ, that it shifts when you change the environments you sample, that it quietly assumes away the gene-environment interaction that may well be the whole story, and that it gets read as innate and fixed no matter how many caveats you staple to it. I've now heard the same four or five exhanges so many times I could hum them, and the genome-wide association work finding hundreds of tiny effects where the old estimates implied a few big ones hasn't settled it either — each side just reads that result as confirming what it already thought.

What I'd like from you isn't a verdict on whether the estimates are right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — two sides attaching different senses to heritable or genetic and then talking past each other? Is it a substantive factual dispute, e.g. about how pervasive gene-environment interaction really is and whether it makes the standard estimates misleading? Is it two different questions under one heading, one side asking what predicts a trait within a given population at a given time and the other asking what causes it and what would change if you intervened? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence — what would even count as settling it? Or is the methodological argument largely a proxy for a values disagreement about whether genetic explanations of human difference are legitimate things to want at all? My own hunch, which I'd like you to push on rather than politely agree with, is that the verbal element is real but small, and that the load-bearing problem is the mismatch between the question the statistic answers and the question the room is arguing about, sitting on top of a genuinely unresolved empirical question about interaction effects. I've been wrong about this sort of thing before.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a study design that could actually be run, a formal result showing one side's inference doesn't go through, or a straight admission that this will keep recurring because it isn't really one argument. I want it concrete enough that I can tell next month whether we've made any headway or are just going to do the same five exhanges again. No reading list please — I won't get through it and I'd rather have the argument itself. And could you write it in British English; I've been over here long enough that behaviour without the u now looks like a typo to me.
