Here's something I keep tripping over. In the US (and similar places) hourly workers get time-and-a-half for overtime — the law treats the extra hour as a burden the worker has to be paid extra to bear. But salaried and "exempt" workers, managers and professionals, are exempt from that, and nobody thinks its odd when they work 60 hours for the same money; if anything it's read as commitment. Then you have Japan, where karoshi, death from overwork, is a recognized thing you can get compensation for — so the same society treats overwork as a compensable harm and as honorable devotion, sometimes in the same case. And meanwhile unemployment benefits almost everywhere come with the demand that you take a job, any decent job, or lose the money, as if work were something you owe rather than something you're owed.

What view of work and leisure does this whole tangle presuppose? Is work a disutility you have to be compensated for, a duty you owe, or a good you're lucky to have — and can one theory cover all of it, or is the practice just incoherent? I'm especially interested in whether the hourly/exempt line really presupposes that some work is different in kind, or is a historical accident dressed up as principle. Reconstruct the theory the practice needs, tell me which parts are strictly required versus merely compatible with it, where it's underdetermined, and what would have to change if we took the idea of a right to leisure seriously.
