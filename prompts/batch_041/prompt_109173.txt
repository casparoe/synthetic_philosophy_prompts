I'm on the promotions panel at a mid-sized engineering firm — thrilling stuff, I know — and we've landed on a case that's turned into a proper row. The candidate is, everyone agrees, strong on delivery and strong on mentoring; the third criterion is 'demonstrates readiness for client-facing leadership'. Nobody can point to anything he's done badly, and nobody can point to a job where he's obviously smashed it either. Two of us want to promote him now, two want to wait six months and look again, and the tie-break clause in the handbook says that where a criterion is met only to a borderline degree we should treat it as unmet unless the candidate is clearly above the bar on the rest. That clause was clearly written by someone who assumed there's a right answer sitting somewhere and it's just hard to see. There's also a bit of awkward history: the last three people we waved through were all in more or less his position, and with hindsight one turned out to be a star and another was a disaster, and nobody can now say which of them 'was' ready at the time.

My colleague Priya, who did a maths degree and has never quite got over it, says the whole argument is confused, because there simply isn't a fact of the matter here — 'ready' is a vague word, this case sits in the grey area, so 'he's ready' isn't true and isn't false, and we should stop hunting for the hidden truth and decide on other grounds (she'd happily flip a coin, which she says is no worse than our tie-break clause and much quicker). My other colleague, Dan, thinks that's relativist nonsense — if the man is ready then he's ready, we just don't know it, and the fact that we can't detect the exact tipping point is a fact about us rather than about him. Someone else has suggested we just score each criterion out of ten and average them, which apparently is what his old company did; I can't tell if that's a genuine solution or a way of dressing up a guess as a number.

I have to write up our decision and chair the next meeting, and I don't want to sound like I'm either pretending there's a hidden fact I can't see or pretending the question is meaningless. Which of them has the better grip on what's actually going on here, and what does each of them have to say about the tie-break clause — does Priya's line actually license us to just decide, or does the clause still bite on her view? I'd also like to know whether 'he's ready or he isn't' is a safe thing to have in the minutes, given what each of them is committed to, and whether the out-of-ten scoring is a third option or just one of theirs in disguise.
