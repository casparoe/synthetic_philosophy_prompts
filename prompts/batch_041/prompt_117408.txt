I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough to recognize the genre: a long, confident post that turns a seminar's worth of historiography into a single thesis, and then asks you to pick a side. The post I have in mind summarizes the debate between cyclical and linear views of history roughly as follows. The cyclical view, represented by Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee, holds that history repeats itself in a fixed sequence of stages. Polybius gave the classic six-stage cycle of monarchy, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and mob rule, and thought every polity was doomed to run through it. Ibn Khaldun applied the same pattern to dynasties: they rise on tribal solidarity, peak, and decay after about three generations. Spengler thought cultures are organisms with a fixed lifespan, so the West is simply repeating the fall of Rome. Toynbee refined the model into challenge and response: civilizations grow when a creative minority meets challenges, and decline when that minority stops being creative. The linear view, by contrast, comes from Judaism and Christianity: time has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and history is going somewhere. So the real divide is between pagan fatalism and Judeo-Christian hope. Since modern progressivism is just secularized linear time, and the evidence of recurring imperial collapses is everywhere, the cyclical view is more empirically adequate.

I suspect this is mostly right in spirit but wrong in detail, and I do not know enough to say where. Please go sentence by sentence and separate what a historian of ideas would accept, what is a common simplification that a specialist would wince at, and what is flatly wrong or a conflation. In particular, I want to know whether the alleged divide between cyclical and linear views is really a divide between two incompatible theories, or whether the theorists named are doing something more complicated — for example, using cycles within a linear framework, or denying that cultures repeat one another in the way the post suggests. Then tell me whether the concluding claim that the cyclical view is more empirically adequate follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is a non sequitur that would survive even if every factual claim in the summary were corrected. I am less interested in whether cyclical history is true than in whether this characterization of the debate is fair to the people it names.
