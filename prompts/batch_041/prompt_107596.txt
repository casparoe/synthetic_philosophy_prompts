I'm a TA for a moral psychology seminar and next week I have to run the discussion on the honor / dignity / victimhood trichotomy that everyone's been throwing around lately. My problem is that the students sort themselves by vibes -- whoever grew up in a house where you hit back is "honor," whoever thinks you should just call the police is "dignity," whoever posts screenshots of the group chat is "victimhood" -- and none of that tracks what they actually believe about the interesting questions. So instead of a reading I want to hand out a taxonomy: a tree where every branching node is a question that splits the positions, phrased so you can answer it without already knowing which label you'll land on, and every leaf is either the name of a position or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, with one sentence on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow.

Here's the angle I care about, because I think the usual three-way split is doing two jobs at once and muddling them. The job I want is: when someone has been wronged, where does the repair come from? Is standing restored by what the wronged party does themselves -- their own retaliation, their own withdrawal, their own refusal to care -- by what the community does (recognition, adjudication, collective outrage), or by what the wrongdoer does (apology, submission, punishment accepted)? Then the second axis I want woven in, since it's the thing my students actually fight about: is anger at a wrong a justified claim about what you're owed, or is it a failure of self-command regardless of how legitimate the grievance is? Please build the tree out of those two questions first and only afterwards place the familiar positions inside it, so I can show the class which combinations are occupied and which are empty.

I need these to show up as leaves somewhere: plain honor culture; plain dignity culture; plain victimhood culture; the view that victimhood is itself a form of high status, so victimhood culture is just honor culture with the sign flipped; the view that all three are really the same thing -- a pecking order of who gets believed -- wearing different costumes; and the one my most annoying student holds, that the whole taxonomy is descriptive sociology with zero normative content, so asking whether one of them is morally superior is a category error. I'd also like at least one leaf for a position I don't think anyone actually holds, if the logical space has an obvious hole in it -- something like a culture genuinely indifferent to slights rather than above them -- and a line on why you think the hole is there. And if two of your leaves are so close that the tree can't really separate them, flag that instead of pretending the questions do the work. Keep it to eight or ten leaves and no more than six or seven branch questions, and for gods sake don't let any node just ask "do you think honor matters?"
