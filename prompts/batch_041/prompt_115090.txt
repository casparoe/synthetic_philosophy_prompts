I'm in a long-running argument with two friends about what to eat and we keep talking past each other, so I'd rather get the conceptual issue straight than win the fight. We all more or less agree farmed animals matter and we all know the standard 'eat lower on the food chain' line, but our rankings keep coming apart and I've started to suspect the disagreement isn't empirical at all — it's about what the right unit of comparison even is. Priya ranks foods by deaths per calorie and ends up thinking beef is one of the least bad meats, since a single cow is worth hundreds of chickens and each of those chickens was a seperate death. Sam ranks by suffering per calorie and puts chicken worse than beef because of how the birds are kept, but then also says eggs come out worse than either, and plants best of all, which feels alot like a cheat to me. And I keep bringing up the stuff nobody wants to count: the field mice and insects killed by harvesting, and, more than that, the wild animals whose habitat the cropland replaces. If wild animal suffering counts at all then every hectare of land use is doing something, and the tidy 'plants are basically harmless' intuition starts to depend on a picture where the alternative to a farm is some pristine wilderness rather than the managed land that's actually there.

What I want from you is the conceptual anatomy of this, not a factory-farming lecture. Concretely: what exactly is being assumed when someone says food X is worse than food Y, and how does the answer move around when you change (a) the unit — deaths, suffering-years, or some aggregate of expected welfare summed over every sentient creature affected, insects and all; (b) whether you treat death itself as a harm to an animal who would otherwise have had a decent life, and what the replaceability thought does to that, i.e. the idea that killing a happy animal isn't a net loss as long as a comparable animal gets to live in its place; and (c) how much moral weight small creatures get, since the whole crop-death argument seems to scale with whatever you decide about nematodes and insects.

I'd love to see the two or three load-bearing premises named, and then the hardest case walked through under two or three different sets of assumptions — chicken versus beef, or grass-fed beef versus soy, whichever makes the premises visibly pull apart — so I can see which ranking is riding on which assumption and where the thing actually breaks. Sam also keeps proposing that we just multiply 'expected suffering-years per thousand calories' by 'probability the creature is sentient' and call it a day; tell me whether that tidy formula is basically right or whether it hides the whole problem, and if it hides something, what it hides.
