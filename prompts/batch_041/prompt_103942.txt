Construct a case in which the doctrine of double effect returns opposite verdicts on the same act depending on a single structural fact about the causal path, while the agent's act, intentions, evidence, and the certainty and magnitude of every outcome are identical across the two branches, and the agent cannot tell which branch she is in. Constraints: one realistic setting (a hospital, a rescue, a workplace), no longer than a paragraph; no trolleys, levers, footbridges, bombers, or transplant surgeons; the uncertain fact must be one no reasonable person in her position could have settled before acting; and the numbers must be such that proportionality is satisfied in both branches, so that the means clause is the only condition doing any work. Open with one sentence giving the bottom line, namely what the case shows about the doctrine, then present the case, then run the check: name the act, the good effect and the bad effect, say whether the bad effect is a means on each branch, and give the doctrine's verdict on each branch and why, using the four conditions it standardly imposes (the act must be permissible in itself; the bad effect must be neither intended as an end nor used as a means to the good; it must be merely foreseen; and the good must be proportionate to it). Then test the case against a condition I stipulate: call a criterion transparent when it cannot declare an act permissible in one causal branch and impermissible in the other while the agent's evidence and intentions are the same in both. Say whether the doctrine is transparent in your case, whether reading the means clause as a claim about the agent's plan rather than about causal structure restores transparency, and which feature of the case is load-bearing. Close with what a defender of the doctrine should concede, and what becomes of the case if the agent could have settled the structural fact cheaply.
