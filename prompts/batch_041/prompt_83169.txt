I'm a science writer and I'm doing a feature on the World Happiness Report — you know the one, it lands every March with the happiest-country-on-Earth headline, built on that Gallup poll where they ask people to picture a ladder and put themselves on a rung. My angle is two questions stacked on each other: are we actually measuring happieness, or are we just measuring how people answer a survey about happiness? And even if it's real, should a country be trying to top the list at all?

I want to read Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through that, instead of the trolley-problem reading everyone reaches for. You know the story — the shimmering festival-day city where everything is good, and the whole thing runs on one kid locked in a cellar in the dark, and the arrangement is that everybody eventually learns about the kid and most of them just live with it. A few walk away.

So, concretely: would Omelas top the ranking? What would the ladder question even be measuring there? Bring in the split between experienced well-being — the moment-to-moment stuff you'd catch with something like the day-reconstruction method — and evaluative well-being, the rate-your-whole-life-on-a-ladder thing the rankings actually use, including the awkward finding that more money tends to lift the second without touching the first. And bring in the adaptation problem: people report being content with lives they'd never have chosen, which is the old happy-slave worry, and which makes self-reported satisfaction look like a very low bar. Does Omelas's happiness survive that scrutiny, or is the story showing us precisely what the indices can't see — the basement?

Then push the other way, because I don't want to reduce the story to an illustration of a methods fight. Le Guin is not writing about survey design. Where does the story resist my framing — where is it about complicity and the act of looking away, full stop? And the ones who walk away: is that the story's answer to whether a country should chase the top of the list — that the only honest move is to refuse the metric? Or is that too tidy a lesson to hang on it?

No citations or reading lists needed, I know the well-being lit well enough. I just want a genuine reading that takes both the story and the measurement question seriously, and ends by telling me what the story knows that the data can't.
