I am a platform engineer at a mid-sized software company, and I have no formal background in philosophy or economics. I own an internal shared compute cluster — a few thousand CPU cores plus a GPU pool — that every product team draws on directly, with no chargeback and no per-team budget: the cost sits on one central line. Every quarter or so we have the same fight. Two or three teams run large batch jobs that saturate the queue for days; interactive work from everyone else sits behind them; and the only fixes anyone proposes are add capacity, which we cannot get funded, or impose hard quotas, which the loudest teams say will destroy their roadmaps. Someone always calls this a tragedy of the commons and the conversation ends there.

When I tried to read up on it, I found that the people who study shared resources for a living seem to say the classic story is wrong in a specific way, and that there is a mirror-image failure I had never heard of in which the problem is too much exclusion rather than too little. Here is the half-digested version I am working from, and I would like you to correct it rather than take it on faith: a common-pool resource is one that is rival — my use subtracts from yours — but from which people are hard to exclude, whereas a public good is non-rival; the famous tragedy is really about unmanaged open access rather than shared ownership as such; communities have repeatedly governed shared resources for long stretches without either privatization or central control, using rules the users themselves set, monitoring that is cheap, and penalties that start mild and escalate; the monitoring and sanctioning are themselves collective-action problems, so a governance scheme can fail at the second level even when the first-level rule is sound; and the anticommons is the reverse pathology, where many separate holders of exclusion rights each block the others and the resource ends up underused.

What I want is not a lecture. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run when a contention complaint comes in. Please give me: the questions to ask, in the order to ask them, phrased so that an on-call engineer can answer them from data we already have; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what the procedure does when the answer is missing or contested; the point at which the procedure terminates with do nothing, the resource is not actually being depleted and no intervention is warranted; and the point at which it stops and hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is about whose work matters more, which is a value judgment and not something the procedure can settle. Give me a short diagnostic sequence — a handful of things we can measure or check in under an hour, such as the ratio of demand to capacity, the spread of consumption across teams, whether a team has a workable substitute for the resource, and how visible each team's usage is to the others — and say what each diagnostic is actually a proxy for. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive the translation into a checklist and which I can safely drop, and be honest about the cases where the procedure will misfire or produce an answer that looks rigorous but is really a judgment call in disguise.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the two batch-job teams that are saturating the queue; a proposal from my director to give every team a use-it-or-lose-it share of the cluster, which is popular because it sounds fair; and a case where the honest answer is to do nothing. I expect to dislike at least one of the outcomes, so do not soften them.

Constraints: I have roughly two weeks before the next planning cycle, no economist on staff, and any policy change has to be signed off by my director together with the product team leads — so the procedure has to be usable by people who will not read the underlying literature, and it has to say when to escalate to those leads rather than to me. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and in particular whether the framing I brought you is even the right diagnosis for my situation, or whether I am forcing a story about herders onto a problem that is really something else.
