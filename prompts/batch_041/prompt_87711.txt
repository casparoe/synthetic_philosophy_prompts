You are an expert in the metaphysics of causation and in the law of omissions — someone who can move between analytic philosophy of causation and doctrinal tort law without collapsing the two into each other. Think carefully before you answer, and work in stages: first brainstorm what this particular audience already believes, then identify where my view threatens those beliefs, then write the sheet. I'd rather have six objections I will actually get than twenty I might.

The talk. I argue that the long quarrel over causation by omission is not a quarrel about the causal facts but about which contrast class we have tacitly selected. My claim is that a sentence like "her omission caused the outcome" is true only relative to a baseline — an alternative course of events against which we measure the actual one — and that baselines are fixed by normative and pragmatic considerations (what was expected, what was owed, what was salient), not by anything in the causal structure. So there is no distinct relation of omissive causation to be discovered: there is ordinary causation, a stipulated baseline, and a social practice of treating departures from that baseline as the thing needing explanation. I call this baseline relativism. It cuts both ways, which is the part I expect to cost me: it says the lawyers are right that the act/omission line is really a duty rule wearing causal clothes, and it says the metaphysicians are wrong to think the line tracks any difference in the causal facts. Each side, on my view, has chosen a baseline and mistaken its own choice for a discovery.

The audience. A two-day workshop on causation and the law. Roughly a dozen analytic metaphysicians who work on causation — several interventionists, a couple of Humean reductionists, one or two mechanists — plus half a dozen torts scholars and a criminal law person or two. Most of the philosophers have never opened a casebook; most of the lawyers are allergic to metaphysics and suspect that anyone announcing "there is no separate relation here" is quietly trying to get their clients off the hook. The lawyers will care about the duty to rescue and the misfeasance/nonfeasance line. Two or three people in the room have published on omissions specifically and will hear my view as the claim that their life's work is a verbal dispute.

What I want. A Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period, ordered by likelihood times damage — how probable each objection is and how badly I'd bleed if I fumbled it. Phrase each objection the way this audience would actually phrase it, and note that the metaphysicians' version and the lawyers' version of the same complaint are different questions wearing the same surface. For each objection: who in the room is likeliest to raise it, the two-minute reply I should give, what I should concede up front rather than be forced into conceding, and where the reply is soft — the exact spot a sharp follow-up lands. Steelman each objection before you grade it, and tag each one concede, hold, or concede the letter and hold the spirit. Flag the two objections I should pre-empt in my opening instead of waiting for, and name the crux of each disagreement — the one premise that, if I lost it, would sink me. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, stated as bluntly as the person who asks it would state it.

Two constraints. No citations, no reading list, no "see so-and-so on this" — I want the arguments, not the bibliography, and I would rather you reason from the concepts than gesture at authorities. And skip the generic objections any audience could raise; I want the ones that grow out of these people's specific commitments.
