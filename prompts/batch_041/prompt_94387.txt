I work as a career counsellor, and I keep meeting a pattern I cannot name. A client has two offers that, after weeks of weighing, come out exactly equal on every dimension they care about: pay, location, colleagues, prospects. They say they cannot choose, because choosing one over the other would be arbitrary, and an arbitrary choice is irrational. But they also cannot stay undecided, because the offers expire and the delay is itself costly. I see the same structure in a committee deadlocked between two candidates who are equally strong on every agreed criterion, and in a friend choosing between two flats they have rated identically. What interests me is that the agent does not treat the tie as a reason to pick either; they treat it as a reason to keep looking for a difference, and when they find one, they immediately discount it as trivial. I know the old story about the donkey between two identical bales of hay, but that seems to be about paralysis leading to starvation, whereas my clients are stuck on whether an arbitrary choice can be rational. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—choice under complete indifference, where the agent demands a reason and the absence of a reason becomes a reason to delay? How does it differ from cases of incommensurable values, where there is no common measure, and from mere indecisiveness? And does the concept say anything about whether the demand for a reason is itself the problem, or whether the agent is right that choosing without a reason is irrational? If my framing is confused—if two options cannot really be exactly equal, or if the question is not well-formed—please say so plainly rather than humouring me. Answer in British English.
