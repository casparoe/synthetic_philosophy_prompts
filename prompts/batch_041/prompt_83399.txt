I'm a high school student in our philosophy club, and I need a poem for the last meeting of the term. Please write it as a philosopher of metaphysics who also writes formal verse, and think the problem through carefully before you draft.

Our unit was material constitution -- the statue and the clay -- and I want to close on the part we never got past. Use the case I invented for my presentation: a bronze church bell is melted down in wartime and recast as an equestrian statue of the general who ordered the confiscation; seventy years later, the statue is melted down again and becomes the pipes of an organ. One material, three objects.

Write a sestina in the voice of the bronze. I want the sestina specifically for what the form does: six words return in every stanza in a rotating order, so the material is fixed and only the arrangement changes -- which is the question I want the poem to make a reader feel, whether the same stuff in a new order is a new thing. Choose the end-words so that at least one of them has to change its sense or its grammatical role from one stanza to the next, and never let 'same' be said twice in the same sense. For most of the poem let the bronze speak about itself and about the bell it used to be, then in the last third let it turn and address the statue directly; that turn should be the poem's pivot. The envoi, where all six words arrive at once, should be the place where the poem admits it cannot say how many things it is about.

It has to be right about the philosophy rather than merely dressed in it. The bronze would survive being squashed into a lump; the statue would not, and that is why we say they are two things. But nothing seems to be added when the statue appears, since the bronze already does everything the statue does, so the second thing looks as though it does no work -- and yet we count it. Hold both halves of that and do not resolve them; a poem that picks a side is not the poem I want. Someone who knows the puzzle should be able to point to the line where the modal point lands and the line where the counting worry lands.

In your reply, begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary before it; after the envoi you may add a few sentences naming those two lines and explaining what you did with the end-word whose sense shifts.
