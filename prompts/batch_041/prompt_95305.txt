I am a high school junior, and I am putting together a final project for a history seminar on how we forecast the decline of empires. Our teacher introduced the outside view—the idea that instead of asking what is special about the case in front of us, we should ask how a broad reference class of similar cases has behaved—and then immediately complicated it with the reference class problem: the base rate for the fall of an empire depends on whether you count all states, all empires, all Mediterranean hegemonies, all agrarian tributary empires, or only those that share the particular fiscal and military features of the one you are worried about. I am also stuck on the fact that the whole exercise is haunted by survivorship bias, since we only have durations for empires that have already ended, and the empire I am trying to forecast has not. I have been trying to write a poem that makes that problem felt rather than just explained.

Could you write a ghazal spoken by an actuary employed by a late imperial court, someone whose job is to price the empire's survival? The radif—the phrase repeated at the end of the second line of every couplet, and at the end of both lines of the first—should be on average, with a rhyme immediately before it. Each couplet should take up a different reference class (all empires, all empires that lasted long enough to be called empires, all Mediterranean empires, all empires that look like this one, and so on), and the repeated on average should work as the outside view that survives every inside-view anecdote. The couplets should be able to stand alone, as ghazal couplets do, because that autonomy is part of the point: the reference classes do not cohere into a single narrative, and the actuary cannot make them. The last couplet should sign the poem with the actuary's name or title, and the whole poem should leave me unsure whether the base rate is a counsel of humility or a counsel of despair. I know the form is demanding, and I would rather have a real ghazal with a few rough lines than a smooth free-verse poem that merely mentions base rates. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or the names of theorists; I want the idea to be in the poem, not attached to it.
