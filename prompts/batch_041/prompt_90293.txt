My grandmother died two weeks ago, she was 89. She never finished college but she was the one the county historical society called whenever they needed to know why something around here happened the way it did, and she was basically a founding member of the society, so her memorial is being held in their meeting room. So the room is half family (my cousins, her two sisters, people who just knew her as Grams) and half society people, a few of whom have read more real history than I ever will.

Here's my problem. Her whole thing — and she'd have hated me calling it her whole thing, but — her whole thing was that she thought single-cause explanations were almost always a lie. She had this rule she trotted out constantly, we all called it "the doors." For any big thing that happened, she'd say, you can nearly always find two or three separate chains of events, and any one of them by itself would've been enough to bring the thing about. So "what really caused it" is usually a bad question; the good question is why we're so desperate to name one culprit. Her favorite example was why our town never got the railroad spur, and she'd give you three completely different stories about it that each would've done the job on its own.

The trouble is that a eulogy is the single-cause genre. Everyone in that room, me included, wants to say she was the way she was *because of* one thing — the Depression, or my grandfather, or the fire. And she'd have called that a lie, gently, the way she did.

So write me the eulogy. It has to land for the cousins who never once heard her talk about this and for the society people who will absolutely notice if I fudge what she actually thought. Dont turn it into a lecture on causation — it's a memorial, people are grieving, and she'd hate a sermon. But let the doors thing do real structural work: the speech should genuinely resist the one-thing-explains-her move without leaving the room with nothing to hold onto. About five minutes. No quotes, she didn't do quotes.
