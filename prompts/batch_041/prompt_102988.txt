You are a metaphysician specializing in the metaphysics of identity, individuation, and modality, and I would like your help with a question that has been nagging me in a seminar on individuation. I am trying to assign a credence to the identity of indiscernibles (PII) — the thesis that there is no possible world containing two numerically distinct entities that share all their qualitative properties — and I keep getting pulled in different directions by the two-sphere scenario, in which an otherwise empty universe contains two perfectly qualitatively identical iron spheres. (I take it that if such a world is possible, PII is false as a necessary truth; if it is impossible, PII survives at least this test.) My own credence that PII is true as a necessary truth is around 0.35, while a friend in the seminar puts hers at around 0.75. I am less interested in a bare number than in seeing the reasoning laid out, so please think carefully and, before you answer, spend a few sentences mapping the space of positions — for instance, whether PII is a conceptual truth about identity or a substantive metaphysical thesis, whether it ranges over all properties or only intrinsic ones, and whether it is a necessary truth or a contingent generalization — and then give me your own number together with its decomposition.

Specifically, I would like rough probabilities for the sub-questions you think your credence depends on, one line of justification for each, and then the combination. The sub-questions I have in mind, though you should add or replace them as you see fit, are: (a) whether PII is analytic, a consequence of the meaning of identity, or a substantive claim; (b) whether the two-sphere scenario is genuinely conceivable, and whether conceivability is a reliable guide to possibility here; (c) whether the two spheres are discernible by their relational properties (they are related to each other, albeit symmetrically) or by their identities (sphere A has the property of being identical to A, sphere B of being identical to B), and whether the latter move trivializes the principle; (d) whether the scenario requires haecceities or bare particulars to be intelligible; and (e) whether the scenario violates an independent principle, such as the principle of sufficient reason or the requirement that a possible world be fully determinate. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it most.

Finally, I would like you to assess two variants that I have been toying with, which may or may not be the usual ones. First, suppose the two spheres are not spatially separated but are two numerically distinct objects occupying exactly the same region of space and time — call them coincident spheres — and are otherwise qualitatively identical. Second, suppose there are three spheres arranged in an equilateral triangle in an otherwise empty universe, so that the qualitative facts are invariant under a 120-degree rotation. Does either variant make the case against PII stronger, weaker, or merely a restatement of the original, and does it shift your credence? Please keep the discussion self-contained; I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list, just your reasoning and your numbers. I would also like you to say which of the two of us — me at 0.35 or my friend at 0.75 — is more likely to be making an error, and where the disagreement most likely lives.
