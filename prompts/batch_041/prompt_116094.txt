I have recently begun using Bayesian methods in my own research — I am a doctoral candidate in epidemiology, and my current model requires a prior over a positive continuous quantity, a transmission rate, about which I have essentially no background information. I have been using a uniform prior and defending it by an appeal to symmetry. Last week a colleague in mathematical statistics sent me a short note which he regards as settling the matter against me, and I should say at once that I lack the technical background to judge it and am inclined to think he must be right, if only because he is so confident. This is the substance of what he wrote:

'Bertrand's paradox is the classic refutation of the principle of indifference. Draw a chord at random in a circle and ask for the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Choose the chord by picking its two endpoints at random on the circumference and the answer is 1/3; choose a random radius and a random point along it and the answer is 1/2; choose a random point inside the circle to serve as the chord's midpoint and the answer is 1/4. Three equally defensible ways of being indifferent, three different answers. The appeal to symmetry and ignorance that underpinned Laplace's classical definition of probability is therefore arbitrary, and the classical programme collapses. This is why probability theory was rebuilt on Kolmogorov's axioms, and why no serious person now treats probability as a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis: probabilities are either frequencies or subjective degrees of belief. The one serious attempt at a rescue is Jaynes's maximum-entropy argument, on which the answer is 1/2 because the uniform distribution over chord midpoints is the unique distribution invariant under the transformations that leave the problem unchanged; it remains a minority taste, and the same failure disposes of Jeffreys's programme of invariant default priors. The paradox also shows why conditioning on an event of probability zero is illegitimate, since a "random chord" is really a conditional distribution on a null set.'

I should like an audit rather than a defence. Go through the note claim by claim and tell me which parts a statistician or a philosopher of probability would accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications of a more careful statement, and which are simply wrong or conflate two different things. In particular: does the three-way disagreement really refute the principle of indifference, or does it show something narrower about how the principle must be applied? Does the appeal to Kolmogorov's axioms do the work he says it does? Is his description of Jaynes's argument accurate, and is the verdict that it remains a minority taste a fair account of the current state of play? And does the closing remark about null events belong in this discussion at all? Finally, bring it back to my case: if I can specify the experiment that I take to be generating the rate — and my colleague's own objection was that a uniform prior on the rate and a uniform prior on its logarithm give different answers — does the paradox still tell against me, and what exactly should I concede and what should I refuse to concede?
