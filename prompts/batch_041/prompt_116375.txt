I have been asked to write the centenary history of a professional association I have belonged to for twenty years, and I have spent the last eight months in its archive. Two discoveries there have made the commission harder than the subject matter warranted.

The archive is not a neutral heap of paper. It was sorted in the 1950s by a committee whose brief was to show how the association had come of age. The losing side in the dispute of 1931 survives only as quotations inside the winners' reports; the correspondence of the man who argued the losing case for thirty years amounts to four letters and a receipt. Those who turned out to be right kept everything. Nobody thought to ask the others.

And the association's own account of its purpose — that it has always existed to do X — is a retrofit: the founding documents are explicit that the point was Y, and X first appears in a policy statement from 1964. I am asked to write a history that explains who we are, and it is not clear to me that I can do that without either repeating the retrofit or producing something the committee will find unusable.

My stake in this is not one I entirely trust. I was on the losing side of a more recent fight, and my side's records are thin too; one of the losers in 1931 was my teacher. I would rather that motive not do the work and call itself method.

Write an essay on whether a non-triumphalist history is possible in a case like this — whether the story of progress toward the present, what is often called whig history, is a distortion to be corrected or a legitimate and perhaps unavoidable form of understanding — and reach a verdict, weighing the strongest case on each side. If you conclude that the distortion can be corrected, say what does the correcting and how far it can go when the evidence itself was selected by the winners; if you conclude that it cannot, say what an honest version of the triumphalist history would look like and what it would cost. Don't close on a both-sides note, and keep the argument anchored in this archive rather than in historiography at large.
