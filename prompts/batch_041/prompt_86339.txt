I present a weekly podcast on science and ideas, and next month I am recording a live episode in front of an audience at a science festival. The format is a forty-minute solo segment at the microphone before my guest joins me on stage. There will be no slides, no whiteboard and nothing for me to point at, and the house will be general readers: people who have read two or three popular neuroscience books, who use the phrase "neural network" without being able to say what one is, and who have absorbed the idea that the brain is like a computer so completely that they no longer register it as a comparison. I do not want you to explain computationalism to me; I want you to plan the forty minutes.

The topic is deliberately the narrower and stranger version of a familiar question. I am not asking whether machines can think. I am asking whether the brain, considered simply as a piece of matter, literally is a computer, and what turns on the answer. Please give me the order in which the pieces have to arrive and why each depends on the one before it; the single example or case that carries the whole thing, and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, with the point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off; what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and say so; and one question I can put to the room at the end that would tell me whether the idea landed rather than merely washed over them. Two constraints shape the plan. First, because the segment is audio only, the carrying example has to be something I can build inside a listener's head out loud, without a diagram. Second, I do not want to end on the observation that philosophers disagree; the two-camps framing is exactly what this audience expects and it teaches them nothing, so I would rather they leave with one question sharpened than with a scorecard.

For the carrying example I have been considering the tide-predicting machines of the late nineteenth century: a frame of pulleys and wires driven by a hand crank, which mechanically sums a stack of sine curves and draws the result as a curve on a moving roll of paper, so that the machine's output is the tide at a given port. It is usually described, tellingly, as an analog computer. My reasoning is that it is a physical system that genuinely computes, that no one has ever been tempted to call conscious, and that is continuous rather than digital, which ought to knock out the assumption that computing means shuffling symbols inside a silicon box. Tell me whether it can carry that load, what it establishes and what it quietly gets wrong. I have a specific worry: if the machine only computes the tide relative to our interpretation of its output, the example may prove too much, since it suggests that nothing computes anything on its own. If the example cannot survive that worry, tell me what should replace it and why.

As for what they will get wrong, my guesses are these. They will hear "the brain is a computer" as "the brain contains a processor and runs software". They will hear it as "so my mind could be copied onto a hard drive". They will hear it as "so thinking is just arithmetic". And somewhere past the halfway mark, someone will object that no arrangement of computation could account for what it is like to taste coffee. Tell me which of these are the real obstacles and which are noise, what I have missed, and where in the sequence each has to be met, bearing in mind that I cannot simply say "that is a different question" unless I have earned that distinction much earlier. One further thing I want the plan to accomplish: by the end, the audience should be able to see the difference between the claim that the brain can be described as computing something, the claim that the brain's causal organisation literally is a computation, and the claim that the brain is a computer in the sense that a laptop is, because I am convinced that most of the confusion lives in the slide from the first to the third, and the segment fails if they leave still sliding.
