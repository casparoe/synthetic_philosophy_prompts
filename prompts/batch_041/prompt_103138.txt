Consider two accounts of when a technical artifact can be said to have a politics of its own.

The first is the position Langdon Winner develops in his 1980 essay in Daedalus on whether artifacts have politics. On Winner's view, an artifact can be political in two distinct ways. In the first, a design settles a question of who does what, who goes where, and who has access in a particular community: the arrangement itself is a form of order, and its politics shows up in the distribution of access it produces, independently of anyone's stated intentions. In the second, certain technologies are inherently political: their operation requires, or is strongly compatible only with, a particular pattern of social organization, so that adopting the artifact amounts to adopting the social order capable of hosting it.

The second account is one I will call valence determinacy. It begins by defining the profile of an artifact in a context: the mapping from social positions (operator, owner, worker, passenger, non-user, refuser) to the bundle of capabilities and constraints the artifact confers on each position in that context. The account then holds that an artifact has a determinate political valence exactly when its profile is invariant across the range of contexts in which it can plausibly be deployed, up to the identity of the positions involved; where the profile varies with context, the artifact is politically plastic, and whatever politics it appears to have is borrowed from surrounding institutions rather than carried by the artifact. On this account there is no separate category of inherent constraint: what Winner calls an inherently political technology is simply an artifact whose profile is invariant because its functioning presupposes a particular institutional context.

Please compare the two accounts, reaching a position rather than surveying the options.

First, does valence determinacy genuinely differ from Winner's view, or is it a redescription of it? Identify the sharpest point at which the two come apart, and say which account fares better there.

Second, test both against a case of this shape. A hospital adopts a scheduling system that assigns shifts by optimizing for continuity of care. Nothing in the software mentions any social group. In a workforce spread evenly across neighborhoods the system is neutral with respect to group membership; in a workforce where one group disproportionately lives far from the hospital, the same algorithm systematically assigns that group the unpopular overnight shifts. Does the scheduling system have a politics, or does it merely inherit one from the housing pattern? Which account gives the more defensible answer, and why?

Third, consider an artifact whose politics appears to reverse across settings, such as a low-cost modular building system meant to enable self-built communities that in practice becomes a tool for standardizing and policing them. Can valence determinacy accommodate the reversal without locating the artifact's politics entirely outside the artifact? Can Winner?

Finally, state which account you would rely on if you had to advise someone deciding whether to build or ban such an artifact, and spell out what that choice commits you to. Where you find one account's answer evasive or question-begging, say so plainly.
