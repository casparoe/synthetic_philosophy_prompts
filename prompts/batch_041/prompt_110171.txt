My seminar group spent a whole session on Orwell's essay about politics and the English language, and my friend Rachel came out of it with this argument, which she still defends. Every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is obviously way to strong, so: which step do I cut, and what does cutting it cost me?

(1) Political writing is mostly fog: euphemism, dying metaphors, prefabricated phrases, jargon.
(2) Orwell's own point is that the fog isn't just a symptom of muddy political thinking, it's also a cause of it, and that the corruption runs both ways. Take him at his word: if the only words you've got for a policy are fog, you can't hold the policy clearly in your head.
(3) If you can't hold it clearly, you can't really assess it.
(4) If you can't assess it, you can't be knowingly endorsing it — you're endorsing a phrase, not a deed. Which is why nobody signs off on a massacre, they sign off on pacification.
(5) So the fog is load-bearing: it's what makes the worst politics livable for ordinary people, and clearing the fog takes the cover off.
(6) Which makes the fight against bad prose not a hobby for English teachers but something close to the centre of politics. Orwell says as much — he calls thinking clearly the first step toward political regeneration.
(7) But come on. The last century's worst politics was often sold in blunt, plain, punchy prose, and often run by people who knew exactly what they were doing and said so in sentences any composition teacher would praise. No writing seminar ever stopped a genocide.
(8) So one of (1)-(6) is false.

For what it's worth I'd much rather keep (2) than (7). (7) feels like it just asserts the opposite of what I want to be true, and I don't want to end up saying language is a neutral window onto thought. So where's the break, and what do I pay for it?
