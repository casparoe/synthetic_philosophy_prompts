OK so I've somehow ended up running the "philosophy hour" at work next Thursday — 45 minutes, about a dozen people, mostly engineers plus one lawyer, zero philosophy background but they're sharp and they will push back. Topic I picked: causation by omission, i.e. can not-doing something actually be the cause of something happening. I don't need you to explain it to me, I need you to plan how I explain it. (For context I've been lurking on rationalist blogs for like fifteen years, so I've got the vocabulary but not the rigor.)

Specifically: what has to be in place before the question even makes sense — I assume some grip on counterfactual dependence ("if I hadn't done it, it wouldn't have happened"), but does something have to come even before that? — and then the order everything else goes in, so each step actually leans on the last one.

The carrying example: I want to build the whole thing on a case I made up, so tell me honestly if it can carry the weight or if I need to swap it. Version A: a hospital ventilator is built so a nurse has to press a button every hour to keep it running (some deadman-switch design), nurse gets distracted, patient dies. Version B: identical machine, but the button only exists because the manufacturer built it that way — it could trivially have been built to just run forever. Version C: the nurse did press it, but someone earlier had disabled the alarm that would've reminded her.

What I want the plan to use those three versions for is getting them to the weird bit — that we seem to locate "the cause" by what was expected or required, not by what made a difference, and that once you see that, the action/omission line stops looking like it's carved into the world. I keep calling this "omission laundering" — when the background design quietly turns a doing into a not-doing. Should I introduce a coined term like that, or will it just muddy things?

Misconceptions I'm expecting: (a) "if you could've prevented it, you caused it," (b) that this is secretly about blame or legal liability rather than about causation, (c) that omissions are obviously just as real as actions, so what's the fuss. Where in the sequence do I head each off — and should I head them off, or let them walk into the wall first and then back out?

Also give me one question I can ask at the end that'll tell me whether the idea actually landed vs whether they just got comfortable with the vibe, and tell me what I should openly say I'm not going to cover at this level.

Last thing, and I actually care about this: tell me where you're least sure. Which part of the plan would you bet against, and what would change your mind about it — like, if my three-version case turns out not to separate omission from background design the way I think it does, what would you swap in, and how would I know?
