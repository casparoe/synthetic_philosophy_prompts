I'm a novelist and I'm writing a book about Émilie du Châtelet, and somewhere in the research I've become half a believer in the philosphy of happiness she works out in her Discourse on Happiness — the one she wrote in the last few years of her life and that didn't get published until long after she was dead. As I read her: happiness is relative to your situation, so don't waste your one life trying to change your circumstances; what you need is health, passions and tastes, no prejudices, virtue, and you should guard your illusions rather than trade them for clear sight, never repent, and keep the sad ideas — death above all, your own and other people's — out of the house. She's upfront that this is advice for someone whose fortune is already made, which is a big asterisk.

I've got a salon scene where a character pushes back on all this, and I definately want him to be right rather than just sneer. So what counts against her view of happiness? Which objections would she have to concede, which are just cheap shots, and is it even the kind of thing that can be refuted, or is it insulated because it's advice and not a theory? Rank whatever you give me from the ones that would really hurt to the ones that are merely awkward. I'll say up front that the illusions part is what I'd most need convincing on — the same woman who spent her life on Newton telling us to preserve our illusions is either profound or a cop-out and I can't decide which.
