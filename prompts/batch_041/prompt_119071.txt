I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on decision theory and I need a decision tree that sorts responses to the St. Petersburg paradox into actual positions rather than into a list of famous names. The game: flip a fair coin until it lands tails, and if that happens on round n you get 2^n dollars; half the time you walk away with two dollars, and the expected value is infinite. Build me the tree.

Root node should be whether the offer is even a well-posed decision problem -- whether the paradox is an artifact of an impossible setup -- and then run the rest of the tree along the one distinction I actually care about: does a given resolution treat the infinity as an artifact of the setup (the bankroll, the coin, whether a payoff that size could be delivered at all, whether probabilities that small are real chances in the first place) or as an artifact of the decision rule (maximizing expected value, or expected utility, is just the wrong rule)? Every leaf needs one sentence on the awkward commitment it can't shake. For the "just pay up" leaf, that's stuff like: you should be indifferent between having the game and having a million dollars plus the game, and you should hand over a million for something whose most likely payoff is two dollars.

Nodes should be concrete probes, not label questions -- I've seen versions of this tree before and they always end up asking "do you think utility is bounded?", which is useless in a room where half the people haven't picked a side yet. Use things like: would you pay a dollar, a hundred, a million? Would you rather have the game, or a million dollars plus the game? If you played it ten thousand times, what would you roughly expect your average winnings to be? Is there a price at which you'd be indifferent, and if I changed the bank standing behind the game, would that price move?

These need to come apart somewhere on the tree, so make sure each one gets a leaf or a branch:

- utility is bounded -- no outcome is more than some fixed amount better than the baseline
- utility is unbounded but grows slowly, something log-shaped, so expected utility comes out finite anyway
- tiny probabilities get downweighted, or treated as not genuine chances at all below some physical or epistemic threshold
- expectation is the wrong summary of a distribution -- go by typical or modal or median payoff, or by maximin, regret, safety-first
- the expected value really is infinite and you just pay up
- the game simply has no finite value: the values of its finite truncations run away without settling on anything, so there's no fact of the matter about a fair price and the rational response is to refuse to price it
- what you should pay depends on the process -- the coin, the bank, what happens to the money -- and not on the abstract distribution

Two variants I want the tree to handle, because I think they're where the leaves actually separate. First: same doubling payoffs, but the chance of reaching round n is one over four to the n instead of one over two to the n, so the expected value is finite. Some resolutions swallow that easily; others have to admit the paradox was never really about the infinity. Second: a game where the terms you'd add up never settle on a sum -- you can rearrange them and get any total you like. Which leaves survive that one, and which have to fall back on a completely different story?

Audience is second-year grad students who know what a utility function is and nothing else. Aim for eight to twelve leaves. Where the tree leaves an answer-combination with no position sitting in it, say so, and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. And flag any two leaves the tree can't actually seperate -- I'd rather know than have a fake branch. No citations, no reading list, no "so-and-so held this"; if a leaf has no defender, just mark it unnamed.
