My cousin has fallen down the libertarian YouTube rabbit hole, and now any time the family starts talking about student loan forgiveness, tolls, public libraries, whatever, he just says "taxation is theft" and leans back like the case is closed. I tried to argue with him over Thanksgiving and realized I couldn't even say what was wrong with the line, because I'm honestly not sure what claim it's supposed to be making. Is it saying that collecting a tax is literally the same kind of act as someone stealing your bike? Or that it's morally on par with theft? Or just that it's an involuntary transfer of property, and "theft" is a loaded name for something that's really just... an involuntary transfer of property? I want the actual menu of things that one sentence could be saying.

Lay it out for me as a list, roughly from the boring/trivial readings up to the radical ones. For each one I want to know what exactly it's claiming, whether it's a claim about the meaning of words or a substantive moral claim, what it has to presuppose about property (like, whose money is it before the tax gets levied, and who gets to decide that), and whether it's true or at least defensible. A few things I specifically want sorted out: (a) whether "theft" is being used as a legal category or a moral one, since those come apart, and whether the slogan turns out to be circular if "theft" just means "unjust taking" — because then "taxation is theft" is barely more than "taxation is unjust taking," which seems to assume the conclusion; (b) the difference between "this particular tax is theft" and "the institution of taxation is theft in its very nature"; (c) whether theft needs a victim, and if so whether the victim is a person or some collective; and (d) whether any reading makes the slogan self-undermining, since property titles seem to be enforced by the same state that does the taxing, so "taking what's yours" might already lean on an arrangement that taxation partly creates. Also tell me which reading my cousin most likely means and which reading would actually justify treating it as a conversation-ender, since I suspect the one that sounds decisive and the one that's actually true are different things.
