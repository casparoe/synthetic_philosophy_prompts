I keep a mental ledger of what I will and will not take someone's word for, and I want to know whether a principle underlies it. My sister says the chili is too hot for the kids: I believe her and move the bowl, no tasting required. A friend says the new album is mournful: I believe that before I hear a note. A critic I trust says a cathedral I have never visited is beautiful, and I come away believing it is beautiful. But when a colleague says the joke he just heard is hilarious, his word gives me nothing until I hear it and laugh, and when a reviewer says a novel is exquisite, or that it moved him to tears, I do not think I have acquired that belief until I read it myself. Strangely, when a friend who knows my taste says I will love a film I have not seen, I believe him and rent it. My half-formed guess is that testimony can hand me facts about the object — how hot it is, how mournful, how I would find it — but never the value the thing has in itself, which would mean giving up the cathedral verdict.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle that best fits these verdicts, stated precisely, or a small set of candidates if no single one will do; (2) which of my verdicts each clause of it is built to save, and how it handles the cathedral; (3) its verdicts on three cases I have not mentioned — a smell someone tells me is disgusting, a proof someone tells me is elegant, and a friend telling me the water is too cold to swim in; (4) the verdict of mine the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would surrender the verdict or add a clause; (5) the case most likely to break the principle. Write in American English.
