My research is in formal social epistemology, but my methodological training is in statistics, and I teach our department's graduate sequence on estimation and inference. I would like you to take the classical Condorcet jury theorem — the claim that a majority vote among voters each individually more likely than not to be correct is, as the electorate grows, increasingly likely to be correct, together with its darker twin, that a majority of voters worse than random becomes more reliably wrong as it grows — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: a fixed but unknown parameter, estimators with their bias and variance, consistency and efficiency, correlated rather than independent errors, the bias–variance tradeoff, and the distinction between estimating a quantity and choosing an action under a loss function. Treat each voter as an estimator of a fixed unknown quantity, the vote as a discretization of the estimate, and the majority rule as an aggregation of estimators. Then give me the residue report.

I want that report in three parts. First, what carries over without strain: which of the theorem's claims are, once translated, ordinary facts about aggregation — for instance, whether the statement that the group is more likely to be right than a typical member is the same statement as the claim that the aggregate is a consistent estimator, and whether competence above one half is simply doing the work of a signal-to-noise condition. Second, what translates only under pressure, and what has to be distorted to make it fit. Here I am especially interested in whether the independence assumption is the same thing as zero error covariance, and in whether the theorem's binary, per-voter notion of competence can survive being replaced by a per-item notion, as in an item-response model in which each question has its own difficulty and each voter a discrimination parameter of their own. Tell me whether the theorem's central conclusion even has a statement in that setting, or whether it quietly depends on competence being a fixed property of persons rather than of person–item pairs.

Third, and this is the part I care about: what does not survive the translation at all. The theorem is put to work by political philosophers to say something about the authority of majority rule, the general will, the legitimacy of outcomes, and the difference between a decision being correct and its being binding. I see no counterparts for any of that in my vocabulary, and I want to know whether that is a defect of the vocabulary — something it cannot represent and therefore cannot criticize — or a sign that the original claims were doing less work than their proponents suppose. Be honest about the direction of translation: do not quietly import the estimator's assumptions (a real-valued parameter, a symmetric loss, exchangeable units) and present them as neutral renderings of what the jury theorem says. Where you must choose, tell me which choice you made and what it costs.
