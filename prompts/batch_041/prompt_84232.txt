Can you write me a poem that is basically the St. Petersburg game, with the form doing the structural work? Heres the constraint I want: stanza 1 is one line, stanza 2 is two lines, stanza 3 is four, stanza 4 is eight, stanza 5 is sixteen -- the stakes double every stanza while the odds halve, so each stanza adds exactly the same amount to the total, and the poem should make that flatness visible even as it escalates. Every stanza should name its own stake and its own odds (heads on the nth toss), first person, someone sitting at a table who already paid the entry fee and is now watching the coin come up tails yet again. Write four or five doublings and then stop, and make the stopping the point: the series doesn't converge so the poem can't either, but the ending should feel like a person folding rather than a poet cheating -- a last short stanza (a line or a couplet) that states the arithmetic of the speaker's own willingness to pay instead of the game's. Somewhere in there it has to make the classic move from money to utility (infinite expected value, finite willingness to pay), and the moment it makes that move is where the escalation should stop mattering to the speaker -- the lines can keep doubling, the speaker can't. If it fits, one line noticing that this same infinite sum is what makes Pascal's wager feel fishy would be a nice touch, but don't force it. Someone who knows the paradox should be able to point to the coin, the doubling, the divergent sum and the resolution; someone who doesn't should just get a good poem.
