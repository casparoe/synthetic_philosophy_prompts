Me and a friend have been arguing about many-worlds for weeks and he finally wrote his case down as an arguement, and I can't find the bad step. I'm not attached to many-worlds, I'd be fine if it were dead, but I don't believe this kills it, because the same shape of move seems to prove things I'm sure are false. Here it is.

(1) Many-worlds just says the Schrödinger equation always holds, nothing ever collapses, and every term in the superposition is a real world.
(2) So if I measure a spin in a superposition of up and down, there ends up being a successor of mine who sees up and a successor who sees down, and both are just as real.
(3) So when I ask, before looking, what my chance of seeing up is, I'm asking which of my successors I'm going to turn out to be.
(4) But nothing could make me one of them rather than the other. Each is a complete continuation of my memories and my character. There's no fact of the world that makes one of them the real me.
(5) So my chance is one in two, a half. And in general it's one over the number of worlds.
(6) But quantum mechanics says the chance is the square of the amplitude, and for amplitudes of root-one-third and root-two-thirds that's a third and two-thirds, not half and half.
(7) So many-worlds gives the wrong numbers, and not because of a technical slip — the wrong numbers fall straight out of what the theory says exists.
(8) A theory that gives the wrong frequencies is refuted by experiment. You can save it only by bolting on a separate probability postulate, which throws away the point of the theory, or by saying the question has no answer, which makes it untestable.
(9) So many-worlds is dead.

Two things bother me. First, steps (3) through (5) don't seem to have anything to do with quantum mechanics. You could run the same reasoning on a classical gadget that copies me into two rooms, one with a red light and one with a green light, and there a half is obviously right — so the argument looks like it's really attacking the idea that I can have a rational degree of belief about something the world has already settled, which is absurd, because I can have one about a coin that's already landed under a cup.

Second, a guy in our group said you can take a state with equal amplitudes and still make three worlds out of it, by letting the down outcome also record some irrelevant fact, so you get two down-worlds and one up-world. Then my friend's rule gives a third for up, but the amplitude on up hasn't changed, so quantum mechanics still says a half. My friend says that's a red herring. Is it?

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it's (4) or (5), tell me what I'm allowed to put there instead — I've heard the fix involves decision theory or not knowing which branch you're on, and I don't know what either of those means or whether they actually get a third. I'd rather not give up (1) or (7). And if the honest answer is that no step is bad and the price gets paid somewhere else, say where. No reading list, just the diagnosis.
