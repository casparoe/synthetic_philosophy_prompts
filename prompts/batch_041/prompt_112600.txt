I took early retirement from a career in analytical chemisty a few years back and I've been making up for lost time with philosophy. I've drifted into a fortnightly reading group, and the standing argument is about my friend Ruth, who spent thirty years as an actuary and now describes herself as a professional sceptic about other people's numbers.

I've been quietly testing her. Two urns: A has exactly fifty red and fifty black, B has a hundred balls in some mix nobody will reveal. Bet on red, she takes A. Bet on black, she takes A again. She isn't guessing about B, she wants nothing to do with it. Fair coin against a coin of unknown bias, same stake, same payout: the fair coin. Fair coin against a bet on which of two grandmasters wins a match next month: the fair coin. Fair coin against a bet on whether a painkiller that was never properly tested will work in a trial: the fair coin. And when the coin is paying only even money while the grandmaster bet is paying double, she still takes the coin.

She won't accept the obvious description of herself, though. She says she has no trouble at all not knowing things, that half her working life was spent pricing things nobody understood. What she can't stand, she says, is a bet whose odds rest on somebody she can't check -- the urn-filler, the players, the trialists. One of the group says that's just Ellsberg with a story bolted on, and she says the story is doing real work.

Two new bets come up next week, each offered alone, with nothing to compare it to. One: whether my neighbours apple tree will be carrying fruit before September -- no numbers, no bookmaker, just the tree. Two: whether the man in the flat above hers, who she has never met, will leave the house before nine tomorrow morning. Same stake and payout either way.

What does she take, and what won't she? I want the rule she's really running, what each plausible version of it predicts about those two bets, your best guess and why, and the case that would settle which version it is.
