Here's the setup. In our reading group someone reconstructed the usual exorcism of Maxwell's demon as a clean argument. Here it is the way he put it.

(1) The demon has to find out which molecules are fast and which are slow. Call that its measurement step.
(2) The measurement itself needn't cost anything thermodynamically. You can in principle couple the demon to the gas so that the gas ends up in the same thermodynamic state it was in before, only now the demon's memory is correlated with the gas. So the cost of the demon is not in the looking.
(3) Armed with that correlation the demon can sort molecules and extract work from a single heat bath — the same trick as the one-molecule piston thing (Szilard's engine), at most kT ln 2 per bit — cycle after cycle, until its memory is full.
(4) To run a further cycle it has to return its memory to its standard state. By Landauer's principle, resetting one bit costs at least kT ln 2, dumped into the environment as heat.
(5) So the work the demon extracts per cycle is cancelled, to the bit, by the heat it must dump when it erases its record.
(6) Net work over a full cycle is therefore ≤ 0, and the second law survives.

Fine so far, that's the standard story. Here's the part I can't shake. He then added:

(7) Notice that (1) through (6) never used anything specific about *this* demon. Any device that runs in a cycle, returns to its starting state, and yields net work from a single heat bath would have had to get its "knowledge" of the bath from somewhere, and would have to shed that knowledge to get back to its starting state. So the same bookkeeping move — find the information it handled, charge kT ln 2 a bit to erase it — can be turned against any alleged perpetual motion machine of the second kind.
(8) But a law of physics that can always be saved, after the fact, by charging an entropy cost to some piece of information nobody ever measured isn't a claim about the world. It's a rule for keeping books.
(9) So on this account the second law isn't an empirical law of nature at all; it's a convention about how we're permitted to describe systems.
