My aunt Ruth died three weeks ago, at 68, after about thirty-five years as a hospice nurse in a small town in Ohio, and somehow I'm the one giving the eulogy. Here's my problem. I've been reading rationalist blogs for like fifteen years and I'm alergic to sentiment, but this is bigger than that: Ruth had a whole ethic she actually lived by and would never once have called it an ethic. She never in my memory said "do the right thing" or cited a rule. What she said was "notice them," and "show up," and "you can't love people in general, that's just a way of not loving anybody." She was basically a care ethicist before I ever heard the phrase, and she was suspicious of anyone who loved the idea of helping more than the actual person in the chair. She also couldn't stand being turned into an example. And a eulogy is a machine for turning one particular person into a general lesson, which is the exact thing she'd have hated, so I feel like I'm walking into a trap.

The room is a mess. Her two sisters are church people who want me to say she's up there watching over us and will be hurt if I don't; her daughter is finishing a PhD in moral philosophy and will visibly flinch if I get saccharine or flatten her mom into a saint; and about twenty of her hospice colleagues are coming, people who knew her better than I did and will know imediately if I'm faking it. So write me the eulogy. It has to comfort the sisters without asserting anything Ruth would have thought false, be recognizable to the daughter as actually being about care ethics without turning into a lecture (and please, do not quote any philosophers at us), and do its work through concrete moments instead of adjectives. I want the care-ethics ideas to be load-bearing in the structure, not sprinkled on top. And I'd honestly rather you lean into the tension I mentioned -- that the whole form of a summing-up speech fights the view -- than pretend it isn't there. After the eulogy, add a short note on how confident you are in the philosophical moves you made and the single point where you're least sure you've got care ethics right.
