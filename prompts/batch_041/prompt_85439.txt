I have been reading a popular introduction to metaphysics, and the chapter on the statue and the clay has left me unsure how much of it I should trust. Here is the gist of it as I would paraphrase it, following the author's order:

'The puzzle is straightforward. A sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue. Now there seem to be two things where a moment earlier there was one, the lump and the statue. The lump existed before the statue did, and if the statue were squashed back into a ball the lump would survive while the statue would not; so the two cannot be the same thing. Yet they are made of the same matter, fill the same region of space, and share their physical properties. Since Leibniz's Law says that identical things must share all their properties, and these two plainly do not, they must be two distinct objects occupying the same place at the same time, a conclusion that nearly all contemporary metaphysicians now accept. The puzzle is therefore solved by distinguishing the clay from the statue; the only question left is which of the two is more fundamental, and the author says it is the lump, since the statue is just the lump arranged statuewise. She adds that the puzzle is really a puzzle about our concepts rather than about the world, that statue and lump are simply different sortals, meaning different labels we attach to one and the same bit of matter, and that identity is relative to a sortal, so that asking whether the statue is the clay is an incomplete question. She treats the case alongside the Ship of Theseus and personal identity, as three instances of one problem of identity over time, and remarks in passing that the case shows ordinary objects like statues do not really exist, which she takes to be the standard view among specialists. Finally she draws the practical moral: because the statue and the lump are two things, ownership can come apart, so a museum may own the statue while the artist still owns the clay.'

My instinct is that parts of this are more contested than the chapter lets on, but I do not know the literature well enough to say which parts, and the author is a professional, so I have been assuming the summary is fair. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on material constitution would accept as correct, what is a defensible position stated as though it were the consensus, what is a harmless simplification of a real distinction, and what is simply mistaken. I would also like to know what a philosopher who holds that there is only one object here would say in reply, and whether the chapter's practical conclusion about ownership survives once the questionable premises are flagged. Where you have to choose a side, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely disputed, and please do not report a contested view as the consensus. I would rather have the reasoning than a list of names and dates I cannot check.
