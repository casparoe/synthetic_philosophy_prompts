I serve on the elected school board of a mid-sized public school district. I have no academic background — no genetics, no statistics, no philosophy beyond what I have read on my own — and I have been trying to work out, for myself, what to do with a certain kind of argument that keeps arriving at our meetings. Before nearly every vote we take on a program (a new reading curriculum, an expansion of pre-kindergarten, a tutoring corps, a change in how we group students), someone will tell the board that the trait we are trying to move is "about seventy per cent heritable," or "mostly genetic," and will draw a conclusion from that number: that the program cannot work, or cannot work for the children we are most worried about, or that the money should go elsewhere. Occasionally the same sort of number is used for the opposite conclusion — that because a trait is only partly heritable, a program aimed at the environment is bound to succeed. I am suspicious of nearly all of this, but I do not want to dismiss a genuine consideration merely because it is usually deployed badly, and I cannot tell from the inside whether my suspicion is judgment or prejudice. We vote in two weeks; I want to have this settled before then.

What I want is a procedure I can run myself, in an evening at my kitchen table, on a claim of that shape, which tells me whether the number, whatever it is, bears on the vote in front of us, and if it bears, in exactly what way. Please give me an ordered sequence of questions to ask — no more than ten steps, and if a step is really two questions, split it, but do not pad — with a clear statement of what would count as a sufficient answer to each, what I should do when I cannot get an answer, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Some steps should be able to end the inquiry outright with a finding that the claim does not bear on this decision at all, and I want that finding to be available even when the number is real, the study is sound, and the scientist is honest. Other steps should be able to do the reverse and show that a particular claim does bear, and that we should change what we are doing, because a procedure that can only ever dismiss claims is not a procedure; it is my prior with steps. If a step requires a number or a document I cannot realistically obtain, say what a defensible substitute is, or say plainly that the step cannot be completed and what that failure means for the rest. I will be running this alone; the vote belongs to the board, and I need to know which questions I should not try to answer by myself.

Three things I would like the procedure to handle specifically, because they are where my own grip slips. First, the difference between a number that describes why children in this district differ from one another and a number that says anything about what would happen to those children if we changed something; I have a feeling these come apart, but I cannot state how. Second, the case where what the board actually cares about is whether children will differ in how much they benefit from the program — whether some gain and others do not — and whether that variation has anything to do with the number being quoted at us; if it is measured differently, say so and tell me what I would need to look at instead. Third, the point that such a number is always relative to a particular population and a particular range of environments, so that it may not carry over to ours; I want a step that makes me check whether the study was done on students like ours, measured the way we measure things, at the same age.

Then run the procedure on three claims, and tell me where it gives an answer I will not enjoy. (1) A parent tells the board that reading ability is roughly seventy per cent heritable, and that this is why the proposed curriculum change will not close the gap between our strongest and weakest readers. (2) A consultant argues that since the same trait is only about thirty per cent heritable, the environment accounts for the rest, and our new program should therefore work. (3) A vendor offers to screen incoming kindergarteners with a DNA-based score to identify those likely to struggle, citing a high heritability estimate from a study carried out in another country and another school system. For each, I want to know which steps decide it, what the number does and does not tell us, what the board should actually do, and what the procedure leaves unsettled.

I would also like the procedure to be honest about its own failure modes: the kinds of claim it will wrongly wave away, the kinds it will wrongly take seriously, and the point at which the honest answer is that the board is arguing about values rather than about genetics and should say so out loud. I would find it useful if the procedure supplied a sentence or two I could say at a meeting, in plain language, when the finding is that the number does not bear on the decision — and also a warning about when saying that would be unfair.

Please give me the procedure first as a table with these columns, one row per step:

- step (number)
- question (the question I ask)
- sufficient answer (what counts as enough to move on)
- if I cannot answer (what to do instead)
- effect on the decision (one of: stops the claim, narrows the claim, hands off to the board)

After the table, give me the three worked cases as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: a top-level "procedure_limits" array of strings; and a "cases" array, each element having "case_name", "claim_as_made", "steps_that_decide_it" (array of step numbers), "verdict" (one of "does_not_bear", "bears_in_a_limited_way", "unusable_as_stated"), "what_the_number_does_say", "what_the_board_should_do", "what_the_procedure_cannot_settle", "whose_judgment_call", and "plain_language_line" (the sentence I could say at the meeting).
