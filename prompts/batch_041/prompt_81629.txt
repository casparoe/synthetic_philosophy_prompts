I'm teaching the expressivism unit again and I cant face another year of the lying modus ponens. The students can all recite the standard examples and the standard reply, and they still don't see that the real demand is that a moral predicate has to behave like a predicate in every construction, not just the handful of spots the literature happened to pick out. So give me one example of ordinary moral talk -- a sentence, or a two-line exchange, something a person could actually say -- where the moral word turns up somewhere the standard cases don't reach. I'd rather it be grading or comparison (worse than, slightly wrong, not as bad as) than another conditional, since that's where I think the attitude story starts to look thin. Then, in a few lines: what does the expressivist have to add to handle your example, what does the realist get for free there that the expressivist has to earn, and does the addition buy them real logical standing or only a better-looking surface? No citations, no reading list, just the example and the diagnosis.
