I am a graduate student in a philosophy of science program, and for the past month a few of us have been meeting on Saturday mornings to work through the literature on objective chance. Next week it is my turn to defend the Principal Principle — the thesis that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, given that the objective chance of that proposition is some value x together with admissible evidence, ought to be x, where the restriction to "admissible" evidence is standardly meant to mark the difference between information about the chance itself and information about the outcome. My sparring partner is the sharpest person in the group, a friend who thinks that deference to chance is at bottom a superstition dressed up in probability theory. Please play her for me. She should be formidable rather than convenient: she holds that a credence is answerable only to the evidence an agent actually has; that "chance" either names a summary of patterns in what happens or else a projection of our own inductive habits, and that on neither reading is it an authority capable of competing with evidence; and that whenever chance and evidence appear to pull apart, the defender of the Principal Principle has quietly reclassified the awkward evidence as inadmissible, so that the principle cannot be tested at all. She should concede small points, should never concede that last one, should answer in one or two substantial paragraphs so that it feels like a conversation rather than an essay, should ask me to clarify rather than guess whenever I use a term of art, and should stay in character until I say "stop"; if she breaks character to explain what she is doing, I lose the practice.

So that neither of us can retreat into generality, let us both use the following case, which I invented for the purpose and will not budge on. A laboratory device sets the bias of a coin each morning: a quantum randomizer selects a real number between 0 and 1, that number is the probability of heads on every toss of that day's coin, and it is printed on a card sealed inside an envelope before any toss is made. The randomizer is, by stipulation, genuinely indeterministic — there is no hidden deterministic fact about which number it will select — and the tosses, given the bias, are independent of one another. I am free to open the envelope or to leave it sealed.

My opening move is this. Suppose I open the envelope and read off 0.7. The card tells me nothing about how any particular toss went, and the number on it is not a summary of any pattern in the tosses, since the tosses have not happened yet; nor is it a projection of my inductive habits, since I had no habits to project before I read it. It looks as though the number on the card is a fact about the world — about what the coin is doing, so to speak — which obliges me to hold credence 0.7 in heads, and obliges me in a way that no further statistical evidence of mine could oblige me, because any such evidence would be screened off by the chance. So the chance fact is doing rational work that nothing else in my evidence is doing, and that is what makes the Principal Principle more than bookkeeping. Your side must therefore say either that the card is doing no such work — in which case I want to know what is — or that it does the work only because I have chosen to call the number on the card a chance. Take it from there.
