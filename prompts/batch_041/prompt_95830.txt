I coordinate the simulation lab at a nursing school and I'm the one who signs the competency forms, which means I'm the person who decides wether a student is allowed to do a thing on a real patient without someone standing next to them. It's three or four of these a month, about twenty minutes each at a bench in the sim room, and I've got a rubric from the accreditors that mostly asks whether they performed the steps in the right order. Here's why I'm asking you instead of just re-reading the rubric. Years ago I got signed off on a procedure I now think I couldn't actually do -- I could recite the whole thing, I knew every number and every contraindication, and nobody ever found out that my hands didn't know it until I was alone with a patient who hadn't read the textbook. So the thing I'm actually trying to detect isn't on the form. Somebody in my book club said that philosophers now think knowing how to do something just *is* knowing a fact -- knowing a way of doing it -- and that this is basically a licence for checklists; someone else said no, that skill is its own kind of thing and doesn't survive being written down as information. I've also never been able to shake a point somebody made about regress: if doing a skill means following a rule, and following a rule is itself a skill, then no checklist can ever fully capture what I'm certifying. Take that seriously as a design constraint, not as a curiosity.

So build me a decision procedure I can actually run in twenty minutes on one student at a bench. What do I ask them to do first, and what am I watching for while they do it; what counts as a sufficient answer or a sufficient performance at each step and *why*; at which point does the procedure stop with a no; and where does it stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is about values rather than evidence (I'm fairly sure the pass line is one of those, and I want it labelled as such rather than buried). Tell me which of the distinctions from that knowing-how literature have to survive into a form a clinical instructor can use and which ones I can just throw away, and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire -- I'd rather have a form that tells me a truth I don't like than one that ratifies what the school already wants to hear. Two students it has to handle: the one whose hands are perfect but who can't explain a single thing they just did, and the one who can narrate the entire protocol flawlessly while their hands do something else.

Then run it on the three I've actually got right now: (a) flawless hands, can't say why, (b) beautiful explanation, shaky hands, (c) did it once in sim last month with me prompting her through two of the steps and hasn't touched it since. I want to see at least one of those come out in a way that'll make the remediation committee unhappy. And at the end, tell me where your own procedure is most likely to be wrong, and what you'd have to learn -- some fact about how skill actually transfers from a sim room to a ward, say -- that would make you throw the whole thing out and tell me to weight the written exam instead.
