I cover science and ideas for a general-reader magazine, and I'm working on a feature about whether viruses are alive -- and about how much the answer turns on which definition of life you start from. Please take the role of a philosopher of biology who has spent real time on this and who distrusts pat answers. Before you rank anything, spend a paragraph brainstorming the tensions any definition has to navigate, then produce the ranking.

Sort the following ways of drawing the line into tiers, from most to least defensible, on one dimension: how well each disposes of the awkward cases -- viruses, prions, mitochondria, transposons, a flame, a self-replicating computer program, and a self-sustaining chemical cycle that arose in a lab but has no lineage and cannot evolve -- without either gerrymandering the category or redefining "alive" into something no working biologist would recognize.

(a) the textbook checklist: reproduces, grows, responds to stimuli, metabolizes, and so on;
(b) self-maintenance: a network that continuously rebuilds its own boundary against decay;
(c) Darwinian: a system capable of evolution by natural selection;
(d) thermodynamic: a dissipative structure that holds itself far from equilibrium and exports entropy;
(e) informational: anything that stores and copies heritable information;
(f) a criterion a researcher put to me that I can't place -- that life is a matter of improbability, so that the more steps of prior selection an object's structure requires before it could exist, the more alive it is, with assembly depth rather than any single capacity doing the work.

For each, give me a sentence on why it lands in its tier and name the one awkward case that most strains it. Then tell me where (f) really belongs, and whether it is a distinct view or a dressed-up version of one of the others.

I would rather have calibrated judgment than a confident-sounding survey: state how confident you are in the ranking as a whole, and single out the placement you are least sure of, with your reason.
