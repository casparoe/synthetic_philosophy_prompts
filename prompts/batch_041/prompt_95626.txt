I'm in the second half of a metaphysics course and we've just finished a unit on what my lecturer calls the special composition question, which he glosses as the question of when some things compose a further thing — when, as he puts it, a plurality is really one object rather than merely many. I came away with the impression that only two answers deserve serious attention, "always" and "never," and that everything in between is gerrymandering to save ordinary speech; he more or less said as much when he described the intermediate positions as ad hoc. I also came away with a set of worries I can't shake. If the question has no non-arbitrary answer, doesn't that mean composition is a matter of convention — that whether the chair I'm sitting on exists is somehow up to us and our linguistic habits? That seems plainly false, since the chair's existence isn't a decision anybody made. The "never" side troubles me as well: if nothing is ever composed, then there are no chairs, no trees, and no people, which looks self-undermining, given that the person asserting it is obviously a composite object. And since fundamental physics tells us the world is ultimately just fields and particles, hasn't science already settled the matter on the "never" side, leaving composite objects as convenient fictions?

What I'd like is not a survey of positions but a diagnosis: which of these impressions are mistakes, and where exactly does each one go wrong? In particular I'd like to understand what kind of question the special composition question is supposed to be — whether it's asking after a fact about the world, a fact about our concepts, or something else entirely — and whether the slide from "no non-arbitrary answer" to "it's up to us" is one I should be making. If it helps, tell me how someone who thinks composition is entirely mind-independent would respond to each of my worries, and whether that response costs them anything.
