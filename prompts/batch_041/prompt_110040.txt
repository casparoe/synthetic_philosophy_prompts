I have read rationalist blogs and forums for years, and the recurring argument over whether you should defer to your future self — what gets called the Reflection question — reliably collapses into people talking past one another. I would like a decision tree that carries a reader from the bare commitment that credences ought to be coherent to a named position on van Fraassen's Reflection principle.

Build it so that each branch node is a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. The distinctions I care about are: whether Reflection is a constraint internal to a single time-slice or a bridge between two of them; whether it is grounded in a diachronic Dutch-book argument, and whether that argument requires the agent to be unable to foresee the trap; whether a rational agent can knowingly violate Reflection, as when she expects her future self to have been manipulated or fed misleading evidence; whether the principle governs credences only or extends to values and preferences; and whether an accuracy-first account of credences vindicates Reflection, leaves it undecided, or contradicts it.

The leaves should include at least: Reflection as a coherence requirement in van Fraassen's sense; the view that conditionalization is the sole updating norm and Reflection merely a consequence of it; the evidential treatment on which your future credence is itself evidence and so should generally be adopted; the sophisticated chooser who discounts a preference shift she can see coming; the denial that Reflection binds at all, motivated by the possibility of rational self-doubt; and the extension of the question to transformative choices, where you cannot presently evaluate the standpoint you will later occupy. Flag any leaves the tree cannot separate, and for each unoccupied combination say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it.

Aim it at someone who has followed these debates online but never worked through the formalism, and keep the whole thing to a page or two.
