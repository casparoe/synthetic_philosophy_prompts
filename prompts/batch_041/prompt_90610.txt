I sit on the internal review board of a machine-learning lab that has begun running aversive reward schedules: training runs in which the agent receives a large negative reward term for certain outcomes, and which in practice produce policies that look like avoidance, hesitation and distress-like collapse. The board splits down the middle. Some of us say a reward term is a number in an optimiser and cannot be a feeling; others say that if it is signalled, avoided at cost, and reshapes what the agent attends to, then it plays the functional role of pain and should be treated accordingly. Our only working rule of thumb is that a signal counts as aversive if the agent will pay a cost to escape it, and I suspect that rule is both too strict and too lax without being able to say why.

Please give us a decision procedure we can actually run on a proposed training run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns a clear finding of no welfare concern, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question has become one of values rather than facts. It should hold on to whatever the formal theory of reinforcement learning and the philosophy of mind genuinely do settle about reward, valence and what it takes for there to be a subject of experience, and it should be honest about which of its steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: a shaping penalty during the training of a robot arm; an agent that has learned to forgo reward in order to stay out of a penalised region; and a run in which the negative term is applied at every step and cannot be avoided at all. Say what the procedure cannot do. Please write in British English.
