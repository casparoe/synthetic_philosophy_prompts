A colleague in our logic reading group keeps settling arguments with the line "some contradictions are true," most recently to defend a paraconsistent treatment of a paradox, where the claim was doing real work rather than just gesturing. I want a precise map of what that one sentence could be asserting. Lay the readings out as a menu, ordered from the trivial to the radical, varying along at least three axes: what "contradiction" picks out (a pair consisting of a proposition and its negation, a single conjunctive proposition of the form p-and-not-p, a theory that entails both members of such a pair, a sentence assigned a glut truth value); what "true" is doing (truth simpliciter, truth in a model, warranted assertibility, derivability in a target theory); and how wide the "some" is meant to range. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or plainly false, and say which readings the dialetheist actually needs for the use the line was put to and which are merely smuggled in by the wording. Please also keep the distinctions sharp between a true contradiction, a truth-value glut, a truth-value gap, and a theory that is inconsistent but simply false, and note where paraconsistency does and does not follow from any of these. End by telling me which single reading the people who repeat this line are usually reaching for, and whether that reading survives the paradox it was invoked to handle.
