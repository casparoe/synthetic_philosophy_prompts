I'm a TA for a graduate seminar on machine ethics, and I need to draft one of the essay questions for the final. The reason I'm asking rather than just writing it myself is that my roommate works on ML safety and we got into this long fight last night that I can't stop chewing on. His line is that "implementing ethics in a machine" is basically a solved-shaped problem: you collect a big pile of human moral judgments, you train a model to predict them, and if it generalizes well enough you've got a machine that reasons ethically. My line is that this is a category error, but every time I try to say why I end up gesturing at the rules-versus-learning distinction or dredging up a trolley case, and both of those are exactly the answers I don't want on the exam because students have them memorized. What actually bugs me is something narrower: a system trained to match human approval is optimizing for approval, not for the good, so the better it gets the more it will track what people will sign off on — which is sycophancy wearing the mask of ethics — and it'll have nothing to say on cases where the training signal is silent, the genuinely unprecedented ones. And here's the part I want to smuggle into the question: there's an old debate in ethics about whether someone who just outsources every moral call to a reliable authority is really doing moral thinking or only ventriloquizing it, and I think that's the sharpest way to press my roommate's view, because it forces you to say what "grasping a reason" adds over being disposed to output the right verdict. But I don't want to just hand students the analogy pre-chewed either.

So: write me one essay question for this seminar — upper-level grad students, they've read the standard stuff — that makes them confront whether a system that reliably produces correct moral verdicts by tracking human approval can be said to reason ethically at all, or only to imitate ethical reasoning, and that forces them to get specific about what would have to be true of a machine for us to credit it with grasping a reason rather than merely being disposed to emit the verdict. It should make them take a position on whether the moral-deference analogy holds and, if it does, exactly where it breaks when the deferring agent is a network rather than a person. And it should force them to say something concrete about the unprecedented case — what a system that only tracks approval can and can't do when there's no precedent to match, and whether that gap is a bug you patch with more data or a structural limit. No bibliography, no names-and-dates recitation, and please don't let it be answerable by reciting the top-down-rules vs. bottom-up-learning taxonomy. Then give me a model answer and a marking scheme: the model answer no more than 600 words, and the scheme built around five must-hit moves with marks that total 20, plus a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong, and a clear statement of what separates an A from a B. Keep the whole thing under about 1300 words total.
