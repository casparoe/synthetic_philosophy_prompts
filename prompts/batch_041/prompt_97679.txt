For most of my adult life I have managed to avoid the business of blaming people, but a few months ago I was asked to chair the conduct committee of the housing co-operative where I live, and I have since found myself on both ends of an argument I had never examined. I have had to censure neighbours for breaking rules that I have broken myself; and I have been taken to task, in front of others, by people whose own conduct is no better than the conduct they were attacking in me. Each time, someone told me the same thing: that the criticism did not count, because the person making it had no standing. The first time I heard it I was the one being blamed and I wanted to believe it; the second time I was doing the blaming and found it less persuasive. I would rather not settle the matter by seeing which way the wind happens to be blowing for me, so I would like you to draft the joint statement that two camps in this dispute could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

The two camps are as follows. The first holds that hypocrisy — more carefully, a mismatch between the standard a blamer invokes and the blamer's own conduct — defeats the blamer's standing to blame: the criticism may be perfectly accurate, yet it is not something that person is in a position to make. The second holds that hypocrisy does not defeat standing: a hypocrite may still blame, and whatever is wrong with hypocrisy, if anything, is a separate fault that does not remove the blamer's entitlement to hold another to account.

Please produce a document in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims that both camps can sign: about what blame is for, about what the notion of standing is meant to do in this debate, about which cases are uncontroversial, and about what would have to be true for either side to be right. Second, the points on which they still differ, each written in wording that the camp in question would accept as a fair statement of its own position rather than a caricature of it. Third, a table of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — giving for each crux a case or argument that both camps would agree bears on it, together with each camp's prediction, recorded in advance, about how it comes out.

I would particularly like the collaboration to test a distinction that I have found useful but do not fully trust, between inconsistency that is concurrent (blaming someone for the very thing one is doing at that moment) and inconsistency that is residual (blaming someone for something one did in the past and has since given up). Let them run that distinction against three cases: the reformed wrongdoer who now blames others for the wrong she has left behind; the person who has never committed the wrong herself but benefits from a system in which it is widespread; and the person who blames on behalf of a group to which she belongs and which is itself implicated in the wrong. If some part of the disagreement turns out to be merely verbal — the two camps using the word 'standing' for different things — say which part and why. Please write in British English.
