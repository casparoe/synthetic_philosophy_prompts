I keep going back and fourth on something and I'd like you to help me compare two ways of thinking about everyday local conventions — the usual suspects: tipping percentages, which side of the escalator you stand on, queueing vs. just milling around at a bar, whether you jaywalk when there's no traffic. Concept A: a convention is nothing but whatever regularity a bunch of people have settled into because it solves some small coordination problem, and it has no moral weight of its own — the only reason to follow it is downstream (you'll annoy people, you'll get worse service, you'll get a weird look). Concept B: a convention is a genuine social norm, so once you're in the relevant place you actually owe it to the people there to conform, even when the coordination payoff is trivial and nobody's watching. I want a real comparison, not a survey of the literature: where do these two come apart in particular cases, and which one better explains our actual reactions — including the reactions we're a bit embarrassed by?

Now here's the twist I actually care about. Add a third thing, which I'll call a "convention transplant": a visitor who keeps following their home convention (leaves 5%, stands on the left, waits to be seated) because that's just what's normal where they're from. Under Concept A the visitor looks fine so long as they're not harming anyone; under Concept B they're doing something wrong simply by not conforming. Both feel too quick, because some local conventions are harmless coordination (which side of the escalator, which door you use) while others have real distributional stakes (tipping norms that decide whether a server can make rent; jaywalking norms that set the de facto rule for who yields to whom). So: does the moral force of a convention depend on whether it's thin coordination or thick and stake-laden? And does splitting them that way change whether a visitor is obligated, merely permitted, or even required to keep their own convention? Tell me which of the two concepts survives once we seperate conventions along that line, and where each one has to be patched up to stay standing.
