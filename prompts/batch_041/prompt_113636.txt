Im building a handout on Sen's liberal paradox and I need a fresh worked example, because the Prude-and-Lewd one is worn out, and half the seminar now suspects the whole paradox is just an artifact of the prude being a busybody who can't stand other people enjoying themselves. So: build me a case to order, and the busybody has to be banned.

The frame: two people, call them whatever you like, each with one small private matter of her own to decide (keep a dog or not, take the night shift or not, plant the tree or not — ordinary stuff). That gives exactly four outcomes, one per combination of the two choices. Each person is to be decisive over the pair of outcomes that differ only in her own choice, with the other person's choice held fixed. I want the right to be strictly personal in that sense — no right that quietly smuggles in the other person's business.

Then I want the standard mess: a cycle in the social ordering, one edge coming from A's personal right, one from B's, and one from weak Pareto, i.e. the pair where both strictly prefer one outcome to the other.

Here's the constraint that makes it interesting. No busybody preferences. Nobody may prefer an outcome even partly because it stops the other person doing her thing — no quiet disapproval, no preferring that neither of us does it. Each person cares about her own choice. But she is allowed a taste for her own choice that depends on his: she wants to do her thing only if he's doing his, or only if he isn't, so that the appeal of her own option flips with his behaviour. If your case ends up implying some ranking across his choice at fixed hers, that's fine, but you have to show it falls out of the conditional taste for her own option and isn't a separate dislike of what he does. I'm fairly confident this is enough, and that the flip is exactly where the work gets done, but show it rather than assert it.

Give me, in order: the scenario in two or three sentences with the two options named; each person's complete ranking over the four outcomes, in symbols; the derivation, naming the pair each right covers and the pair Pareto covers, then the three social edges and the cycle; a check that nothing else you've generated contradicts the edges you need; and then the honest part — which single preference is load-bearing, flip it and show the cycle dies, and admit it if a nosy preference has crept back in through the back door.

If you conclude it genuinely can't be done, don't fake it: say why, then give me the case with the smallest possible dose of busybody preference and point at the one preference doing the work.

Last, a paragraph for the handout: what does a defender of Sen say to the objection that the paradox is only an artifact of nosy preferences, and does your case help that reply or hurt it?
