I'm a third-year student in a course on collective behaviour and rational choice, and next week I'm leading the forty-minute discussion of our session on information cascades and rational herding — fifteen classmates, all of whom have done the same reading and most of whom have come away with nothing more precise than "a cascade is when people copy each other." I don't want cascades explained to me; I want a plan for explaining them. Work out the order in which the components have to go so that each step feels forced rather than asserted — I'm assuming it has to start with Bayesian updating from a private signal, then the distinction between the action being observable and the signal not being, then the second person's inference, then the third person's, and only then the definition of a cascade — but tell me where that order is wrong, what can be collapsed, and whether the class should derive the third person's inference themselves or be walked through it. Choose the single example that carries the whole session. My candidate is two study spots of unknown quietness, where each arriving student has one private observation and can see only where the earlier arrivals sat; tell me whether to keep it or replace it, and say exactly what the example must have (identical and independent payoffs, sequential arrival, no communication) and must not have (payoff externalities, prices, a first mover who is visibly expert), since I suspect the canonical urn and restaurant versions smuggle in intuitions I'd rather they built for themselves. Then map the misconceptions. Mine, in the order I expect them: that herding is a lapse of rationality; that a cascade means the group landed on the truth; that any conformity is a cascade; that a cascade needs a crowd or a confident leader; and that a cascade, once started, is broken by someone with a strong private signal — I believe the asymmetry is that public information breaks it and private signals don't, and I want to know whether that should be the climax of the session. Tell me which of these are not misconceptions at all, which I have in the wrong order, what I've missed, and the precise moment in the sequence where each should be headed off. Finish with one question I can put to the room that will distinguish students who have understood the structure from students who can recite it, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so out loud. Be committed: where I've offered you a choice, make the choice and defend it rather than laying out the options. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the ordering and the example you're committing to — then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
