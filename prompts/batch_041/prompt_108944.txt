I'm a high school junior, and next month I'm presenting a proposal to my school's student council about how we choose the end-of-year class trip; last year plain majority rule produced a trip that was almost nobody's first or second choice, so my plan keeps majority rule for the overall ranking but gives every student one personal veto token, which they may spend anonymously after the preliminary ranking is posted to flip a single pairwise comparison between two trip options. The idea is that each student gets exactly one matter that is genuinely theirs to decide while the group decides everything else by majority, and I think that design dodges the famous conflict between giving people private spheres and respecting unanimous preference — my civics teacher says I have reinvented the liberal paradox in a hoodie, and the AP Economics teacher who taught our unit on voting systems has already told me she will be there. The council is mostly not philosophers, but those two will do nearly all the questioning and the rest will follow their lead. Give me exactly five objections to expect, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way one of those two teachers would actually phrase it, each followed by my best reply in no more than two sentences and a brief note on where that reply is soft; make the fifth the objection I am hoping nobody raises, say plainly what I should concede up front, and write it as a numbered list of five entries with no introduction and no conclusion.
