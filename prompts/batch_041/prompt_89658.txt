I retired a couple of years ago after forty-odd years in physical chemistry, and I've been using the time to actually read philosophy instead of just arguing about it over dinner. I've gotten a bit stuck on Hempel's raven paradox. Not the puzzle itself, I get the puzzle. I'm stuck on whether the standard Bayesian line really dissolves it or just dresses it up.

Here's the position I've talked myself into. There is no paradox, because there was never a genuine intuition to rescue. A non-black non-raven, a white shoe, a red herring, whatever, really does confirm that all ravens are black, by a vanishingly small amount, and the only reason it feels wrong is that we slide from "this barely moves my credence" to "this is not evidence at all." Separate the amount of confirmation from its existence, accept that confirmation survives logical equivalence, and Hempel's result is just a correct theorem with a misleading feel to it. Case closed.

So give me the case against that. Not a survey of the literature, and please don't send me off to read things, I can't look anything up right now anyway. I want the actual defeaters, sorted by how decisive they'd be. The kinds of things I'm fishing for: arguments that the Bayesian move changes the subject, because Hempel was after a qualitative, entailment-flavoured notion of confirmation (his satisfaction criterion) and answering in degrees might be answering a different question than the one he asked. Or results showing the "tiny increment" isn't so tiny in some setups, or that it hangs entirely off the prior in a way that makes the whole resolution circular. Or the equivalence condition itself, whether anything makes it fail or makes it vacuous. Or the tacking/irrelevant-conjunction business, where the Bayesian story seems to confirm far too much. And I want to know whether the position predicts anything its rivals don't, or whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability, where every awkward case just gets absorbed by fiddling with the prior. If it has, tell me straight whether that's a real defect or just what a good account of confirmation is supposed to look like.

Two things to fold in. First, a variant I came up with walking the dog, and I want to know if it's a real wedge or just me being a chemist about it: drop the universal generalization and make the hypothesis the singular prediction that the very next raven I examine will be black. Does the non-raven evidence confirm *that*? If the paradox dissolves for the universal but not for the singular prediction, or the other way round, what does that tell us about which notion of confirmation was doing the work all along? Second, my own field's version of the thing: I spent decades caring whether every sample of a certain compound is diamagnetic, and by the raven logic, checking that some random rock isn't magnetic would count as evidence for it. I never once treated it that way, and neither did anyone else. Is that a datum the Bayesian account has to explain away, or is it just the same small-increment story and I'm being sentimental about my old lab?
