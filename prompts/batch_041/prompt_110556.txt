I'm a novelist, mostly, so take what follows with a grain of salt. I'm about halfway through a popular philosophy of mind book a friend pressed on me (I won't name it, partly because I'm only halfway and partly because I suspect it's doing something sneaky), and I want to know how much of its treatment of epiphenomenalism I should actually trust before I let it color the character I'm writing, who's a neuroscientist and a bit of a fatalist about her own mind. Here's roughly how the book lays it out, in my own paraphrase. Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experience is a byproduct of brain activity: the brain causes the experience, but the experience does nothing in return. Your arm moves and your mouth says "ow" purely because of neurons firing; the feeling of pain is like the steam whistle on a train -- a sign of what the engine is doing, never part of what drives it. Since the mental never pushes back on the physical, the author says epiphenomenalism turns out to be a strange bedfellow of the eliminativists: it's really a roundabout way of saying consciousness is an illusion, just one our brains are wired to keep reporting. He then raises what I think he calls the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If experiences never cause anything, how come we're so reliable about them? When I say "I'm seeing red," it's supposed to be the redness of the experience that explains the words -- but on epiphenomenalism the experience can't explain anything. He says this is a flat contradiction at the heart of the view and more or less finishes it off: a theory that says our reports about our own minds are caused by the very things it says can't cause anything. The only exit, he says, is to claim that the brain state produces both the experience and the matching report -- but that just relabels the mystery as a coincidence. From there he conlcudes that any view that treats consciousness as non-physical but real is doomed, and that we should all be identity theorists instead, treating the experience as simply being the brain state.

So: how much of that is fair? I'd like it claim by claim -- which parts a philosopher of mind would nod along to, which they'd wince at, and which are just wrong -- and then a verdict on whether his final conclusion actually follows from the parts that survive. I have a suspicion the eliminativist comparison is off and that "contradiction" is doing more work than it should, but I don't know enough to say, and I'm especially unsure about the last move: even granting the paradox is a real problem, does it really take down everything non-physical, or only the views that make experience causally inert? No reading list, please -- I just want the audit.
