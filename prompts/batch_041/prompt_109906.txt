I'm a science journalist — I write a column that's mostly about arguments that have escaped the seminar room and are now being repeated at dinner parties. Theres a move I keep running into and I don't know what to call it, or whether it's one thing or three.

The shape is always the same: someone claims your views can't all be right, not because any one of them is false, but because if a clever enough opponent got you to act on them one at a time, you'd end up worse off no matter how the world turned out. Three places I've seen it lately:

- A friend who's relaxed about her probabilities — she'll cheerfully say there's a 60% chance of rain and a 60% chance of no rain, and shrug when I point it out, on the grounds that nobody is ever going to make her bet on it. Someone told her this made her "incoherent," and she asked: incoherent to who?
- A colleague who insists a certain moral view refutes itself, because if you really held it you'd agree to a chain of trades that leaves you in a position you'd hate. I can't tell whether that's a real argument or a magic trick — it seems to depend on whether you'd actually take the trades.
- A philosophy podcast where the guest argued that a man with a cycle of preferences — A over B, B over C, C over A — is being irrational, even though he's never once in his life been offered the full round of trades, and would probably refuse the whole loop if he saw it coming.

I'm fairly sure the probability one has a name, and I suspect the preference one does too. What I actually want to know is whether there's a name for the general thing — the style of argument that says a set of attitudes is defective because it's exploitable, or extractable, or can be turned into a guaranteed loss — and whether it's genuinely the same argument in all three cases or a family of cousins that happen to share a slogan. Where does the analogy hold and where does it break? Does it matter that no adversary exists, or that the trades would never really be offered, or that the person could simply refuse once she sees the loop coming? Does the argument show the attitudes are wrong, or only that they're risky to hold? And is there a case where someone has exactly this exploitability problem and is nonetheless completely fine — where the whole style of argument misfires?

I'm not after a reading list, I want the concept nailed down and the edges marked, including the cases where whatever name I've probably got in mind turns out not to fit.
