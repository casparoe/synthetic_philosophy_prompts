Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about how children ought to be chosen, and I lost; on Sunday I have to give a short speech at the naming ceremony of her niece, and I would like your help writing it.

The argument, briefly. She holds that when parents can screen embryos they are not merely permitted but required to choose the one expected to have the best life, and that this requirement runs continuously from screening out a devastating disease to breaking a tie, at the margin, on a trait that has nothing to do with health. I said I found the extension repellent. She asked where the line falls. I said disease is different because it is a disease. She said that is a label doing the work of an argument, and that if there is a line I ought to be able to say why it falls where it does rather than one trait further along.

Then she gave me the argument that kept me up. The child who was born is not the child who would have been born had a different embryo been transferred, so the child I will hold on Sunday was not harmed by the selection; and the embryo that was not transferred never became anyone, so was not harmed either. Whatever is troubling about what her sister did, she says, it is not that anyone was made worse off. She asked whether my objection is really just distaste at the form of the decision — the unease one feels at someone choosing a spouse from a spreadsheet — or else a claim about the kind of people who do this. I could not answer, and I am not willing to say either of those things in front of her family.

Here is the occasion. The parents are old friends of mine, both scientists, and they are entirely open and unapologetic about what they did: two rounds of IVF, three embryos, a clinic report that scored them, and a choice settled by a marker unrelated to any disease. They have asked me to speak for five minutes because they want someone to say what we hope for the child rather than merely to welcome her. The room will hold about thirty people. The baby's grandmother is devout and believes the whole business is a trespass; she will hear any warmth about the clinic as agreement with it. A great-aunt is a retired midwife. A cousin works for a disability rights organisation and listens for euphemism. And my friend, the one I argued with, will be in the second row, waiting to see whether I quietly drop my position or quietly concede hers.

Write the speech. Five minutes is roughly 650 to 750 words, and it should be a speech — addressed to a room, with the child's name in it (call her Nora), spoken by someone who is fond of these people and not embarrassed to be.

It has to respect four constraints. It may not say or imply that Nora's worth depends on her traits, or that her parents' love for her is conditional, or that she is fortunate to have been chosen. It may not pretend that the selection did not happen or that it was morally neutral, and it may not put the parents in the dock. It may not claim that genes do not matter, which the parents would find absurd and the cousin would find evasive. And it may not rest on a rule that my friend has already shown me I cannot state.

What I want out of it is a position, not a shrug — something I can still defend to her at the reception without pretending the last four hours did not happen. It need not persuade the grandmother or the cousin; it needs to be true in front of both of them.

After the speech, add a short note of four or five sentences telling me which sentences in it you regard as load-bearing and what each of the four constraints cost me, so that I know what I have conceded if someone presses me on it afterwards.
