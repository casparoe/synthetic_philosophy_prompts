For a couple of years now a close friend and I have been arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I've noticed that her judgments on particular cases hang together in a way I can't quite pin down, so I want your help predicting what she'll say next and, more importantly, working out the rule she's actually running. She isn't an academic and she doesn't use any jargon, but she's thought about this a great deal and is remarkably consistent. Here is her track record. A woman who dies peacefully in her sleep at ninety, having finished everything she cared about, with no wishes left unsatisfied and nothing she was looking forward to, is in my friend's view not harmed at all; she calls it about the best ending a life can have. A man who dies painlessly at thirty, in the middle of a happy and busy life, with a family he adores and work he is absorbed in and plans stretching years ahead, is on her view gravely harmed; she says it is among the worst things that can befall a person. A person in an irreversible vegetative state, with no consciousness and no prospect of recovery, who then dies, is not harmed in her view. And a man in the last stage of a painful illness, in constant suffering, who has lost all interest in the future and wants only for it to end, is not harmed in her view either; she thinks dying may even be good for him. For what it's worth, she also holds that whatever happens to your reputation after you are dead cannot harm you, though she admits she can't say exactly why that is, and she treats it as the same kind of point as the rest.

Now the case I want you to predict. A young man, healthy and in his prime, has through years of discipline reached a state of complete equanimity: he wants nothing, fears nothing, is content in each present moment, and has no desires at all about his future. He dies suddenly and painlessly. What no one knows is that, had he lived, he would gradually have come out of this state, formed new attachments, and enjoyed several more decades of good life. What will my friend say about whether his death is bad for him? I care much less about the verdict than about the rule. Set out the two or three principles that fit all of her earlier verdicts equally well — I have a feeling one of them is a deprivation or comparison view and another turns on the frustration of the desires a person actually holds, but you may find better candidates — and say what each one predicts about the young man. Then tell me which rule you would bet she is running and why, and what principle she would be committing herself to with each of the alternatives. Finally, name a further case, different from any I've given, that would tell the candidate rules apart, and say which way each rule would have to go in that case. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a friend — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
