I was a bystander to an argument at a departmental reading group last term and I should like it adjudicated rather than simply decided. I have no stake in the outcome and I have tried to report both sides fairly; where I could not remember the exact wording I have paraphrased, so please judge the substance of each move rather than any turn of phrase.

The two participants were Mara, who works on the philosophy of social science, and Dev, a historian of science. The question was whether 'socially constructed' means the same thing when applied to money, to race and to gender, or whether the phrase does different work in each case. Mara held that it means one thing throughout, and that the differences people point to are downstream of the construction rather than part of what construction is. Dev held that the phrase is contrastive — a kind is constructed as opposed to something, and unless you say what, the claim is empty — and that money and race are constructed in different senses. To discipline the discussion they agreed on two tests of their own invention, which I set out because everything turns on them:

The off-switch test: imagine the practices that sustain the kind abolished. Does the kind disappear, or does something with much the same extension and effects survive?
The feedback test: do the classifications feed back into the things classified, so that members of the kind are altered by being classified?

They also each used one invented case, and I do not think the argument can be judged without them. Dev's was a currency union he called the Transitional Mark: one morning the notes lose legal-tender status by decree, and while the paper survives, the kind does not. Mara's was a census that invents the category 'Highland-descended' for one round of counting and drops it for the next.

The exchange ran as follows.

(M1) Mara: all three kinds are constructed in one sense — their existence depends on collective acceptance, and nothing in the world fixes their extension independently of us. On this standard a great many things are constructed: a border, a species, a way of sorting quarks. She says this is not a reductio but the point, since 'constructed' contrasts with 'unconstructed' and nothing is unconstructed.

(M2) Dev: if nothing is unconstructed, the claim does no work. Money fails the off-switch test; quarks and species pass it. Race, he says, sits between them in a way that matters: the rules of racial classification are ours, but they are imposed on a population whose existence does not depend on the rules, so our categories are made while the thing categorised is not. A banknote, by contrast, is money because of the rules and only because of them. Mara's single sense flattens a real difference: money is constituted by convention, whereas race is a convention applied to something that pre-exists it.

(M3) Mara: the pre-exists-it move smuggles biology back in through the side door. Human phenotypic variation is real, but which variation becomes the basis of a kind is a social choice, and ancestry looks like the natural basis only because institutions have spent centuries reproducing it as salient. His off-switch test is also unstable: what would it be to abolish the practices that sustain race? He gets the asymmetry he wants only by describing the counterfactual so that the practices vanish for money and persist for race. Hold the counterfactual fixed and both kinds go.

(M4) Dev: grant the counterfactual point; the tests still come apart. Money has no feedback — a banknote does not adjust its conduct on learning that it is money, the central authority can void it by decree, and nothing classified has an interest in the classification. Race does feed back: those classified respond to the classification by living up to it, resisting it or internalising it, so the kind is partly constituted by the responses it elicits, and no administrator can void it. He calls this the difference between kinds we merely use and kinds that use us back.

(M5) Mara: the feedback point shows race is more socially real, not less. By Dev's own test it ranks race above money in constructedness. Money's causal powers run through one centralised convention that a decree can switch off; race is sustained by diffuse practices that reproduce bodies, beliefs and institutions together, which is precisely why nobody can switch it off. She adds that his off-switch argument is an argument about harm and politics dressed up as metaphysics: he has shown that it is worse to be wrong about race than about money, not that race is less constructed.

(M6) Dev: he accepts that he ran two questions together, and reformulates. The difference is not a matter of degree of constructedness but of what constitutes membership. For money the rules are exhaustive — nothing outside them contributes to a note's being money. For race they are not exhaustive, because they must be applied to a population they did not make, and the application is what produces the harm. There are therefore three positions rather than two: constructivism about the category, realism about the population, and a mixed view of the kind. Mara replies that the mixed view is constructivism with extra steps, since a population sorted for centuries is a product of the sorting. Neither would move.

Please adjudicate this point by point. Do not simply award the exchange to one side: where a move reaches a defensible conclusion by a defective route, or is right about one thing and wrong about another, say so.
