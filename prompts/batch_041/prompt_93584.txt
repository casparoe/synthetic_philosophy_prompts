I have been following the rationalist blogs' long-running arguments about the Cartesian theatre since the old days, and I want to teach a seminar that reads Christopher Nolan's Memento through the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction rather than the usual personal-identity angle. Open with your verdict in the first sentence, then argue for it: does the film genuinely dramatize the puzzle Dennett raises, or does it quietly assume the very thing he denies?

Assume this much going in. Dennett uses the color phi phenomenon, in which a blue dot flashed at the top of a screen and a red dot flashed shortly afterward at the bottom are seen not as two dots but as a single dot travelling between them and changing colour in mid-flight, to separate two hypotheses about the timing of experience. On the Stalinesque hypothesis, named for the show trials whose verdicts were fixed in advance, the brain reconciles the two stimuli before either reaches consciousness, so the subject literally experiences the patched motion and the mid-path colour change. On the Orwellian hypothesis, named for the Ministry of Truth, the subject first has an ordinary unpatched experience and then the memory of it is rewritten in light of the second stimulus. Dennett's interest is not in choosing between them but in the suspicion that the choice is empirically idle, because both presuppose a privileged instant at which unconscious processing becomes experience, a finish line the subjects themselves could never locate from the inside.

Now read Memento against this. Leonard Shelby cannot form new memories; he rebuilds his world from tattoos, Polaroids and notes, and by the end we see he is willing to doctor that record in order to keep a purpose. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, so please take up at least these:

- Where the film maps onto the Orwellian case, an experience that genuinely occurred and is then falsified in the record, and where it maps onto the Stalinesque case, a past fabricated before it was ever his to experience: the Sammy Jankis story he may have confabulated, the borrowed memories, the self he assembles out of them.
- The reverse-chronological structure puts the audience in an Orwellian position, since we watch a scene and only later learn its true antecedents, which forces us to revise what we thought we saw. Is that a formal enactment of memory-revision, or a trick that flatters us with the illusion of a real sequence lying underneath?
- Dennett's whole point is that there may be no fact of the matter about what Leonard experienced independent of the record he leaves behind. Does the film agree, or does it keep gesturing, Cartesian-materialist fashion, at a true past that the record merely distorts?
- Where the film resists the reading: the timescales are vastly longer than in the color-phi case, so the analogy may strain, and Leonard's grief may be doing emotional work that the argument cannot absorb.

Close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that the color-phi case cannot, some way of inhabiting from the inside the underdetermination Dennett describes from the outside. No preamble and no restating of the question.
