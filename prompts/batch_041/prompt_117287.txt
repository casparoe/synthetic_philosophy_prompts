Some friends were over for dinner on Saturday, and my fourteen-year-old nephew was at the table, working his way through a plate of food and a phone. Somebody asked him what seventeen times twenty-three was, and he asked the phone, and then, before anyone could say anything, he asked the phone to work it out a second way so he could see whether the two answers matched. That started an argument between two of my friends, Dana and Owen, that outlasted dessert and then the washing-up. I stayed out of it — partly because I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, and partly because the boy was sitting right there — but I have been chewing on it since, and what I want is not a general essay about phones and young people. I want to know where each of them was right and where each was talking past the other.

Dana's position was that getting smarter just is being able to hold more in your head and see further ahead before you act, and that this is precisely the difference between us and the animals — that we can run the thing inside ourselves and let the idea die instead of us. Hand that job to a machine, she said, and the capacity goes unused, and an unused capacity does not grow; worse, the boy cannot tell when the phone is wrong, because telling takes the very thing he has handed over. She said the ladder has a top rung, and the phone is not it; the phone is a way of skipping a rung. Owen said the ladder he had read about in some book on animal minds has four rungs rather than three: creatures that come out of the egg already knowing what to do; creatures that learn from what happens to them; creatures that can try things out inside their heads first; and creatures that think with things outside their heads — a knotted string, a tally, a book, another person. On that picture the phone is the fourth rung doing its job, no more a betrayal of the third than writing was, and the boy asking for a second method is doing something a lone memorizer cannot do. He added that Dana's own diary and her recipe book are outside her head too.

Dana's answer was that a notebook is not a phone that answers: to put something in a notebook you have to have worked it out first, so the notebook preserves the third rung rather than replacing it. And she said Owen's line about the idea dying instead of us only earns its keep if the thing that dies is mine — if nothing of mine is tested, nothing of mine is learned, and the phone's answer is not something I can check, because checking is the third rung. Owen replied that this is exactly why the fourth rung counts as a rung and not a swindle: no single head can hold everything, so the checking moves outward — to other people, to other tools, to the habit of asking twice — in the same way the third rung had moved it inward, and if the phone is a poor tool because it is opaque, that is an argument for better tools rather than for climbing back down. Dana's last word was that this cannot be right as a general rule, because some tools plainly do hollow out the rung below them, and a story in which every step outward is a step upward has no way of telling the two cases apart. Then the table moved on to something else and nothing was settled. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Dana, Owen, Dana, Owen, Dana — saying of each move whether it is sound, half-right, or mistaken, and why, then identifying the single point on which the whole dispute turns, and finally saying what both of them are missing? Please do not flatten it into the usual debate about whether technology makes us stupid; I am interested in the structure of the argument they actually had. End your reply with a line in this form: Verdict: [who had the better of it overall, and the one point on which the other was nonetheless right].
