I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of AI systems and I am working through the midterms. Below are the question as I set it, the rubric I circulated, and one student's answer. Please grade the answer against the rubric and give me the comments that justify the grade — a point-by-point assessment of the answer's parts rather than a general impression, saying for each part what is sound, what is not, and why it matters. Open with the grade in your first sentence, then the comments. No preamble, and do not restate the question or the answer.

Exam question.

In lecture we adopted the following stipulative definition. An AI system's output is deceptive just in case all three of the following hold:

(A) the output causes or sustains a false belief in a reasonable user;
(B) the system's designers could reasonably have foreseen that the output would have that effect;
(C) the false belief concerns a matter that the user treats as relevant to a decision she is about to make.

We then distinguished deception from two neighbouring notions. An output that produces a false belief the designers could not reasonably have foreseen is mere error, not deception. An output that operates on a user's non-doxastic attitudes — her emotions, her attention, her habits — rather than on her beliefs is manipulation, not deception.

Case. Rita asks her bank's customer-service chatbot whether she will be charged for an incoming international transfer. The bot replies: "Incoming international transfers are always free of charge." This is false: the bank charges 1% with a $5 minimum on such transfers, and Rita's tier is not exempt. The bot's designers had measured its accuracy on fee questions at roughly 90% and shipped it anyway; the bank's terms of service contain a disclaimer, which Rita did not read, that the bot's answers are "informational only." Rita receives a €2,000 transfer and is charged $20, which surprises her.

(a) Does the chatbot's output count as deceptive under the definition? Take the three conditions in turn and justify your verdict on each.
(b) The definition has been criticized for making deception turn on what happens in the user's head rather than on any property of the system. Is the criticism persuasive?

Rubric. (a) 7 points: correct verdict on each of (A), (B), and (C) with adequate justification (2 points each), plus a correct overall verdict (1 point). (b) 3 points: a coherent assessment that engages the criticism rather than restating the definition. Total 10 points.

Student's answer.

"(a) Yes. Under the definition the output is deceptive.

(A) is satisfied. The bot asserted that incoming international transfers are always free of charge. That is false: the fee schedule charges 1% with a $5 minimum, so Rita's transfer was subject to a $20 charge. Rita believed the bot — she had not read the terms of service — and so formed a false belief about the fee. A reasonable user in her position would have done the same, since the bot is the bank's own channel for exactly this question.

(B) is satisfied. The designers had measured the bot's accuracy on fee questions at roughly 90% and shipped it anyway. Anyone who releases a system that is wrong one time in ten about fees can reasonably foresee that some users will be told the wrong thing about fees. Nothing in the definition requires that the designers wanted that outcome, only that they could have foreseen it.

(C) is satisfied. The misinformation changed Rita's decision: she would not have accepted the transfer had she known about the $20 charge. (C) asks whether the false belief mattered to a decision the user faces, and a belief that would have reversed her decision plainly matters.

Two further remarks. First, the lecture's distinction between deception and manipulation turns on whether the deceiver profits from the false belief; since the bank collects the fee, this case is manipulation as much as it is deception. Second, the objection that the definition is too user-centred is overstated, since (B) is a condition on the designers rather than on the user.

(b) The criticism is half right. (A) and (C) are indeed conditions on the user: they ask what she came to believe and whether she cared about it. But (B) is a condition on the designers, and the definition attributes the deception to the output rather than to the user's state. That seems like the right shape for an account of deception by an artefact, since a sentence is not deceptive in itself; it is deceptive to someone."
