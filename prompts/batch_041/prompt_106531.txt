I sit on the staff of a state sentencing commission, where I am the analyst responsible for converting the commission's philosophical commitments into the worksheets that probation officers and parole examiners actually fill out in the field, and we are midway through the first substantive revision of our guidelines in over a decade. The chair has asked me to design a decision procedure that lets an examiner who has no legal or philosophical training weigh two things our current guidelines simply mash together into a single grid: the proportionality judgment that a particular sentence is what the offense and the offender actually deserve, and the forward-looking judgments about risk, deterrence, and the prospect of rehabilitation. I have been reading the long-running exchange between Dennett and Caruso on free will, moral responsibility, and punishment, and I keep going back and forth on how much of it is usable by someone who will never read either of them. Caruso's line, as I understand it, is that the skeptic can keep nearly everything we do with offenders — containment, deterrence, rehabilitation, even moral reproach — and needs to discard only the retributive core, the idea that suffering is good because it is deserved. Dennett's line, as I understand it, is that the skeptic is giving away something we need and can actually keep, and that the compatibilist notion of responsibility survives determinism because our practices track reasons-responsiveness rather than some metaphysical gateway. I am not asking you to adjudicate between them or to hand me a reading list — I have read enough of the debate and citations are useless to me anyway. I want the translation: what does each position actually commit a practitioner to, once you strip out the rhetoric, and what is the least distorting procedure that honors both the limiting role of desert and the forward-looking role of risk.

Please write that procedure for me. It needs to be runnable by an examiner in under an hour per case, with the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and — this matters most to me — the points where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. I am especially interested in whether desert should function as a ceiling that forward-looking considerations may lower but never raise, or as an anchor that risk may adjust in either direction, and I want you to tell me which choice the Dennettian and the Carusonian each push toward and why; a procedure that pretends this is a technical question rather than a political one will be worse than useless to me. Keep the distinctions that have to survive the simplification and tell me which ones you deliberately dropped. Note also the two ways the procedure can misfire: it can release someone the public will be right to fear, or it can detain someone past what any desert-based story licenses because a risk score says so, and I need the procedure to be honest about which of those it is more likely to do.

Then run it on three cases and show me the machinery. First, a twenty-four-year-old with one prior conviction for a non-violent offense, now facing an armed robbery charge, whose actuarial risk score comes back low. Second, a sixty-one-year-old first offender who embezzled a large sum from an employer, has already begun restitution, and scores as a negligible risk. Third, a case I expect to be handed to me within the year: a defendant whose offense is minor and whose desert-based sentence is short, but whose risk assessment is high and whom the legislature has publicly signaled it wants locked up — give me the answer the procedure yields even if it is the answer I will not like, and tell me plainly whether it is the procedure or the legislature that ought to give way. Present the three worked cases as a table with these columns: case; desert-based range; forward-looking adjustment; final recommendation; the step that did the work; whether the procedure decided it or handed it to a human; and what the losing side would say. After the table, give me a JSON object with the fields "judgment_calls" (an array of the steps that are values dressed up as procedure), "cannot_settle" (an array of questions the procedure deliberately refuses to answer), and "most_likely_failure" (a string). Finally, tell me where your reasoning is weakest — which consideration, if I came back with it, would lead you to weight the desert anchor differently — and do not smooth over the places where you are guessing at what the commission would accept.
