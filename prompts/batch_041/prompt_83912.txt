I'm in a seminar on early American moral philosophy and we're spending two weeks on Franklin's Autobiography, and I keep getting stuck on the section where he describes his project for arriving at moral perfection — the thirteen virtues, the little book with red lines and black spots, the whole apparatus. The part that's really hanging me up comes a few pages later, after he admits that the scheme of Order gave him the most trouble. Here's the passage I keep rereading:

[...] that such extreme nicety as I exacted of myself might be a kind of foppery in morals, which, if it were known, would make me ridiculous; that a perfect character might be attended with the inconvenience of being envied and hated; and that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance.

Right before this he tells the story about his neighbor the smith and the man who buys an ax — the buyer wants the whole face of it ground as bright as the edge, gets tired of turning the wheel, and ends up saying he likes a speckled ax best. And right after the passage above, Franklin says he never did arrive at the perfection he was ambitious of, but that he was a better and happier man for having tried, and compares himself to someone copying engraved letters who never matches the originals but improves his hand anyway.

So I guess my question is what Franklin is actually arguing, because these pieces seem to pull in different directions and I can't tell if that's deliberate. Is the stuff about foppery and being envied and hated a genuine moral point, or is it the voice of the temptation he's describing and resisting? And what does "keep his friends in countenance" even mean here — why would my being morally perfect be a burden on my friends, or is the thought that a friend who's flawless makes everyone else look bad? I'd also like to know whether he's saying moral perfection is unattainable, or attainable but undesirable, because those seem like very different claims and he sort of gestures at both. And how does the ax fit — is the man who settles for a speckled ax meant to be a cautionary figure, or is Franklin quietly agreeing with him at the same time he says the striving was worth it? If you could walk me through how the Order case in particular functions as the illustration here (why that virtue and not, say, chastity or silence), that would help too. I don't need background on who Franklin was or what the thirteen virtues were, I've got all that — I want the actual argumentative structure of this little stretch of the text.
