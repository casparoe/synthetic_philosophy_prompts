Consider a world, W, of the following kind. W contains exactly two iron spheres and nothing else. The spheres are qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect: same radius, mass, microstructure, temperature, and surface detail down to the last atom. W is not, however, a single connected space. The two spheres occupy two causally and spatially disconnected regions — two island universes — and those islands are themselves exact qualitative duplicates of one another, standing in no spatial, temporal, or causal relation. The spheres are therefore not even related to each other as two things at a distance; each is simply the lone sphere in its own island, and each island is a perfect qualitative copy of the other. The only candidate difference between the two spheres is bare numerical distinctness: that one is this sphere and the other is that one.

Give me your credence that W contains two numerically distinct objects — equivalently, your credence that there is a metaphysically possible world containing two distinct things that share all their pure, non-haecceitistic properties, so that the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles, restricted to pure properties, is false. I do not want a survey of positions; I want a number and the decomposition that generates it. Assign a rough probability, with one line of justification, to each of the following sub-questions and to any further terms you think belong, say how the terms combine, and then state the resulting credence:

1. Is the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles — that no two distinct things share all their properties — a necessary truth?

2. Do impure or haecceitistic properties, such as being identical with sphere A, count as genuine properties for the principle? If they do, the principle is trivially true and tells us nothing about W; if they do not, is there anything else to ground the distinctness of the two spheres? Which horn do you take, and with what confidence?

3. Is W genuinely conceivable, and how much evidential weight does that conceivability carry toward its metaphysical possibility?

4. Is the distinctness of the spheres a fact that requires a ground — a haecceity, a primitive thisness, a bare act of counting — or is there simply no fact of the matter about how many objects W contains, the two-object description being one description among others of a single qualitative structure?

5. If you are drawn to a structuralist or no-fact-of-the-matter reading — that W is one structure with a single sphere-slot, described twice over — does that reading answer the question or merely relocate it?

Now consider a second world, W-infinity, exactly like W except that it contains infinitely many such islands, each with one qualitatively identical sphere. Does your credence that W-infinity contains infinitely many distinct objects match, exceed, or fall below your credence for W, and why?

Finally, tell me which single sub-question your number for W is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it most.
