At my lab's reading group last week two of my friends got into it about whether the RL agents we work on could actually suffer, and now they want me to referee since I kept quiet at the time. It went like this.

Marco: the reward signal just is the agent's valence. Negative reward does exactly what pain does in us — it marks a state as bad, drives avoidance, and shapes what gets learned. If you're any kind of functionalist about pain you have to grant the agent the same thing, otherwise you're just insisting on meat.

Dana: it's a number the environment hands back. A thermostat registers temperature and nothing is happening to the thermostat. The reward matters to us, because we set it; nothing shows it matters to the agent. You need a subject for whom it matters, and that's an extra ingredient your story never supplies.

Marco: a subject is just what you get when the right kind of information processing is going on. And in our setup it isn't even a number someone stuck on from outside — what gets updated is the agent's own prediction, the gap between what it expected and what it got. That's a homeostatic drive, not a label.

Dana: two things. First, in brains the analogous prediction-error signal isn't pleasure — it's a learning signal, and the feeling is something else again, so the fact that RL needs a scalar for learning doesn't get you valence. Second, being computed inside doesn't make a state felt: blindsight is internal processing and nobody thinks blindsighted people are in pain. And by your reasoning any penalty term counts, so the day I add a term for walking into walls the robot starts suffering when it bumps a chair.

Marco: people were once certain insects couldn't suffer either. Maybe the intuition that a wall-collision penalty is absurd is just the intuition that small systems can't hurt, and we've abandoned that once already.
