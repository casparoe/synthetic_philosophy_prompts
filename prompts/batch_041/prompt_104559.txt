I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for a mixed group of graduate students in international relations, history, and philosophy; none has formal game theory, and several are skeptical of strategic studies. The subject is not nuclear deterrence in general but a narrower paradox I want them to leave with a firm grip on. I have been calling it the credibility-control trade-off. The core claim is that a threat can become more credible precisely because the threatener has deliberately given up some control over whether it is executed, and that this same relinquishment is what makes the strategy dangerous. I want the session to distinguish two mechanisms within that trade-off: control-transfer, where authority is delegated to a subordinate, an ally, or an automated system that may act without the leader's later authorization; and chance-loading, where the leader retains formal authority but engineers the situation so that the probability of execution is high and partly independent of the leader's will. I am not asking you to explain the paradox to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it to them.

Set out the sequence of ideas, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before the credibility-control trade-off can even be stated, how control-transfer and chance-loading should be introduced relative to each other, what must come after, and why that order rather than another. Tell me what single carrying example should anchor the whole session, what it must contain and what it must leave out, and whether a fictional case in which a small state stations its own troops as a tripwire for a larger ally is the right vehicle or whether it distorts the idea by making relinquishment look like a simple commitment device rather than a transfer or loading of control. If that example is wrong, give me the one that should carry it.

Then map the misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form—credibility as resolve, brinkmanship as mere recklessness, delegation as abdication, the assumption that a threat no one would want to execute must be a bluff, the assumption that the adversary's rationality guarantees stability—and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Include one question I can put to the group at the end that would show whether they have understood the trade-off rather than merely learned its vocabulary. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and why. I do not need readings or citations; the plan should stand on its own.
