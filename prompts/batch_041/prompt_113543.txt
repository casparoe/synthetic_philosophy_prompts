I'm in a decision theory seminar and we just spent two weeks on updateless decision theory — the idea that instead of picking acts one at a time by conditioning on what you've observed, you evaluate whole policies (mappings from observations to actions) and pick the one with the best expected value across every situation you might have been in, including ones where you'd have observed something completely different. The part that keeps tripping me up is the insistence that once you've settled on the policy you don't get to re-litigate it from inside whichever branch you actually landed in.

Anyway I rewatched Arrival with this in my head and now I cant unsee it. Louise learns the heptapod language, her experience of time goes nonlinear, and she sees her daughter Hannah's whole life — including that Hannah dies at twelve of an illness nobody can cure. And she has her anyway. There's a reading where that's the most literal illustration of updatelessness anyone has ever filmed: she isn't choosing an act, she's ratifying a life, and the fact that she now knows which branch she's in doesn't count as new information she's permitted to act on. It's also weirdly Newcomb-shaped, except the correlation between her present choice and what she knows about her own future is logical rather than causal.

What I want from you is a reading that takes both the film and the theory seriously, including where the analogy breaks and I have to say so. My worries: her foreknowledge is quasi-perceptual rather than a probability distribution, so she isn't maximising expected value under uncertainty at all; and the film says nothing about the standard objection to not-updating — that an agent like this ends up paying blackmail to someone who threatens a version of it in a world that isn't actual. So is Arrival dramatising updatelessness, or is it fatalism wearing decision theory's clothes? And does the UDT reading earn its keep — does it see something in the film (why her choice and her knowledge seem inseparable, why the movie refuses to make this a tragedy) that a flat 'she accepts her fate' reading misses? I have to write this up for the seminar and my prof will know if I just turn the movie into a diagram of the theory.
