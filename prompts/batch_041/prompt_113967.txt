Here's the answer:

"Camus argues against the death penalty in 'Reflections on the Guillotine' on several grounds. First, he rejects the deterrence justification. He points out that in countries where the death penalty has been abolished, crime rates have not risen, and that most murders are crimes of passion that are not premeditated, so the threat of death cannot deter them. He also notes that since executions are now carried out in private rather than in public, the exemplary function of the punishment has been lost, though he concedes that public executions might deter, he thinks they are too barbaric for a modern society. Second, Camus argues that capital punishment is a form of state revenge, an 'eye for an eye' that only perpetuates violence. He famously calls it 'the most premeditated of murders,' because the state plans the execution in advance and holds the condemned at its mercy for months, whereas a criminal's act is often impulsive. Third, Camus grounds his opposition in the sanctity of human life: since every human life is inviolable, no state has the right to take it. He also notes that in a secular age, the death penalty cannot offer the condemned a chance for atonement, and that the possibility of judicial error makes the punishment irreversible. As an alternative, Camus proposes forced labor for life, which he considers harsher than death but at least reversible, and he suggests that the condemned should have the option of suicide."
