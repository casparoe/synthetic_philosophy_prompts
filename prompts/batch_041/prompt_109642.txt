I'm the TA for an upper-level seminar on democratic theory -- 24 students, one 90-minute session, and the prof has handed me the whole thing on electoral systems, specifically the proportional-representation-versus-first-past-the-post fight that everyone thinks they already have opinions about. Room has movable tables, a big whiteboard, a projector I won't use, and I'm bringing a bag of fun-size chocolate bars as the only prize. I want a game where they live through it instead of hearing me say "FPTP wastes votes" one more time, and I don't want them hearing the words "Duverger" or "Alabama paradox" until the debrief, so no lecture up front. My rough idea, which you should feel free to improve: everyone is assigned a region and a party, the payoffs are the seats themselves, and nobody eats unless their party ends up in the coalition that controls the chocolate -- so a party with 5% of the vote has a concrete reason to be furious about zero seats. Round one, single-member districts. Round two, the exact same votes re-run as a national list under a largest-remainder rule, so they can see what changes when nothing about the voters changed. Then, and this is the part I care about most, so protect it if we run short: parliament votes to expand itself, or a new region joins and its seats get added on top with everything else supposedly held fixed, and somebody who did nothing wrong loses a seat. There's a classic example with three blocs where going from ten seats to eleven costs the smallest one a seat; I want them to blunder into that arithmetic themselves on the whiteboard with their own numbers before I say anything. If there's time I'd also like four of them to redraw the district boundaries, because watching your own voters get packed into one district feels different from being told about gerrymandering.

So: rules, materials, the payoff scheme (chocolate only, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and then the debrief, which is where I need the most help. Map each round onto the idea it's carrying -- wasted votes and district magnitude in round one, coalition bargaining and the "does PR hand the balance of power to tiny extremist parties" worry in round two, and in the expansion round the bigger point that "proportional" isn't one thing: the allocation step has its own axioms (quota, house monotonicity, the population and new-state business) and I'm fairly sure you can't satisfy all of them at once, which I want to be true but you should tell me if I'm overstating it or if it only applies under conditions I'm glossing over. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this -- I'm already worried the arithmetic will eat the clock and that whoever lands in a party with no seats just checks out -- and be straight with me about what the game can't show, since I assume it can say nothing about the long-run party-system effects people argue about, because that would need several elections and real parties entering and exiting.
