I keep a notebook of what I can only call the moves behind creative work — the recurring shapes that creative acts seem to take — and I've reached the point where I need someone to tell me whether the thing I keep circling already has a name, because I can feel myself reinventing a wheel I can't identify. The clearest instance is musical: a pianist takes a well-known standard and alters a single chord, or a drummer moves an accent by one eighth note, and the change is tiny, the theme is unmistakably still there, yet the result can land as a genuine discovery rather than a decoration. What interests me is that the person doing it doesn't seem to know the space of possible variations in advance; the axes along which you can vary the thing — which features are even adjustable, and how far — are discovered only by moving through them, and only afterward does the move look like it was aimed at something. I see the same shape in a programmer refactoring a function, a cook swapping one ingredient for another, a novelist changing a single character's age. Now the harder case, which is the real reason I'm writing. Sometimes the move doesn't turn a knob at all; it changes what counts as a knob. Instead of asking how fast the piece should go, you ask what instrument should be playing; instead of varying the recipe, you vary the very notion of a recipe. This second kind feels categorically different from the first — it's the difference between exploring a space and rebuilding it — and yet I can describe both in exactly the same words, as variation on a theme, and once I do that the theme has simply hopped up a level, where it can be varied again, and I can imagine a further level above that, and I can't see where the regress stops or whether it ought to. A friend who reads a lot of Hofstadter told me this is basically his claim that variation on a theme is the essence of imagination, and that he had some phrase about twiddling knobs or dials for the low-grade version — but whatever he meant, it only covers the first half of what I'm after, and I'm not even sure the friend remembered it right.

So what I want from you, working as a philosopher or cognitive scientist who specializes in creativity and conceptual change, is not a label slapped on a vague impression. Begin by brainstorming the candidate ideas and terms that might apply, then tell me, carefully, which established concept — if any — actually fits, and where each near-miss breaks down. In particular I want the carving: is there a principled distinction between varying the values within a space and varying the space itself, or is that difference merely one of degree, so that any line I draw is arbitrary and the honest answer is that it's a continuum? If there is a real distinction, what is its basis, and what are its boundary cases — the moves that are hard to classify either way? If nothing established fits the whole thing, coin a term and define it sharply enough that I can tell it apart from the concepts you rejected, and say explicitly which parts of my description you're treating as central and which as incidental noise. I'd also like to know where the familiar vocabulary in this area sits relative to my distinction: which of the standard notions captures the first kind, which (if any) captures the second, and which capture something else entirely that I may be conflating with both.

Please be candid about the state of the question rather than smoothing it over. Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — if specialists disagree about whether the distinction is real, about what grounds it, or about whether the second kind is even a distinct phenomenon rather than a relabeled version of the first, say so, and don't present any contested view as if it were the consensus. Where you're offering your own synthesis rather than reporting a received view, flag it as such. Write in American English.
