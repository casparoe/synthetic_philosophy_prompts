Six weeks ago my boss left and I inherited the job of running strategy at a mid-sized foundation — forty-odd staff, we give away about twelve million a year. Which means every program team now sends me a "theory of change" document, and the board wants one five-year strategy assembled out of them by spring. I thought I knew what I was doing until I noticed that maybe half of these documents quietly assert something like a law of history. "Once a country's income per head passes roughly the level X, it becomes a democracy and stays one." "The case for Y is already won, its just a matter of time." "If we don't fund Z now, the window closes for a generation." The board loves it, because it makes a big multi-year grant feel like going with the grain of history instead of a bet.

A friend lent me a Popper book about why there can't be laws of history, and while I think he overstates the case in places, the tools in there are obviously the ones I need. I don't want a seminar though. I want something a program officer can run on a proposal in half an hour without me in the room, and that I can defend to a grantee who's about to push back hard.

So: act as a philosopher of social science who knows Popper's critique of historicism cold — the trend-versus-law distinction, the argument about the unpredictability of future knowledge, the objection that you can't describe the whole of a society, and his Oedipus effect point about predictions helping to bring about the thing predicted. Before you write anything, spend a few lines thinking out loud about which of his moves actually survive translation into a checklist and which ones collapse into "it depends, use your judgment." I would much rather you throw a move out than smuggle it in as a question nobody can answer.

Then give me the procedure itself. What I need from it:

- One page, plain English, at most ten questions, in the order we'd ask them. No jargon my program officers can't defend in a meeting, and no reading list — I'm not going to read a reading list.
- For each question: what a sufficient answer looks like, what a fail looks like, and what to do when nobody can tell.
- At least one place where the procedure stops dead with a no, and at least one place where it hands the call to a person, with an honest account of why that hand-off can't be engineered away.
- A short section on where this procedure will misfire or be gamed — including the case where a proposal is genuinely a bet on the future and that's fine, and the procedure is just being asked to launder it.

Then run it, out loud, on three real proposals:

1. The income-threshold claim above, being used to justify an eight-year governance grant in a country that just crossed the line.
2. "If we keep building this coalition, our side wins within twenty years" — a campaign proposal where I suspect the prediction is doing most of the motivational work.
3. Our flagship program, ten years of "shifting the culture," whose document says the old settlement is already collapsing and the next decade belongs to whoever is organised. This one is mine and I'm attached to it.

For each one, tell me which single question in the procedure did the real work, what verdict it produced, and what it could not settle. If the procedure makes me give up something I like, say so plainly.
