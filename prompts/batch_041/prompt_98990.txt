I'm assembling material for a small in-house satire zine that a few of us in the philosophy department put out each spring, and I want to contribute a piece built around Hegel's master–slave dialectic — not another three-paragraph précis of lordship and bondage, but something that dramatizes the thing and gets its internal logic right. The conceit I've settled on is this: write it as an internal performance-review memo from the Human Resources department of a mid-sized firm that has, without anyone at the firm noticing, organized itself entirely around the master–slave relation. Two employees are up for their annual review. The first is a senior figure whom everyone calls the Principal — he is the master. The second is his long-serving, indispensable assistant, the Associate — he is the bondsman. The memo should be deadpan, bureaucratic, and thoroughly self-assured, formatted as numbered sections with a ratings scale, a "development plan" for each employee, and a closing note about next steps; roughly 700 words. One constraint I care about: the memo must never use the words "master," "slave," "bondage," or "Hegel" — the whole effect depends on the firm's bland corporate euphemisms doing the dialectic's work behind everyone's back, including the memo author's.

The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right, so that a reader who knows the Phenomenology laughs at the accuracy rather than wincing at a caricature. That means the Principal's review should come out poorly for reasons that are exactly Hegel's: his satisfaction is mediated through someone else's labor, and the recognition he receives from a merely dependent, subordinate consciousness is not the reciprocal recognition of an equal, so it leaves him hollow and strangely insecure — the memo should register this as a "stakeholder-engagement gap" or an "over-reliance on a single internal validation source." The Associate, by contrast, should quietly be rated higher, because his work is formative activity: through labor and through an early, career-defining brush with being fired (his version of the fear of death) he has developed an actual self-consciousness and an independence the Principal lacks, and by the end of the review the Associate's own notes should reveal that he has begun to see the Principal as the dependent party. Please let the asymmetry of the original struggle show up too — the Principal got where he is by being willing to risk something the Associate wouldn't — and let the reversal emerge from the review's own logic rather than from anyone in the memo explaining it. The "development plan" the firm prescribes for the Principal should, without the author realizing it, describe the very self-consciousness the Associate already has. I'd like the piece legible to a colleague who's never read Hegel — nothing in it should require prior exposure — but sharp enough that someone who has will recognize every move. Please don't let anyone step out of character to wink at the reader, and don't end on a thesis statement; let the last section be a dry administrative sign-off.
