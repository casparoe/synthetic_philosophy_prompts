I teach a final-year seminar on archaeological inference — how one gets from objects to claims about what past people did, how their communities were organised, and what they thought — and I am writing the final examination. I want a single essay question: closed book, 90 minutes, 20 marks, no internet access for the students. It has to be unanswerable by reciting the standard textbook opposition between reading the record as adaptation and reading it as meaning, or by deploying "middle-range theory" as a slogan. The difficulty should sit entirely in the inferential work, not in recall.

The distinction I have been teaching all term, and which the question must force students to apply rather than merely name, is between two kinds of underdetermination. A claim is evidentially underdetermined when the record as it stands does not settle it, but a further observation of the same general kind — more excavation, residue or isotopic analysis, refitting, use-wear — could in principle settle it. A claim is semantically underdetermined when no further observation of that kind would settle it, because the material pattern is compatible with any number of incompatible meanings, and the step from pattern to meaning rests on a bridge assumption that is not answerable to more objects of the same kind. Such a claim can be supported only by something independent of the pattern — a text, a depiction, a documented analogue whose relevance to this case is argued for — or not at all. My students collapse the second into the first: they treat every interpretive claim as a claim waiting for more data. I want the question to make that collapse visible and to cost them marks.

To that end I have invented a site dossier, deliberately fictional so that nothing can be recalled or looked up, and internally consistent. Please reproduce it in the question as the material students receive.

The site: Harrow Field, a settlement with an adjacent cemetery, occupied over roughly three centuries and excavated in three phases, the lowest waterlogged and the upper levels dry.

1. Thirty-eight round houses, rebuilt repeatedly on the same footprints, arranged in two spatially separate clusters; a single larger rectangular building stands at the centre of the eastern cluster.
2. A cemetery of 124 graves inside a ditched enclosure about 200 metres from the houses. The ditch fills are clean; domestic refuse at the site is deposited in a midden beside the houses.
3. Grave goods: 110 graves contain at least one pot; 14 contain copper-alloy objects; 4 contain stone from a source 300 km away; 3 contain miniature four-wheeled vehicles; 2 contain bird figurines. The richly furnished graves are scattered rather than grouped, and goods correlate weakly with the sex and age of the skeleton.
4. Twenty-two graves were reopened after burial: the fill is disturbed and some skeletal elements, mostly long bones and skulls, are missing, while the goods are undisturbed.
5. Animal bone: skulls and foot bones are concentrated in and around the houses; meat-bearing bones are concentrated in the midden. In the cemetery, small quantities of meat-bearing cattle bone occur in 9 graves.
6. Three storage pits contain a complete pot set upright on the base with no domestic refuse; the remaining pits contain refuse or are empty.
7. About 2–3 per cent of the lithic material in every phase comes from sources more than 150 km away; the proportion is stable across the sequence.
8. No writing, and no figurative art apart from the two bird figurines.
9. The rectangular building's floor carries a burnt layer with charred grain and a scatter of broken pots. The round houses have central hearths and nothing comparable.
10. Dating between the two house clusters and between phases is coarse, with a resolution no finer than a century.

Students are to assess the following claims about the site:

(a) The dead were buried in two stages, with some bones removed after the flesh had decayed.
(b) The community believed that the dead had to be released before they could join the ancestors.
(c) The settlement was occupied by two contemporaneous descent groups, one per cluster of houses.
(d) Access to copper-alloy and imported stone objects was restricted to a few families and passed down by inheritance.
(e) The rectangular building was a communal structure in which grain was consumed on repeated occasions.
(f) Cattle were slaughtered seasonally, in late summer.
(g) The community distinguished a domain of the sacred from the ordinary.
(h) The two bird figurines represent a supernatural being.
(i) The community was in sustained, low-level contact with groups at least 150 km away.

The answer should have three parts: first, sort the claims into those supported by the dossier as it stands, those that are evidentially underdetermined, and those that are semantically underdetermined, justifying each placement by naming the specific dossier item that does the work and the bridge assumption the claim needs; second, take one claim from each class and say what further evidence would raise or lower confidence in it and whether that evidence is of the same kind as what the claim currently rests on; third, state and defend a general principle about which claims concerning past minds the material record can and cannot settle, and apply it to one further claim of the student's own devising that the dossier does not raise.

Please write the question as the students would see it, including the dossier and the claim list; a model answer of about 750 words; and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks, built on five must-hit points, giving the marks for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically lose each point, and an explicit statement of the moves an A answer must make that a B answer need not. No citations are needed anywhere — students cannot consult anything, and I will supply the reading myself.
