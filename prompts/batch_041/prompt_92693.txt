Retired chemist here — thirty-odd years in industry, and since I stopped I've been working through all the philosophy I never got to. I've got myself stuck on Aristotle's sea battle in De Interpretatione 9. The argument goes: if it's already true that there'll be a sea battle tomorrow, then there can't not be one, so it isn't contingent, and if it's false then it can't happen either. Aristotle's way out is that statements about particular future events aren't yet true or false. I assumed that was the end of it. Then I found out people have been going at this for two and a half thousand years — Łukasiewicz inventing a three-valued logic so that there will be a battle gets a third value, the Ockhamist move where the truth was there all along but its a soft fact about the past rather than a hard one, the branching-time people arguing over whether there's one thin red line through all the branches. I follow maybe half of it.

What I can't stop thinking about is Macbeth. The witches tell him things about the future, he takes them as settled and starts killing people to bring them about, and the whole thing comes apart because the predictions were true in ways he couldn't see — the wood moves, the man wasn't born of a woman. Here's my puzzle. In the sea battle, nobody is trying to make the battle happen; the prediction just sits there being true or not. Macbeth is the opposite: the prophecy comes true partly because he heard it and acted on it. There's even a moment early on where he wonders whether he needs to lift a finger, and then he does anyway. And Banquo gets a prophecy about his sons and just goes on with his life — the old lazy argument, do nothing and let it happen — and that works out for him. So the play seems to run the experiment twice and get two different answers.

So I'd like a reading of Macbeth through the future-contingents literature. Does it actually dramatize the sea battle problem, or does it change the subject by making the prediction causally load-bearing in a way the logic never contemplates? Is Macbeth's error logical — treating will be as must be, running together the necessity of the consequence with the necessity of the consequent — or just greed dressed up as inference? And the thing I keep tripping on: the prophecies are hedged and riddled with conditions, so are they even the kind of proposition the debate is about? Half of me suspects that's a real objection, half of me suspects it's just me wanting the statement to be well defined before I'll reason about it. Tell me which of the going positions the play ends up closest to, if any, and where it resists the reading.

Don't flatten the play into an illustration of a puzzle, and don't bend the philosophy to fit the plot. Finish by saying what the play shows that the argument on its own doesn't.

And tell me how confident you actually are in the reading, and flag the one claim in it you're least sure about — I'd rather see where you're guessing than get a smooth essay.
