For the past three summers I have been one of roughly thirty volunteers who look after a stretch of sand dunes on a popular coast. The arrangement is deliberately informal: any one of us, acting alone and without consulting the others, is entitled to rope off a section of the dunes when we judge that foot traffic is doing lasting damage. The rope is cheap and easily removed, and most of the time it is plainly the right thing to do — a few days' rest and the marram grass recovers. But the dunes also carry the only firm track down to the beach, and the inshore lifeboat's tractor uses that track to reach the water. On rare occasions — I would put the odds at about one in two hundred, though several of the others think it far lower — a rope across the track at the wrong state of the tide can cost the crew several minutes. Each of us, standing there on a given afternoon, sees only a tiny chance of that coincidence and concludes that roping off is the responsible choice. Between us we roped off the same section more than forty times last summer. Nothing calamitous happened, but the lifeboat crew are understandably angry, and our co-ordinator has now instructed us that before roping anything off we must each ask whether it would be acceptable for everyone in our position to do so. I should add that I am the only volunteer who has actually watched the crew lose time this way, which is why my estimate is higher than the others'. My difficulty is this: I can be as sparing as I like and it will change almost nothing, because if I leave the rope down the next volunteer along will put it up. I do not want to be the one who let the grass die, nor the one who delayed a launch. Is there a way of thinking about my own decision that is not simply a way of shifting the burden onto somebody else? And is the co-ordinator's test the right one, or does it require me to set aside the one piece of evidence that I alone possess?
