I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past two years I have also served as the non-specialist member of the editorial board of a small bilingual poetry press that publishes facing-page editions. We meet next week to decide which of two commissioned English versions of a single modern poem will go into our spring list, and I must circulate a written recommendation before the meeting. The original is a twelve-line poem in a language none of us on the board reads fluently, though we have independent literal translations. Its formal constraint is not, according to the poet, decoration: each line ends with a word that is a homophone of the word beginning the next line, and the first letters of the lines spell a name. Translator A, a poet, has preserved the homophonic chain and the acrostic, but in doing so has changed the poem's central image and added a line of her own. Translator B, a scholar, has rendered the literal sense almost exactly, but the homophones and the acrostic are gone, and the result reads, to my ear, like a prose gloss broken into lines. The board is split, and the press cannot afford to publish both in the same season.

Draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete decision—publish A, publish B, publish both in some form, or commission a third version—with a time frame and with any conditions you think should attach, such as an afterword on the constraint, a parallel text, or a note explaining the losses. It should engage seriously the strongest argument for the option you reject, and it should address the underlying question our split keeps circling: whether a translation of a constrained poem is a translation at all if it abandons the constraint, or whether fidelity to form is itself a kind of semantic fidelity. Assume the board knows the poem only through the literal translations and the two versions; no bibliography or citations are needed, and do not rest the argument on any particular theorist's terminology.

I was at a dinner last week with a translator who insisted that the only honest version would be one that keeps the constraint and lets the sense take its chances, and a poet who said that a translation which keeps the constraint but loses the image is a beautiful forgery. I mention this because the memo should be usable by people on both sides of that table, not just by me.
