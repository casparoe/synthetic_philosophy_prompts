A colleague's draft for a feature I am editing opens with this gloss on the self-deception literature: 'Philosophers long held that self-deception was impossible, on the grounds that to deceive yourself you would have to know the truth and not know it at the same time — a contradiction. That puzzle has since been dissolved. The mind is modular, so one part can hold the unwelcome truth while another part keeps it out of view, much as one person can deceive another. On this picture self-deception is simply ordinary deception with both parties under the same skull; that is why it is so pervasive in studies of motivated reasoning, and why the old paradox was never more than an artefact of treating the mind as a single, undivided knower. The practical upshot is that we should stop treating introspection as a route to the truth about ourselves.'

The prose is assured and I am half inclined to believe it, but something feels off and I would rather not print it unexamined. Please audit the summary sentence by sentence: what would someone working on this topic accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation — in particular, does the modularity move actually dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it, and is self-deception genuinely continuous with ordinary deception? Finally, does the closing claim about introspection follow from anything true in the summary? Please answer in British English.
