ok, weird one. I'm writing a satirical piece for a small history-and-archaeology zine — the kind of place where half the readers are academics and half are people who just like ruins — and I want it to be a mock consulting memo. The conceit: a strategy consultancy has been engaged by the court of a late Bronze Age palace economy, and the deliverable is their engagement summary on how the client should handle the coming "period of reduced complexity," a phrase the memo uses everywhere because nobody at the firm is allowed to write the c-word.

Around 650 words. Deadpan the whole way through — no winking, no "get it?" asides, no narrator stepping out to nudge me. Give the firm a name and a proprietary framework (a 2x2, a maturity model, a five-stage continuum, whatever you like) and then apply that framework straight-faced to things it obviously can't handle. The client's problems should be real ones: drought and harvest failure, tin and copper supply lines running through a web of interlinked palaces, earthquakes, displaced people moving along the coast, mercenaries who've stopped being paid, a scribal bureaucracy that costs more to run every year and returns less. The joke has to come from getting the substance right, not from anachronism for its own sake — the anachronism is the form; the content should be accurate.

Two things I especially want it to do:

- Never let the frame slide into treating this as simply a disaster. The memo's own interests should quietly determine what counts as a problem — the palace sees a catastrophe, and the memo should keep implying, without ever saying so, that for most of the people in the fields it might just look like a change in who's collecting.
- Include a "comparable engagements" section that treats other famous cases as precedent deals — the Maya cities of the southern lowlands, Rapa Nui — in the same bloodless language. This is where I want the real debates to show through: whether what happened is better described as collapse or as transformation, whether the cause was one blow or a pile-up, and whether the cautionary tale we tell about each case is what the evidence actually supports or a story later people told about it. Use the cases the way the memo would use them — as benchmarks for risk pricing — but pick details that someone who's read the actual literature would recognise as fair.

Don't invent fake findings and pass them off as settled. Where the scholarship is genuinely contested the memo can be confidently wrong, but I'd rather you be right about what's contested.

Then, once the memo ends, drop the voice completely and give me a table with these columns:

- "Memo's spin" — the claim or framing as the memo states it
- "Debate it's parodying" — the real argument among archaeologists and historians
- "Case(s)" — which of the three (Bronze Age, Maya, Rapa Nui) it's usually told about
- "What the evidence actually supports" — your honest read, one or two sentences
- "Collapse or transformation?" — your verdict, one word or a short phrase

Six to eight rows. No citations or reading lists, obviously — just say what you think the evidence shows.
