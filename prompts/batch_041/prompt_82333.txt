I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of biology seminar, and our final assignment is an adversarial-collaboration exercise: two members of the group hold opposing views on free-floating rationales, and the rest of us have to write the document the two of them could actually sign. My draft is due first, so please write it.

The two parties are these. The first holds that natural selection can install a rationale in a lineage — a structure of in-order-to claims, with conditions of success and failure — that no creature and no designer ever entertains; on this view such a rationale is a real feature of the world, does genuine explanatory work in biology, and licenses talk of a system's behaviour being correct or mistaken even where nothing represents anything. The second holds that a reason is always a reason for someone, that ascribing a rationale to a system that represents nothing is at best a convenient façon de parler, that whatever normativity such talk seems to carry is contributed by us rather than found in the lineage, and that everything the first party explains with rationales can be explained by mechanism plus selection history without remainder.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of at least six claims that both parties can sign. Each must be specific enough that a hostile reader could not call it vacuous — about the mindlessness of selection, about what as-if descriptions are good for, about whether the quarrel concerns causes or explanations, about what would count as a system's representing something, about what both concede the other gets right.

2. A table of the disagreements that remain. Columns: the question at issue; the first party's answer, worded so that the first party would accept the wording as its own; the second party's answer, worded likewise; and whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, with a one-line reason.

3. For each row you judge substantive, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — and, for each crux, one case or argument that both parties would accept as bearing on it, together with what each party predicts about it, stated in advance and in a form that could in principle be checked.

Two cases must appear among your crux materials, and you may add others. (a) An artificial forager: a population of simulated agents whose controllers were tuned by an evolutionary algorithm on nothing but calories gathered, and whose best performers reliably leave a food patch just as its current yield drops to the habitat average. Nobody wrote that rule down, no agent computes it, and it happens to be the rule an optimality model would derive. Say what, if anything, the rationale "leave when the marginal rate falls to the average" explains here, and have each party predict what happens in a variant where the environment is then altered so that the same rule becomes a trap. (b) The textbook digger-wasp case, in which an interrupted provisioning sequence simply restarts from the beginning: have each party say how such a routine will behave under a novel perturbation and what that behaviour would show.

4. A closing paragraph of no more than five sentences saying which part, if any, of the dispute is merely verbal, and why.

Keep the whole document under 900 words, in the neutral register of a co-authored statement rather than a dialogue. Do not include citations, suggested readings, or quotations of any kind — paraphrase whatever you need; I want the positions, not the bibliography.
