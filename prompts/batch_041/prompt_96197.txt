Ok so I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar I'm TA'ing — cross-listed between econ and philosophy, about 15 people — and the session is on the socialist calculation debate. I dont want a list of names with definitions, because everyone just memorizes "Mises said no, Lange said yes" and then we have nothing to argue about for 90 minutes. I want a decision tree (or a taxonomy, whatever shape actually fits) where the reader answers concrete questions and lands on a position they can then fight about.

Rules I care about:

- Start everyone at one commitment: a planning board with unlimited computing power, complete data about resources and technology, and the authority to enforce whatever it decides. Branch out from there. The reader should be able to answer every node without knowing which position it leads to, so no node phrased as "do you accept the Austrian critique of socialist planning?"
- Branches should be concrete cases, not abstractions. A few I want in there: (a) the board can run any optimization you like, but it has to get the numbers from the people on the ground first; (b) the board has to choose between a new steel mill and a new chip fab with genuinely uncertain future demand; (c) a huge company that has split itself into thousands of internal microenterprises buying and selling from each other at internal prices seems to hold together — does that show economy-wide planning works, or does it show internal prices need an outside market to calibrate against?; (d) a central algorithm sets retail prices continuously from transaction data and nobody haggles — is that a market?; (e) the state is the only buyer and seller of capital goods — can prices for those goods mean anything?
- 10-12 nodes, 8-10 leaves. Every leaf gets a name (a person's, a school's, or "unnamed" if nobody really holds it) plus one sentence on the most awkward thing it's committed to.
- I want these leaves to turn up somewhere: the no-capital-market argument, the "prices are a discovery procedure" argument, Lange-style trial-and-error market socialism, the mathematical-programming/optimal-planning school, big-data cybernetic planning, worker-managed market socialism, bottom-up participatory planning, "the real problem is incentives, not information", and "markets fail too, so we're really comparing imperfect institutions".
- Tell me which leaves your tree cant separate and why, and which answer-combinations come out empty — and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just never got defended.
- No citations, no reading list, no page numbers — I won't check them and the students can't either. If you name real people, keep it to the ones any grad student would know. Dont reach for obscure stuff to show off.

One more thing: where something is genuinely contested among people who know this literature — what Lange's model actually required, whether Mises or Hayek made the stronger case, whether the Soviet record counts as evidence at all — say so, and say which way the disagreement runs. Don't hand me your own take dressed up as the consensus.

American spelling please.
