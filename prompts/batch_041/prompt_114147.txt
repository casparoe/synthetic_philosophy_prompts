In my philosophy of science seminar we have been reading about the problem of induction, and this week's handout summarised Solomonoff's theory of prediction roughly as follows. Kolmogorov complexity is the length of the shortest program that outputs a given string; and since any two universal Turing machines can simulate one another, a string's complexity is a well-defined, objective quantity, with the choice of machine making at most an additive difference. Solomonoff induction gives each hypothesis a prior probability proportional to two raised to the minus the length of its shortest program, and then updates by Bayes's rule, so it automatically assigns more weight to simpler hypotheses and thereby vindicates Occam's razor. Solomonoff showed that this predictor converges on the true hypothesis in the limit whenever the data are generated by a computable process, so the problem of induction is in principle solved. The sole drawback is that the predictor is uncomputable; but this is a practical rather than a theoretical obstacle, since real learning algorithms are simply approximations to it. I am inclined to think the handout is broadly right, but its confident tone makes me suspicious, and I cannot see where the seams are. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of science or a learning theorist would accept, what they would treat as a harmless simplification, and what is actually wrong or unsupported; then say whether the closing claims — that Occam's razor is vindicated and that induction is solved in principle — survive once the rest is corrected. I don't need citations.
