Szasz's main target was the idea that mental illness is a disease in the same sense that cancer or diabetes is. His argument is that diseases are defined by physical lesions or bodily malfunctions, and since no consistent lesion has been found for most psychiatric diagnoses, they are not diseases. He doesn't deny that people suffer; he calls these problems in living, which are moral and social troubles rather than medical ones. That's why he was against involuntary commitment: if there's no disease, the state can't legitimately lock someone up for treatment. He was also a critic of the insanity defense for similar reasons. A contemporary objection is that this is too narrow a picture of disease. Psychiatry has good evidence that schizophrenia and bipolar disorder have biological components, and the DSM has moved toward dimensional and neurobiological criteria. Also, Szasz's view is a form of labeling theory, like Scheff's, which says mental illness is just a label for deviance. So while Szasz was right to worry about coercion and the expansion of psychiatric power, his blanket claim that mental illness is a myth is outdated because we now have biomarkers for many conditions. The strongest version of his argument survives for things like grief or unhappiness, but not for serious mental illness.
