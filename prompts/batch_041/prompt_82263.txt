I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book about art forgery, and I've hit a wall that I'm pretty sure is a philosophy wall and not a plot wall. Two of my characters have been circling each other for the entire middle section and I've started to suspect they aren't really disagreeing — they're just using the same words to mean different things and getting furious about it. So I want you to write the document they'd both sign at the end of a long, honest, adversarial collaboration. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right. The thing they could both put their names to.

The two positions, roughly.

Person A is my conservator, thinks with her hands. Her line: a painting's value as art is bound up with its history — who actually made it, when, in what circumstances, whether the hand on the canvas is the hand the label claims. A perfect copy isn't the same work even if it's molecule-for-molecule identical. The difference is real even when it's undetectable, and "undetectable" is doing no work for us — we don't get to say a thing isn't there just because our instruments are weak.

Person B is my critic, thinks with her eyes and ears. Her line: that's mystification. If a copy and its original are indistinguishable to every possible observer in every possible circumstance, then the "difference" is a fact about paperwork, not about art. What we value is the experience of the thing, and if the experience is identical the valuation has to be too, or else we're valuing a certificate of authenticity and calling it beauty.

Now here's where I need you to be careful, because it's where the actual book lives. I've invented a few cases for them to fight over and I don't want them waved away or filed under "see the literature." I've definately read enough; I want the argument itself, no reading lists and no footnotes, I can't use them anyway.

Case one, the Understudy. An old master runs a big studio. A canvas goes out under his name; in fact his assistant laid in most of the picture under his close direction, and the master added a few touches and signed it. Decades later the same assistant, now old, paints a near-exact copy of that canvas from memory and sells it as another work by the master. So we have two pictures. One is authentic, one is a forgery. The same pair of hands made both, in the same studio, from the same memories, under the same name. Whatever the history view is tracking, it isn't simply whose hand held the brush.

Case two, the Restoration. A portrait in a museum has been restored so many times that maybe nine-tenths of the paint on it now is modern, applied by people who never met the sitter and never saw the picture intact. Meanwhile a copy made in 1890 is, materially, more of the original paint than the original is. If value tracks material continuity, the copy is winning. If it tracks history, the original is winning. I want to know which one they think is the work, and why, in a sentence I could put in a character's mouth.

Case three, the Estate. A forger is hired by an artist's estate to produce new works by the dead artist — new canvases, new compositions, made in the artist's style, with the estate's blessing and the estate's paperwork. Nobody is deceived about the facts; everyone who buys knows. Is this a forgery? If not, what did the deception-based account of forgery ever capture? If yes, can a forgery be authorized, honest, and openly advertised?

And I want one case where they have to admit they might just be talking past each other: the copy is so good that the original is destroyed, and the copy takes its place, becomes the thing everyone points to, gets the wall, the insurance, the grief when it burns. Ask them whether the copy has now become the original — and whether that question is even the same question they started with.

What I want back:

1. A numbered list of claims both of them can sign — about what a work of art is, about what "the original" picks out, about what copies preserve and don't, about what does and doesn't follow from undetectability. Not truisms. If a claim is one they'd sign only because it's empty, cut it and say why.
2. A table of the disagreements that survive, each one stated in wording its own side would accept — A's position in A's voice, B's in B's, not a strawman the other side would reject on sight.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would force at least one of them to move. For each crux, give the case or argument both of them would agree actually bears on it, and write down in advance what A predicts and what B predicts about that case. I care about this part most.
4. Any part of the dispute that's just words — where pinning down a definition would make the disagreement evaporate or turn into a different one — say which part and why.

And keep it honest. If a claim in section 1 turns out to beg the question for one of them, say so rather than papering over it. They've been at this for a year and I'd rather they end up with a smaller, sharper arguement than a big fuzzy agreement.
