I am a software engineer who volunteered to TA an upper-level undergraduate course in technology ethics, and I have been asked to grade a student's take-home answer on repugnant markets and commodification. I do not have formal training in philosophy, so I want a careful, point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. The instructor uses a 20-point scale, but the written comments matter more to me than the number; please give both.

The question the student was answering was this: Consider three proposals. (A) A regulated market in which competent adults may sell one kidney to a stranger for cash. (B) A ride-hailing platform that multiplies fares during a hurricane evacuation. (C) A platform that allows citizens to sell their votes to the highest bidder. Use the scope test and the two-ledger model. The scope test asks: if the background distribution of resources and the background institutions were fully just, would the objection to the practice remain? If it remains, treat the objection as a commodification objection proper; if it disappears, treat it as a background-justice objection. The two-ledger model says that an objection can run on (i) the act of exchange itself, insofar as it treats the good as fungible or as a mere means; (ii) the social meaning or practice the market creates, even if each transaction is voluntary; or (iii) the distributive baseline that makes the offer coercive, exploitative, or unfair. For each of A, B, and C, say which ledger or ledgers the strongest objection runs on, whether the objection survives the scope test, and whether repugnance is doing any independent moral work. Then say whether any of the three practices should be legally prohibited, and why. A strong answer will apply the scope test consistently, distinguish repugnance as evidence from repugnance as a moral reason, and give a reasoned verdict on prohibition.

The student wrote: The scope test is a way of separating objections that are really about commodification from objections that are really about injustice. If an objection disappears once we imagine a just background, then it was a background-justice objection, not a commodification objection. I think this test is useful but not decisive. For kidney sales (A), the objection survives the scope test. Even if everyone had enough money and no one was desperate, many people would still find it wrong to buy a kidney from a stranger. The two-ledger model explains why: the strongest objection runs on ledger (i), because the exchange treats a body part as fungible, and ledger (ii), because a market in kidneys may crowd out altruistic donation and change the meaning of the body. The fact that people are repulsed by the idea is at least prima facie evidence that something is wrong; otherwise we could not explain why selling a kidney feels different from selling a car. So A is a genuine commodification objection. For price gouging (B), the objection does not survive the scope test. In a fully just background, a fare increase during a hurricane would just be a price signal allocating scarce rides; no one would be forced to accept it, and the exchange itself is not treating anything as fungible. The objection is really about exploitation of emergency, so it runs on ledger (iii). That means it is a background-justice objection, not a commodification objection. I would not prohibit price gouging in a just society, though I would prohibit it now because the background is unjust. For vote selling (C), the objection runs on ledger (ii), because it corrupts democratic practice and turns citizenship into a commodity. It does not run on ledger (i), because a vote is not a thing that can be treated as fungible; it is a relation between a citizen and a collective decision. It also does not survive the scope test in the same way as A, because in a just background people would not need to sell their votes. So C is best understood as a background-justice objection, not a commodification objection proper. Still, I would prohibit it, because the consequences for democracy are severe. Overall, the two-ledger model shows that commodification objections are strongest when they target the act of exchange itself. Repugnance is not a reliable guide on its own, but it can point us toward ledgers (i) and (ii). The scope test is the key: if the objection survives a just background, it is about commodification; if it does not, it is about justice.
