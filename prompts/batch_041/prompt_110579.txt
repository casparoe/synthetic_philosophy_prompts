I'm a software engineer on the design-systems team at a consumer app company, and I've been asked to write down the ethical assumptions our choice-architecture conventions are actually built on. The pattern is this: settings we believe benefit the user — personalized recommendations, autoplay, activity reminders — ship enabled by default and take a four-step confirmation to turn off, while settings that mainly benefit us — sharing usage data with ad partners, training models on user content — ship disabled by default and toggle both ways in a single click. Our own design principle states that we never make a user's preferred option harder to reach, yet the added friction appears only on the opt-out side and only for the user-benefiting settings. I want to know what this practice commits us to. What theory of paternalism and of consent does it presuppose — that steering users toward their own good is permitted but steering them toward ours is not, that friction itself is a form of persuasion, or something else? Does the asymmetry rest on a distinction between harm to self and harm to others, or between a fiduciary and an adversarial relationship with the user? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, say whether its two halves rest on consistent commitments, and describe what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly autonomy-respecting one. Give the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
