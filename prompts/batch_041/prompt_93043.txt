Bit of an odd one, but I'd like a proper audit of an analogy.

I'm in a reading group on welfare economics. My flatmate (did a masters in it, never lets me forget) has one move for every argument about comparing one person's welfare with another's, and it's this: utility is like hardness on the Mohs scale. You can line minerals up -- talc, gypsum, calcite, up to diamond -- but the numbers are just ranks. You can't say diamond is ten times harder than talc, you can't add two minerals' hardnesses together, and comparing the hardness of a mineral with the hardness of, say, a loaf of bread is nonsense. So, he says, any policy that trades off one person's suffering against another's -- QALYs, cost-benefit analysis, all of it -- is a category error dressed up as arithmetic. Rank things, then let politics decide.

I half-beleive him. But I want to know exactly where the analogy gives out. So:

1) The correspondence table first. What maps onto the scale, the ranks, the reference minerals, the act of scratching, the non-additivity? Which bits are doing real work in his argument and which are decoration?

2) Where it breaks. I've got a suspicion that one break might be fatal: the Mohs scale is a crude ordinal scale for a property that does admit of real measurement -- the indentation tests they use in metallurgy give you actual numbers for hardness, not just ranks -- so 'hardness is only ordinal' starts to look like a fact about our instruments rather than about hardness itself. Does that carry over to welfare, or is there something about welfare that stops a better instrument helping even in principle? And if it does carry over, what's left of his argument?

3) He adds that the numbers would be meaningless even if we had them, because there's no fact of the matter about whether my 7 and your 7 are the same amount of welfare. Is that the same objection restated, or a different and stronger one? What would have to be true for a cross-person comparison to be meaningful -- a metaphysical fact we're missing, or a decision we have to make?

4) When he's feeling punchy he switches to a second version: two currencies with no exchange rate. I suspect that one's actually worse for him, since currencies do get exchange rates, and they get them by trade rather than by fiat. Is the same route open for welfare, and what would it cost him if it were?

5) Then the verdict. What can the analogy actually carry? I assume something narrower than 'comparisons are meaningless' -- maybe 'no summing', or 'no ratio-scale talk'. Which conclusions survive that narrower reading, and is there a better analogy for whatever he's really getting at?

No references needed, I just want the argument. If the honest answer is that his analogy is fine and my intuitions are the broken ones, say that.
