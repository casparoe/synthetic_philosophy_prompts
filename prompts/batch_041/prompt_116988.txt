I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like a careful essay on a problem I ran into while designing a data model — a problem that, I am told, has a long history in philosophy under the name Bradley's regress and is usually discussed in connection with the unity of the proposition.

Here is the situation in my own terms. In our schema, a relationship between two records is not a bare pointer; it is itself a first-class row in a link table, with its own identifier and attributes (who asserted it, when, with what confidence). Making it first-class has an obvious consequence: the link row is now an entity like any other, so if I want to record how it connects to the two records it relates, I need further link rows — one tying the link to its left record, one tying it to its right record. Those further link rows are themselves first-class, so each of them requires two more links, and so on without end. A colleague says this is the oldest known objection to treating relations as things in their own right, and that my schema has merely rediscovered it.

I would like an essay on the following controversy: is this regress a genuine objection — does it show that something is wrong with treating relations as items that must themselves be bound to the things they relate — or is it harmless, an infinite chain that a correct account can simply accept? The essay should set out the strongest version of each side and reach a verdict, rather than ending on a noncommittal note.

There are several distinct complaints tangled up in the secondhand summaries I have read, and I suspect they are not the same complaint. I would like the essay to sort them out:

1. The regress as an argument that relations are not real items at all, so that the world contains only qualities or monadic facts.
2. The regress as an argument that no analysis of a relational fact into a relation plus its relata can ever explain why those items together constitute one fact rather than a mere list — a complaint about unity rather than about relations.
3. The regress as a demonstration that something must be taken as primitive: at least one binding item has to be stipulated not to require binding in turn.
4. The regress as a harmless infinite chain of distinct, fully grounded facts, each step of which is unproblematic, so that nothing is left unexplained at any finite stage.

For each of these, I would like to know whether it is a distinct claim at all, whether it is defensible, and whether it actually delivers the conclusion it is advertised as delivering. Then, for the fix my team actually adopted — a typing rule under which rows in the link table are not themselves linkable, so the regress is cut by fiat — I want a verdict on whether that is a principled resolution or a stipulation dressed up as a principle. My suspicion is that our typing rule is doing exactly the work philosophers do when they deny that the binding item is a relation at all and call it a tie instead, and I would like to know whether that manoeuvre is legitimate or whether it merely relocates the problem.

Constraints: no reading list and no citations, please — I have no way to check them and they would not help me. Argue from the ideas themselves, and assume I know nothing of the technical literature beyond what I have described here. Roughly 1,000–1,400 words.
