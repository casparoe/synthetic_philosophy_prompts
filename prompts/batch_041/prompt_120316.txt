I'm a science journalist working on a feature about how medicine decides when a patient was conscious. I've been reporting on a large hospital system's quality-improvement policy for intraoperative awareness. The policy is concrete: an episode counts as a confirmed awareness event only when the patient later reports explicit recall of events or pain during surgery. If the patient responds during surgery to a command—say, squeezing a hand or moving an isolated forearm after the anesthetic has been administered—but has no postoperative memory of it, the episode is logged as responsiveness without awareness and excluded from the system's awareness rate. The anesthesiologists I've spoken with defend this on the grounds that memory is the only reliable evidence, and that a response without recall may be a reflex or a state that never became an experience. But the philosophers I've talked to say the policy is quietly taking a side in a much older dispute.

That dispute is the one raised by the color phi phenomenon. In the standard demonstration, two dots are flashed in different locations in rapid succession; subjects report seeing a single dot move from the first location to the second, and if the dots differ in color—a red dot in one position, a green dot in another—they report the color changing during the motion, sometimes as though the change happened before the second dot appeared. The phenomenon makes trouble for any view that treats experience as a simple record of what has already happened. It is often used to distinguish two ways a later representation could shape what seems to have been experienced earlier: an Orwellian revision, in which the experience is written and then rewritten in memory, and a Stalinesque revision, in which the experience is not fully constructed until after the fact, so the earlier content never really occurred. Start by brainstorming at least three candidate reconstructions—one that reads the policy as Stalinesque, one that reads it as Orwellian, and one that says the policy is neutral between them or presupposes something else entirely. Then give me your considered verdict: what theory of the timing and constitution of experience does the policy actually require, if any? Does it strictly presuppose that experience requires reportability or memory, or only that memory is the best available evidence? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Are its parts consistent—does it treat the intraoperative response as evidence of something real but inaccessible, or as a non-experience? And if we replaced the presupposed view with the rival, what would have to change in the policy, in the quality metric, and in what anesthesiologists are taught to count?

Be self-critical rather than tidy. In your answer, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
