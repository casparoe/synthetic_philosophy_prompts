I've been following the independence fights in Scotland and Catalonia for a few years now and something about the way everybody talks about them bugs me. The rule seems to be that a region can only hold a binding independence vote if the central government in the capital says it's allowed — Scotland got a referendum because London agreed to one, Catalonia held one anyway and got the courts and the police instead — and yet the same institutions that enforce that rule keep affirming that all peoples have the right to self-determination, like it's a bedrock principal of the whole international order. If it's a real right, why does it vanish the moment the state you're inside says no? Nobody thinks your right to leave a church or a marriage depends on the other side's consent. And as far as I can tell the decolonization wave was supposed to be self-determination in action, but it only ever applied to overseas colonies, never to regions sitting inside a mainland state, and there's a whole set of borders in Africa and Latin America that were just colonial administrative lines drawn on a map and are now treated as untouchable. Meanwhile we look back at successful breakaways as legitimate or at least as done deals, and call the ones that lost illegal, so it's hard not to feel that the right gets discovered after the fact, once somebody has already won.

What I'd like is for you to take the practice as it actually operates — the requirement of consent from the parent state, the sanctity of existing borders however they were originally drawn, the willingness to recognise a breakaway once it holds de facto control on the ground, the way the self-determination of peoples gets affirmed in the same breath as the territorial integrity of states — and work out what view of political legitimacy, of who counts as a people, and of the moral status of existing borders it has to be presupposing. Don't give me a survey of what famous philosophers have said about secession; reconstruct the implicit theory from the practice itself, and be clear about which parts the practice strictly requires and which it merely happens to fit with, and where it's compatible with several different theories so that we can't really infer much of anything.

And please don't smooth over the disagreements here — tell me which of these claims are settled among people who actually work on this stuff and which are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no intro and no conclusion.
