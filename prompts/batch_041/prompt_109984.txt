A colleague of mine, who teaches comparative religion, explains karma with the bank-account picture: every action is a deposit or a withdrawal, the balance carries across lives, and in the end you receive exactly what you put in — so karma is, at bottom, a theory of moral desert, and anyone who denies it is watering the doctrine down. I want the analogy audited, not restated. Set out the correspondence — what is supposed to play the depositor, the account, the balance, the interest, and the banker — and say which of those roles the doctrine can actually fill and which it cannot. Then tell me where the picture breaks: is there anything in karma corresponding to an owner of the account; what becomes of the picture when merit is transferred or dedicated to another person; and does the doctrine need a judge to make the books balance, or is it meant to be impersonal causation? Separate the disanalogies that bear on her conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and if a better picture is available, give me that instead. Finally, commit: is the bank-account model a defensible reading of karma as desert, or does it smuggle in a self, an owner, and a cosmic accountant that the doctrine denies? I would rather have a position I can argue with than a survey of options.
