Carnap's philosophy contains a tension that I would like to have explained. He was a non-cognitivist about value judgments: sentences that ascribe value or prescribe conduct do not state anything true or false, and their function is expressive or practical rather than descriptive. Yet he was also a socialist who took seriously the thought that the economic and social order can be planned, and whose principle of tolerance assigns the choice between rival frameworks to convention and practical decision rather than to truth. Please explain the conceptual issue that this combination raises — not by surveying what commentators have said, but by working out where the pressure actually lies.

I am especially interested in one suggestion, and want you to explain what follows from it: that we transpose Carnap's own distinction between questions internal to a framework and the external question of which framework to adopt from the domain of ontology to the domain of practice, so that the question whether a given arrangement is unjust becomes internal and answerable, while the question which evaluative framework to adopt becomes external and settled by decision or convention. Explain the consequences for the coherence of Carnap's position. In particular: must the internal questions of morality be truth-apt for the analogy to do any work, and if so, does that compromise his non-cognitivism? What is the status of the socialist's advocacy once ultimate ends are placed outside the domain of truth — a proposal, a prediction, an expression of attitude, or something else? And does the notion of rational planning presuppose a standard of success that non-cognitivism is unable to supply?

Lay out the issue rather than settling it, and make clear at each step which of Carnap's commitments is doing the work. No citations needed.
