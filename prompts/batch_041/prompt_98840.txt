I am not an academic; I have just been thinking about the self on my own. A friend keeps defending the idea that an "I" can be spread across more than one brain by comparing a person to a tune: the tune is not in any one instrument, it can be played on different instruments, and it can still be recognized when the notes are divided between players. He uses this to say that if two people each carry part of my memories, habits, and ways of thinking, then I persist in them, and that this is what I have seen attributed to Hofstadter when he talks about the self surviving in others. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a summary of Hofstadter. What maps onto what—the score, the instruments, the performance, the audience, the composer, the copyright, the silence between notes? Where does the comparison break for the specific claim that an "I" can be distributed across brains: does a tune require a single continuous performance, a listener, or a causal chain from composer to cover band, and what happens if the two brains never interact? Which disanalogies matter for whether the distributed "I" is the same self or merely a copy, and which are irrelevant? Does my friend's conclusion survive once those breaks are on the table, and if not, what would be a better analogy? Lead with a one-sentence verdict, then give the correspondence table, then the breakdown. Hard limit: 800 words.
