I host a small podcast that mostly does long-form interviews about decision theory and longtermist-adjacent stuff, and next weeks guest is someone who's been going around arguing that the real fix for the unilateralist's curse is a strong norm of conformity. Recap of the setup, the way I've always understood it: some number of actors can each unilaterally do an action A; A is either good or bad in itself; each actor does it if their own fallible assessment says it's worth doing. The curse is that when A is actually bad, the more actors there are the likelier it is that at least one of them wrongly talks themselves into doing it -- so the fact that you've concluded A is good is much weaker evidence than it feels, and bad unilateral actions get over-produced. My guest's remedy is roughly: don't act unless you'd expect the other actors, working it out independently, to land on the same verdict.

He has one move he's very good at. Press him on anything and he says the norm was only ever meant for irreversible actions, so whatever you're worried about is off-model, and then he restates the setup. I want eight to ten questions that make that escape hatch progressively harder to use. No "what got you into this" stuff -- I've been reading these blogs for fifteen years, I know what the obvious questions are. At least one question on each of the following, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give:

- whether the argument survives once the actors' judgments are correlated rather than independent -- everyone reads the same blogs, uses the same models, hires from the same pipeline. If the errors are shared, doesn't the correction collapse back into ordinary fallibility? If he says independence is just an idealization, make him say how much correlation it takes to kill the result and whether any real case is under that line.
- the self-application problem: the conformity norm is itself a public good, and defecting from it -- acting anyway -- is a unilateral action whose payoff is highest exactly when everyone else is conforming. Is there a second-order curse here, and does his norm have anything to say about who pays the cost of maintaining it?
- the good-once-bad-many-times cases (geoengineering, releasing a capability, a gene drive) where the clean binary framing of A as good-or-bad just breaks down.

Then close on something that puts the whole thing together: if the curse is driven by the number of actors, the logical endpoint of his own argument is a single decider with no competition. Does he bite that bullet, and how does he weigh the curse against the failure mode of putting the whole decision in one fallible pair of hands?
