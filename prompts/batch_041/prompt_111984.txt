Okay, so I retired last year after 30-odd years in atmospheric chemistry, and I've been reading philosophy in my spare time. I got really taken with this Jain idea, anekantavada -- the many-sidedness thing. As I understand it (and I'm sure I'm mangling it), reality has infinitely many aspects, and any assertion we make is only true from a particular standpoint or in a particular respect. They even have a formal scheme for it, syadvada, where every judgment gets a syat qualifier -- in some sense, from some perspective. And nayavada is the theory of standpoints, I think. There's the blind men and the elephant, but I gather that's more of a teaching story than the actual doctrine. I spent my career saying things like the ideal gas law is true at low pressure, which is really shorthand for true within a domain of validity, and I think that's why this grabbed me.

Here's an argument I scribbled in my notebook, and I definately can't decide whether it's a reductio of the doctrine or a reductio of my understanding of it. I'd like you to tell me which step to give up and what it costs.

(1) For any claim, it's only true in some respect or from some standpoint; never true full stop.
(2) My old forecast example: a layer of air is stable with respect to small vertical displacements and unstable with respect to large ones. Both are true, each in its own respect.
(3) Both of those respects are legitimate -- neither is a mistake.
(4) So the layer is stable and the layer is unstable are both true, each in its own respect.
(5) If two apparently contradictory claims can both be true, then the appearance of contradiction is not by itself a reason to think one of them is false.
(6) And no respect or standpoint is privileged over any other, since each is partial.
(7) So for any claim anyone makes, there is a respect from which it's true, and no respect from which its denial is privileged.
(8) Therefore there is no claim we're ever entitled to call false.
(9) That can't be right. It would mean I can't tell my old lab partner he's wrong about the greenhouse effect, or that flat-earthers and round-earthers are just reporting different standpoints. And I don't think the Jains meant that -- they were pretty keen on logic and on not being fooled.

Where does this go off the rails? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, since it's the part that grabbed me, and I'd rather not give up (3) either. But if I have to choose, tell me what each option costs. And please answer in American English.
