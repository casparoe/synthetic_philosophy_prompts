I've been thinking about Hannah Arendt's essays on lying in politics alongside Wag the Dog, and I'd like a reading of the film that treats her account as a genuine philosophical lens rather than a source of talking points. Arendt distinguishes the traditional liar, who knows the truth and seeks to deceive, from the modern image-maker, who engages in what she calls defactualization: the systematic replacement of factual reality with a coherent fiction, often accompanied by self-deception. In her reflections on the Pentagon Papers, she argues that the Vietnam-era lying was not merely about deceiving the public but about organizing a world in which the liars themselves could live; in her essay on truth and politics, she worries that organized lying can destroy the common world that makes political action possible. Read Wag the Dog through this lens. The film's spin doctor and Hollywood producer fabricate a war in Albania to distract from a presidential scandal. How does it dramatize the difference between the traditional liar and the image-maker? Does the producer, Stanley Motss, fit Arendt's image-maker, or is he something else—an artist who creates a fiction that the public willingly inhabits? What does the film suggest about the role of self-deception in political lying? Arendt suggests that the liar often has an advantage over the truth-teller because he knows what he wants the world to look like; does the film vindicate that? Where does the film complicate or resist Arendt's account—for instance, by showing that the fabricated war eventually becomes real, or that the public is complicit in the deception? I'm especially interested in the producer's insistence on credit for his production and the ending's suggestion that the fake war is becoming real. Does that ending support Arendt's worry about the destruction of factual reality, or does it point toward something more like the hyperreality of a media-saturated world? I want a reading that is faithful to both the film and Arendt, and that says what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, I'm wary of readings that treat post-truth as a simple moral failing; I want the film's own ambivalence about truth and fiction taken seriously.
