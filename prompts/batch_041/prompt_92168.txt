There is a position about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics that I have been defending, and I want to know how much of it is argument and how much is construction. The position is this: the debunking arguments are sound against a particular form of moral realism — the form on which our moral beliefs answer to mind-independent, causally inert facts that selection could not have been sensitive to — but they leave standing a naturalistic realism on which the moral facts just are the natural facts about cooperation, reciprocity, vulnerability, and the costs of defection that causally explain why we hold the beliefs we hold. On this view the genealogy is not a defeater but a partial discovery of the truth-makers; there is no gap between what our moral faculty is for and what it is about, so its having been shaped for fitness does not impugn its accuracy. I suspect the view is a minority one. Map its exposure rather than reassure me.

Tell me what would count against it, ranked from what should make me abandon it to what would be merely awkward, and drawn from every quarter: a consequence the view is committed to that turns out unacceptable; a case or a piece of moral phenomenology it cannot describe; a result showing that it collapses into a rival it was meant to displace; an empirical finding about the actual shape of the moral faculty — that much of our moral repertoire is a byproduct of disgust or of coalitional psychology, say — that its identity claim cannot absorb. I want especially to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, about moral disagreement, moral error, and moral progress, and whether those predictions are in fact borne out.

Then tell me which of these defeaters the view's own defenders would concede and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds. My guess is that the pressure comes from three directions, and I want a verdict on which is most serious: that identifying the truth-makers with the causes of the beliefs makes morality parochial and makes error and reform unintelligible; that the identity claim is unmotivated, since the cause of a belief need not be its truth-maker; and that the view quietly retains the very realist apparatus — a fact of the matter, correctness, the possibility of being wrong — that the debunking argument was supposed to have removed.

Finally, an honest verdict on whether the position risks anything at all. Stated loosely enough, no evidence could tell against it: any failure of fit between the moral faculty and the moral facts can be redescribed as a discovery about which natural facts are the moral ones. Is that a defect, or is it simply what a naturalistic realism must look like? If it is a defect, describe what a version of the view with genuine empirical content would look like, and what observation would falsify it.
