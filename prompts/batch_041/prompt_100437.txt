I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a living — metrics, incentives, institutional failure, that beat — and I've been circling an essay on Goodhart's law for months now, ever since an economist source of mine who's spent years inside hospital and school bureaucracies told me that basically every scandal he's ever worked on was Goodhart's law wearing a nicer suit. I've just rewatched The Wire for the third time and I keep coming back to the idea that it's the best dramatization we have of the whole thing: the show is about institutions chasing a number — clearance rates, test scores, and in the final season prizes and pageviews — and the people inside them doing whatever it takes to move the number, because the number is the only part of their work anyone above them can actually see. What I want from you isn't a greatest-hits list of moments where somebody cheats on a stat. I want a real critical reading of the show through Goodhart, one that takes the drama seriously as drama and the economics seriously as economics.

The reason I want it done properly is that I don't think the show is making one point — I think it's making several, and they get collapsed together whenever people say Goodhart. As I understand the way the idea gets chopped up, there's a version where the proxy and the goal come apart because the proxy only tracked the goal in the world as it was before anybody started optimizing it, so the correlation quietly stops holding once you push on it; a version where people actively game the measure, harder the more of their paycheck depends on it; and a version where the goal is really the best of something — the extremes — so the measure is fine in the middle and useless in the tail, meaning the moment you start seriously demanding improvement the measure stops telling you anything true. Tell me which of those the show actually dramatizes and where. The patrol cops cooking the books; Colvin's free zone and whether that's a cynical workaround or an honest refusal to play the game at all; the school where the entire apparatus bends around the test and some kids get quietly shuffled out of the denominator; the newsroom in season five, where the incentives point straight at making things up. Which of those is the mechanism done right, and which is just people are corrupt, bosses are liars, and nothing more? Does the show have an actual theory of the institution, or only a mood about it?

And then the part I really care about, since I'd like to use this without embarrassing myself: where does the show resist the Goodhart reading? Some of what goes wrong on it looks like plain scarcity and racism and a city that's been abandoned, not metric gaming, and if I paper over that I'm doing the same thing the show accuses its cops of — telling a cleaner story than the facts allow. Is there an exit in the show, or is every exit just relocating the cost onto someone with less power? And what does it get that the papers don't — is there something about how this feels from inside an institution, about the way people know the number is a lie and keep feeding it anyway, that a regression table can't reach? A good reading should be able to say what the show adds to the debate and also where it cheats, and I'd rather you tell me honestly if the show turns out to be a worse fit for Goodhart than my third-rewatch enthusiasm suggests.
