Our reading group has been working through the dialogue in *The Mind's I* in which Einstein's brain is preserved as an enormous book — every neuron, connection, threshold, and lingering trace recorded, so that a patient reader can trace an input through the tables and recover the reply Einstein would have given. A friend has distilled what he takes to be the argument the dialogue circles but never quite states, and I cannot find where it goes wrong, though its conclusion costs me everything I believe. It runs:

(1) To have a mind just is to have a functional organization: specify completely how a system would respond to every possible input, and you have specified its mind.
(2) The Book contains exactly such a specification of Einstein; for any question, it encodes the answer he would have given.
(3) Whether those answers are computed or merely recorded makes no difference to the specification, which is complete either way — read or unread.
(4) So the Book is itself a complete mind, Einstein's, whether or not anyone ever opens it.
(5) A mind is conscious; there is something it is like to be it.
(6) So the Book is conscious even sitting closed on the shelf.
(7) But that is absurd: a closed book is paper and ink, nothing is occurring within it, and no one is undergoing anything while it sits unread.
(8) Therefore (1) is false.

I would surrender almost any other claim before (1), since functionalism is the only account of mind I can get to work. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs — and price the alternatives, including what becomes of the natural thought that a mind persists as long as its record does. Open with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
