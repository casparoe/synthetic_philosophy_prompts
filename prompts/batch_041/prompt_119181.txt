I'm an editor at a science-and-ideas magazine — I write features, but I also run the desk, which means I'm the person who has to decide, like four times a week, whether a writer gets to say that a wildfire is "contained," that a tract is "old growth," that a lake is "polluted," that a drug is "safe," that a 63-year-old is "elderly," or that a variant is "more transmissable." Every one of these turns into a fight: with the writer, or with a scientist source who says we got it wrong, or with readers who think we're weaseling when we reach for "roughly" and "on some definitions." I keep feeling that the philosophy of vagueness is obviously the right toolkit here — the sorites stuff, supervaluationism, the idea that a claim about a borderline case comes out true on every admissible way of sharpening the predicate, or false on every one, or neither — but I've never seen anybody turn it into something a copy desk can actually run at 6 p.m. on a Friday. The epistemicist line (there really is a cutoff, we just can't know where) and the degree-theoretic line (truth comes in degrees) both seem to have something going for them too, and I'd like the procedure to at least tell me what to do differently depending on which one you take seriously.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure, written out in words, that my editors can run on a single sentence in about ten minutes without a philosophy degree. Take me through the steps in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, say where the procedure stops and just forbids the bare predicate, and mark clearly where it hands the decision to a person — me, or the editor-in-chief — because the question is really one of values or because the sharpening itself is vague (I gather that's the "higher-order vagueness" problem and I suspect it's where the whole thing gets shaky, so please don't paper over it). Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure simply can't do. Then run it end to end on three sentences from stories we've actually run into: "the fire is contained," "this is an old-growth forest," and "the patient is elderly." At least one of them should come out with an answer I'll hate, and I want you to say so rather than soften it.

Keep it usable — no citations, no reading list, I won't be able to chase anything down before we go to press. American English throughout, and please end with one final line that begins "Bottom line:" and states the whole procedure in a single sentence.
