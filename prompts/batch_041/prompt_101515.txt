I'm putting together materials for a grad seminar I teach on historical method — the course is basically 'how do we know anything about the distant past', running from Herodotus and Thucydides up to present-day source criticism — and I keep hitting teh same wall with students. They've all absorbed the lazy version of the story where Herodotus is the credulous storyteller and Thucydides is the hard-nosed critical one, and they definately think that's the whole of it. What I actually want them (and honestly, me) to get clearer on is the logic underneath the heuristics, so I'm asking you to help me think it through. Take on the role of someone who really knows ancient historiography and the philosophy of historical inference — a proper specialist, not a summariser.

Here's the specific knot. There's this old workhorse rule in ancient-history and biblical-studies circles, usually called the criterion of embarrassment: roughly, if a source reports something that damages its own side — a founder behaving badly, a community admitting a scandal — that detail probably wasn't invented, because authors don't normally fabricate things that hurt their own case. Fine. But I keep meeting the opposite thought in the literature, and I don't have a tidy name for it — call it the credibility-by-concession objection: a small, calculated admission is one of the cheapest ways to buy trust for everything around it, and ancient rhetoricians understood this at least as well as we do, so a shameful detail may be sitting there precisely to make you swallow the rest. I was arguing about this with a colleague over coffee and we couldn't even agree on which of the two is the more fundamental idea.

So: compare these two ideas for me. Which is prior, and which is parasitic on the other? Under what conditions does the embarrassment criterion actually licence the inference people want to draw from it, and when does the concession objection simply defuse it? And — this is the part I care about most — how would a working historian really tell the two apart in a concrete case, instead of just shrugging and saying 'it depends'? Please think it through carefully, maybe start by laying out what each principle is really claiming before you compare them, and then write it up. A few hundred words is plenty, and please don't send me reading lists or citations — I'm not trying to build a bibliography, I just want the argument straight in my own head.
