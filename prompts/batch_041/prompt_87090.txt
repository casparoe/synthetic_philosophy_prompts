I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on modality and possible worlds, and our final assignment asks for a poem that engages one of the seminar's central disputes rather than merely describing it. I would like you to write a pantoum of five quatrains on the disagreement between transworld identity and counterpart theory. Follow the standard pantoum rule: the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza should return to the opening lines so that the poem closes on itself. The speaker should be someone trying to decide whether the person they seem to remember from another possible life is them or merely a counterpart of them. The form should do the philosophical work: because the repeated lines travel across stanzas, the reader should be able to hear them either as one and the same individual recurring in different worlds, which is the transworld identity reading, or as a counterpart recurring with a family resemblance, which is the counterpart theory reading. The poem should not settle which reading is correct; the repetition itself should be the argument. It should also engage, at least in passing, the standard objection that counterpart theory changes the subject, since when I say I could have been a poet I seem to mean me and not a mere counterpart, and the counter-move that transworld identity has its own costs, especially about how the same individual can have different properties in different worlds without those worlds collapsing into one. Avoid name-dropping and footnotes; the concepts should carry the poem. It should be accurate enough that someone in the seminar who knows the debate would recognize both positions in the structure, and good enough that someone who does not know it would still find it a poem. Write the poem.
