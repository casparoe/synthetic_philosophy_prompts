I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and I run a small monthly meetup; next month's session is on self-reference, and I would like to open it with a poem rather than a proof. Specifically, I want a pantoum about Russell's paradox, with the form doing the work rather than ornamenting it.

The reason for the pantoum is the whole point. It is the received form that eats its own tail: its opening lines return at the close — in the standard closed pantoum the first and third lines of the first stanza are the poem's last two lines — so the poem is, in the only sense a poem can be, a member of itself, which is precisely the move the set in question is forbidden to make. I want that contrast to be the engine of the thing: the poem performs the self-containment the paradox rules out.

Write it as twenty lines in five quatrains, standard interlocking, so that lines two and four of each stanza become lines one and three of the next, and the third line of the opening stanza is the poem's final line. Let that final line be the fatal question — whether the set is a member of itself — so that the poem ends by asking, in the same words with which it began, the thing its middle stanzas have tried and failed to settle.

Accuracy matters more than prettiness. A reader who knows the paradox should be able to point to the unrestricted comprehension principle in the opening, the definition of the set of all sets that are not members of themselves, both horns of the contradiction, and the diagnosis: what fails is the axiom licensing any predicate to determine a set, not set theory or mathematics as such. The poem should not name Russell, should not use the word paradox, and should not close by announcing a moral; the loop back into the opening lines should be what tells the reader the question has not been resolved but merely returned.

Keep both responses live and unadjudicated — that unrestricted comprehension has to go, and that the contradiction might simply be true and the logic adjusted around it — and do not let the poem pick a side. Above all, I do not want a proof in verse: the repeated lines should be carrying the argument, not illustrating it.
