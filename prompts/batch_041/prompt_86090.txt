I'm a novelist — about two-thirds of the way through a book about a family of pawnbrokers, three generations of them, all convinced they're providing a public service — and I keep circling back to The Merchant of Venice. I've read the play a dozen times and I still don't trust my own reading of the trial, so I'd like you to do a proper job of it: read the play, mainly the trial and the casket scenes and the business with the rings, through Aristotle's thinking about money and exchange. Not the shallow version, please; I'm definately not asking for greed versus mercy.

The Aristotle I've got in mind is the awkward one. Money, he says, is a matter of convention and nothing by nature, and it can be made worthless overnight if the people using it change the currency, which means a man can be enormously rich and still starve — hence his fable of Midas, everything he touched turning to gold and nothing left that he could eat. That's why he insists on seperating getting a living for a household, which is naturally limited because needs are limited, from money-making for its own sake, which has no limit at all and which he treats as a perversion of the art. Usury he calls the worst of the lot, since money was invented for exchange and the usurer makes it breed. And then there's the bit in the Ethics where exchange has to rest on a proportion between things that are utterly unlike one another, where he admits that on the face of it they can't be commensurable at all, and rescues himself by saying that it's need that holds the whole thing together and that money is only the stand-in for need. I don't think he ever solves it. I think the gap is still there, and I think the play lives in it.

So: the bond is an attempt to price a pound of a living man, which is Aristotle's problem in its nastiest form, and the trial is where the play decides what it thinks about that. Portia's ruling — exactly a pound, not a drop of blood, and if you spill any then your lands and goods are forfeit — gets called a legal quibble by everyone I know. But is it really Aristotle's equity, the correction of a written law where its general wording fails in the particular case? Or does she just beat Shylock at literal-mindedness, and if so, what is the play saying about justice? And does the play actually back Aristotle's line that usury is against nature, or does it quietly show that Antonio's Venice is just as transactional — bonds, sureties, forfeitures, a ring demanded back — so that the distinction between the natural and the unnatural economy doesn't survive contact with the plot? I want an honest answer on that one, even if it wrecks the reading.

Then the caskets. Gold, silver, lead, and a test that punishes the man who takes the shiny thing for the valuable thing, which is very close to Aristotle's point that the measure of exchange is itself useless for use. Which casket is Aristotle's, and does the play know it's making that joke?

And the rings: a gift that can't be priced, given away to the very lawyer who saved the day, then demanded back, and treated as a graver crime than the bond ever was. I suspect there's a second economy running through the play alongside the money one, and I'd like to know whether the play is aware of it or just stumbles into it.

Finish by telling me what the play dramatises that the argument can't — what Shakespeare knows about money that Aristotle doesn't — and where the reading strains. I know the play well enough that you don't need to quote lines at me, and if something in it genuinely won't fit the lens, say so rather than forcing it.
