So I'm about 200 pages into a novel — German family, 1920s, father's a schoolmaster, one son back from the front and one buried in France — and I keep stalling on the same scene. It's the morning the paper prints the clause from the peace treaty where Germany "accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage." And the reason I keep stalling is that I've been reading about the fight historians had over this later — Fischer and the crowd arguing about whether Germany alone willed the war or whether the whole continent slid into it together — and it finally clicked for me that the clause was never meant as a moral verdict at all. It was plumbing. A legal hook so the Allies could send a bill. The men who wrote it were apparently surprised anybody read it as an accusation. Yet that's exactly how it read in every kitchen in Germany: an accusation, handed to a whole people, including the ones who'd been against the war, the ones who'd just chucked out the Kaiser, and the ones not born yet.

So here's what I actually want, and its not history, it's the thing underneath the history. When we take a war and pin it on a nation — in a treaty, in a reparations schedule, in the way everybody afterward says "they started it" — what is that practice secretly committed to? Does it need nations to be agents, the kind of thing that can intend, decide, and be blamed the way a person is? Or could it run on something thinner — strict liability, where fault never enters and you simply owe for damage that happened to come from your side? Is "caused" doing the work, or "guilty," and are those the same job or two different jobs? And which parts of the practice are load-bearing and which are just decoration, because I suspect the whole thing is a mess holding together on vibes.
