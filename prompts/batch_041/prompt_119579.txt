I teach a second- and third-year seminar on perception and philosophy of mind — sixteen students, one 90-minute slot — and I've decided to retire the way I normally do Molyneux's problem. Every year it goes the same way: I state the case, someone says obviously he couldn't, someone else says he could just reason it out, and we spend the hour trading intuitions that half the room has already met in some podcast. So I want to rebuild it as an activity where they run a version of the experiment on themselves before the puzzle is ever named. Here's what I've sketched so far, and I'd like you to turn it into something actually runnable — materials list, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, scoring, and a debrief, not a lesson plan that's secretly a lecture. The rough design: I cut eight flat blobs out of foam board, each with a different number of lobes around its edge, all the same colour and roughly the same area; nobody is told that lobe-count is the thing that makes them different. Everyone first writes a sealed prediction — yes or no, plus one sentence of why — to a question I put up on the board without naming anyone: if someone learned to tell these objects apart purely by feel, with no vision at all, and then got sight for the first time, could they pick them out by eye alone before touching them? Then they pair up; one partner is blindfolded and spends six minutes being told only that this one is a krim, this one is a blorp, and so on, with no rule given. Blindfold comes off, the blobs are laid out on the table, and they have ninety seconds to name them from sight alone, no touching, no feedback. Then we score it out loud and I ask the ones who got it right to say how they did it. My hope is that the room splits into people who quietly noticed the lobes and people who memorised weight and texture and temperature — and that the split, not the score, is the interesting thing. I want a second round with a nastier set: identical outlines, identical colour, differing only in surface roughness, so the identity is carried by something with no visual counterpart at all. I expect everyone to fail that one, and I want the debrief to explain why the failure there is the *uninteresting* failure while the first round is the real question.

For the debrief I want you to map specific moments in the activity onto the actual shape of the historical debate, since that's the payoff. The original question came from William Molyneux writing to Locke in 1688 — and, in the weeds detail I'd like them to hear, his letter actually tacks on a second question about whether the man could tell by sight whether the objects were within reach of his hand, which is really a question about distance and not shape at all. Locke printed a version of it in the second edition of the Essay, and both of them said no; Berkeley later said no for a much stronger reason, namely that the ideas of sight and touch aren't even similar; Leibniz and several others said yes, on the grounds that the man could reason his way from what he knew by touch to what he was seeing. What I want the debrief to make vivid is that the answer depends entirely on what you think the blind man's idea of the shape consists in: a structure, or a way of feeling. The lobe-count group had a structure and transferred it; the roughness round had nothing transferable. Then I want them to go back to their sealed predictions and see whether they were right for reasons or by luck. Don't give me citations or further reading — I'm not going to hand anything out, and the model I'm picturing this for can't look things up, so keep the history to what you can state in a sentence or two from your own knowledge.

Things I know will go wrong and want you to plan around: half of them will peek under the blindfold; someone will announce the lobe trick out loud within the first two minutes and spoil the encoding split, so I need a way to contain that without shutting them up; the ninety-second naming round will either be far too easy or produce total silence and awkwardness; and the blindfold setup is a genuine accessibility problem, since I may have a low-vision student in the room and in any case I don't want blindness functioning as a gag. Tell me honestly which feature of the original problem the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. And a practical constraint: I have about £20 and a pair of scissors, the departmental paper cutter is broken, and I can't run over the hour — my room is booked straight after.
