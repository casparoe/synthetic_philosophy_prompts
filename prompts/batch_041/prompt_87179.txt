I'm on the student-life committee at a small residential college (about 1,400 students) and the trustees have asked us to send them a recommendation by the end of the month on the future of our student-run honor system, and I've been drafted to write the memo. Some background, since it matters: we still run the old arrangement where students report each other, a student panel hears the case, and the only sanction on the books is expulsion, so every confirmed lie or theft or bit of cheating becomes a single-sanction case. Two things last year broke it. A junior reported her lab partner for fabricating two data points; the panel voted 4-3 to expel him, the dean could only knock it down to a suspension, and she spent the spring getting anonymous messages calling her a snitch. Then a first-year wore a costume to a Halloween party that mocked a religious group, a photo went around, a group of students put up a public accountability post with his name and dorm room, and the administration suspended him for a semester after the pressure -- half the faculty think he got what he deserved and half think the crowd got what it wanted. The options on the table are: keep the honor system as is; hand discipline to staff with graduated sanctions; or keep a student-run tier but bolt on a restorative track where the person who was wronged leads the process and the community is expected to back them up.

I've been trying to frame this for the committee with the three-way contrast people make between honor cultures (you answer slights yourself, and standing is something you can lose), dignity cultures (thick skin plus impartial institutions, and standing is something you just have), and victimhood cultures (the wronged party's suffering is what generates the moral claim, and it gets broadcast to an audience for support). The trustees will want the abstract question answered too, not just the practical one -- whether any of the three is realy morally better than the others, or whether each one only looks good or bad depending on facts about who the audience is, how much people trust the institution to be fair, and whether the sanctions are proportionate to the wrong. And there's a specific knot I can't get untied, which I'd like the memo to take a position on: is the real axis between honor and dignity about what's at stake (a status you can lose versus one you can't), or about who enforces it (peers versus officials)? Those two come apart, and I think our recommendation flips depending on which one we decide is the actual difference.

So please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Open with the recommendation itself -- pick one of the three options, or a specific combination with a timeline for phasing it in -- then the argument for it, then the strongest case for the option you're rejecting, argued as well as it can be argued before you explain why it doesn't win. Don't cite literature at us; a bibliography won't move nine people who already dissagree with each other and the trustees won't read it anyway. Make it something I can hand them and have them actually vote on.
