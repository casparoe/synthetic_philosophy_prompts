Write a pantoum of four quatrains in the voice of someone who has been given a psychiatric diagnosis and who has also been trained to assess it. Let the form carry the argument: the pantoum's repeated lines should enact the looping effect Ian Hacking describes, in which a classification of people alters the people classified, and their altered conduct then alters the classification's extension and the evidence offered for its validity. The poem should begin with a category that seems simply to name what was already there, pass through repetitions in which the speaker inhabits the category and then bends it, and end with the same lines functioning at once as clinical evidence and as self-description, so that the loop closes without either the classifier or the classified winning. Keep the clinical detail invented and plausible; do not quote or paraphrase any actual diagnostic manual. It should be accurate enough that a reader who knows Hacking's account of interactive kinds recognizes the mechanism, and vivid enough that a reader who does not know the theory still feels the vertigo of a category chasing its own tail.
