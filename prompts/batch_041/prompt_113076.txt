Ok so I've somehow been voluntold to run the next session of our local rationalist-ish reading group — it's about fifteen people, smart, numerate, mostly programmers and a couple of med students, nobody with actual philosophy training, and we have 90 minutes and a whiteboard. Topic is the inefficacy objection: the "my individual contribution to this collective harm makes no difference, so what's the point" thing, across the big three (climate, meat, voting). I don't want you to explain the topic to me — I can more or less do that. I want you to plan how I should teach it. Like, the teaching plan.

What I need from you, roughly in order of how much I care:

1. The order the ideas have to go in, and why each one depends on the previous. My instinct is that the whole thing hinges on one move — getting people to stop thinking of causation as on/off ("did I cause it?") and start thinking in terms of difference-making and marginal effects — and that until that's in place everything else is noise. But I might be wrong about where the load-bearing bit is. Tell me what genuinely has to come first, and what I'll lose if I skip a step.

2. The one example that carries the whole session, and what it has to have. I was going to build on voting, but I suspect voting is a bad carrier because it has a clean threshold — there's a fact of the matter about whether you're pivotal, so students can just do the probability arithmetic and walk away feeling fine. Meat has the marginal-production story, climate has neither a clean threshold nor clean attribution. Which do I build on, and what does that example need to have (and lack) to keep the real puzzle alive? Also, I vaguely remember some thought experiment with a thousand torturers each turning a dial a thousandth of a degree, so nobody's pain is perceptibly worse but the victim is being tortured — I think it's Parfit, maybe not, but something like that. Should that be the carrier, or is it too tidy?

3. The misconceptions I should expect, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: (a) "the outcome would've been the same without me, therefore no difference" — the fixed-pie / supply-doesn't-respond fallacy; (b) "one vote can't decide an election" conflating being decisive with having influence; (c) "no perceptible difference = no difference"; (d) assuming the arithmetic settles it, when the arithmetic answer is itself the contested part. Which of those are real, which am I making up, and what am I missing? Especially: do people reliably conflate causal contribution with complicity, or do they keep them apart better than I think?

4. How I'll know by the end whether they actually got it, rather than just feeling bad about eating burgers. One question, ideally.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so.

And one thing I really care about: keep the settled stuff and the genuinely contested stuff separate. I have a nagging feeling the blogosphere presents "buying meat causes animals to be bred and killed" and "your vote has nonzero expected impact" as much more settled than they are among people who've actually worked on this, and I don't want to stand up there and present one camp's answer as the consensus. Where the field is split, say so, and tell me which way you're leaning and why. Don't send me readings or a bibliography, I'm not assigning anything and I'd just be bluffing about citations anyway.
