I'm leading a session for a graduate reading group in philosophy of biology; the attendees know some evolutionary theory but have never encountered the function debate. I want to teach the distinction between the selected-effects (etiological) and causal-role accounts of biological function — not explain it to me, but plan the explanation. Tell me the order the components must come in and what depends on what, so that the two accounts emerge as answers to one underlying question rather than as two rival definitions; the single concrete case that should carry the whole session, what it must have and lack to make the disagreement visible, and why the heart is the wrong choice even though it's the obvious one; the misconceptions they'll predictably form — my guess is that they'll hear "etiological" as merely backward-looking and "causal-role" as merely forward-looking, and that they'll assume the accounts are rivals in every case — and exactly where in the sequence to head each off; the one question I should close with to tell whether they've grasped the disagreement rather than memorized two slogans; and what I should deliberately leave out and flag as out of scope. The plan has to land them on a case I care about: a trait that arose as a neutral byproduct and is only now beginning to be maintained because it happens to help, where I want them to see that the two accounts can accept the same facts and still split on whether to call it a function yet. Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or closing.
