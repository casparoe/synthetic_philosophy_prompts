I recently moved from a compliance job at a Western development NGO to running a small grant program in a country where gifts between officials and the communities they serve are simply how relationships have always been maintained. Last week a colleague and I were arguing about whether one particular exchange — a case of good whisky and a live goat, delivered after a permit came through — counted as a bribe or as ordinary reciprocity. She ended the discussion by saying, as though it settled the matter, that corruption is just "the abuse of entrusted power for private gain," and the whole room nodded. I didn't, because I genuinely could not tell what that sentence was asserting, and I have since noticed it is the definition everyone in my field repeats without ever unpacking it.

You are a political philosopher who works on corruption, clientelism, and development ethics, and who has a good ear for definitions that quietly smuggle in a culturally specific institutional picture. Before you answer, brainstorm privately about the different things the sentence could be doing; then give me only the finished analysis.

Take the formula apart. What distinct, precise claims could "the abuse of entrusted power for private gain" actually be making? I want the readings laid out as a menu rather than a single preferred interpretation, ordered from the thinnest to the thickest: for each, what it commits you to, what work the key terms ("abuse," "entrusted," "power," "private," "gain") have to do, and whether it turns the definition into something near-tautological, something substantive and defensible, or something false or question-begging. Pay particular attention to readings under which the formula is purely formal — deferring to whatever the relevant office or legal order happens to say — versus readings under which it invokes a universal moral standard that can indict a local practice regardless of local norms, and say what each reading must presuppose about who entrusted the power and to what end. Then tell me which reading my colleague most likely intended, and whether any reading actually supports the conclusion she drew — that the goat settles the matter — or whether the definition, on the reading that gives it teeth, cuts against her instead.

Present this as a table with four columns — Reading, What it commits you to, Status (tautological / substantive / false), Supports my colleague's conclusion? (yes / no / only with an extra premise, and which) — followed by one short paragraph naming the reading you think the formula's ordinary users mean and what would have to be added to make it do the work they want. No more than 900 words in total.
