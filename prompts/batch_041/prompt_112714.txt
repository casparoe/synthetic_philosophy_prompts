I lead a small decision-support team at a foundation that commits a fixed budget each quarter across interventions that differ sharply in how measurable their effects are and in how much of their value lies in low-probability, high-consequence outcomes. The commitments are large and hard to reverse, and none of us has formal training in decision theory. Twice now, board members have articulated preferences that come back around on themselves — one option preferred to a second, the second to a third, the third to the first — and our estimates for a set of mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive scenarios have summed to well over one. I have read enough to know that an agent whose preferences or credences break certain structural conditions is said to be incoherent, and that this is supposed to be the whole reason expected-utility maximization is the only defensible policy: a counterparty who knows the pattern can be relied on to extract a sure loss. I want a procedure my team can run, before an allocation reaches the board, to audit it for that kind of incoherence.

Lay it out as a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what counts as a failure. It has to isolate the structural conditions separately rather than collapse them into a general impression of consistency — in particular the condition governing how preferences order options, the condition governing how a preference must survive the mixing of each option with a common alternative, and the condition governing how probabilities combine across a partition — and test each on its own. For every failure it finds, it must distinguish a real violation from an artifact of how we elicited the preference or the estimate, and it must say which failures are mere bookkeeping and which are evidence that the representation itself is the wrong tool for the case. It must also confront the objection that the exploitation arguments only bite against an agent who cannot see the sequence of trades coming, and that an agent who can anticipate it, or who binds herself in advance not to be moved, may escape without revising anything; I want the procedure to state precisely where that defense is available and where it is not. And it must mark the points where the procedure has to stop and hand the matter to a person, because what looks like a violation is in fact a pair of options that are neither better, nor worse, nor equal, or a commitment that is meant to hold absolutely. It should be usable in an afternoon by people who lack the theory, and it should produce something short enough to attach to a recommendation.

Then run it on three cases and give the verdict at each step. First, the circular preference, where the colleague who holds it insists the cycle is stable and survives every way we have of presenting the options. Second, the scenario estimates that sum past one. Third, a board member who maintains that no expected benefit, however large, justifies any increase in the risk of an irreversible harm, and who treats this as a matter of principle rather than of estimation. I expect at least one verdict I will not like; I would prefer clean answers, and I suspect the procedure will refuse to give them, so be explicit about which refusal is principled and which is a failure of nerve. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do: what it will misclassify, how running it will leave my team more confident than the underlying argument actually licenses, and which question it will never settle for us.
