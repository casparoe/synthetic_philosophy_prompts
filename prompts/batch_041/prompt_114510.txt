I'm assembling a handout for the opening meeting of a political philosophy reading group this term, and the theme is the debate over whether the world is becoming less violent. Rather than write a summary, I want the handout to be a decision tree: something that walks a reader from a few plain starting commitments to a named position, so that people locate themselves instead of grabbing a team label. Please build that tree for me. The first branch should be about the scope of the key term — does violence cover only direct, intentional physical harm by identifiable agents, or also deaths and injuries from deprivation that someone plausibly had a duty to prevent, or also structural and slow violence such as enforced poverty and preventable disease, or finally also dignitary and psychological harm? The second branch should be the denominator: is the claim about per-capita rates, about absolute counts, or about something like harm per person-year — and if per capita, per capita of whom (world population, states, or persons actually exposed to the relevant harm)? I want the tree to force the reader to notice that a person can be a declinist on one denominator and not on another. A third branch should ask whether there is a fact of the matter at all: whether the claim that violence is declining is straightforwardly empirical, or is only true relative to a chosen concept of violence and a chosen measure, or is an evaluative claim in descriptive clothing. A fourth branch can cover baseline and timescale — all of recorded history, the decades since 1945, or the period since 1990 — because I want it to emerge that some of the shouting is really a disagreement about the window.

Each leaf should carry the name of a position where a name is available, and I expect the usual declinist, the long-peace institutionalist, and the structural-violence skeptic to appear, along with at least one position holding that there is no single fact of the matter here. Give every leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and mark any combination of answers that nobody actually occupies as empty, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Phrase every branch node as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the gap, and keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words.
