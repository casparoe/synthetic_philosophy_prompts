Ok so I'm building a set of prompts for a philosophy-of-science reading group and I need one on Popper and Darwinism. Draft me the joint statement that could come out of an adversarial collaboration between two people who've been arguing past each other for years. On one side: a Popperian who thinks Darwinian natural selection doesn't really clear Popper's own bar for a scientific theory — that it's close to a definitional circle (whatever survives is by definition the fittest, so where's the risk?), that in practice it licenses an endless supply of after-the-fact adaptive stories, and that Popper was right to file it under metaphysical research programme rather than testable theory. On the other side: an evolutionary biologist who thinks that's logic-chopping, that selection theory makes hard, risky, quantitative predictions all the time, and that if the Popperian's criterion condemns it then the criterion is broken, not the theory.

What I want is the document the two of them could actually both put their names to. So:

- a numbered list of claims both can sign — about what natural selection is, what it's for, what it predicts, and what the row is and isn't about;
- the remaining disagreements set out in wording each side would accept as fair to its own view (not strawmanned, not fudged into agreement);
- the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling things one way or the other would actually move at least one side;
- for each crux, a concrete case, argument or bit of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction for it written down in advance;
- and, if you reckon part of the dispute is just verbal — about what 'testable' or 'falsifiable' or 'theory' is doing, or about what Popper actually meant later on — say which part and why.

A few things I'd like you to fold in, because I don't want a generic essay. The difference between the two versions of the tautology worry (the tight one about defining fitness as survival, and the loose one about retrospective adaptive storytelling). The thought that fitness might be pinned down independently, e.g. by engineering/optimality analysis of a design problem or by measurable ecological parameters, so the circle gets broken. The idea of running Popper's own method of situational analysis on an organism's problem situation and seeing whether that yields anything falsifiable. And the distinction between selection as a mechanism that's happening now versus selection as a historical narrative about why some trait is here. Also worth a line on whether the whole units-of-selection argument is a red herring for the demarcation question or whether it actually cuts into it.
