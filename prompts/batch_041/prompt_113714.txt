I'm a software engineer reading Augustine's City of God on my own, and I've gotten stuck in Book XII. Here is the stretch that stops me:

"The cause, therefore, of the blessedness of the good is adherence to God. And so the cause of the others' misery will be found in the contrary, that is, in their not adhering to God. ... For we justly blame the fault because it mars the praiseworthy nature."

The first sentence I can accept. The second seems to explain a bad will by the absence of a good one, and the third then treats the fault itself as evidence that the nature it damages was made good and praiseworthy. That looks circular to me, or at least like no explanation at all: I want to know what made the will turn, and I am told only that it failed to adhere. Could you unpack what Augustine is actually claiming here? In particular: is he deliberately offering something weaker than a cause — an absence rather than a positive source — and if so, how is an absence supposed to explain anything? How can the occurrence of a defect be proof that the thing defective was made well? And does the argument only go through if one already accepts that evil is a privation rather than a thing, so that the whole passage is a restatement of a premise rather than an argument? I'd find it useful if you also said what would count as a counterexample to the claim, and where the reasoning is strongest and weakest.
