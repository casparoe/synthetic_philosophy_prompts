I work on causal inference — structural causal models, the do-calculus, potential outcomes, counterfactual and interventional reasoning, causal discovery, and the modularity and invariance assumptions that make those tools work. That is my native vocabulary and I am fluent in it; Islamic philosophy I know only from a general education, so treat me as a competent outsider in that domain and do not assume I already read the text.

I want you to restate the position on causation that al-Ghazālī defends in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa — the denial that there is any necessary connection between what we habitually call causes and what we call effects, the claim that the fire does not itself burn the cotton but that God creates the burning on the occasion of the contact, and the accompanying claim that a miracle involves no contradiction because the connection could always be otherwise — using the vocabulary I actually use. Put it in terms of variables and their parents and descendants, structural equations and functional dependence, exogenous and endogenous nodes, intervention and the do-operator, counterfactuals, identifiability, invariance and modularity, and the distinction between the observational and the interventional distribution. I do not want his words re-dressed in my jargon; I want a genuine mapping, in which you say which of his claims corresponds to which formal object in my framework and which correspondences are no more than suggestive.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Go through the elements of his position one by one and say, for each, whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in my vocabulary. I am especially interested in four things. First, what happens to the do-operator in his world: if the fire is not a parent of the burning, is an intervention on the fire simply uninformative about the burning, and does that make his view a claim that all variables are exogenous with a single common cause, or something my formalism cannot render at all? Second, the modal status of the structural equations: my framework already grants that the equations are empirical and could have been otherwise, so what is left over in his claim that they are not necessary, and does the leftover concern the equations themselves or the moment-to-moment act that sustains them? Third, whether his God is representable as an exogenous variable or a latent confounder, or whether the concept of a free agent that is not governed by any distribution, and the notion of a created habit rather than a probability, fall outside what my framework can express. Fourth, whether causal discovery is even a well-posed problem in his world, given that there are no stable mechanisms to find.

Finally, adjudicate the residue. Where my vocabulary fails, is that a defect of my framework — something causal inference genuinely cannot say — or is it a sign that his claim was less clear than it looked, or that he was making a different kind of claim (metaphysical, theological, dialectical) that my framework was never designed to capture? And tell me whether, once the translation is done, there is a substantive disagreement left between the occasionalist and me, or whether it dissolves into a difference over what we are willing to count as an explanation. I am aware that there is a scholarly dispute about whether the Tahāfut position is his considered metaphysics or a dialectical concession; do not let that derail the translation, but say at the end whether it changes the residue.
