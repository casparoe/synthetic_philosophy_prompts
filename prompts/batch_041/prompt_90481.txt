I write a column on science and ideas, and I've drafted the following short essay on the absent-minded driver problem. I'd like your comments, not a rewrite: where the argument is inaccurate, where it is unoriginal, whether the diagnosis is interesting enough to carry a short column, and where it fails on its own terms.

The absent-minded driver problem is usually presented as a puzzle about memory. A driver faces two intersections. At the first, exiting yields nothing; continuing leads to the second. At the second, exiting yields a large reward; continuing yields a small one. The driver cannot tell the intersections apart and cannot remember whether he has already passed one. The ex ante best policy is to continue at the first and exit at the second, but he cannot implement it because at any intersection he does not know which one it is. The standard question is what he should do given that he must choose the same action at both.

I want to argue that the problem is not fundamentally about memory. It is about the impossibility of conditional plans when the agent cannot verify which node of the decision tree he occupies. To bring this out, consider a variant: the driver can leave himself a note. At the first intersection he writes, "If you are reading this, you are at the second intersection." He leaves it on the dashboard. When he arrives at an intersection, he sees the note. But because he is absent-minded, he cannot remember whether he wrote it or whether he has already read it. He knows that a note is present, but he cannot verify that it was written at the first intersection and not planted there, or that it has not been moved. The note does not distinguish the intersections for him; it is just another feature of the scene. If, by contrast, the note were physically impossible to see at the first intersection—say it is printed on a sign that only exists at the second—then he could follow the optimal policy. So what solves the problem is not memory as such, but a reliable signal that is correlated with the node and whose reliability the agent can recognize. The absent-minded driver's real deficit is that he cannot certify the provenance of his own information.

This diagnosis has a consequence. It suggests that the problem is not dissolved by giving the driver more memory, or by letting him precommit, unless the precommitment device itself is recognizable as such at the moment of choice. A precommitment that he cannot identify as his own is no better than a note he cannot trust. The puzzle is therefore a special case of a more general issue: how an agent can make his later choices depend on information that only becomes available later, when the agent's later self may not be able to tell that the information is the right kind. I suspect this is why the problem feels deeper than a mere memory lapse.

Is that right? I'd like comments on accuracy, on whether the note variant really adds anything to the standard problem, and on what a defender of the view that he should simply continue at both intersections would say against my diagnosis. Also, is there a counterexample where the agent can implement the optimal policy without any recognizable signal, and if so, what does that do to my claim? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
