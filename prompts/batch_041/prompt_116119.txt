I'm pretty much sold on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation — the Ramsey / Savage / de Finetti style line where, if your preferences break the axioms, either you can be Dutch booked or money pumped, or else there's just no way to represent you as maximising anything at all. I want a map of what should count against it. Not the obvious complaint that people don't have complete preferences over every lottery — I already know that, and it feels like a dodge. I mean the real defeaters, sorted by how much they should actually move me.

A few things I'd like kept seperate:

- The two strands of the argument, because I suspect they have different exposure. One is the representation-theorem strand: no utility function and probability function make your preferences come out as expected-utility maximising. The other is the pragmatic strand: you're exploitable, a bookie who knows your preferences can take you for everything. Would a defender concede either of these if the other fell? Which one is doing the real work?
- What the world or the agent would have to look like for it to fail. A genuinely unexploitable agent with cyclic preferences? The objection that a Dutch book only works if you accept a whole sequence of bets you'd never be offered in one go — and the reply that you can be pumped in a single decision, or that refusal is itself a preference. Dynamic cases where resolute or sophisticated choice seems to get you out. Non-Archimedean or lexicographic preferences, infinite payoffs, anything where the continuity axiom is the one that snaps. For each of these, is it fatal, awkward, or already absorbed?
- Whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — into the tautology that whatever a rational agent does just is what maximises expected utility — and if so whether that's actually a problem or just what a consistency constraint looks like from the outside.
- What it predicts that its rivals don't. Compare it against thinner accounts, say that rationality only demands acyclicity, or only local requirements at each choice point.

Then give me a verdict on how exposed the thing actually is. If you had to pick the one result or argument that should most shake someone who holds it, what is it? I think I'd personally be most moved by a case where an agent with non-EU preferences is unexploitable in any sense that isn't question-begging, but tell me if that's the wrong place to look.
