I run the forecasting desk at a small research shop — six analysts, we sell probability estimates on policy and geopolitical questions to NGO and government clients, and our numbers end up in other people's budget documents, so when we're wrong it isn't just our reputation. We have a norm I've always liked: if you're going to revise a number, you should have been willing to hold the revised number the day before — no quietly waiting to see how things shake out and then moving. But the norm cuts the other way too, and that's what's been bothering me. If I'm confident that in 48 hours, after I've read the two reports sitting in my inbox, I'll think this is a 30% and not the 60% I'd say today, then it seems like I should already be at 30% now. A colleague told me there's a name for this — van Fraassen's reflection principle, roughly that your credence now should match what you expect your credence to be later, or else you're incoherent — and I'm fairly sure she also said there's an asterisk, I just can't remember where the asterisk goes.

So what I want is a procedure the desk can actually run, laid out as questions in order, for deciding when to defer to a later or better-informed version of ourselves and when deferring is a mistake. It needs to get through the ordinary cases — the analyst who's about to read a new batch of polling, the analyst who'll have spent another day thinking — and the ugly ones: someone who knows they'll be fried and rushed on deadline; someone whose later judgement will be downstream of a client leaning on them; the case where we know information will be lost before we revisit, which actually happened when the person who built our main model left and the rest of us are working off her notes; and the self-referential ones, since a lot of what we forecast is stuff where being seen to expect X makes X less likely. Two things I want built in. First, we have two years of revision logs, so use them — my impression is that revisions made after a client call are worse than revisions made after new data, but the procedure should say how to check that rather than take my word for it. Second, the case I care about most: a senior analyst says she's confident that in a week she'll see the flaw in the argument she's making right now, but she can't yet say what the flaw is. Should she move today or not? My suspicion is that the honest answer is that the procedure can neither make her move nor tell her to stay, and if that's right I want it said plainly rather than papered over. Constraints: revisions happen in the 24 hours before delivery, the analyst has about an hour, and anything that moves a number more than 15 points has to be signed off by me and logged with a written reason — so the procedure has to specify what a good reason looks like and what a bad one looks like. Run it end to end on three cases: the new-polling one, the client-call one, and the senior analyst one, and flag which steps are just judgement dressed up as procedure.

Skip the reading list, please — no papers, no citations, I'm not going to read them. If a problem has a name, name it, that's all I need.
