At a friendsgiving last month two people I know got into it over what I thought was a dumb remark and it turned into the most interesting argument I've heard all year. I was just sitting there eating pie and I honestly could not tell who was winning, so I'd like you to go through it move by move.

Setup: someone at the table said the Dutch are blunt, basically. Marta (the host) said that's just a stereotype. Her brother-in-law Dev said a stereotype can still be statistically true, and she said it doesn't matter whether it's true, there's no such thing as the character of a country. Then they went at it for like 40 minutes. Assume for the sake of the argument that the survey data really does show a difference in average directness between countries — that part was stipulated early so it's not the fight.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Marta: national character is nonsense. A country doesn't have a character.
2. Dev: that can't be right as stated. Countries are homogenizing machines — schools, a shared language, media, legal systems, immigration policy — so a shared statistical profile isn't mystical, it's the expected output of shared institutions. If "the Dutch health care system" is a legitimate abstraction even though every hospital differs, so is "Dutch directness."
3. Marta: those are seperate things. The health system claim has a mechanism and predicts something specific. "Dutch directness" is a claim about persons, and the grouping is an administrative boundary that doesn't line up with the causal boundaries — the trait is probably carried by region, class, religion, migration cohort, urban vs rural. So the national part is a bucket, not a cause.
4. Dev: fine, then call it a residual claim. Dutchness is legitimate iff, once you control for region, class, religion, age and urban/rural, the country variable still predicts something. That's a compression test: if adding country to the model buys real predictive gain, the abstraction is doing work; if not, it's folk taxonomy. That's a completely standard move, you don't get to call a concept incoherent just because it's a leftover.
5. Marta: your test isn't runnable by anyone at this table, and it tests usefulness, not coherence, and it isn't what people mean anyway. When someone says the Dutch are blunt they don't mean there's a small residual coefficient, they mean a disposition that attaches to a person by virtue of being Dutch. A character, in the old sense. And that's the incoherent thing, because a population statistic isn't a property anybody has.
6. Dev: that's a complaint about sloppy speakers, not about the concept. You can define national character as the distribution of a trait across people raised inside a set of institutions — coherent, measurable, non-mystical. And watch the trap: your argument also bans "men are taller than women," since no individual man is taller than any individual woman by virtue of being male. If that's absurd, the sin can't be attributing a statistic to a person. It has to be something else.
7. Marta: the height one isn't a claim about character, isn't used to sort people the same way, and has no history. And it's not only about accuracy — even if the Dutch claim is true and even if it passes your compression test, saying it to a Dutch guy in a job interview does something to him that has nothing to do with the sentence being true. It makes him answer for a statistic. The wrong is the act of classification, and no amount of predictive gain cures that.
8. Dev: then say that. You keep sliding between incoherent (does the sentence mean anything), unfounded (is there evidence), and rude (is it wrong to say). Those are three different fights, and you keep winning one and claiming the other two.

What I want: adjudicate it point by point. Rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification, and tell me who's right about which specific thing. I'm especially interested in whether Marta's character-vs-statistic distinction is doing real work or whether Dev is right that it's just a complaint about sloppy speakers, and whether there's any version of the compression test that actually settles anything rather than just relocating the question. And say what both of them are missing — I suspect there's something about who's doing the classifying and why, but I couldn't put my finger on it. Don't just split the difference, I want to know where each of them actually went wrong.
