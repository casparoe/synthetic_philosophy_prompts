Last night I lost an argument about pure time preference, or at any rate failed to win one, and I have been turning it over since. My friend is an economist who has spent a decade on climate cost-benefit analysis, and her complaint was that everything we call the discount rate is really three different parameters wearing a single name: a descriptive one, capturing how much people in fact prefer present goods to future ones; a prudential one, capturing how much a single agent ought to weight her own later welfare once you have already netted out the probability that she will not be alive to enjoy it; and a moral one, capturing how much a future person's welfare counts relative to a present person's. The provocative part came next. She claims the prudential and the moral parameters cannot be prised apart as cleanly as the literature assumes, because any nonzero value for the moral one commits you to weighting persons at different times unequally, which contradicts the very impartiality that is supposed to motivate the debate; and she pressed the point with an analogy I could not answer, that we would never hunt for a spatial discount rate to tell us how much a distant stranger's welfare counts, since distance is not the kind of thing that could ground a difference in moral weight — and that if we refuse to do this for space we should refuse to do it for time. Her conclusion was that the real question is not what rate to use but whether persons at different times are the right units of moral concern at all. I think something has been smuggled in, but I cannot yet say what.
