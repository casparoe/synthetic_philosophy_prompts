In most rich democracies, a proposed regulation has to be justified by an appraisal that expresses every expected effect in money and weighs the total against the cost. Where the effect passes through a market — a firm's outlay, a consumer's saving — the analyst reads its value off prices and observed choices, on the principle that what people will give up for a thing is what the thing is worth to them. Where it does not — cleaner air, the survival of a species, a death the rule would prevent — the analyst falls back either on surveys that ask people what they would pay, or on a single per-unit figure that the state sets and applies to everyone alike, so that a death averted among the poor enters the ledger at exactly the value of a death averted among the rich. What I find odd is that this flat figure was itself originally extracted from market behaviour, from the wage premiums workers demand for dangerous work, and those premiums climb steeply with income — precisely the sensitivity the flat figure discards. Meanwhile, some analysts now propose to abandon money as the common currency altogether and to reckon all benefits in units of self-reported wellbeing. What theory of value does this apparatus presuppose? Does it commit itself to the view that a person's welfare simply is the satisfaction of the preferences their choices reveal, or does the income-blind, uniform figure betray a commitment to something else — a value that holds independently of what anyone would pay? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say where it is underdetermined: which parts would survive equally well on a hedonic account, a preference-satisfaction account, or an objective-list account of welfare? Where its parts pull against one another, is that a defect that better data would cure, or evidence that two incompatible theories of utility are being run under one name? And if we replaced the presupposed theory with one of its rivals, what in the practice — the surveys, the flat figure, the very insistence on a single money metric — would have to go?
