For a while now I have been bothered by a pattern I first noticed in Maimonides and now seem to see everywhere, and I have never been able to decide whether it is one thing with a name or just a family of vaguely similar things I am lumping together. The place I first met it was his explanation of the sacrificial commandments in the Guide of the Perplexed. What he does not say is that sacrifice is intrinsically good or that it is what God most wants; he says that the Israelites had grown up in a world in which worship just meant sacrifice, that a religion suddenly stripped of it would have struck them as unintelligible and been rejected outright, and that the commandments therefore take over the institution they already knew and redirect it toward the one God instead of abolishing it. On that reading the sacrifices are a concession to where people actually were: the familiar form is kept, for the time being, so that the substance can be changed from within. I have since heard the same shape of story told about other things -- the law of kingship in the Hebrew Bible (the people insisted on a king, so a king is granted, but hedged about with rules), the divorce law (permitted, on the standard reading, because of people's hardness of heart rather than because it is the ideal), and the way a new religion will take over an old festival's date and trappings and pour a new meaning into it so that the change goes down more easily. In each case the defence of the rule is not that the rule is good but that the people for whom it was made could not yet have done without it.

I am taking a seminar on medieval philosophy and brought this up, and the discussion only made me less sure of myself. Is there an established name for this specific move -- a rule or institution that is defended, not on its own merits, but as a concession to what people were already accustomed to, kept in its old outward form while its point is quietly relocated, and understood by those who defend it as something to be outgrown rather than as a permanent ideal? I cannot tell whether the word I want is accommodation, or whether that term is reserved for something narrower (divine condescension to human language and human frailty), or whether I am really describing syncretism, or the biologists' exaptation, or merely gradualism and transitional arrangements under a grander name. I would like to know which of these, if any, is the concept I am circling, and exactly where the boundaries fall. Does the pattern require that the concession be deliberate, as it is in Maimonides, rather than an unplanned survival of an obsolete practice? Does it require that the old form be preserved and only its meaning changed, or does it also cover cases where the old practice is simply tolerated alongside the new until it withers? And does it matter whether the concession is understood as temporary scaffolding or as a permanent second-best?

If nothing established fits, I would rather have you name and define the thing yourself than hand me the nearest label and stop there. Either way, please give me the carving and not just the word: a definition, the cases that are clearly inside it and clearly outside it, and for each of the neighbouring concepts you consider, the reason it is close but not the same. Please state your final answer on the last line in the form \answer{...}, containing either the established term you settle on or, if there is none, the term you coin.
