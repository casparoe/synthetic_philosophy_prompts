I run a philosophy-of-law reading group — twelve participants, mixed backgrounds (a few lawyers, several philosophers, two undergraduates), one 90-minute session, no preparation expected beyond a single page handed out at the door. I want to run a self-amending rule game that makes them feel the problem of legal self-reference in their hands: not the tidy puzzle about whether a rule can authorize its own amendment, but the moment when the rulebook stops being able to say what it says, and everyone notices that the only thing holding it together is their continued willingness to treat it as holding together.

Design the game and give it to me ready to run.

1. It must be playable in 90 minutes by people who have never seen it, with nothing but the one-page rulebook they receive at the start, paper, pens, and some way of keeping score. There is a known tradition of self-amending rule games; if you know it, do not hand me its standard opening rule set. The seeded rules should be designed for the phenomena below, and you should give me their exact text, ready to print.

2. There must be real stakes. A scoring scheme — points, tokens, an accumulating prize, whatever — such that at least one player is materially better off exploiting an ambiguity or a gap than playing straight. The paradox should arrive through somebody trying to win, not through a discussion prompt.

3. Seed the rulebook with at least these three pressure points, and design each so that it can only be resolved by something the rulebook does not itself contain: (a) a rule that specifies how it is itself to be amended, where the specified procedure is one that the rule also governs; (b) two rules that conflict under a foreseeable move, with no rule giving precedence, and where the obvious repair — adding a precedence rule — would have to be enacted through a procedure the conflict may already have disabled; (c) a clause declaring some rule or feature of the rulebook unamendable, together with a move by which a player can plausibly amend it anyway.

4. I want them to hit the regress, not just the local paradox. Include at least one role or procedure whose authority to settle disputes about the rules is itself a rulebook matter, so that any appeal to it re-raises the question. If you give me a referee, say plainly how the referee's authority is grounded inside the fiction and what happens when a player disputes the ground.

5. Tell me how the game ends — what counts as winning, and what I should do if the group reaches a state where the rules no longer determine the outcome. I don't want a rule that conveniently resolves everything; if the honest answer is that the session ends unresolved, say so, and give me a way to close the session that doesn't cheat.

6. A timeline with minute markers: setup, the first amendments, roughly when to expect the first rule conflict, the explosion, and the debrief.

7. Debrief: map the specific moments of the session onto the distinctions that matter — a rule being valid versus a rule being accepted; amending a rule versus replacing the framework that makes amendment possible; a procedurally impeccable amendment that produces an intolerable result versus a legitimate one; and the difference between a paradox showing the rulebook is inconsistent and one showing it is merely incomplete. Put the ideas in your own words; I don't want a reading list, and I don't need anything attributed.

8. What predictably goes wrong the first time I run this: the failure modes to expect from adults who are not used to being bound by arbitrary rules, which are fatal to the demonstration and which are instructive, and precisely what I should and should not do when they occur — especially when someone declares the whole thing void and refuses to keep playing.

9. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I have to supply as facilitator. I have a suspicion: that a game can reproduce the mechanics of self-reference but not the felt authority of law, because players treat the rules as mechanics to be gamed rather than as binding. Tell me whether that is right, and if the activity can do better than I think, say how.

Be committed: choose specific rules, specific numbers, and one ending rather than offering me variants to pick from, and give your reasons where a different design was available. If you think one of my constraints is a mistake, design to it anyway and tell me why you would have dropped it in the debrief notes.
