Below is an exam question from a course unit on the is–ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, together with one student's answer. Please grade the answer and supply the specific comments that justify the grade.

The unit drew a working distinction between two charges that are often run together under the heading of the naturalistic fallacy. The derivation charge is that an argument moves from premises that are entirely non-normative to a normative conclusion, with no normative premise doing work. The definition charge is that a normative term has been identified with, or defined in terms of, a natural property. The unit insisted that the two charges be kept separate and that neither be assumed fatal on its own.

Exam question. Consider the two arguments below. (i) For each, say whether the derivation charge or the definition charge applies. (ii) Say whether the charge, if it applies, is fatal to the argument as stated. (iii) Say whether the two charges are independent: can an argument commit one without the other, and can a term be defined in natural terms without committing either?

Argument A: Acts of gratuitous cruelty cause more suffering than they prevent. Therefore acts of gratuitous cruelty are wrong.

Argument B: Whatever maximizes total welfare is right. This policy maximizes total welfare. Therefore this policy is right.

Student's answer. In A every premise is descriptive and the conclusion is normative, so the derivation charge plainly applies; read strictly, A is not valid, since nothing in the premises tells us that suffering is bad or that what prevents it is right. B, by contrast, is valid as it stands, and I take the unit's point that validity is what is at issue. Still, I think the derivation charge applies to B as well: "maximizes total welfare" is a natural property and "right" is a normative one, so the step from the first premise to the conclusion is a move from is to ought, merely compressed into a single premise rather than spread across two. The definition charge is a different accusation, aimed at how a term is fixed rather than at how an argument moves; it descends from Moore's open-question argument and is not the same as Hume's point, and B is a better candidate for it than A, since its first premise reads like a definition of "right" in natural terms. On the unit's claim that a suppressed normative premise could rescue A: adding such a premise does not answer the derivation charge, it only relocates it. The added premise is itself normative and stands in need of support, and supporting it will require either a further normative premise or a derivation from descriptive ones — so we end up back where we started, which is precisely what the is–ought observation was meant to bring out. The two charges are independent, and a definition can be given in natural terms without committing the derivation charge.

Grade the answer and give the comments that justify the grade. Work through the answer point by point, and for each of the three parts of the exam question state what a reader should conclude and whether the student got there.
