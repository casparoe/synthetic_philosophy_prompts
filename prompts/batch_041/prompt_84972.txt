I'm a PhD student in cog sci and I keep circling back to this old Hofstadter project — Letter Spirit, I think it's called — where the aim was to model two things at once: how every 'a' is still an 'a' (blackletter, geometric sans, a five-year-old's scrawl) even though they barely share any geometric features, and how a single style can run through all twenty-six letters so the whole alphabet looks like it came from one hand. I'm going off memory here so dont lean on the details of the project, it's the shape of the problem I care about.

I'd like a short story that dramatizes it instead of explaining it. The thing I'm really after, and the part that usually gets skipped, is this: a letter isn't pinned down by any necessary feature, it's pinned down by its place in a system of contrasts — an 'a' is an 'a' partly because of how it sits against the 'o' and the 'd' and the 'q' in the same font — which means you can push a style far enough that an 'a' quietly stops being an 'a' without any single stroke ever turning into something it's supposedly not allowed to be. Recognizability and style are pulling against each other the whole way through.

For the setup, your call: a forger inventing an entire hand for a manuscript that has to pass as the work of one scribe; a type designer; or someone deciphering marks and having to decide whether they're one letter written twenty wild ways or two different letters. Give it human stakes, keep it not too long, and please dont wrap it up with a tidy moral.
