I am a software engineer by training and I am serving as a teaching assistant for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on technology, governance, and collective action; the students are mostly engineers, and the assignment asks them to apply the rivalry/excludability taxonomy to a set of infrastructure resources and then assess a claim about the tragedy of the commons. I have to return one student's response with a grade and written comments, and because my own background is in distributed systems rather than philosophy, I would like a careful second opinion on what the response actually gets right and wrong. The grading scale is out of 10: 3 points for correctly classifying the three resources, 3 points for the conceptual explanation of the governance problem each poses, 2 points for the evaluation of the Hardin/Ostrom claim, and 2 points for clarity and use of the examples. Please assign a numerical grade and then provide point-by-point comments that identify the specific sentences or moves that earn or lose credit. Do not just give a general impression; I need comments I can hand back to the student, including any place where the answer is subtly wrong even if it sounds plausible.

The question was:

Consider three resources. (a) A municipal wildfire-evacuation alert system that broadcasts a common warning to everyone in the county at no additional cost per listener and cannot practically exclude any resident. (b) A shared internal compute cluster with a fixed number of GPUs, open to all members of a research consortium, where each job run by one member leaves fewer GPUs for others. (c) A proprietary climate-model dataset licensed to a consortium of twelve universities, where any member institution may use the dataset without diminishing its availability to the other eleven, but non-members are excluded. For each resource, state whether it is rival or non-rival in consumption and excludable or non-excludable, name the category it falls into, and explain the distinctive governance problem it poses. Then evaluate the following claim: Hardin's tragedy of the commons shows that any shared resource without private property will inevitably be depleted, so Ostrom's work mainly provides empirical illustrations of that inevitability.

The student's answer was:

The evacuation alert system is a public good: it is non-rival, because one more resident receiving the warning does not reduce anyone else's warning, and non-excludable, because the county cannot feasibly stop a resident from hearing it. Its governance problem is underprovision: since no one can be charged for receiving the warning and no one's use reduces another's, private actors will not supply it, so it must be funded collectively. The compute cluster is a common-pool resource: it is rival, because each GPU-hour used by one lab is unavailable to another, and non-excludable within the consortium, because all members have access. Its governance problem is overuse or congestion: each member captures the full benefit of running a job but bears only a fraction of the delay imposed on others, so the cluster will be over-subscribed unless access is rationed. The licensed dataset is a club good: it is non-rival, since one university's use does not deplete it, and excludable, since the license keeps non-members out. Its governance problem is mainly one of maintaining the exclusion mechanism and sharing fixed costs fairly among the twelve members. So the three resources differ along the two dimensions exactly as the taxonomy predicts: public goods are non-rival and non-excludable, common-pool resources are rival and non-excludable, and club goods are non-rival and excludable.

The claim is false. Hardin's parable does not show that depletion is inevitable; it shows what happens under a particular open-access regime where no rules constrain appropriation. Ostrom's work is important precisely because it documents communities that have avoided depletion through institutions such as clearly defined boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, and conflict-resolution procedures. So Ostrom does not merely illustrate Hardin's inevitability thesis; she refutes the thesis that no shared resource can be governed without private property or state control.

That said, the underlying logic of the compute-cluster case is still a free-rider problem: each member would prefer that others restrain their use while still running their own jobs, so the cluster tends to be underprovided. Hardin's own conclusion was that the only real solutions are privatization or top-down state regulation, and Ostrom's cases succeed mainly because they involve small, homogeneous communities with shared norms; scaling them to something like a national climate-data infrastructure is therefore doubtful. A complete answer should therefore say that commons dilemmas are sometimes solvable but that the solutions depend on the same exclusion and monitoring capacities that make club goods work.
