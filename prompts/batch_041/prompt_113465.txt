Hey, I'm a philosophy grad student and I've definately fallen down a rabbit hole on this thing I've seen EA-adjacent people call "moral trade." The gist, as I understand it: two people with different moral views can both come out ahead by swapping — I do something you care about more than I do, and you do something I care about more than you do. Classic toy example: a vegetarian and a dedicated carnivore agree that the vegetarian eats meat on Sundays and the carnivore goes vegan the rest of the week, so each trades away a commitment they hold less dear for one they hold more dear. On paper it looks like an obvious win-win. But I can't shake the feeling that there's something rotten in it — like it turns morality into haggling, and maybe destroys the very commitments that get traded.

Could you write me a dialogue (say 800-1200 words) set at a dinner party, between two genuinely sharp people — call them Maya and Jonah. Maya is the moral-trade enthusiast who thinks this is a wildly underrated way to make the world morally better; Jonah thinks certain commitments just aren't the kind of thing you can put a price on without changing what they are. I really don't want Jonah to be a scold who only says "ew, that's gross." Give me the strongest version of his side (think: integrity, the non-fungibility of certain commitments, the point that a promise you're willing to sell stops being a promise) and the strongest version of Maya's replies (all deliberation is already trading one value off against another, refusing to trade just means refusing to help, the alternative is worse, etc.). Let them actually go back and forth and respond to each other's specific points rather than talking past each other.

And here's the knot I most want them to pull on. I've been calling it the difference between *thin* and *thick* trades. A thin trade stays inside one currency — we both agree welfare matters, so we swap welfare-affecting acts. A thick trade crosses currencies — say a deontologist who cares about rights trades with a utilitarian who cares about total welfare: the deontologist will violate one right if the utilitarian will save a thousand lives. Is a thick trade even coherent, or is it really just two people each quietly trying to drag the other over to their own view? And can Maya defend thin trades without getting forced to swallow thick ones too? You're basically a professional philosopher here, so take that role seriously: before you write a word, brainstorm the strongest version of each position so neither turns into a strawman, and let at least one of them concede a point or shift a little by the end. Oh, and please don't have either of them cite papers or books — just argue it out.
