I'm a novelist — third book out last spring, the one about the dam — and through a chain of events I still don't fully understand (the town clerk heard me read at the library and decided I was 'a person of letters') I've been asked to give the short speech at our town's naturalization ceremony next month. Twenty-two people taking the oath, plus their families, a few local officials, someone from the paper, and a lot of folding chairs in a school gym. My problem is that I spent the last year reading about political obligation for a character in my new book — she spent four years in a country where the police were the main problem, and I needed to know what she'd think she owed anyone — and now I know just enough to be paralyzed. Everyone expects the speech to say something like: you've chosen us, so now you owe us your loyalty, because you consented, and because of everything this country gave you, and because of what you owe the people who did the work before you. But consent doesn't quite work — nobody signs anything, and the kids in the room, the ones who came over at four years old, never agreed to a thing — and the benefit arguments don't quite work either, because getting a lot out of a country doesn't obviously explain why you owe obedience to this one rather than to whichever one would treat you best, and anyway some philosophers will tell you flat out that there's no general duty to obey the law at all, only reasons to go along with it when it's decent. I don't want to hand twenty-two people a lie in a gym, and I don't want to hand them a shrug either.

Here's the one move I keep circling, and I'd like the speech built around it if it can bear the weight: the oath is sworn to the other people in the room, not to the government. Not 'I will obey the state' but 'I will be one of us' — a promise made to neighbors, which is a thing human beings can actually make and keep, unlike a promise to an abstraction. If that's right, then the rest of us are on the hook too, since we're the ones being promised to. But I'm not sure it isn't just a sentimental dodge — possibly the same trick as saying obligation comes from consent, only with the pronoun changed — so I need you to actually think it through rather than take it as given, and to say plainly where it fails.

The other thing I want in there, and I can't figure out how to do it without it sounding like a loophole: I admire the people in this country's past who broke its laws on purpose — the ones who got arrested, went to jail, didn't run — and I want these twenty-two to inherit that too, to understand that a country that can't survive being disobeyed openly isn't worth the promise. But I don't want to say 'obey the laws you happen to agree with,' which is just lawlessness with a better haircut. So somewhere in there I need the difference between breaking a law as a citizen addressing the rest of us and breaking it as a person who's opted out, and I need it in language that lands for a room that includes a guy who's been waiting eleven years for this occassion, a twelve-year-old who is bored out of her mind, and a local reporter. Can you write me the speech? Five to seven minutes, spoken not read — I'll be standing there with shaking hands — and keep it honest about what the theory doesn't settle. If you had to leave things out to make it work as a speech, tell me at the end, in a few lines, what they were and why.
