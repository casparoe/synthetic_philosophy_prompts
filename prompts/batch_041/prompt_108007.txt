I've been reading a fair amount of comparative constitutional design lately — partly for work, partly because I've spent too many evenings in comment sections arguing about it — and I keep coming away with the nagging sense that the presidentialism-versus-parliamentarism debate, as it's usually posed, is arguing about the wrong variable. I can't tell whether that's a genuine insight or just the familiar sensation of having read enough blog posts to mistake contrarianism for clarity, so I'd like you to help me think it through in a form that has actual stakes. A friend of mine sits on the advisory panel for a constitutional assembly in a country that has just come out of roughly three decades of single-party rule — a negotiated transition, not a collapse — and the panel has about two years, three full-time staff, and, crucially, no realistic opportunity to reopen the design after ratification. They've asked me, as the resident philosophy person, to help them set an agenda rather than assemble a wish list of institutions.

So here is what I want from you: within the debate over how executive and legislative power should be related, which open questions actually matter most for people in that position, and in what order should they take them up? I care much less about the final ranking than about the reasoning behind it — for each candidate, what makes it upstream of the others, how much of it is a conceptual question that political theorists can make progress on in a couple of years rather than an empirical one that requires decades of data from countries that aren't this one, how neglected it is relative to how much it matters, and what would move it up or down the list. To make this concrete, I want you to take seriously a framing a colleague has been pushing, which I'll state in my own words and which I don't think maps cleanly onto any standard position. The proposal is that the usual axis bundles together two things that ought to be separated: (a) whether the executive's survival in office depends on retaining legislative confidence, and (b) whether the legislature's survival depends on the executive's forbearance about dissolution. On this view the design variable that actually matters is the asymmetry between those two — who holds the cheaper unilateral exit option, and what it costs them to use it — and the pathologies commonly attributed to presidentialism or to parliamentarism are really pathologies of asymmetric exit costs in one direction or the other. I suspect this is at least half wrong, but I want the ranking built in a way that would reveal whether it is.

Please give me a defended agenda rather than a survey: a ranked shortlist of perhaps four or five questions, with the criteria you're using stated explicitly, the trade-offs between those criteria acknowledged, and an honest account of which questions the literature treats as central but which are red herrings for this particular purpose — where more philosophical work simply won't change anything a drafter could act on. I'd also like the shortlist to say which items are genuinely conceptual and which are irreducibly empirical, and to flag the sub-questions that are weedy enough to be worth a philosopher's time but not so weedy that no one outside the seminar room cares — things like the design of caretaker periods between the fall of a government and the seating of its successor, whether a no-confidence motion should be required to name a replacement, whether election timing should be fixed or contingent, how much discretion a head of state should have in appointing a formateur, and whether the link between budget failure and loss of confidence should be formalized. Finally, tell me what evidence over the next two or three years would most change the ordering, and which single question, if it turned out one way rather than another, would reorganize the rest.
