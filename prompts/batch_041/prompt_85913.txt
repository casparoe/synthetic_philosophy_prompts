I edit the back page of a popular-science magazine, and the freelancer I commissioned for next month's issue has just flaked, so I'm writing the piece myself and would like your help with it. The slot is an advice column — the syndicated kind where a reader writes in with a problem and a warm, unflappable voice writes back — and the twist I want is that the agony aunt is a fully committed believer in the thesis, best known from Hofstadter and Sander's book on analogy, that analogy-making is not one mental faculty among others but the core of cognition: that perceiving, categorizing, remembering, speaking and joking are all acts of analogy; that recognizing this letter d as a d is an analogy to the d's I have met before; that concepts are fluid rather than definitional, a category being a web of remembered exemplars whose membership is a matter of degree and context rather than of necessary and sufficient conditions; and that the classic letter-string puzzles — abc turns into abd, so what does ijk turn into? ijl, or ijd, or abd, depending on which aspects of the first pair you decide to make count — show how much room a single situation leaves for being seen differently.

Give me two letters and two replies, about 700 words altogether, in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics for emphasis, no tables, and mark the second letter with an ordinary sentence of transition rather than a label. The first correspondent cannot decide whether to accept a job in another city and wants a method; the second has just been left by a partner and cannot stop reading the whole relationship backwards as a rehearsal for the leaving. Your aunt is not a fool and must never misstate the theory — every move she makes has to be one the theory genuinely licenses — and she has to be written from inside the position, deadpan, with no winking and no authorial nudges: nothing in the column may signal that it is a joke. In each reply she should do what the theory says we cannot help doing anyway: diagnose the trouble as a badly chosen analogy and offer a better one drawn from an unexpected domain (a garden, a lighthouse, a mistranslation, a jury), while conceding that the old analogy cannot simply be switched off, since analogies are made for us rather than by us. Each reply should close on a prediction that cannot fail, because on her picture whatever happens next will be the new analogy working itself out. I want a specialist to laugh because she recognizes both the theory and the objection that nobody in the column ever voices — that if every act of thought is an analogy, and any two things resemble each other in indefinitely many respects, then "you are running the wrong analogy" is true of everyone at every moment and so tells nobody anything — and I want an ordinary reader to laugh because the aunt is so calm.
