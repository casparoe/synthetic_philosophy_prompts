I'm a TA for an ethics and public policy course, and my grandmother just moved into a care home where they use QALYs to decide who gets what. Its got me thinking about prioritarianism way to much. Anyway, I have to grade this student's answer. The question was:

Explain the leveling-down objection to egalitarianism and assess whether prioritarianism avoids it. Then distinguish prioritarianism from utilitarianism and from maximin. Finally, consider this case: There are two groups. Group A is badly off (well-being 10). Group B is very well off (well-being 100). Policy X gives Group A +2 and Group B -20. Policy Y gives Group A 0 and Group B +20. Policy Z gives Group A 0 and Group B -20. Which policy would a prioritarian prefer, and does the case pose a problem for prioritarianism?

Student's answer:

Egalitarianism says equality is intrinsically good. The leveling-down objection says that if equality is intrinsically good, then it can be good to make the better off worse off even if no one is made better off. For example, if everyone is blind, that is equal, but it is not obviously better than a world where some can see. Prioritarianism avoids this because it does not value equality intrinsically. It gives priority to those who are worse off, but it does not care about inequality as such. So a prioritarian would not support Z. In the case, a prioritarian would choose X over Y because X benefits the worse off. This is basically the difference principle: maximize the position of the worst off. However, unlike utilitarianism, prioritarianism does not simply maximize total well-being; it may sacrifice some total for the sake of the worse off. The leveling-down objection therefore does not apply to prioritarianism. One might object that if prioritarianism gives absolute priority to the worse off, then it would choose X even if the cost to Group B is enormous, but that is just what it means to give priority to the worse off.
