Her defence, which she's now given in like three interviews, is basically this: "a biopic is a portrait, not a photograph. A portrait isn't trying to record the face, it's trying to get at the truth of the sitter, and sometimes you have to invent in order to do that." I've been argueing with her about it and I keep losing, so I want a proper audit of the analogy rather than my half-remembered intuitions.

Act as a philosopher of art and of history — someone who actually works on the ethics of representing the dead — and think this through carefully. Give me the correspondence table. What plays the role of the sitter, the face, the portraitist, the pigment, the "truth of the sitter", the gallery wall, the viewer? Be explicit about each pairing and don't skip the ones that are awkward.

Then say where the mapping holds and where it snaps. I especially want you to think about the things the portrait analogy quietly imports that a biopic doesn't have. A portrait's sitter can sit for it, can reject a bad likeness, can say "that's not me" — the historical dead can't do any of that. A portrait is of an individual and arguably aims at character; a biopic is of actions and events, and it's the actions that the family is angry about. And there's a question about whether a portrait even asserts anything that can be true or false about the world in the way that "X betrayed Y in 1863" can. Which of these asymmetries actually matter for what she's using the analogy to do, and which are just differences that don't touch the conclusion?
