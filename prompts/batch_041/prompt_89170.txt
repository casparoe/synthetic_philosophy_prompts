I am assembling a specification that I can hold candidate theories to before reading any of them, and I want you to write the desiderata for a theory of market epistemology: an account of what a price tells you, and of how a market can function as a device for aggregating information that no single participant possesses. Assume the theory has to cover two pictures at once, since these are the two by which any such theory will be judged — the price as a summary of dispersed information, and the price as the reward that pays participants for gathering information in the first place. My purpose is a standard I can apply in an advanced seminar on the economics of information, not a survey of the literature.

Give me the requirements list. Order it so that the requirements you regard as non-negotiable come first, and mark each of the remaining ones as a preference of one camp rather than a genuine condition of adequacy. For every requirement, give one or two lines on the consideration that forces it — the case, puzzle, or formal result behind it. The list should cover at least the following ground:

- What a price must be evidence about, and for whom. Say what it tells a participant who has no other source, and whether the theory owes different answers to a specialist and to someone who merely reads the tape.
- The no-trade results, which say that when traders share a prior, when rationality and the information structure are common knowledge, and when the starting allocation already leaves no unexploited gains, private information by itself will not generate trade. A theory that permits speculative trade must say which of these assumptions it gives up, and whether giving it up is principled or merely convenient.
- The circularity that a price informative enough to be worth reading is a price that pays nobody to inform it. The theory must say what the equilibrium looks like once information acquisition is costly and endogenous, and whether the resulting equilibrium is stable.
- The distinction between information about fundamentals and information about other participants' beliefs, including the higher-order case in which the price is read as a forecast of what other readers of the price will do.
- Whether persistent disagreement is a datum the theory must explain or a premise it may assume. If a theory accounts for trade by differences in priors, say what stops it from explaining everything and therefore nothing.
- Reflexivity: publishing a price changes what the price is a price of. Say whether this destabilizes the theory's object and how the theory accommodates it.
- Empirical content: what observation would count as a refutation of the claim that prices aggregate information, and what the theory predicts that a rival does not.
- Distinctions it must not collapse: informative versus accurate; aggregation of information versus agreement among participants; trade for hedging or risk-sharing versus trade for speculation; a fully revealing equilibrium versus an efficient one.

Then the part I care about most. Say which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and distinguish a genuine impossibility from a difficulty that merely calls for more modeling. My own suspicion is that the informativeness requirement and the incentive requirement are in direct conflict once information is costly, and that any theory retaining common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and no unexploited gains has to get its trade from outside the model entirely. Tell me whether either suspicion survives scrutiny. End by naming the single requirement that does the most to eliminate candidate theories.

Cap the whole response at 800 words: numbered list plus a closing paragraph, no table, and the impossibility verdict at the end rather than buried in the list.
