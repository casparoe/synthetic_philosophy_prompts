I took a philosophy MA and then drifted into industry; for the last few years I have been on the evaluation team for a company that puts LLM-based agents into a regulated workflow, in practice insurance claims adjudication, and I keep running into a pattern I do not have a word for. We grade two things. The process score asks whether the agent consulted the right sources, applied the right rule, and wrote down its reasoning. The outcome score asks whether the decision was actually right, judged months later by human auditors and by what happened to the claim. What unsettles me is not that the two disagree — everyone expects some slack between them — but the direction in which they disagree at the top. Among our highest process scores, the correlation with outcomes is slightly negative. The agents are not gaming the score; I have read the rationales and they are sincere, accurate, and useless, because the process encodes what a careful reviewer does on a typical case, and the agents who follow it most exactly are precisely the ones who never notice that the case in front of them is atypical and the checklist is the wrong instrument. Meanwhile a bad outcome that occurs under a high process score gets filed as an accident rather than as an error: the record shows every step followed, so there is nothing to correct, and the same failure recurs without ever accumulating into a lesson. When we tightened the standard after one such failure, compliance rose, outcomes did not improve, and the failure became harder to see, because the new standard required a documentation step that recast the same bad decision as a considered and justified departure from the default.

The thing I want a name for is narrower than the well-worn observation that a measure stops measuring what it was meant to measure once it becomes a target, and it is not the ordinary process-versus-outcome trade-off, which we all know about and which at least keeps the failure visible. What I am after is the way a process standard relocates the question of whether something went wrong: the error is defined as the deviation from the procedure rather than as the bad outcome, so once the procedure has been followed the outcome has nowhere to register as a failure, and the more faithfully the standard is followed the less visible its failures become. I have since noticed what looks like the same shape in hospital incident reporting and in bank compliance files, which is why I doubt it is a peculiarity of AI evaluation.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration, and if so, how does it differ from the nearest things I have already considered and found unsatisfying — normalization of deviance, goal displacement, the streetlight effect, the various forms of blame-free accident reporting? I am not after a label to wave around; I want the carving. Tell me what the phenomenon is and what it is not, whether it is one thing or two, since the blindness to atypical cases and the conversion of error into accident may not be the same mechanism, whether it requires an actual written standard or whether any entrenched habit of justification would do the same work, and what would count as a case that resembles it but is not it. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me what it excludes.
