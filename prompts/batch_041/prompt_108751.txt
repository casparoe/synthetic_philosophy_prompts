I am a senior at a small religiously affiliated high school, and in three weeks I have to give the farewell remarks at our spring honors assembly for the teacher who is retiring after thirty-one years. She taught our ethics and scripture courses, and she is the reason I have read Plato's Euthyphro at all. I have been given about four minutes.

Here is the difficulty. The head of school speaks before me and will almost certainly describe our school's values as rooted in its founding tradition. About half the room will be families who chose this school precisely for that tradition, including several clergy. The other half will be families like mine, who came for the small classes and are not religious. My teacher's own family will be in the front row and are, as far as I know, not religious either.

The further difficulty is what she is actually known for. Eight years ago she declined to enforce a rule the administration had just tightened, and she accepted the consequences quietly instead of making a case of it. I do not want to relitigate that, and she has asked me not to turn her send-off into a protest. But everyone in that room knows about it, and it is a large part of why she is being honored rather than merely thanked.

What I want is a speech that honors her, and that lets the Euthyphro problem do the structural work rather than mentioning it. My teacher's own way of putting the problem was that it is not really a question about why we should be good; it is a question about whether admiring anyone makes sense. If being good just means doing as you were told, then what we admire is the telling rather than the person, and an award for goodness is really an award for obedience. If instead goodness is something the telling is answerable to, then she was good and the rule was simply wrong. Please write the speech so that it finds a way through that does not collapse into either half: it may not say she was good because she obeyed, and it may not say the tradition she taught in is irrelevant, or that the people who wrote the rule were simply mistaken about their own faith.

Constraints: do not name the incident directly; do not require the audience to know the dialogue or the word dilemma; take no position on whether God exists; the head of school must be able to hear it without feeling attacked, and my teacher's secular family must be able to hear it without feeling that she is being recruited for something; and it must be warm, because it is a farewell and not an essay.

After the speech, please add a short note addressed to me rather than to the audience, saying which reading of the Euthyphro problem the speech ends up committing to, where that reading is contested and where the speech is most likely to fail with one half of the room or the other, and what I would have to decide or learn — which argument or consideration — for you to have structured the speech the opposite way.
