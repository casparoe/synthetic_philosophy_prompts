'Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics is best known for its defense of utilitarianism, but its central problem is the dualism of practical reason. Sidgwick argues that our practical reason delivers two conflicting verdicts: the intuitionist verdict that we ought to follow self-evident moral rules, and the utilitarian verdict that we ought to maximize general happiness. He tries to show that these two can be reconciled, and largely succeeds, but he cannot reconcile either of them with egoism. The only way to reconcile egoism with morality would be if the universe were morally ordered, e.g., if God ensured that virtue is rewarded in the long run. This is where his psychical research comes in. He hoped that evidence of survival after death would show that the universe is morally ordered, and thus vindicate utilitarianism as the true moral theory. He was one of the founders of the Society for Psychical Research and served as its first president. In the end, the evidence was not forthcoming, and he remained an agnostic. So his psychical research was a continuation of his ethical project, but it failed.'
