I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of religion this term and I keep ending up in the modal-version-of-the-ontological-argument corner of it. I'm a math person by training, so the only one of these I have any patience for is Gödel's, since at least it's an actual derivation rather than an intuition pump, and our syllabus skipped it entirely. So I went looking and found a long essay on a philosophy blog about it (the writer is a grad student somewhere, there are footnotes, which is why I trusted it). Here's the gist as best I remember it, and I'd like you to tell me how much of it holds up. Gödel worked out a proof that God exists in modal logic, never published it, it turned up in his papers and leaked out through Dana Scott's notes from 1970, so what actually circulates is basically Scott's version. The setup: take 'positive' as a primitive — a property is positive just in case its negation isn't, i.e. positive properties are the ones it's better to have; being God-like is having all the positive properties; and necessary existence is itself a positive property. From those you get that a God-like being is possible, and then, since a God-like being can't exist contingently — it either exists in every possible world or in none — it follows that it exists in every possible world, so God exists and couldn't have failed to. The essay's bottom line was that this is just a proof in S5, the strongest and least controversial of the modal systems, and since the axioms are really only definitions of what 'positive' means, the whole thing is about as secure as anything in mathematics — the derivation itself has even been machine-checked. The only real escape route, it said, is refusing S5, the principle that what's possibly necessary is necessary, and almost nobody wants to pay that price. It also dismissed Kant's old objection that existence isn't a predicate, on the grounds that the argument doesn't treat existence as a perfection or a predicate at all, only as a modal status. One commenter underneath said the axioms actually make every true statement necessary, so the proof proves far too much, and the author replied that this is a known quirk of one axiom that can be patched without giving anything up.

That all sounded suspiciously tidy to me, and I don't know enough modal logic to adjudicate it myself. So: go through it claim by claim. What would someone who actually works on this accept as fair, what would they wince at as a simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong? I especially want to know whether the bottom line survives — that the only serious way out is to reject S5, and that anyone who keeps S5 is stuck with the conclusion. And if some of the middle claims are wrong, does that make the argument weaker, or does it just relocate the argument somewhere else?
