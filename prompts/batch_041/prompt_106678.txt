I'm drafting a policy memo on stunning and slaughter standards for finfish and decapod crustaceans in aquaculture, and before I get to the practice I want my philosophical footing to be sound. Here is the principle I've been working with, and I'd like to know whether it holds up. Call it the verificationist test for welfare standing: an animal is entitled to welfare protection only if we can verify that it consciously feels pain, and where the evidence leaves the question open, the default is that it does not. My reasoning is that sentience is the only thing that could ground a welfare claim; that the science on fish and crustaceans is genuinely unsettled, since it remains disputed whether they merely detect and respond to tissue damage or actually suffer it; and that in the absence of verification the honest default must be no. This strikes me as the same logic by which we decline to attribute feelings to a machine that flinches when struck. So my question is this: if the science cannot settle the matter, isn't it arbitrary to single out these animals for protection? And if it is not arbitrary, what is actually doing the work — is it that sentience is not all-or-nothing, that the burden of proof runs the other way, or that protection does not in fact require sentience at all? I would rather be told where the test goes wrong than be helped to apply it. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write an email.
