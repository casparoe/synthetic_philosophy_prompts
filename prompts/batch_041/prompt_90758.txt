I have been reading Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute alongside David Lynch's The Straight Story, and I would like a serious critical reading that takes both ends of that pairing seriously rather than using one as wallpaper for the other. Let me set out the Ciceronian structure as I understand it so you can hold me to it, and correct me if I have mis-set the text.

Cicero's dialogue, dedicated to Atticus, puts the defense of old age in the mouth of Cato the Elder at eighty-four, with Scipio Aemilianus and Laelius listening. Cato answers four standing complaints: that old age withdraws a man from active affairs; that it weakens the body; that it takes away nearly all the pleasures; and that it is not far from death. His replies run roughly as follows. Great affairs are carried on by counsel, judgment, and authority rather than by bodily strength, so old age withdraws a man only from what he should be glad to be rid of, and the old man who is still consulted is still at the helm. The body's decline is to be managed rather than obeyed, and the mind decays only if it is left unexercised; Cato cites his own late study of Greek letters as evidence that the mind can still be stocked in old age. The loss of the coarser pleasures is a liberation rather than a deprivation, and what remains, chiefly conversation and the pleasures of the mind, is the better part; the banquet is worth attending for the talk, not the wine. Death is either the end, in which case it is no more to be feared than sleep, or a passage to a better state, in which case it is to be welcomed; either way it is not a thing for an old man to whimper about. I take the argumentative spine to be a distinction between the goods aging removes and the goods it cannot remove, plus a claim that the authority of old age is earned by having lived well, so that contempt for the old is a sign of the old person's failure rather than of aging itself. I also take it that the dialogue is not a neutral report: Cicero wrote it in his early sixties, in political eclipse and not long after the death of his daughter, and Cato is a mask he is plainly speaking through. I mention this because the reading I want has to be willing to ask whether the whole consolation is a piece of motivated reasoning that Cicero is running on himself.

Now the film. Alvin Straight, in his seventies and too infirm to hold a driver's license, learns that his estranged brother Lyle has had a stroke, and travels roughly 240 miles from Iowa to Wisconsin on a riding lawn mower, first a failing one and then a 1966 John Deere, at about five miles an hour. He declines a ride from a man who offers to drive him the rest of the way. He is a sober alcoholic and a veteran; he has lost half his children; his daughter Rose has lost custody of hers after a fire. On the road he talks with a pregnant runaway, with a group of cyclists, with a woman who has just killed a deer with her car and who cannot stop weeping about it, with a fellow veteran to whom he confesses a friendly-fire killing and the drinking that followed, and with a priest who tells him that Lyle never mentioned having a brother. Alvin arrives, drinks his first beer in years, the tractor stalls just short of the cabin, and he and Lyle sit on the porch and look at the stars without saying much.

Here is what I want from the reading, and I would rather have the honest complications than a tidy verdict.

First: is Alvin's journey a Ciceronian performance or a Ciceronian refutation? He refuses the ride, which looks to me like a precommitment device of the kind the blogs I read would recognize: he makes the journey slow and irreversible so that he cannot arrive without having done the thing on his own terms, and so that he has time to think on the way. Is that Cato's point that the great affair is carried on by counsel rather than by strength, or is it a piece of stubbornness that Cicero's framework would have to call vanity, the very thing Alvin later names as one cause of the estrangement? I want you to say which reading the film actually supports, scene by scene, and where the two readings come apart.

Second: the four complaints, one at a time. Take the withdrawal-from-affairs complaint. Alvin's "affairs" are not senatorial; they are a family rupture and a debt of presence owed to a brother. Does the film broaden the Ciceronian notion of res gerendae in a way that strengthens the defense of old age, or does it expose the defense as having quietly presupposed a public role all along, so that for a man with no office and no audience, the consolation is thinner than Cicero lets on? Take the body complaint. The mower at five miles an hour is a physical analogue of the failing body, and Alvin's repairs and bargains are a kind of management rather than obedience. But the film also lets the machine fail, twice, and lets other people rescue him; does that undercut the self-sufficiency Cato claims, or is accepting help the more honest version of it? Take the pleasures complaint. Alvin is a recovering alcoholic, and his relation to pleasure is not Ciceronian tranquility but something more fragile and more earned; the single beer at the end is not a renunciation-as-liberation, and I want you to say what it is instead. Take the death complaint. Alvin is close to death, has buried children, and has killed a man by accident. Cato's either-sleep-or-passage dilemma seems designed to make death indifferent; does the film show a man for whom death is indifferent, or a man for whom the relevant thing is not death but the unfinished business that death would freeze? The scene with the deer seems to me central here, and I want to know what you make of Alvin cooking and eating the animal the woman cannot stop mourning.

Third: the ending, and the question I care most about. Cicero's defense of old age is delivered as a speech; the whole dialogue is a rhetorical performance, and Cato's authority is the authority of the man who can still give an account of himself. The film ends with two old men on a porch, saying almost nothing, and the arrival itself is the entire content of the act. Does the film therefore dramatize something the Ciceronian defense misses, namely that what old age owes and what it can give is presence rather than argument, and that the consolations are for the consoler? Or is the silence at the end itself a Ciceronian move, the old man's refusal to whimper, which the dialogue's rhetoric only appears to replace? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of the four complaints, and it should not soften Cicero into a therapist of the porch. Where the film resists the Ciceronian frame, say so plainly, and where Cicero has a resource the film's silence does not, say that too.

Fourth, and shorter: the question of respect. Cato seems to hold that old age is respected when it deserves to be, and that contempt for the old reflects the old person's failure. The film's America is almost uniformly deferential to Alvin; even the mechanics who overcharge him are bickering rather than contemptuous, and he bargains them down. Is that a real feature of the world the film depicts, or a premise the film has to posit in order to make the journey possible, and does the Ciceronian account of earned authority survive once the audience is not a senate but a roadside?

Finally, tell me what the pairing adds that neither text supplies alone. If the film gets something right about aging that Cicero's four replies cannot reach, name it. If Cicero has a distinction the film's reticence cannot make, name that. I am not looking for a verdict on which is wiser; I am looking for the residue that each leaves in the other.
