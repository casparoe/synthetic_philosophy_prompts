Ok so I've been trying to get my head around this moral equality of combatants idea, and I think I must be missing something, because it looks like a fuss over nothing. The way I read it, it's basically just the claim that every person has equal moral worth — a soldier on one side counts for exactly as much as a soldier on the other, no more, no less. But thats true of everyone, soldier or civilian, so it's just the ordinary equality of persons. Why would anyone write books defending that? And why would it be about combatants specifically — if it's equal moral worth, the combatant part is doing no work at all.

Heres the case that started me thinking. A conscript in an invading army is ordered to hold a village; a soldier from the country being invaded is trying to take it back; they shoot at each other and the invader kills the defender. My gut says the defender is doing something better — hes defending his home — and the invader is doing something wrong, even though he didn't choose the war and maybe even hates it. But I keep being told the moral equality of combatants says the two are on a par. On a par in what sense? Both equally in the right? Both equally in the wrong? Or just both equally allowed to shoot at each other? And if it's that last one, isn't it just the claim that the laws of war apply to both sides, like the rules of a sport apply to both teams — which is true of any rulebook and seems to have nothing to do with morality? Someone also told me the whole thing hinges on a difference between being liable to be killed and being to blame for something, and that once you see that the puzzle dissolves, but I couldn't follow it. So which is it: is there a real moral thesis hiding in here, or is the whole thing either trivially true or just a convention about rules?
