I'm a science and ideas reporter at a general-interest magazine, two thousand words into a feature about how public institutions make decisions whose risks they cannot put numbers on. My case study is a state health authority that has decided to keep recommending an older treatment, whose complication rate is well measured, over a newer one whose long-term profile nobody can yet quantify; the officials defending the decision keep saying that it is better to stick with the devil you know. When I put this to a researcher who wants the authority to switch, she agreed with the proverb and turned it around on me — the incumbent is the devil we know, she said, and it is killing a known number of people every year — which left my editor asking, reasonably enough, whether the phrase is doing any work at all. I dimly remember the Ellsberg urn experiments from college, in which people prefer to bet on the urn whose mix is stated over the one whose mix is not, and prefer it for either colour, which is hard to square with the thought that the second urn simply has odds we happen not to know; a source tells me the live dispute is whether that preference is a bias to be corrected or a defensible policy, and that it matters a great deal whose ignorance we mean — the regulator's, the patient's, or the public's. Please lay out for me the distinct claims that 'better the devil you know than the devil you don't' could be making, from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, defensible, or false — and tell me which readings, if any, would license the authority's decision, which the researcher's reversal depends on, and which one the officials most likely mean.
