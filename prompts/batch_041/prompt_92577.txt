I am building a teaching instrument for a graduate seminar on historical explanation, and I want a decision tree that carries a student from a handful of commitments about how individual action relates to large historical outcomes to a named position in the Great Man versus structural-explanation debate. The tree's running example will be the counterfactual question of whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, but it must generalize beyond that case, and I want the branches constructed from the logical space of the debate rather than from a roll-call of famous names.

Each branch node should be phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Please build the tree around at least the following distinctions, stated concretely:

- What becomes of the surrounding conditions when the individual is subtracted: are they held fixed, so that the outcome vanishes, or allowed to re-equilibrate, so that a substitute steps into the role?
- Whether the individual's contribution is a trigger (occasion, timing, coordination of forces already present) or an author (the content and direction of the outcome), and whether the student permits these two to come apart.
- A substitutability gradient — replace the individual with (a) a near-double sharing the same dispositions but a different identity, (b) a competent but ordinary contemporary, (c) a rival with opposite aims, (d) no one at all — and which substitutions preserve the outcome.
- Whether the individual's removal merely displaces an outcome that would have arrived anyway or diverts history onto a path it could not otherwise have reached.
- Whether structure determines the set of possible outcomes, only their relative probabilities, or neither.

The leaves should be named positions — Great Man theory, structural determinism, and whatever intermediate views the space genuinely supports, such as the individual selecting among structurally available options or shifting their probabilities — each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate rather than pretending it can. Identify the combinations of answers that no one appears to occupy, and give a one-line diagnosis of whether each is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it.

Keep the tree to six to nine branch nodes and no more than ten leaves. Write every node as a question rather than a label, and make sure that a student who answers honestly is sorted by what they say about the cases, not by which school they already prefer.
