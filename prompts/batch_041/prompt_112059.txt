I spent thirty-five years designing and building machines, and since retiring I have been reading Marx, so I want to put a puzzle to you about a piece of workplace practice I know at first hand, and about what it must be assuming.

When a firm announces that a line will be automated, the negotiation that follows is invariably about terms: severance, retraining, early retirement, a share of the productivity gains. No one at the table proposes that the workforce should decide whether the machines are installed, how they are designed, or at what pace they run. The very people who insist that the technology is neutral, and that only its use is at issue, also insist that the firm has no real choice, since a competitor will automate if it does not. The displaced hands are compensated; the machinery itself is never contested. In Marx's notebooks of 1857–58 there is a long passage, usually called the fragment on machines, in which he imagines production in which scientific knowledge rather than labour time has become the principal productive force; he writes that "the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time", and argues that capital is thereby caught in a contradiction, since it goes on measuring wealth by the labour time it is displacing.

So: what theory of technology does the bargaining practice presuppose — that machines are instruments whose effects are fixed by their design and whose benefits are distributable, that technical change is an exogenous force arriving from outside the social relation, or something else again? Which elements of the practice strictly require such a view, and which merely sit comfortably with it? Can the practice be squared with Marx's own account, or does his account rule it out? If his account is right, what would the negotiation have to become — what would workers have to control, and at what point in the design of a machine would the contest have to begin? And does Marx's position itself hold together, or does his talk of machinery degrading and displacing labour quietly smuggle back in the intrinsic-effects view he denies?
