I'm in a reading group on Athenian democracy — eight of us, mostly classicists plus one philosopher who keeps derailing us into Plato — and we did the Mytilene debate last week. What's stuck with me isn't Cleon, it's the sequel: the assembly votes one day to kill every grown man on the island and sell the women and children, then the very next morning votes the other way, and the only thing standing between the first decree and the second is another trireme rowing like mad to overtake the first. The second vote is close as well — the show of hands is nearly equal — and Diodotus carries it without ever pleading for mercy; he says outright that hes not arguing on the Mytileneans behalf at all, but for Athenian advantage. I want to close the session with a poem about that, and I want the form to carry the point rather than the commentary.

So: a palinode. Two halves, twelve lines each, no fixed metre, and the second half has to take the first one back — but the retraction mustn't be allowed to erase anything. The first half stays on the page; that's the whole point. Have the second half reuse the first half's line-endings in reverse order, so the recantation is visibly parasitic on what it recants: same words, opposite verdict. And in the second half I want no compassion vocabulary at all — no mercy, no pity, no forgive, no shame, no humanity. It has to win the way Diodotus wins, on interest: that the men already condemned are past deterring, that the many aren't the few, that killing a city's commons is a bad bargain for an empire that lives off it. Someone who's read book three should hear Cleon in the first half and Diodotus in the second without me labelling either, and someone who hasn't should still be able to follow the turn.

For the ending — the first ship apparently made no haste, the errand being so horrid, while the second crew rowed through the night eating barley-cakes kneaded with oil and wine and sleeping only by turns, having been promised great things if they arrived in time. End on the rowers, not on the saved city. Not a hymn to democracy catching its own mistake, and not a sneer at it either: I want a reader to feel that a democracy can reverse itself and still be the same body that gave the order, and that what saved Mytilene was not a change of heart but a boat. If the last line can land on the decree being read out and only just interrupted, perfect. It doesnt need to rhyme, but it does need to be accurate enough that someone who knows the debate won't wince, and readable enough that the philosopher stops derailing us for five minutes.
