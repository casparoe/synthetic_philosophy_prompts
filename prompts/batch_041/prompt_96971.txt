So I keep running into the same thing when I teach the Lisbon earthquake: everyone treats it as the moment optimism died. Voltaire writes the poem about the disaster, then Candide a few years later, and the story everyone tells is that Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds line got flattened by thirty-odd thousand dead in a morning. But when I actually make students read what Leibniz claims, rather than the Pangloss puppet, they can't tell me what the earthquake is supposed to refute. So I want to build a case analysis around this.

The episode: 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day, Lisbon. Earthquake around mid-morning, then a tsunami, then fires that burned for days because of all the candles lit in churches for the holiday. Something like 30 to 50 thousand dead in the city and more in Morocco. Pombal, the minister, runs the recovery: he sends a questionnaire to every parish asking what time the shock came, how long it lasted, which way buildings fell, how far the sea rose, how many died — basically the first large-scale empirical study of an earthquake. And the rebuild uses a design with an internal wooden cage frame inside the masonry so the walls can flex instead of collapsing. Meanwhile Rousseau writes back to Voltaire and says, roughly, that the disaster wasn't nature's fault so much as ours: people died because they crowded into tall houses in a city, and they wouldn't have died if they'd been scattered in the woods like they were meant to be.

What I want: analyze this whole thing through Leibniz's actual doctrine of the best of all possible worlds. Not the bumper sticker — the real machinery. The claim that God's choice is constrained by composability, that this world is the one with the greatest variety plus order, that evil is permitted because a world with certain goods requires it, that physical evil follows from general laws of nature operating uniformly rather than from special interventions. Map the episode onto that.

Concretely: under the doctrine, what exactly is the earthquake? Is it metaphysical evil, physical evil, or does the doctrine just not have a slot for a specific event? What does the theory predict about how God would relate to a single catastrophe — and is the answer that the theory was never about single events at all, which would make Voltaire's poem a category error rather than a refutation? Then where does it actually break. I suspect the real pressure point isn't the death toll but the candles — the fact that a liturgical calendar plus a building code produced the firestorm, i.e. that the specific harm is downstream of contingent human conventions that God also had to choose when he chose this world. Say whether that's a genuine problem or just the general problem of evil in a new costume.

Then the other direction: what does the episode show about the doctrine? Pombal's questionnaire and the caged buildings look like a practical answer — don't adjudicate God's reasons, engineer the failure mode out — and Rousseau's reply looks like a third position that's neither Leibniz nor Voltaire, since it locates the evil in human settlement choices. Does either of those constitute an unanticipated case for the theory, a way of dodging it, or an implicit admission that the theodicy question was malformed from the start?

Be disciplined about the mapping. Tell me who the players are, what the doctrine says they faced, what it explains, and where it goes silent instead of forcing it to talk. And at the end I want a verdict: was Lisbon a refutation of Leibniz, a refutation of a caricature that was never his, or a case that shows the doctrine was never the kind of thing an earthquake could touch.
