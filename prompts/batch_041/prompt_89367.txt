For my school's philosophy club, I am preparing a presentation on the view that consciousness is a kind of user illusion. As I understand it, the claim is that the felt sense of being a single, unified self that consciously authors its own thoughts and decisions is not a transparent report of what the brain is actually doing, but a simplified summary the brain generates for its own use, much as the icons on a computer screen summarize processes in the hardware without resembling them. I also want to bring in a distinction that I think is often blurred. On one reading, the illusion is a misrepresentation that occurs within consciousness, so experiences are real but systematically misrepresent what they are experiences of. On the other reading, the illusion is all there is, so there is no consciousness to be misrepresented, only the brain's disposition to think and say that there is. Please describe the most important implications of this view, separating the two readings wherever the implications differ, for how seriously a school like mine should take a student's sincere first-person report of their own mental life. Two cases I keep thinking about are a student accused of plagiarism who insists that he did not realize he was doing anything wrong, and a friend who explains a cruel remark by saying that he never meant it that way. I would like the implications ordered by how much they should actually change what the school does, and I would appreciate it if you would flag any that only look important because of a confusion.
