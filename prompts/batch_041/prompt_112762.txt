I am a novelist, eight months into a book whose entire technical problem is a single scene: four people in one room on a wet afternoon in 1974, and four flatly incompatible accounts of what happened there. I want the reader to finish the chapter unable to say that any of the four is simply lying or simply mistaken, and equally unable to conclude that the afternoon itself was a blank and that everyone has their own private truth. The ordinary tools of point of view — limited focalization, free indirect discourse, a controlled dose of dramatic irony, one narrator I have deliberately made unreliable — keep giving me the soft version of the effect and not the hard one. A friend who reads Indian philosophy tells me there is a tradition that spent centuries on precisely this, and that its answer is stranger than tolerance: that a single object has indefinitely many aspects; that any assertion about it must be made with a qualification, roughly that it holds in some respect; that there are standpoints, catalogued and even ranked, from which partial and mutually incompatible descriptions are each legitimate; that there is a seven-fold scheme running from it is, through it is not and it both is and is not, on to it is inexpressible, with the combinations; and that there is a four-fold way of unpacking what a word may mean when we use it. My grasp of all this is second-hand and I assume it is partly wrong. Before anything else, correct my gloss where it needs correcting, briefly, and without footnotes or a reading list.

Then the work I actually want. Translate that apparatus into the vocabulary I think in when I am deciding a scene, which is not the vocabulary of theory but of craft: who is seeing this and what the sentence lets the reader know; what a character can be right about and what the book must keep from her; the difference between a character's belief and the world of the book; what an omniscient sentence forecloses that a limited one holds open; the difference between irony and contradiction; what it means for a voice to be mine, hers, or neither. Go term by term where you can, and where you cannot, say so plainly rather than gesturing. I will know you are cheating if you translate the doctrine into a general lesson about tolerance or the limits of knowledge.

Then the part I care most about: the residue report. What crosses over cleanly into craft decisions I could actually make on a page; what crosses only if one side or the other is distorted, and which side bends; and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I suspect the real break is this — that my multiplicity is epistemic and psychological, four finite people each knowing a part, whereas the doctrine's multiplicity is a claim about the object itself — and if that is right I want to know what follows for the chapter, not merely that the analogy fails. Say too where my ordinary devices are already doing the doctrine's work under another name, and where the doctrine would forbid something I do routinely and unrepentantly, such as a sentence whose irony quietly tells the reader that this character is simply wrong. Finish with a verdict, and be decisive about it: is the residue a defect of the novelist's vocabulary, something craft cannot express, so that I should stop trying — or a sign that the original doctrine is doing a kind of work the novel has no need of? Give the residue as a list I can paste into my notes.
