Ok, I've been down a rabbit hole on the two-envelope problem and I've come out with a position I'm definately too attached to. I want you to try to break it.

Here it is. The two-envelope "paradox" isn't a paradox and isn't deep. You're shown two envelopes, one contains twice what the other does, you open yours and see $10, you reason "the other one is either $20 or $5, equally likely, so it's worth $12.50, I should switch", and then you could run the same reasoning again on whatever you end up holding. I think that's just a botched conditional probability. The mistake is treating "the other is 2x or x/2" as equiprobable *after* you've already seen x -- the probabilities of those two cases are fixed by whatever prior you had over the amounts, and once you insist on a proper (normalisable) prior, there's no amount you could open at which switching is guaranteed to be the better move. So: no paradox, and no real lesson for decision theory beyond "be careful with conditionals". Anyone who thinks it shows something about expected-value maximisation is chasing a ghost.
