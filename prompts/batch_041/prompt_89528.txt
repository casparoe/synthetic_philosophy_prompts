I'm the product lead for a small habit-and-character app (working title: Errata), and our design discussions keep turning into an argument about Benjamin Franklin. I've been rereading his account of the project of arriving at moral perfection: the list of virtues with a short precept attached to each, the little book with a page per virtue and red-ink marks entered for each fault, the plan of working on one virtue at a time rather than all at once, and his frank admission that the virtue of Order was the one that defeated him. Half my team thinks we are finally building the instrument he wanted and never had; the other half thinks we are building precisely the thing he would have distrusted. I want a careful prediction of what Franklin would actually say about our build, not a pep talk about self-improvement.

Here is what the current version does. (1) It scores the user every day on a fixed list of virtues, and the score decays if a day is skipped. (2) It includes a leaderboard and a public ledger, so users can post their marks and watch friends' streaks. (3) It includes an LLM confessor that takes a nightly account of the day and assigns a small penance. (4) It sells anonymized virtue data to insurers and employers, and offers a Character Index that employers can pull on candidates. (5) It lets users pay a stranger to keep the tracking and the weekly review for them. (6) It computes a Humility metric from how often the user reports having acted humbly. For each of the six, I want a verdict — he would endorse it, he would reject it, or he would accept it with a specific modification — with the reasoning reconstructed from his stated method and from what he actually did and failed to do, rather than from the general reputation of the man. Then tell me which of his own commitments our design strains hardest, and whether the strain lies in the design or in Franklin.

I would also like your view on two matters he never faced. The first is direct intervention on character — drugs, neurostimulation, or other means of making oneself temperate or industrious without the daily bookkeeping; would he class that as cheating, or as continuous with the small instrumental tricks he himself used? The second is the possibility, which I take seriously, that measuring and publicly committing to a virtue changes the behavior being measured and sometimes licenses the opposite behavior afterward; would Franklin have treated that as a fatal objection to the whole project or as one more erratum to be entered and corrected? Please state how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point in the entire answer you are least sure of, saying what evidence or argument would move you.
