I serve on the disciplinary panel of a state board of professional engineers. We are hearing a case against a licensed engineer who approved a foundation design using a standard spreadsheet. The spreadsheet's own output, combined with a soil report the engineer had read, entailed that one load case was understrength. The engineer did not notice, and the flaw was caught before occupancy. Our rules permit discipline for what a licensee knew or should have known. The board chair proposes what he calls the derivability test: if a conclusion follows from what the engineer knew by steps a competent engineer could perform, then the engineer knew it, whether or not he ever performed them. The defense says that standard would make every engineer responsible for every consequence of everything they have read, and would turn the board into an oracle. I have been reading about the problem of logical omniscience, and I think it bears directly on the test we adopt. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the hearing. It should recommend a concrete disposition of this case—suspend his license, fine him, or dismiss the complaint—and propose a workable test for should have known that the board can apply later, one that does not assume people see all the consequences of what they know but also does not excuse inferences a competent practitioner would actually draw. Give the strongest argument for the chair's position its due, and say what the test cannot settle. I do not need a literature review or citations; I need something the panel can act on.
