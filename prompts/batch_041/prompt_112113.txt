I am a novelist, and I think in the vocabulary of my trade: scene and summary; point of view and focalization; free indirect discourse; the gap between the story-world and the telling of it; unreliable narration; dramatic irony; the neutral description that a director can shoot without ever deciding what is really there; and a practitioner's feel for exactly how much a reader can be trusted to infer at any given moment. I have read enough philosophy to follow an argument but not to referee one, so I am asking you to translate rather than to adjudicate.

I am at work on a novel in which a great deal turns on whether a character truly saw what she says she saw, and I keep circling a much older dispute: the argument that because a hallucination can be indistinguishable, from the subject's own vantage, from a genuine perception, the two must share a common core; and the opposing claim that they are fundamentally different kinds of occurrence which merely happen to be indistinguishable. Restate the argument and the reply in the vocabulary I have just given you, as faithfully as you can manage, as though explaining in craft terms what each side is actually claiming about the relation between a scene and the page on which it is written.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate come through intact? I suspect that "indistinguishable from the inside" will arrive as nothing more than "indistinguishable to the reader," and I want to know whether that is a distortion or a fair rendering. Which parts arrive maimed? Which have no expression in my vocabulary at all — the phenomenal character of experience, the first-personal, the subject's warrant or entitlement are my guesses, but I would rather be told than confirmed. And take as test cases the scene I deliberately refuse to settle, which functions whether or not the figure was there, and the scene whose status I reverse in a later draft: can the second view accommodate either, or must it call them failures to specify?

Finally, tell me where the residue points. Is my vocabulary simply missing something the philosophers possess — a way of speaking about experience from the inside that narrative craft cannot supply — or does the residue suggest the quarrel is thinner than it appears, and that what they call two kinds of state I would call one scene with two possible ontologies, distinguished only by facts outside the text? A translation that smuggles my assumptions in and calls them equivalents is worth nothing to me. I want no names, no dates, and no reading list; I want the shape of the disagreement, and an honest account of what my own terms cannot carry.
