"Molina thinks there are three stages in God's knowledge. First, natural knowledge: God knows all necessary truths and everything that is possible — basically what any creature could do. Second, middle knowledge: God knows what any free creature would freely do in any set of circumstances he might place it in. Third, free knowledge: God knows what actually happens, which he knows because he decided to create this particular world. Middle knowledge is called 'middle' because it sits in between the other two — it isn't about what's merely possible (that's natural knowledge) and it isn't about what's actual (that's free knowledge), it's about what's conditional.

The point of all this is to protect providence and freedom at the same time. God wants to bring about certain things — say, that a certain person ends up in a certain city — but he doesn't want to drag them there against their will. With middle knowledge he can survey all the possible circumstances and see which ones a free creature would respond to in the way he wants, and then arrange for those circumstances to obtain. So God gets his result and the creature still acted freely, because God didn't cause the choice, he just knew it in advance. It's a bit like knowing your friend will order the fish no matter which restaurant you pick — you can steer the evening without forcing them.

Middle knowledge is logically prior to God's decree to create, though of course God only knows these counterfactuals because he has decided which world he's going to actualize.

The main objection is the grounding objection: if God knows from eternity exactly what I'm going to do, then I can't do otherwise, so I'm not really free, and middle knowledge just relabels foreknowledge. Molina's answer is that knowledge isn't causation — God's knowing that I'll do X doesn't make me do X, any more than my knowing the sun will rise makes it rise.

There's a related worry about what makes the counterfactual true in the first place. God's natural knowledge is grounded in necessary truths and his free knowledge is grounded in his own decree, but what grounds the counterfactuals of freedom? Not the creature's nature, because then the choice would just follow from what the creature is, and it wouldn't be free. Not God's will either, because then God would be determining the choice, and again it wouldn't be free. So the counterfactuals seem to float free of anything that could make them true, and critics say the Molinist is stuck with brute, ungrounded truths. I think Molina's move here is to say the counterfactuals don't have a truth value until the creature actually acts — that way there's nothing that needs grounding ahead of time. Some defenders also say the counterfactual is grounded in the free act itself, i.e. in what the creature does in that possible world, which isn't part of the actual world at all, so demanding a ground for it is a kind of category mistake."
