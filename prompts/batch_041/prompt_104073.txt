I'm a PhD student working on notation and type–token identity, and I'm writing a seminar paper that reads The Scarlet Letter through the question of what makes a letterform a letterform: what must every 'a' share, across typefaces, handwriting, and embroidery, to still count as an 'a'? Is graphemic identity fixed by a geometric skeleton, by topological features, by a role in a script, or by a community's willingness to recognize the mark? Hawthorne's novel seems to me to stage this problem rather than merely illustrate it. Hester's A is remade in gold thread, glimmers in the sick-chamber as a taper of mercy, is read by the townspeople as meaning Able rather than Adultery, and is finally embodied in Pearl, whom the narrator calls "the scarlet letter in another form; the scarlet letter endowed with life." I want a reading that works in both directions: what the novel shows about how a form persists through changes of material, style, and meaning, and what the letter-theoretic question becomes once the letter is a stigma worn on a body and, in Pearl, a person. Does the A remain the same A when its meaning flips, or when it is alive? Is Pearl a token of the type, an instance that breaks the type, or something the type–token vocabulary cannot handle? And does the novel's refusal to settle whether the mark on Dimmesdale's breast was self-inflicted, magical, or remorse made visible bear on how we identify instances of a form when no authoritative copy exists to compare them against? Please stay close to the novel's scenes and tell me where it resists the framework rather than applying it tidily.
