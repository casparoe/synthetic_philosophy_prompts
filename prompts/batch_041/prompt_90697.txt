I'm a doctoral candidate in comparative philosophy, and I'm writing a chapter that treats a single film as a case study rather than rehearsing the pramāṇas in the abstract yet again. I want you to read Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon through the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas, and to pitch the reading for someone who already knows the textbook list -- perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogy (upamāna) and verbal testimony (śabda) -- and would be bored by a recital of it. What I need is a genuinely philosophical reading of the film, not a plot summary with Sanskrit tags attached.

The film gives us four mutually inconsistent accounts of the same killing, each delivered by a witness with an evident interest in the telling, and frames them with a woodcutter's supposed direct perception and a priest's scandalised reception of what he hears. Treat those accounts as candidate acts of śabda and press the following questions. First, does the film honour the Nyāya requirement that testimony count as a pramāṇa only when it issues from an āpta, a competent and trustworthy speaker, or does it show that requirement to be either empty or impossible to apply, given that every speaker here is compromised? Second, since Nyāya is a parataḥ prāmāṇya school -- it holds that a cognition's validity is not given with the cognition itself but has to be established from outside, by subsequent confirmation -- does the film dramatise precisely that predicament, testimony that cannot certify itself and must wait on something else to be believed? Or does it instead presuppose the Mīmāṃsā alternative, on which each report arrives prima facie credible until some specific defeater is produced? Third, I'm inclined to argue that the film is at bottom about the priority of pratyakṣa over śabda, because the woodcutter's frame is offered as the perceptual ground meant to adjudicate the reports; tell me whether the film sustains that reading or quietly undercuts it, since the woodcutter's own account is not obviously disinterested either. And fourth, I want to know where the film resists the Nyāya framework altogether -- the honest critic has to say where the categories stop earning their keep.

I don't want a survey of Nyāya or of the film's reception. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that neither reduces the film to an illustration of the categories nor bends the categories to fit the film, and that ends by saying what the film shows about testimony that the Nyāya texts, with their tidy āpta condition, do not. Please state how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Write in British English.
