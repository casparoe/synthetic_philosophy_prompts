I convene a fortnightly reading group for non-specialists — a dozen lawyers and physicians, no formal training in logic or in ethics — and the next ninety-minute session is mine to run on a single question: whether "better than" is transitive, and what the family of arguments usually called spectrum arguments does to that question. I do not want you to give the explanation; I want you to plan the explanation. Assume a whiteboard, no slides, and no assigned reading — nobody in the room will have heard of any particular philosopher or work, and I do not want the session to depend on names, citations, or anyone having read anything.

Plan it as follows.

First, the order of ideas, stated explicitly and with the dependency made plain: which component must be in place before which, and why the later one presupposes the earlier. I take it that the bare notion of a two-place relation and the property of transitivity has to be fixed on a non-evaluative example before any of it can be applied to betterness, but I want you to tell me where exactly the pivot from the logical point to the practical one should come, and what that pivot requires.

Second, the one example that carries the whole session. I have a candidate and I want you to build on it: a studio is producing a single piece of music in ten versions. Version 1 is thirty seconds of extraordinary beauty. Version 10 is many hours of music that is only faintly pleasant. Each version is somewhat longer and somewhat less beautiful than the last, in increments small enough that every version seems a little better than its predecessor — you are plainly getting more music for a barely perceptible loss. Yet no one wants to say that version 10 is better than version 1. Tell me what this example must have and must lack for it to do the work, whether my version satisfies that, and what a *bad* carrying example would look like (in particular, one whose steps are obviously tied or whose endpoint is not genuinely resisted).

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses, which you should correct or extend: that intransitivity just means the items cannot be compared at all; that the puzzle is only the sorites paradox about a vague cutoff and dissolves once you sharpen the boundary; that if transitivity fails then rational choice collapses entirely; and that the transitivity of "better than" is a piece of pure logic that could not be denied. I also want the group to end able to distinguish the spectrum argument, which threatens transitivity, from the small-improvement argument, which threatens something else — completeness — so tell me where that contrast has to be inserted and why it cannot come earlier.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question or task at the very end that would show me the group has grasped the structure of the problem rather than merely felt its pull.

Finally, what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so — the technical machinery, the population-ethics material it is often confused with, whatever else you judge out of reach in ninety minutes.
