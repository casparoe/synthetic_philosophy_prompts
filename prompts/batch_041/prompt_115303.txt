Spinoza's political writing has gotten me into arguments with my housing co-op. Two claims I think I've got right: a commonwealth's right just is its power, so it has authority over me exactly to the extent that it can actually get me to do and say things, with no extra layer of authority sitting above the enforcement; and the one thing nobody can hand over is their own judgment, since you can be made to say and do things but never actually made to believe them. That's why he calls democracy the most natural form of state, and why he thinks a regime that only gets compliance through fear is barely a commonwealth at all. He also says a free person isn't the one who does whatever they want but the one who acts from their own nature, so obeying a rule you'd have chosen anyway doesn't count as unfreedom.

So: next week we're voting on a bylaw that would require every member to publicly endorse the board's position in a rezoning fight, with fines for members who speak against it in public. What are the most important implications of Spinoza's views for how I should think about this? Is the bylaw even coherent, what happens to the co-op's authority if it passes and turns out to be unforceable, and what does it tell me about where the co-op's real power actually lives? And is there any Spinozist reason to prefer our endless general assembly to a competent board that just decides?
