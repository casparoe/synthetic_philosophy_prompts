ok, so I've been chewing on Hofstadter's Platonia thing — twenty people secretly invited, each can wire in their name, if exactly one name comes in that person gets the billion, if two or more come in nobody gets anything, and everyone knows the others are the same kind of reasoner they are. I want a new case in that family, built to order, and I want it to earn its keep. Don't just relabel the prisoner's dilemma.

Here's the spec. Build me a lottery where:

- entering is weakly dominant in the ordinary sense — hold fixed whatever everyone else does, and you do at least as well by entering as by staying out, and strictly better in at least one of those fixed-others cases. That's how the original works, and it's the part I want to keep.
- the superrational / identical-reasoning verdict is determinate. My reading of the original is that 'everyone enters' and 'everyone stays out' tie at zero and the same-answer assumption doesn't pick between them, which bugs me. In your case I want the identical-reasoning assumption to actually select an answer, and ideally not a pure one — I want it to force a specific mixed strategy, a probability you can state and defend. If you think a mixed answer is unavailable here, argue that instead, but then explain what breaks.
- the number of players is uncertain to each player, with a known distribution over group sizes, not a known headcount. I want to see whether the same-reasoning move still bites when the group size is itself something you're guessing at.
- no communication, no precommitment, no contracts, no punishment, no side payments, and no 'they're literally the same agent' stipulation — the symmetry has to be epistemic. Each player knows the others reason the same way; that's all they get.
- mundane. Real money, a web form or a stack of envelopes, no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no infinite anything.
- and dont stipulate the verdicts in the setup. The answers have to fall out of the payoff rule.

Then do the work. State the lottery precisely: payoff rule, what each player knows, when they decide, what happens in every outcome class. Then walk through (a) the ordinary dominance/Nash reasoning, (b) the superrational reasoning including the mixed-strategy derivation if you claim one, and (c) what causal decision theory says here and whether it lines up with (a) or (b). I care about (c) a lot — I want to know whether your case actually separates superrationality from CDT, or whether CDT lands in the same place for a different reason. If they can't be separated here, say so.

Then tell me which single feature of the design is load-bearing. If I delete it — make the group size known, or tweak the payoff so staying out is never privately costly — what does the case collapse into? Name the ordinary thing it becomes.

Then steelman the other side: if someone thinks the superrational verdict here is a mistake, what's the strongest line they have, and does your case survive it? Be specific about what they'd have to deny.

Finally: say how confident you are in each main claim — the dominance claim, the superrational verdict, the CDT verdict — and flag the one you're least sure of. If the honest answer is that the mixed-strategy part rests on a modelling choice you had to make, say that rather than smoothing it over.
