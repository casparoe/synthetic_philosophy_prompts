I teach a ninety-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social choice and political philosophy: eighteen students, one room, no devices, and I can print. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of the liberal paradox — the collision between letting each person settle their own business and letting unanimous preferences settle everything else — without my asserting the collision and without their hearing the name of the result until the last ten minutes. Design the session.

They should ratify the rules themselves before anything goes wrong. Set up nine two-person households, each with one contested item in a shared space and one private switch per member. The two rules, which the group votes through by show of hands and which I then write on the board, are these: a member's own switch is theirs alone, so if two outcomes differ only in what that member does, their preference between those outcomes settles it for the household; and if both members strictly prefer one outcome to another, the household prefers it too. Present them as house rules for a game, not as philosophical theses, and do not hint that they will conflict. Each household must then hand me a signed form giving a complete and transitive ranking of the three available outcomes — A's item up with B's down, B's up with A's down, or neither up, since the space holds only one — derived from those two rules and nothing else. I will accept only complete, transitive forms. Engineer the payoff cards so that the form cannot be completed: member A must rank neither-up above only-A-up above only-B-up, and B must rank only-A-up above only-B-up above neither-up. Invent a case in which those rankings are psychologically credible, so that no one can wriggle out by saying they would never have such preferences, and avoid the stock example of a book that one person wants to read and the other does not. Attach a small real stake, not money, to which outcome a household ends up with; keep both members' rankings strict, and tell me what to do when a household dissolves its own case by declaring that one member does not really care.

Then run an amendment round, under two constraints: a proposed rule must be one the proposers would accept without knowing which household they would be in, and it must apply to every household. Prepare a stress household for each repair they will reach for — one showing what dropping the private-switch rule costs; one showing what dropping unanimity costs, where I want them to land on an outcome both members rank below another one that is on the table; one showing what happens when the private-switch rule is confined to preferences that do not concern the other member's switch, where I want them to discover that the form comes out incomplete, so the household still cannot answer and something outside both rules has to close the gap; and one showing what happens when they keep both rules but give up transitivity, so that whoever chairs the order of pairwise comparisons controls the outcome. Add a variant in which a household implements its decision by a sequence of moves instead of by ranking outcomes — A first, B first, or simultaneous — so they can see that the choice among those is itself a collective decision with a distributional edge. I want rules, materials, a timeline, the exact text of the cards and the stress cases, the facilitator's moves for the amendment round, and the moment at which the forms are collected and held up.

The debrief should map what happened onto the demands the household procedure was trying to meet, and leave each student able to say which demand they personally gave up and why giving it up was not free. Name the result once, at that point, with no citations or reading list anywhere in the session. Be honest about what the activity cannot show: nine households demonstrate that one construction defeats the charter, not that every construction must fail on every profile, and a student who insists that nobody really has the card's preferences has not been refuted by anything they experienced — they have proposed restricting which profiles the charter must handle, and that proposal has a price the stress cases should let them feel. Tell me how to say this so that it sharpens the lesson rather than deflating it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, where the amendment round will overrun, and which repairs the students will try to smuggle in without owning them.
