I'm not a philosopher (one ethics class in college, that's it), but a couple months ago I read a long piece about how much suffering there is in the wild — how many animals die young and die badly, and how basically nobody is doing anything about it because that's just what nature does. I don't even know what I think about intervening. What I can't stop noticing is something else, in how people talk. I've had this conversation maybe fiteen times now and it runs the same script. I describe one animal: a fawn that takes three days to starve, a bird with a broken wing that gets eaten alive by ants while it's still moving. Everyone agrees — yeah, that's awful, that's a terrible thing that happened to that animal. Then I ask whether we should do anything about that kind of thing in general, and something flips. Nobody's talking about the fawn anymore. Now it's "but the ecosystem," "but predators keep the herds healthy," "but if you interfere you'll make it worse for everything down the line," "death feeds life." The same people will say without blinking that of course we should build the wildlife crossing so the deer don't get hit, or cut a whale loose from a net. So it isn't that they're indifferent to animals. It's that the second the question becomes a general one about intervening, the thing they're protecting stops being the animal and becomes the whole system, and the system always wins — but only ever in one direction, only as a reason not to act, never as a reason to act. I've seen a cousin of it elsewhere, like when people say we shouldn't stop a famine because it'll cause overpopulation, and they don't seem to be making any general claim about the right number of people to have.

Is there a name for this? I know environmental ethics has some whole-versus-individual thing going on, and I've heard the phrase about the balance of nature, and maybe this is just the appeal to nature wearing a lab coat. But none of those quite fits, I think — somebody who sincerely thought the system came first would also be against culling elephants so the vegetation could recover, and the people I'm describing aren't, they only reach for the whole when the whole is an argument for doing nothing. That's the part I want carved up. Dosen't this have an established name, or a cluster of near-names, and if so which one is closest and where exactly does it fail? What is the actual structure of the move — is it a real moral position that just happens to get invoked one-sidedly, or is it a rationalization borrowing its force from somewhere else, like the fact that in the story nobody is the one doing the killing? And give me the edges: what would a case look like where shifting your focus from the individual to the system is completely legitimate and not a dodge, and what's the nearest thing that's genuinely a different phenomenon (I keep lumping this in with "well, you can't save everyone," and I have a feeling that's not the same move at all). If nothing established fits, coin something, define it, and tell me what's inside and outside it. American English, and don't soften it — I'd rather have the distinctions than the reassurance.
