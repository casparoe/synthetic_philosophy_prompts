I run a monthly philosophy-in-the-pub night in the back room of a bar near me, and next month it's my turn to lead. The crowd is roughly twenty people: a handful of lapsed undergrad-philosophy types, a lot of curious non-specialists, and a stubborn bloc of engineers and scientists who turn every question into a physics question within four minutes. I get 70 minutes and a flipchart (the bar won't let us put anything on the walls). Topic I've landed on: the identity of indiscernibles, built around Max Black's two-sphere case — the 1952 Mind one, two iron spheres of the same size, colour and temperature, sitting at some distance from each other in a universe that contains nothing else at all. Don't explain the principle to me, I know what it says and I know it's the interesting direction (no two things that differ only in being which things they are) that's the live one, not the boring direction (if it's the same object it obviously can't differ from itself). What I want is a plan for teaching it to this room. What has to come before what: I'm assuming I have to get the boring direction nailed down and visibly *separated* from the interesting one before anything else, but tell me what the other load-bearing pieces are and in what order they have to arrive — where the numerical-versus-qualitative distinction has to land, whether the notion of a relational property needs to be in place before I introduce the spheres or whether I can let the room discover the need for it, and where the possibility question (could there be two such things) has to sit relative to the actuality question (are there in fact any). Which example should carry the session? My plan was to warm up with two identical paperclips from my desk and then move to the spheres, but I can't tell whether that's a useful on-ramp or whether it trains them to hunt for hidden differences and wrecks the spheres when I get there. And what does the carrying case have to have and lack — does the spheres case only work because the universe is empty, or can I leave the bar and the table and the rest of the world in place around them?

The misconception I'm most worried about is the deflating one: they'll hear the principle and think it's just the converse of something obvious, nod, and conclude there's no topic here — so I need to know precisely where in the sequence to head that off. The others I expect: indiscernible read epistemically, as in nobody could ever tell them apart; the objection that I've just stipulated the two spheres are the same thing, so of course they're one object; the engineers announcing that quantum mechanics settled this decades ago because elementary particles are genuinely indistinguishable; someone pointing out that the spheres *do* differ because one of them is the one on the left; and the opposite move, where somebody says the two spheres differ only in being this one rather than that one, which I think might be the actual crux of the whole thing rather than a mistake. Rank those by how likely each is in this specific room, tell me where in the arc to defuse each one, and tell me which of them I should deliberately provoke and let the room fight out instead of shutting down. On that last one especially: do I need to give them the idea of a bare thisness, and can I get it across without handing them a technical word, since the moment I write a term on the flipchart half the room writes it down and stops thinking? I'd rather they feel the pull of it first.

For the ending, I thought I'd test whether it landed by asking them whether it makes sense to say that one of the two spheres has just been destroyed and the other hasn't — is that a decent check or does it just muddy the water, and what would be a better one? Also be blunt about what I should openly refuse to do in 70 minutes with this crowd: I don't want to drift into the taxonomy of weak and strong versions of the principle, or a formalisation, or Leibniz's theology, but tell me if any of those is actually doing load-bearing work for the two-sphere case and I'm wrong to cut it. And finish by telling me the thing I'm most likely to get wrong myself while teaching this — I've definately talked myself into a corner before by being too quick to agree with whoever sounds most confident, and I'd like to know which moment in this session that's going to be.
