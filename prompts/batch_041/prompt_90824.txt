I'm halfway through a health economics masters and I keep running into a philosophy question I can't shake off. The whole field runs on cost-per-QALY: you take a treatment, score the health states it moves people between on a scale running from 0 (dead) to 1 (perfect health, with some versions letting states worse than death go negative), multiply by the years, and out comes a number you compare against a funding threshold. The arithmetic I can follow. What I can't work out is what you have to believe for any of this to be the right way to decide. It seems to assume that my hay-fever pills and your intractable nerve pain are the same kind of stuff, measurable in the same units, so that a big enough heap of trivial improvements can outweigh one person's agony. A friend of mine says that makes me a utilitarian whether I like it or not, then immediately says no, the respectable view is something she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: only suffering is bad in itself, and suffering takes absolute priority until it's off the scene entirely, after which the remaining goodies can be traded off freely against each other. Which is odd, because the system does seem to make exactly that kind of exception. Treatments for the dying get funded at costs that would sink anything else, and severe unrelieved pain is treated as special in a way that mild chronic annoyance never is. So we appear to be running a flat commensurable scale across most of the budget and something like a lexical priority for the worst off in one corner of it. And then there's the disability critique, which says the scale quietly prices some lives lower than others, which I also can't square with the worst-off-first bit.

So: can you reconstruct what this practice actually presupposes? I want the view of pleasure and pain it needs (are they commensurable, and on what kind of scale), what it assumes about comparing and aggregating across different people, and which of those assumptions are strictly required versus which are just packaging the practice happens to come in. Then tell me whether the end-of-life and severe-suffering carve-outs are a genuine inconsistency or whether one coherent theory covers both, and whether the disability objection is a bug in the theory or a bug in how the numbers get picked. Finally, if you swapped the commensurable scale for a lexical threshold view, what changes at the bedside — who gets funded that currently doesn't, and who loses out. One page max, and lead with your bottom line: does the QALY machinery fit with lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, or not?
