My friend and I had a stupidly long arguement about this at a bar on Friday and I want to know whether the whole thing is as confused as it felt.

His claim: blind orchestral auditions — the screen, and whatever else they do so you can't hear shoes on the floor — are basically an admission that perception is cognitively penetrated. The judges literally *hear* a different violinist once they know who's behind the screen. I said that's not what blinding shows at all. It shows the judges' *verdict* is biased, which is perfectly consistent with their hearing being completely unaffected. He said that's a distinction without a difference. I said it's the whole ballgame. Then we both got annoyed, and I realised I couldn't actually say what the practice commits you to.

So take that whole family of practices: blind auditions, blind wine and coffee tasting, double-blind peer review, double-blind drug trials, having a second radiologist read the scan without seeing the first radiologist's report. What does that style of practice presuppose?

The bit I really want nailed down. Blinding works by taking a cue out of the room. My hunch is that means it quietly assumes the bias comes in through the eyes — that the expectation is *triggered by the cue*. But suppose the bias is already sitting in the judge's head, she's absorbed the stereotype that women dont play trombone like that, and that's what's bending what she hears. Then taking the cue out of the room should do nothing at all, because the thing doing the penetrating never left. Is blinding therefore only a fix for one kind of penetration and useless against the other? Or am I overreading the practice?

Related: is the practice committed to the strong claim at all, or is it fully compatible with the old view that perception is a sealed module belief can't get into (encapsulated, or whatever the word is), with all the bias living downstream in judgement? Which views does it actually rule out? Where is it merely underdetermined, so that several theories would license exactly the same practice? And if you swapped out whatever it presupposes for the opposite, what concretely would have to change — would we still blind at all, or would we have to do something else entirely, like debiasing the evaluators, or aggregating across people with different priors?

One more thing that bugs me. If a visible cue corrupts an evaluation badly enough to justify a screen, why do we let jurors stare at the defendant? And why do we talk about a trained taster as a measuring instrument while also accepting that expectation moves what they taste?

Please dont give me a reading list, I'm not in grad school, I just want the commitments spelled out.
