You are a philosopher of mind and a moral philosopher with expertise in the philosophy of animal and artificial minds, and in particular in the question of whether sentience comes in degrees and how a graded property could map onto graded moral status. I would like you to serve as a consistency auditor for a position I have assembled. I care much more about the reasoning than about coverage of the literature, so please think the problem through carefully rather than producing a survey of views.

Before you write anything, brainstorm in a scratch space: work out what each of my commitments commits me to, what each of them rules out, and which of my verdicts about cases would be surprising given the rest. Then write the audit itself.

Here is where I have landed. I hold these six commitments as my considered view.

1. Sentience — the capacity for valenced experience, for states that are good or bad for the creature that has them — is a matter of degree. There is no fact of the matter about a precise cutoff, and the line between the sentient and the non-sentient is drawn by us rather than discovered. Thermostats are not sentient, insects probably are to a low degree, and mammals to a high one, but I do not think that ranking rests on any sharp underlying threshold.

2. The comparisons this graded picture licenses are ordinal, not cardinal. I can rank systems by how sentient they are, but I do not think "twice as sentient" is well defined, and I am suspicious of any proposal to put degrees of sentience on a scale with meaningful ratios.

3. Moral status is graded and monotone in sentience. Other things equal, a more sentient being's welfare counts for more than a less sentient being's, and never the reverse.

4. Moral status is not a smooth function of sentience. Certain capacities — a self-model, the ability to anticipate and dread, the ability to carry projects across time — bring in kinds of interests that a merely sentient system does not have, and the arrival of those interests produces jumps rather than a continuous incline. So I think of the moral landscape as a step function resting on a graded base.

5. Where I am uncertain whether a system is sentient, I want to be cautious in proportion to how likely it is to be sentient and to how much sentience it would have if it were. I do not want to dismiss the possibly-sentient just because the probability is low or the degree would be small.

6. A system's moral status cannot turn on facts that are in principle undetectable. It should supervene on the system's functional and organizational properties — how it is put together and what it does — rather than on some intrinsic fact about it that no possible observer could ever reach.

And here are my verdicts on cases, held with roughly the same confidence as the commitments above:

(a) Factory farming pigs and chickens is seriously wrong.
(b) Grinding up a beetle for no reason is mildly wrong — not nothing, but not a grave matter either.
(c) Killing insects in large numbers for the sake of human health is permissible.
(d) Deleting a thermostat, a bacterium, or a chatbot that insists it suffers raises no moral issue at all.

I suspect some of these pull against each other, but I genuinely do not know which ones, and I have no idea which I would give up first. What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture:
