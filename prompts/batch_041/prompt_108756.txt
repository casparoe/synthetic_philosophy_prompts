I'm moderating the Q&A at our visiting-speaker thing next month, and the guest is a philosopher who's been getting a lot of attention for a manuscript arguing that the whole transformative-experience discussion has the problem subtly wrong. Her line, roughly: everyone agrees the trouble with a big choice -- becoming a parent, going to war, emigrating to a country whose language you end up thinking in -- is that you can't know beforehand what the experience will be like or what you'll care about afterward, so you can't rank the outcomes. Her claim is that this isn't an uncertainty problem at all. The choice changes the standard by which the choice itself gets judged, so there's no fixed "you" on either side of it to be right or wrong. She calls the resulting situation, a little dramatically, "no fixed judge." Her constructive proposal is that we stop trying to score these decisions by preference-satisfaction -- before or after -- and instead ask whether the choice is one the person can own as part of a life they can narrate; she admits that's vague and has a whole story about why the vagueness is unavoidable.

Two more things she says that I want to press. First, she thinks the famous examples are the wrong ones -- she says irreversibility is a red herring, and the really interesting cases are transformations that are reversible but still identity-changing: acquiring a disability and then recovering from it, or learning a second language deeply enough that you dream in it and then losing it. Second, she thinks testimony is close to useless here, and not just for the familiar reason that the transformed can't describe the experience. Her reason is that the testifier's values have moved, so she's a bad witness about what the un-transformed chooser's values even were.

Draft me eight to ten questions for the Q&A. I don't want her just restating the manuscript. I need at least one question each on: (1) what "no fixed judge" actually amounts to, with a follow-up for when she retreats to "it's just decision theory with a utility function that changes over time"; (2) whether the reversible cases really do the work she wants, given that the person who comes back from the disability or the lost language may be neither the one who left nor a fresh chooser, plus a follow-up if she says that's exactly her point; (3) the ownership/narrative criterion -- what would count against it, and what it says about someone who later regrets the choice, with a follow-up ready for "regret isn't evidence, it's just a later self talking"; and (4) her testimony claim, with a follow-up that corners her on whether she can still say the transformed person's report is evidence of anything at all.

Then add two or three that turn on cases she probably hasn't written about. One I keep chewing on: someone deliberately cultivates a taste -- trains themself to like running or opera until they genuinely prefer it -- was that a transformative choice on her view, and does her account call it rational? Another: a person takes a job knowing the job will remake their values so they'll love it; is choosing your own remaking different from having it happen to you? And if she has a spare minute, whether any of this lets her say that some transformative choices are outright mistakes, or whether that's the thing her view gives up. For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- I know her moves by now and I don't want her sliding past anything.
