I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy with roughly eighteen months of funding remaining and a prospectus defence at the end of the coming semester, which means I have to commit to a single line of work rather than continue circling the same cluster of problems. My background is lopsided in a way that bears on the choice: I came to philosophy from mathematical logic, so modal logic, diagonalisation and arithmetised proof predicates are familiar territory, while the epistemology of perception and the psychology of introspection are not. The cluster I cannot get out of is the one around knowledge of one's own epistemic states — whether knowledge, or being in a position to know, iterates; whether there are any luminous conditions at all, that is, conditions such that being in them puts one in a position to know that one is in them; and what the limits of introspective access really amount to.

My two supervisors pull in opposite directions, and I cannot tell which of them has the better nose for where the work is. The first works on epistemic logic and insists that the interesting questions here are formal. He observes that in the modal logic of provability the iteration principle holds — if something is provable then it is provable that it is provable — while the factivity principle fails, since provability does not entail truth; a modality that nobody takes to be epistemic therefore validates the formal analogue of the KK principle and violates the axiom that knowledge entails truth. His conclusion is that the long dispute over whether knowledge iterates is not really a dispute about knowledge, but about which iteration principles a modality of this kind ought to satisfy, and that it can only be settled once we know what a knowledge predicate added to a language rich enough for self-reference can consistently be made to do. The second supervisor works on self-knowledge and thinks the action lies elsewhere. She reads the anti-luminosity arguments as supplying a general template: for any condition that comes in small increments, a sorites-style sequence of cases can be constructed to show that being in the condition does not put one in a position to know that one is in it. Her live question is how far the template reaches — to credences rather than outright belief, to sensations and moods rather than judgements, to standing rather than occurrent states, to states characterised by what one is attending to — and whether any of the alleged counterexamples are genuine rather than artefacts of the template.

I would like you to help me spend the eighteen months well. Please give me a defended ranking of the open questions in this neighbourhood: the two my supervisors are pressing, whatever else you think belongs on the list, and, in particular, anything neither of them would nominate. Rank by how much other work in the area depends on the answer and by how much of it a philosopher with my toolkit could actually deliver, and give me the reasoning rather than the list — for each candidate, what it is upstream of, what would have to be true for it to be tractable, whether it is the sort of question that would simply dissolve if some neighbouring dispute went the other way, and what over the next year should move it up or down. I should mention one further candidate that I keep turning over and cannot classify: the family of paradoxes that arise when a knowledge predicate is introduced into a language with enough arithmetic for self-reference, and what they imply about how much of the KK debate is a debate about iterated modalities in general. If that line is a dead end, say so and say why; if it belongs high on the list, tell me what it would displace. I am not asking for a reading list or for an inventory of who has said what — assume I know the standard literature — so much as for your judgement about what deserves the time.
