I am presenting a paper at a small workshop on the foundations of decision theory, and what I want from you is a question-and-answer preparation sheet rather than a defence of the paper. Write in British English throughout, and do not offer me references, reading lists or citations of any kind; I know the literature and could not use them in the room anyway.

My thesis is that the coherence argument for expected utility maximisation proves considerably less than its friends suppose, because it slides between two demands that are not equivalent. The first is synchronous coherence: at any one time, no combination of trades available to the agent at that time can be accepted by her and leave her worse off by her own lights. The second is sequential coherence: no finite sequence of trades, spread over time, each acceptable to the agent at the moment she makes it, has that result. I argue that the familiar pumping arguments establish only the first, and that the first entails the second only under two further premises which are contingent features of the agent's situation rather than requirements of practical rationality — that her environment is pump-permissive (costless, self-financing trades remain available to her indefinitely) and that she is non-resolute (she will accept later trades that reverse earlier ones). Strip either premise away and cyclical or incomplete preferences can be perfectly rational; what survives is a conditional: if you are pump-permissive and non-resolute, then be an expected utility maximiser. My worked example is an agent with a strict three-way preference cycle who faces a small positive transaction cost on every trade and who can buy a commitment device for a fixed fee; I claim that over a range of such costs she is neither exploitable nor irrational, and that the standard retort — that she would be exploitable were the costs zero — merely restates the idealisation it was meant to justify. I also claim that pump-freedom does not get you as far as a representation theorem without an Archimedean-type structural axiom, which is a substantive assumption about the shape of preference rather than a demand of rationality.

The audience is a mixed departmental seminar of about twenty people: two Savage-tradition decision theorists who think the coherence argument is essentially settled and will go straight at my premises; one behavioural economist who regards the whole question as empirically idle; a formal epistemologist who will attack the status of the Archimedean axiom and the very notion of a trade; an ethicist in the Kantian mould who will say I have conflated instrumental rationality with rationality as such, and will ask why preference satisfaction has any authority; a philosopher of action who objects to idealisation generally, citing unbounded state spaces, no computational limits and no costs of deliberation; and one public-policy economist who will want to know what any of this implies for cost-benefit analysis.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased in the idiom of the person who will raise it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede at the outset so that the rest of my answers are not spent fighting the wrong battle, and finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
