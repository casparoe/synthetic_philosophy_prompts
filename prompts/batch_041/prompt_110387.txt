I'm the best man at my oldest friend's wedding next month. He and his partner are marrying in a back room of a restaurant, about forty guests, and I've been asked to give the toast. The room will be roughly half family: his parents, who are devout and have never quite made peace with the marriage, a few aunts and uncles who will be kind and slightly baffled, and a table of his university friends, two of whom are finishing doctorates in philosophy and will hear every reference I make. The couple have asked me for something short — five minutes, which I take to be six or seven hundred words — and they have asked me specifically not to turn them into a political symbol or a debating point. They want a toast, not an argument.

What I want to build it on is Bentham, and not the famous calculating machine or the prison. A friend of mine who works on him sent me photocopies of the essays he wrote about sex and the law that he never published in his own lifetime — written, in fact, while the law still put men to death for the thing he was defending — and I have been chewing on them for a year. My reading of them is this, and I would like you to correct me before you write anything if I have him wrong. He applies to sex the same test he applies to everything else: pleasure and pain, and nothing besides. He will not accept that something is unnatural, or that he personally finds it revolting, as a reason for anything, since the first is the principle of asceticism and the second the principle of antipathy, both of them in costume. And when the objection is raised that the law must act because such conduct shocks and alarms the public, his answer is that the alarm is the child of the condemnation rather than its parent: you cannot justify punishing a thing by pointing to the distress that punishing it has taught everyone to feel. The whole criminal case, as I understand him, collapses the moment you insist that every entry in the ledger be an actual pleasure or an actual pain.

So here is the task. I want you to take the role of someone who knows Bentham's moral and legal philosophy closely and also knows how to write a short speech that works out loud in a room, and I want you to think this through carefully rather than reaching for the standard sentimental material. Begin by brainstorming: what does a wedding toast ordinarily do — its stock moves, its comforts, the promises it quietly makes on the couple's behalf — and which of those moves are simply unavailable to a speaker who has decided to say only what Bentham's standard allows? Then write the toast, in my voice, warm and funny where it can be, addressed to a room in which half the people have never heard of Bentham and the other half will be checking. Treat the following as real constraints and not as decoration. Nothing in it may rest on nature, sanctity, destiny, tradition, or the special quality of the couple's souls; if a sentence can only be said by smuggling one of those in, cut the sentence. Nor do I want to claim that their marriage makes the world better on balance, because a strict Benthamite cannot compute that ledger and I am not going to pretend otherwise; what I take myself to be entitled to say is that their happiness counts, and counts exactly as much as anyone else's, which in his system is the whole of the good news. And there is one genuinely awkward thing the toast has to survive: his parents are unhappy, one of them is hurt, and by Bentham's own lights their pain is real pain that belongs in the sum. I do not want to pretend it isn't there, I do not want to be cruel to them across a table, and I do not want to concede that it gives anyone a reason against this marriage. If those three things cannot be held together, tell me where the seam is instead of papering over it. After the toast, add a short set of notes for my own use: which Benthamite moves are doing the real work, which lines an ordinary best man would have written that you had to throw away and why, what the philosophers in the room will hear that nobody else will, and what you would have to sacrifice if I asked you to make the thing land with his parents as well.
