Two friends of mine, Ana and Rob, got into an arguement over lunch about a health budget. They're choosing between wiping out a rare disease that gives maybe 200 people a year an agony that takes over their entire lives, and wiping out a chronic condition that gives about two million people a nagging low-grade misery they can mostly live around. (1) Ana said it's obviously the first one: some suffering is so bad that no amount of lesser suffering can be weighed against it, so the two programs aren't even on the same scale. (2) Rob said that's the lexical move, and it only looks clean until you ask where the line is. She can't just say "agony," she needs a criterion, and any criterion will be a matter of degree, so somewhere there'll be two cases differing by a hair and landing on opposite sides of a line that's supposed to make one of them trump any amount of the other. (3) Ana came back with this: the line isn't a quantity of pain at all, it's whether the pain has colonized the person's relationship to their own experience -- the point where they can no longer hold any attitude toward the pain except the pain itself. Below that, suffering is bad but it's still something a person is enduring; above it there's no one left doing the enduring. So it's a difference in kind, not a threshold on a scale, and the sorites worry slides off. (4) Rob said that just relocates the vagueness -- people drift in and out of that state, there are days you can't tell whether someone's crossed it, and anyway the chronic condition, if it's bad enough, also eats a whole life, just more slowly. He added that program two also hands a lot of positive good back to those two million people -- they get to enjoy things again -- and that refusing to put pleasure on the scale against pain isn't a discovery about scales, it's just a refusal, one that conveniently lets her ignore how much better those lives get. (5) Ana said pleasure is beside the point because the comparison is between two lots of suffering, and that the slow version is different because the chronic sufferer keeps negotiating with it, whereas in the agony case there's no one left to negotiate. Rob said that's romanticizing a distinction the two million would find pretty academic.

I couldn't referee this and I'd like to know who's right at each step. Go point by point: where Ana's criterion actually holds up and where it smuggles something in, whether Rob's sorites objection bites a difference-in-kind claim or only a threshold-on-a-scale claim, whether his pleasure point is a real objection to her or a change of subject, and what both of them are missing. Then say who's more right overall.
