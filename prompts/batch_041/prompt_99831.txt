In a departmental seminar last week a colleague defended a holist conception of social explanation by reviving the old analogy between a society and a living organism: the economy, the legal order, and the family stand to a society as organs stand to a body, and just as we explain an organ's behaviour by its contribution to the health of the whole, so we explain individual conduct by its role in sustaining the social whole. The analogy was doing real argumentative work. It was offered to license two claims: that social facts are sui generis and not reducible to facts about individuals, and that a form of downward causation runs from the social whole to its members.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a survey of the individualism–holism literature. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the cells, the organs, the regulatory and signalling systems, the good of the whole, and the boundary between organism and environment — and then say where the mapping breaks. I am chiefly interested in whether the features that carry the explanatory weight are among those that survive translation. An organism's parts are not agents with ends of their own; they cannot deliberate, dissent, defect, or emigrate; they are spatially bounded and causally sealed in ways a society is not; and the whole has a reasonably determinate good — health — that a society arguably lacks. Which of these disanalogies are fatal to the inference my colleague drew, and which are merely picturesque? Be explicit about which features of the organism the analogy quietly imports into the social case without warrant.

Then work in one complication. Pressed on the point about agency, the colleague upgraded the analogy to a colonial organism: the kind of creature, such as a siphonophore, that is a colony of units which are themselves nearly organism-like — each with its own integration and behaviour — bound into a higher-level individual. Take that description as stipulated; you need not know the biology. Does the upgrade repair the analogy by supplying parts that are also agents, or does it merely relocate the difficulty?

Close by saying what a better analogy would be, if the holist conclusion can be supported by any analogy at all — and if it cannot, say what form of argument would have to replace it.
