You are a philosopher with expertise in comparative moral history, the ethics of memory, and the metaphysics of value, and you have been asked to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two camps in a long-running dispute: the comparativists, who hold that historical atrocities can in principle be ranked along at least some common dimensions of severity, and the anti-comparativists or incommensurabilists, who hold that any public ranking of historical evils is either conceptually confused or morally obscene. The two camps have spent years talking past each other in seminars, memorial debates, and op-eds. Your task is to draft the joint statement they could both sign after a genuine, good-faith collaboration. Please think carefully and begin with a short private brainstorm—a bulleted list of candidate agreed claims, suspected verbal disputes, and possible cruxes—and then produce the document itself.

The document should have two main parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can accept: claims about what an atrocity is; about what comparison is for; about the difference between an ordinal ranking, a cardinal score, and a lexical priority claim; about the distinction between a private comparative judgment and a public ranking or memorial practice; about whether severity is agent-neutral or agent-relative; about whether intent or outcome dominates; about whether numbers can be weighed against per-person severity; about whether incommensurability entails incomparability; about whether the word obscene names a property of the ranking or a speech act of desecration; and about what evidence or argument could in principle bear on severity. Second, a disagreements table with columns for the precise point at issue, the comparativist formulation, the anti-comparativist formulation, the crux that would move at least one side, and the case, thought experiment, or historical comparison both sides agree would count as relevant to that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. You may use well-known atrocities as examples—such as the Holocaust, the transatlantic slave trade, the Armenian genocide, the Holodomor, the Rwandan genocide, the Cambodian killing fields, the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the destruction of Indigenous peoples in the Americas—but do not turn the statement into a ranking, and do not appeal to any specific scholarly work, date, or citation, since the statement must stand on its own. If you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say exactly which part and why; if you judge that a proposed crux is not empirically or philosophically tractable, say so and explain what would make it tractable. Keep the tone formal and constructive, as if the document were to be published under both camps' names. End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{short phrase} naming the one residual crux you regard as most important.
