I retired from a career in experimental physics a few years ago and have spent the time since reading philosophy of mathematics on my own, mostly in the evenings. There is a puzzle about intuitionistic logic that I have not been able to settle, and I would rather have the diagnosis than be pointed toward more literature.

I believe I have the outlines: Brouwer's objection to the unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle, Heyting's formalization of the logic that results, and Bishop's later demonstration that a great deal of analysis can be rebuilt along constructive lines; my former students tell me that the same logic underlies the proof assistants they now use. Two things, however, I cannot reconcile.

First, constructivists are said to reject proof by contradiction. Yet the irrationality of the square root of two — a result I have never heard a constructivist dispute — is established by assuming the number rational and deriving an absurdity. So the summary must be loose, and I suspect there is a distinction I have not been given.

Second, and more troubling: in classical propositional logic the law of excluded middle holds on every row of the truth table, so it presents itself to me less as a substantive mathematical claim than as a tautology, and declining to use it looks like refusing a tool that is manifestly in working order. Since the intuitionist accepts fewer inference rules than the classicist, it seems to follow that every intuitionistic theorem was already a classical theorem — that constructive mathematics is a proper fragment of classical mathematics, and that what the restriction buys is not new mathematics but a certain austerity of conscience. (One of the proof assistants I watched, incidentally, demanded an extra axiom before it would accept a step I had taken for granted; I had expected to be adding axioms, not to be needing them.)

I have wondered whether the missing ingredient is a third status between true and false, something like not yet decided, which would explain why excluded middle is unavailable in the first place.

So my question is this: what is the constructivist actually refusing, and is my conclusion correct that constructive mathematics is a proper part of classical mathematics from which nothing new follows? I hold no brief for either side; I would simply like to know where my reasoning has gone wrong.
