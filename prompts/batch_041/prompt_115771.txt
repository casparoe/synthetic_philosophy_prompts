i'm trying to write a short story for a workshop and i keep getting stuck on the philosophy instead of the fiction, so i want you to write a version of it and then tell me where it cheats.

setup: a couple of decades from now there's an international registry that has to rule, case by case, on whether something counts as alive. it exists because of a treaty drafted after a Mars sample-return scare: the treaty bans destroying or commercialising indigenous life on any body, Earth included, and the drafters left life undefined on purpose becuase nobody could agree. so the registry is basically a room full of people arguing about a word, and my protagonist is the junior clerk who has to write the first draft of a ruling.

the case i've invented (this is the part i actually care about): a flooded disused mine, anoxic water at the bottom, air at the top. along the waterline there's a crust, iron sulfide mostly, growing outward in layers. each new layer is templated by the lattice of the layer under it, and the lattice defects get copied along with everything else — once in a while a defect changes how the next layer grows, and that variant outgrows its neighbours and the neighbourhood changes. so: variation, inheritance, differential persistence. but no metabolism worth the name — it doesn't harvest energy for itself, it just catalyses a reaction the pump-driven gradient would run anyway, and if you take the pump away it stops growing. no membrane, no cells, no nucleic acid anywhere. and no individual either: the crust is continuous with the rock and different lineages thread through each other, so there's no boundary you can draw around one. when the pump stops it doesn't die, it goes dormant, and it can sit there for decades and start up again. the pump is run by a company that wants to flood the shaft and walk away; they say the crust is a byproduct of their equipment, not life. a rival group says it's the oldest continuously evolving lineage in the mine and that flooding the shaft is exactly what the treaty forbids. there's also a microbial mat living on the crust that everyone agrees is life — and the ugly consequence is that if the crust isn't life, the mat is a human-introduced contaminant the company is allowed to drown; if the crust is life, the mat might be an invasive species grazing on protected life. i want the story to make the reader feel both rulings are defensible and that the clerk has to sign one.

stuff i want in it: one character who thinks the whole question is a category error — that life is a word like vegetable, a shopping category rather than a natural kind, and that the registry should be asking what can be damaged and who has standing, not whether a thing is alive; one character who thinks the opposite, that there's a fact of the matter and the crust simply isn't it; and one who says we don't have a theory yet, so defining now is like defining gold before anyone knew about elements — we should be collecting the weird cases, not ruling on them. my partner, who does origin-of-life stuff, says the real move is to drop life entirely and talk about evolutionary lineages or self-maintaining systems, but i can't tell if that's a real position or just her being provocative.

can you write it — short, 1500 to 2500 words, i don't need a frame narrative or a twist ending, the decision scene matters more than the scenery — and then add a short note afterwards: which real positions each character is basically voicing, where i've stacked the deck, and whether the crust as i've described it is actually a hard case or whether i've accidentally made it easy by piling on features. if i've botched the setup — if there's something i've given it that settles the question for free — say so plainly instead of writing around it, i'd rather find out now than at the workshop. no reading list or citations, i'll chase the literature myself. (and if the story is just the wrong format for this and i should write an essay, tell me that too, but try the story first.)
