I am a novelist, and for some years now I have been writing fiction about institutions — the way a committee survives an argument it has already lost, the way a procedure outlives the reason it was adopted. I would like your help with a piece in that vein.

Write me a satirical institutional document, in the deadpan register of a civil-service memorandum, from a small city-state that has adopted futarchy. The scheme, as I understand it, is this: the electorate does not vote on policies directly. Instead the government fixes a single measurable definition of collective welfare, and prediction markets are then used to choose the means. For any proposed policy, a conditional market asks whether the welfare measure will be higher if the policy is enacted than if it is not, and whichever policy the market expects to do better is the one adopted. The values are supposed to be settled democratically, in the choice of the metric; the facts are supposed to be settled by the market. That division of labour is the whole of the idea, and it is also where I would like the knife to go in.

The document should be the after-action review of one specific embarrassment. Set it in the invented city-state of Vantorre, whose welfare measure is the Civic Flourishing Index — a composite of some thirty indicators, among them income, life expectancy, ferry ridership and self-reported contentment, re-based every five years by a statutory body. Vantorre's decision markets have just resolved a proposition of roughly this form: that Ordinance 88, the ferry subsidy, will raise the Index by at least one and a half percent within three years. The market resolved yes; the ordinance passed; the Index did rise by one and a half percent; and almost everyone involved now understands that nothing was learned. I want the memorandum to account for that failure without ever being able to say what actually went wrong.

Some material you may use, though you should invent more of the same kind:

- The Index's ferry-ridership component rose mechanically, because the ordinance subsidised ferries — so the policy was, in part, its own evidence.
- The contentment component rose after the Bureau ran a campaign encouraging citizens to report contentment; the survey was not blind to the campaign.
- The market was thin. A single participant, referred to throughout only as the market maker of record, held most of the yes side and stood to profit from the subsidy directly.
- The resolution committee split over whether to score the outcome against the pre-registered Index or the revised one, and settled the question by majority vote — that is, by the very procedure the markets were meant to replace.
- The memorandum's own conclusion is that the episode shows the market was insufficiently liquid to count as a genuine futarchy, and it therefore recommends deepening the market, widening the Index, and creating a further conditional market on whether the Index itself should be trusted.

Aim for roughly eight hundred words, with numbered sections, findings and recommendations, in the passive voice of a body that has learned nothing and would like the minutes to reflect that it has. Do not let the document become self-aware. It should never mention the idea that a measure degrades once it becomes a target, never draw the distinction between facts and values, never concede that the metric is itself a policy; a reader who knows the futarchy literature should recognise each of those objections embedded in the machinery and find the satire fair, while a reader who does not should simply find it funny and slightly frightening. The comedy must come entirely from the substance — from the gap between what the document reports and what its own sentences imply — and never from jokes, asides or winking.
