I have been having a long-running argument with a friend in the statistics department that I now want to turn into a short paper for a graduate seminar on epistemic logic, and I would like your help setting up the analysis. The dispute concerns the reception of the Monty Hall puzzle when it appeared in Marilyn vos Savant's Ask Marilyn column in Parade magazine in 1990: she answered that the contestant should switch doors, and the column drew something on the order of ten thousand letters, nearly a thousand of them from readers with doctorates, most insisting she was wrong; the Hungarian mathematician Paul Erdős is reported to have remained unconvinced until a computer simulation was run for him. My friend reads the whole affair as a straightforward story about human irrationality and stops there. I want to treat it instead as a test case for the problem of logical omniscience — the idealization built into the standard epistemic and doxastic logics, on which an agent's knowledge and belief are closed under logical consequence, so that an ideal agent knows every logical truth and every logical consequence of everything it knows. What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory read against the episode, and the episode read back against the theory.

I know the puzzle and its solution cold, so do not walk me through the probabilities or the standard explanations, and do not spend words on the familiar complaint that ideal agents are unrealistic — I want the frictionless-plane defense of the idealization taken seriously rather than dismissed out of hand. What I am after is first the mapping and then the reckoning. Who in the episode is supposed to be the ideal agent — the contestant, the letter-writers, vos Savant herself? What is that agent given as premises, and what exactly does closure under consequence attribute to it? Then the harder part: as the puzzle was actually printed, the host's procedure is left unspecified, and the familiar answer depends on assumptions about that procedure which were never stated, which means the letter-writers' mistake — if it is a mistake — cannot be located in a failure of deductive closure without begging the question. Work out what that does to the diagnosis. I also want you to say whether the episode is an anomaly for the idealization, a confirmation of it once the premises are spelled out properly, or evidence that it was never meant to model a case of this shape at all, and to say where the theory falls silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble, no conclusion, no closing summary — each item running to a couple of sentences.
