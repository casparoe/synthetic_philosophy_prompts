I am assembling materials for a small reading group on the philosophy of language, and what I want from you is a written dialogue rather than an essay. The group is working through the sceptical reading of Wittgenstein associated with Saul Kripke, according to which there is no fact of the matter about what a speaker means by a word, since neither their past uses nor any facts about their habits and dispositions can single out one rule rather than countless others. I should like the discussion to be tested against an example of my own devising, so that it cannot simply recycle the usual arithmetic illustrations that everyone has already heard.

Please write a dialogue between two members of the group, Anna and Benedict, who take opposing sides on that reading. The example they are arguing about is a small signal code invented by two housemates: a card propped against the kettle is red when the water has only just gone on, amber while the tea is brewing, and green when the tea is ready to pour. They use the code without incident for two years, always leaving the card in one of the three positions their arrangement prescribes, and neither of them ever slips. Then a third person joins the household and is taught the code in a single sentence; a week later she props a card at a slight angle that neither of the inventors has ever produced before, and the two of them fall out over whether it means the tea is ready. Anna takes the sceptical line: she argues that the finitely many past uses, together with whatever can be said about the inventors' habits of mind, are equally consistent with any number of different rules — including monstrous ones on which they would have done something quite else the very next time — so that the disagreement about the tilted card has no determinate answer waiting to be discovered. Benedict resists this: he maintains that the rule was genuinely fixed when the code was set up, and that the fact that both inventors would confidently go on in the same way is not merely a fact about their habits but a fact about what they meant.

Please let each of them answer the other's actual points rather than talking past one another. In particular, Anna should press the difficulty that a disposition tells us only how someone would in fact go on, never how they ought to, and Benedict should say what, if anything, he takes to ground the difference between going on correctly and merely going on as one is inclined to. Neither should be made to look foolish, and the exchange should be left without a tidy resolution if that is where the arguments lead. Kindly write the dialogue in British English.
