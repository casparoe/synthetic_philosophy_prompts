I chair the governance committee of a 480-household housing cooperative, and I need to circulate a written opinion before the annual meeting, where the membership will vote on a bylaw amendment about how board elections work. The immediate trigger is that a four-person slate swept last spring's election on a single platform plank — replacing our aging boilers with a heat-pump system funded by a special assessment — and then voted as a bloc on everything else as well, including matters they had never mentioned to anyone. The defeated incumbents now want a rule that would prohibit slates outright: candidates would run as individuals, group endorsements and joint campaign literature would be barred, and any coordination among candidates would have to be disclosed to the membership. My vice-chair thinks this is sour grapes dressed up as principle, and that a ban would mainly shield sitting directors from organized challenge; she points out that our lowest-turnout elections in recent memory were the ones with no organized groupings at all.

One of our directors, who teaches philosophy, has been arguing for something more radical, and this is the part I most need the opinion to reckon with. She invokes Simone Weil's case against political parties: that a party is essentially an apparatus for manufacturing collective passion, that membership substitutes loyalty for the individual's obligation to attend to what is actually true and to who is actually suffering, and that the party form makes it nearly impossible for anyone inside it to weigh a question on its merits. Her conclusion is that banning slates is cosmetic. If the real worry is that organized electoral competition corrodes judgment, she says, then we should stop treating election as the principal way to fill the board. She proposes that five of the nine seats be filled by lot from the membership, for a single two-year term with no immediate re-selection, leaving four seats elected.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a specific course of action for the annual meeting — which amendment, if any, to put to the vote, in what exact form, on what timetable, and with what fallback if it fails — and it should give the committee reasoning we can defend in open discussion rather than a restatement of our bylaws. It has to engage the strongest consideration on the losing side, whichever side that proves to be: if you recommend the ban, you owe an answer to the worry that it entrenches whoever already holds power; if you recommend against it, you owe an answer to the worry that organized blocs will simply capture the co-op's governance. And if the sortition proposal is a distraction, say why; if it is the more coherent response to Weil's argument, say that instead.
