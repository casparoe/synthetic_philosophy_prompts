The rent stabilization ordinance my city council passed last month covers the building I live in, and my lease is up for renewal in the spring, so I have had occasion to think about the subject more carefully than I expected to. Last week, at a tenants' association meeting I helped organize, two people I like and respect spent an hour arguing past each other, and I still cannot say who had the better of it. I am not asking you for a policy recommendation. I am asking you to referee the argument as it was actually made.

My brother, who is midway through a doctorate in economics, went first. (1) He said that a ceiling on rents below the level at which the market would clear predictably produces a shortage: the stock of rentable units shrinks as owners convert, under-maintain, or exit, and the shortage then allocates apartments by connections, luck, and willingness to tolerate squalor rather than by price. (2) My neighbor, who has run the tenants' association here for a decade, answered that this is a prediction about a market we do not have. In this neighborhood three owners hold most of the rentable stock, the supply of land is fixed, and the posted rent is whatever the owner of the building you can actually move into decides to charge; she said the relevant comparison is not a competitive market but the arrangement we are in, and added that the same simple model, applied to the minimum wage, has not survived the empirical work she has read, so its verdict here is at least not automatic. (3) My brother said that even granting her all of that, the honest description of a rent cap is a transfer rather than a cure — it does not create a single apartment — and that if redistribution is the goal, the cap is a clumsy instrument, since it rewards whoever happens to hold a lease, well-off tenants included, and does nothing for the people who cannot get in at all. (4) She replied that a home is not a widget: a tenant's interest in staying put is not merely a preference with a price tag, and the "market rent" he treats as the neutral baseline is itself the product of law — zoning, tax treatment, mortgage subsidies — so calling a cap an interference only names the set of rules he is used to. (5) He said that this proves too much: every property regime is legally constructed, so the observation cannot tell us which regime to prefer, and the real question is whether this ordinance keeps people housed or slowly shrinks the stock. (6) She said that owners who sell out or let buildings decay are making their own choices and that the ordinance compels no one to do anything, so a law that makes an investment less attractive is not a law that demolishes housing.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and justify the rating briefly. Then identify what both of them are missing, and say who, on balance, reasoned better — not which policy you would favor, but which of the two argued well.
