Next month I am giving my first invited seminar in the joint colloquium run by our department of economics and the gender studies program, and I want to walk in ready for the question period rather than improvising through it. The room is split down the middle and the two halves will not object to the same things. Roughly half are quantitative social scientists -- labor economists and sociologists who work with cross-national survey and administrative data, who will care about identification, measurement, and whether my comparisons survive the obvious confounds. The other half are feminist philosophers and political theorists, who will care about what my framing presupposes, what it naturalizes, and what it licenses politically. I need a prep sheet written for that specific audience, not for a general methods audience and not for a general theory audience.

The paper defends three connected claims, and the objections should target these rather than a generic version of the debate.

First, an interpretive claim about the cross-national pattern in which countries that score highest on standard gender-equality indices also show the largest gaps between men and women in which fields they enter and in measured vocational interest. I argue that what rises with national gender equality is not the underlying magnitude of gender-differentiated preference but its visibility and its optionality: in less egalitarian settings, women's absence from certain fields is substantially constraint-driven and is therefore masked in aggregate statistics that look only at who ends up where, whereas in more egalitarian settings the same underlying distribution of preference is allowed to express itself with less friction. On this reading the paradox measures the cost of suppressing preference, not the naturalness of preference.

Second, a decomposition claim. I distinguish entry sorting -- which field a person initially enters -- from exit sorting -- who stays in it. I argue the cross-national gap is driven more by differential retention and attrition than by the initial choice, which, if true, changes what the phenomenon even is and what would count as an intervention on it.

Third, a justice claim. If the residual sorting is preference-based rather than constraint-based, then outcome-oriented parity policies do not merely fail to achieve their stated end; they risk overriding the very agency that egalitarian societies exist to protect, and that is a distinct and more serious objection than ineffectiveness.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I fumbled it, each phrased the way the relevant half of the room would actually phrase it -- the economists' version of a challenge is not the philosophers' version of the same challenge, and I want to see the difference. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and then a candid note on where that reply is soft, so I know which ones I am surviving on rhetoric rather than argument. Tell me what I should concede up front, before anyone has to drag it out of me, and which single reply has to land for the talk to be judged a success rather than a provocation. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why I am hoping nobody asks it.

A few constraints on how you write it. Do not send me to the literature, and do not pad the replies with citations or names of studies -- I know the field and I need the arguments, not a reading list. Do not attribute specific numbers to specific countries or studies unless you are certain of them; where a figure would help, describe the shape of the pattern instead and flag it as something I should be able to state precisely from my own tables. Keep the objections in the register each half of the room would actually use, including the ones that are more diagnostic of a prior commitment than of a flaw in my argument -- I would rather see the hostile version of the objection than a polite paraphrase of it. And be willing to tell me that one of my three claims is the weakest link and should be cut or demoted before the talk rather than defended in the Q&A.
