I've been reading the rationality blogosphere's more technical posts for years, and a friend of mine who does forecasting for a living and I got into an argument last week about the Judy Benjamin problem. The setup is simple enough: a soldier is dropped into a territory divided into red and blue, and each of those is divided into headquarters and airborne, so there are four quadrants. She has some prior over the four. Then she receives only a conditional constraint — something like, if she is in red territory, the chance that she is in headquarters is one-half — rather than any unconditional fact about which quadrant she is in. The standard Bayesian reflex is to conditionalize on the new information, but there is no proposition to conditionalize on: the information is a constraint on a conditional credence, not a proposition about the world. Different update rules handle it differently; one rule effectively conditions on the material conditional, another minimizes relative information (KL divergence) subject to the constraint, and they do not agree. Worse, updates on such constraints do not commute, so the order in which the constraints arrive changes the posterior, and the posterior also depends on the prior, which in the original story is just whatever the soldier happened to believe.

I'd like you to write a satirical piece that dramatizes exactly this. Please write it as a field manual or FAQ issued by a fictional Bureau of Credence Adjustment to officers in the field, in the deadpan style of an actual government circular. The target is the reflexive assumption that all learning is conditionalization — the habit of treating every piece of evidence as if it were an unconditional proposition to be conditioned on, and the bureaucratic attempt to apply that reflex to conditional information. The humor should come from getting the epistemology right: the absence of a proposition to conditionalize on, the disagreement between the two update rules, the non-commutativity of updates on conditional constraints (so the order in which the officer files the forms changes the recommended action), the dependence of the posterior on the prior (which the Bureau has classified), and the fact that some conditional constraints are already satisfied by the prior and so should do nothing at all. Include a dissenting memo from a frequentist statistician who objects that none of this is well-defined, and a running gag about the Bureau's solution to non-commutativity being to require that all forms be filed simultaneously — without specifying a rule for simultaneous constraints. About 800 words, numbered sections, no winking at the reader; the piece should be legible as satire to someone who knows the problem and readable as a straight-faced bureaucratic document to someone who doesn't.
