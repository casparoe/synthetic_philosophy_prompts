A friend of mine from an old rationalist reading group has, over the years, given me verdicts on a long run of one-off bets, and I have come to suspect that her policy is more principled than she lets on. The offers were always take-it-or-leave-it: the stake was fixed by the counterparty, stated as a fraction of her total wealth, and she could accept or decline but never resize. She treats the stakes as real. Here are six of them, each given as the stake, the probability of the favorable outcome, and her wealth in the win and loss states as a multiple of what she holds now.

1. Stake one tenth, 70% chance the stake comes back doubled, otherwise lost (1.1× / 0.9×): she takes it.
2. Stake three twentieths, 75% chance doubled, otherwise lost (1.15× / 0.85×): she takes it.
3. Stake three fifths, 60% chance doubled, otherwise lost (1.6× / 0.4×): she declines.
4. Stake everything, 40% chance tripled, otherwise lost (3× / 0×): she declines.
5. Stake two fifths, fair coin, even money (1.4× / 0.6×): she declines.
6. Stake one tenth, 40% chance the stake comes back fivefold, otherwise lost (1.4× / 0.9×): she takes it.

The offer now on the table is all-or-nothing and again cannot be resized: stake everything, with a 60% chance that her whole bankroll is tripled and a 40% chance that it is cut to three tenths of what she holds now (3× / 0.3×).

I want four things from you. First, your prediction for the new offer, with a confidence level. Second, the rule she appears to be running, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to offers you have not seen. Third, the other rules that fit all six verdicts equally well — I am fairly sure at least one does, and I want to know how it differs from the first. Fourth, if any one of the six already begins to separate those rules, tell me which and how; if none does, say so, and then name the single further offer that would do the most to separate them, along with what each rule would say about it.
