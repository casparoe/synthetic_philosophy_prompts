Two explanations compete to account for the same phenomenon: the tendency of individuals and organizations to keep pouring resources into a course of action that any outside observer can already see will end badly.

The first explanation is structural. The situation is said to have the form of what is sometimes called the dollar auction — the escalating-bid game in which several bidders compete for a prize, the highest bid wins it, and the two highest bidders must both pay what they bid. Because a losing bid is still paid, and because withdrawing converts an already-spent sum into a certain loss whereas bidding again keeps alive a chance of coming out ahead, each increment can look locally reasonable even as the running total climbs past the value of the prize; the auctioneer collects the difference. On this account the bidders need not be irrational at any single step, since the trap lies in the structure of the situation.

The second explanation is psychological. Agents persist because of sunk-cost reasoning, the need to justify earlier choices to themselves and to others, loss aversion, and an unwillingness to be the one who quits. On this account persistence is a genuine error of reasoning rather than a consequence of the payoff structure.

Compare the two. Say which phenomena each explains that the other cannot, and whether they are rivals or two descriptions of a single thing. In particular: is escalation in a dollar auction a mistake of reasoning, or a trap into which rational agents fall? If it is a trap, is the decisive error at the decision to enter the auction at all, or at each decision to continue? Does the rule that both leading bidders must pay do all the explanatory work, or would escalation survive a variant in which only the winner pays — and what does your answer imply about how much real-world escalation is genuinely auction-shaped? Then consider two further variants and say how each bears on the two accounts: (a) a dollar auction in which it is common knowledge that the prize is worth less than the smallest possible combined outlay, so that every participant is bound to lose; and (b) a dollar auction in which the auctioneer refunds the losing bidder half of what that bidder paid. Finally, if the structural account is right, does anything remain for the psychological account to explain, or does the psychological account collapse into the structural one?

Give me a considered verdict rather than a survey of positions. State how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you are least sure of.
