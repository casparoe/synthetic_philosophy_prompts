I was at a workshop dinner last week and ended up a silent bystander to an argument I could not referee, and I would like you to adjudicate it for me move by move. The two participants, whom I will call D and M, are both people I respect, and neither, as far as I could tell, was arguing in bad faith.

The trigger was a mutual friend — a self-taught guitarist who can reproduce a song after hearing it a single time, but who cannot say what she is doing: she cannot name the chords, describe the pattern, or explain why a given phrase works. If you ask her to slow it down or isolate a passage, she loses it. What followed went roughly like this.

1. D opened by saying this is a knack, not knowledge. Knowledge, he said, requires the ability to give an account — to say what is true and why. The guitarist can only do; she cannot say. Contrast a sight-reader, who can name the notes and the key and could tell you, in words, what the piece is doing. That difference, D claimed, is the difference between knowing how and merely being able.

2. M replied that this smuggles in an articulacy requirement and then mistakes it for a condition on knowledge. The guitarist can transmit what she has — a student can learn the song by copying her — and she can notice when a student hits a wrong note and steer her right. So she is not merely causing behavior; she is guiding it. The ability to verbalize the rule is a separate, second-order ability, and demanding it just privileges the verbally fluent.

3. D conceded the point about transmission by demonstration, but pressed on generality. She cannot transpose the song, cannot vary it, cannot recover once she goes wrong, cannot play it in a new key. What she possesses is a single frozen routine. Knowledge, D argued, should be robust: it should let you handle cases you have not already seen. The sight-reader can pick up any piece placed in front of her; the guitarist can play exactly one, exactly as she absorbed it.

4. M offered two replies. First, robustness is not required for knowledge-that either: I can know that a particular number is Sam's while knowing nothing else about Sam, and a rote-memorized fact is no less known for being narrow. Second, the generality D wants is itself a further skill — transposition, error-recovery — that the guitarist simply has not acquired; it is a second bit of know-how, not a condition on whether the first bit counts as knowledge.

5. D then changed tack and produced two cases he took to be decisive. A concert pianist who is paralyzed can no longer play, yet surely still knows how; and a player-piano can produce every note correctly while knowing nothing at all. So neither ability alone nor articulacy alone is what we mean by knowing how; whatever it is, it is a third thing, distinct from both.

6. M answered that these cases only show the words come apart, not that there are two states. Perhaps "knows how" is ambiguous between a mere ability and a knowledge of a way of doing something. On the first reading the machine has it and it is not knowledge; on the second reading it is propositional after all — the pianist knows that this is a way to play the passage — and D's anti-propositional conclusion does not follow.

For each numbered move, assign a rating on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken through questionable, defensible, sound, to clearly right, and justify each rating in one or two sentences. Then identify the deepest point of genuine disagreement between D and M — the crux — and state what, if anything, both of them are overlooking. Do not simply crown a winner; engage each argument as it was actually made, and be willing to say that a given move is partly right and partly wrong. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain exactly one of: mostly D, mostly M, neither, both equally.
