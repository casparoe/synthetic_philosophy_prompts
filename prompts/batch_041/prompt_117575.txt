After forty years as a research scientist, I retired and began reading history and philosophy in earnest; I am new enough to the latter that I often cannot tell which questions are well-posed. One phrase keeps stopping me: "history repeats itself." I have heard it used to end arguments — most recently by a friend who insisted that because our present economic and political situation resembles the 1930s, we should expect a comparable outcome, and who, when I asked what "repeats" was supposed to mean, replied that the point was obvious.

In my old field, to say a phenomenon "repeats" was to say something reasonably precise: either that it is periodic, or that it obeys a regularity holding across a stated range of conditions, one that can be written down and used to predict. I doubt the historian's phrase carries anything like that content, but I would rather see the options laid out than assume. I gather it is tangled up with a larger dispute over whether history can have laws at all, in the way the physical sciences do.

Please set out the distinct precise claims that "history repeats itself" might be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the strong and contentious. For each, say what it commits one to; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or nearly empty; and — most important to me — whether it would genuinely license the predictive move my friend made, from a resemblance between two situations to a claim about what comes next. I would also like your best judgment as to which reading people usually intend when they deploy the phrase to close a discussion, and how far the claim would have to be weakened to survive the obvious objections. Please treat this as a menu, with a verdict attached to each entry, rather than an argument for a single favored reading.

End with one final line of the form \answer{...}, whose braces contain either the number or the short label of the one reading that is both substantive (not a platitude) and sufficient by itself to support the predictive inference, or the word none if no such reading exists.
