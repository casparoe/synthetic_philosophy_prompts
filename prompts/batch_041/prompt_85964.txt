I've been reporting a feature on a question that turns out to be surprisingly hard to state plainly: what makes a sentence more than a list of its words, and a thought more than a heap of its parts. The philosopher I've been interviewing returns to the same picture every time I press him on it, and the picture is a chemical one. The constituents of a proposition are like atoms; the relation that holds them together is like a bond; the proposition itself is like the molecule that results; and the contrast between parts that can stand on their own and parts that cannot is like the contrast between a saturated compound and one with a free valence. His purpose in reaching for it is to defuse the worry that has dogged this topic since Bradley: if unifying two constituents required a further item, the relation, then unifying the constituents with the relation would require yet another item, and so on without end. Chemistry, he says, shows the shape of the escape, because nobody thinks a molecule needs a second bond to attach the first one — the atoms arrive with their combining powers already in them — and by the same token an unsaturated concept needs no further relation to bind it to its argument, so the unity of the proposition is simply what you get when the parts are what they are. He grants that the comparison is loose, but he insists it is the standard way of putting the point, and it is doing real work in his argument: if it holds, the unity of the proposition is not a mystery to be solved by positing extra entities but a feature of the constituents themselves, and compositionality is safe.

I want to know how far the comparison actually holds, because I suspect it is carrying more of the argument than he admits. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the atoms, the bond, the molecule, the valence — and then tell me where the mapping breaks, keeping separate the mismatches that matter for what he wants the analogy to do from the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially curious about the features the chemical picture quietly imports. A bond in chemistry is not a primitive fact but the visible upshot of forces that physicists can describe, whereas the propositional tie is supposed to be primitive and unexplained. Chemical combination alters the parts — the sodium is no longer sodium once it sits in the salt — while the constituents of a proposition keep their senses intact. Bonding is a process in time, with energies and rates and conditions, whereas a proposition is not the sort of thing that happens. And the cases that look friendliest to him cut both ways: the same atoms arranged differently yield different compounds, which maps neatly onto the difference between one ordering of constituents and another, but an atom with no free valence, a noble gas, refuses to combine at all, whereas an object that is complete in the Fregean sense enters propositions as an argument all the time. So tell me whether the conclusion he draws — that unity is built into the constituents rather than added to them — is something the chemistry can support or something he is bringing in from elsewhere, and if the analogy cannot bear the weight, what comparison would do better, or whether the honest answer is that no analogy will do here.
