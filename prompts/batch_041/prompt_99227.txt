Compare the situational explanation of perpetrator behavior commonly associated with Browning with the culture-and-ideology explanation commonly associated with Goldhagen. Rather than rehearsing the historical dispute, focus on their conceptual commitments: what each must assume about the stability of character, the causal power of ideology, and the conditions under which ordinary social pressures become sufficient for atrocity. Then test both against two contrasting perpetrators—one who takes initiative in killing without prior ideological commitment, and one who holds genocidal beliefs but acts only under direct orders—and say whether the two explanations are rivals or can be combined into a two-stage account of recruitment and moral erosion.
