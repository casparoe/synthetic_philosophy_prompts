I run the content team at a small war museum — four of us, no historians on staff — and we're redoing the labels for a gallery about a border war in the 1990s that both sides still argue about. We'll be writing maybe 120 of these over the next month, so it's not a one-off, and each one has to be defensible to a board that includes veterans' groups and a cultural attaché from the other side who keeps writing us letters.

Here's my problem. Half our artifacts are things our soldiers did, half are things the other side's soldiers did, and structurally they're the same kind of thing — a guy with a rifle in a trench, a guy who held a village, a guy who shot someone who turned out to be a civilian. Right now the labels do the thing where ours are 'defending' and theirs are 'occupying', and I've started to think that isn't something I can actually defend.

I've been reading about the idea that soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals when it comes to how they fight — that whether your war is just, and whether your conduct in it is just, are two seperate questions, and the second one doesn't care which side you're on. I think I buy it, but I can't hand my label writers a philosophy paper.

So: give me a procedure we can actually run, one artifact at a time. The questions to ask, in order; what counts as a good enough answer to each; where it stops and says this one gets labeled neutrally; where it stops and says no, this act gets marked as wrong regardless of side; and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's turned into a values question. It has to fit a 60-word label, my writers get about 15 minutes per artifact, and every call has to be something I can defend to a hostile board member who is looking for a reason to shut the gallery down.

Then run it on three artifacts: (a) one of our soldiers who held a position under fire and killed several attackers; (b) an enemy soldier who did the same thing on the other side of the same hill; (c) a soldier on the enemy side who by every account was a disciplined and restrained fighter, in a war our side believes was plainly unjust. I want to know what label each one gets, and whether a visitor walking through would notice the asymmetry or the symmetry.

And be honest with me about two things. First, where the procedure can't really decide and I'm just dressing up a value judgment as a rule. Second, what happens if the revisionist view is right instead — the one where fighting in an unjust war does make you culpable even if you fought clean. If my procedure collapses under that view I need to know now, because a board member is definately going to bring it up.
