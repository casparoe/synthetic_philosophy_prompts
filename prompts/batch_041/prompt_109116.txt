Hey — I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, metaethics mostly, and I got roped into running next months session of our department's "philosophy through fiction" thing, where somebody brings in a short story that's supposed to make a problem vivid instead of just stating it. Mine is the Frege-Geach problem, which I've been circling for about a year and still dont feel like I have my hands around. Quick version of where I'm at: the expressivist says that when I say murder is wrong I'm not describing anything, I'm giving voice to an attitude — a boo rather than a belief. Fine. But those same words turn up inside conditionals, negations, questions, and she-thinks-that clauses, where nobody is booing, and they still have to mean the same thing there that they mean standing alone, or else any argument that uses them is just a pun. Every repair I've read — the Gibbard-ish norm-acceptance move, the Blackburn-ish higher-order-attitude move, the whole minimalism-about-truth maneuver — seems to either buy the logic back by turning moral judgment into something suspiciously intellectual, or to quietly stop being expressivism when you're not looking. I'd like to see the thing happen in a story instead of in a footnote.

So: set it in a small back-to-the-land commune in the seventies — earnest people, a couple of families, a schoolroom, one big kitchen — who have genuinely adopted the rule that moral talk is just expression. You can cheer, you can boo, but you can't assert that anybody's wrong. Then let some ordinary situation come along that the practice can't carry, because somebody needs to reason hypothetically or negatively about a moral claim. Two things I care about. First, don't use the stock example about lying to your little brother, it's in every intro textbook and I want the group to actually have to work; build the crux out of a different embedding — a negation would be my favorite (I don't see how booing and cheering can keep not-wrong apart from right, and that gap seems like it should matter), or a question, or somebody's attributed belief. Second, show it, don't explain it: no character delivering a lecture on expressivism, I want the reader to hear the gears grind when the words won't do what the community needs them to do. Keep it tight, maybe 1200 words.

Then, in three or four sentences after the story, tell me which of the expressivist repairs would actually patch the hole, what it costs the expressivist to adopt it, and whether the commune could have sidestepped the whole mess by having a different expressive practice from the start. American English throughout, please — the group is all American and I don't want to be the one explaining spellings.
