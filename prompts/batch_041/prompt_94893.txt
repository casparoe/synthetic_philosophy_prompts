I retired from a chemistry department about eight years ago and have spent the time since teaching myself philosophy of science, badly. What I'm stuck on is which bits of a dead theory I'm willing to call real, because my gut verdicts don't hang together.

The ether that light was supposed to travel through: not real, I'd say — but the equations that came bundled with it are still printed in the textbooks and still give the right answers, so something about it was real. Phlogiston: not real, and I don't think anything of it survived, it was just a mistake. Caloric, the fluid heat was meant to be: same, a fiction. Dalton's atoms, though — real, even though at the time they were only a bit of bookeeping to make the combining ratios come out and nobody could see one. The electron: real, obviously.

Here's my trouble. Caloric was as well-supported in its day as the electron is now. So if I say caloric wasn't real and the electron is, what am I going on, apart from the fact that we happen to believe one and not the other? As it stands it sounds like I'm just awarding the medal to whoever wins.

Then a case from my own bench I can't place at all. For years we hunted a surface species we called the 'M-centre' — our whole rate law for the reaction was built round it. Turns out there's no single M-centre; the modern picture is a mess of steps and none of them is it. But the rate law we got by assuming M-centre still fits our old data to within error. So: not real, but its consequences were? I keep flip-flopping on this one and on the ether, and I don't like it.

Someone mentioned there's a name for a view like this, structural realism or something. What I want isn't a reading list — I've no library card these days and no way to chase references — but the principle itself, if there is one, tested against my cases, plus what it says about dark matter (real thing or bookkeeping device?) and an honest line on which of my verdicts it can't save.
