I serve on my university's Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee, and I must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The case is a protocol from a neuroscience lab that proposes to grow human cortical organoids from induced pluripotent stem cells and expose them, in a microfluidic chamber, to patterned electrical stimulation calibrated to produce avoidance-like withdrawal responses. The lab wants to run an aversive-stimulation arm to map what it calls nociceptive-like plasticity; the non-aversive arms are uncontroversial. The principal investigator's position is that the organoids have no sentience because they lack a body, sensory afferents, and integrated thalamocortical loops, so there is no one home to be harmed. A dissenting committee member argues that we cannot know that, that the organoids' avoidance-like responses are exactly the kind of computation that should trigger moral caution, and that the burden of proof should fall on the researchers. Two graduate students have told the committee they will not run the aversive arm. The committee is split, and our current policy is silent on organoids.

I want the memo to decide this, not to survey the field. I have been working with a criterion I call the self-address condition, and I want you to use it: a computation matters morally only if it is part of a system that represents the state as its own, as valenced, and as something that matters to it; complexity, responsiveness, or human-like behavior are not sufficient. Apply that criterion to the organoids as described. If the condition is not met, say so and say what would have to change for it to be met. If it is met, or if we cannot tell whether it is met, say what follows for the aversive arm. The memo should address attribution directly: by what evidence and by whose judgment we attribute sentience to something that cannot report, and where the boundary of sentience should be drawn when the evidence is underdetermined.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a formal decision memo recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- and giving reasons the principal investigator and the dissenting member could both respond to. It should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than restating our authority. It should also say what the committee can decide now and what it must defer. Distinguish what is settled in the relevant science from what is genuinely contested among people who know the field; do not present a contested view as the consensus. No citations, no reading list; I need the reasoning, not the literature. End the memo with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of APPROVE, REJECT, or APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, followed by a one-sentence summary of the recommendation.
