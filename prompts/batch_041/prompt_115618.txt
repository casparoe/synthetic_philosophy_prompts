My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about idealized deliberation, and I would rather end it with a poem than with another round of rebuttals. His view: what I have reason to do is what my fully informed, undistracted, vividly imagining counterpart would want me to do. Mine: that verdict either borrows its authority from what I already care about, in which case the idealizing does no work, or it doesn't, in which case I cannot see why it binds me. He did say one thing I keep turning over — that the point of an ideal deliberator was never to be consulted, since what she would want, if anything, is that I have the habits and attachments that make consulting her unnecessary, so the right response to her verdict might be to stop asking for it.

Write a villanelle in which the two refrains are, respectively, the ideal deliberator's verdict and my reply as the actual person who has to live with it. Each line has to survive every repetition: the ideal's must be able to read as instruction, as description, and as concession, and mine as objection, as admission, and as agreement, with nothing in the poem announcing which reading is live at any turn. The form will force the two refrains to rhyme with each other, which I take to be part of the question rather than an inconvenience. In the closing quatrain they should meet so that my objection turns out to be how the ideal verdict is finally obeyed. First person, no jargon, and accurate enough that someone who has spent a career on idealization and normative reasons would recognize the position and not merely the vocabulary.
