I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a graduate seminar in public policy — students who are comfortable with basic set notation and proof sketches but who have never encountered social choice theory — and the topic is Arrow's impossibility theorem. I do not want you to explain the theorem to me; I want a teaching plan. Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced so that the result arrives as a consequence of commitments the students already hold rather than as a surprising technical trick, saying explicitly what depends on what: the primitives (a finite set of alternatives, a collection of orderings, an aggregation rule), the conditions usually labeled unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship, and the shape of the proof idea. I care most that the plan makes clear which condition does the real work and which one people instinctively resist, and why the non-dictatorship condition is not the weak claim students assume it is.

For the carrying example I have one firm constraint: it must not be an election. I teach this in a policy school, and the moment anyone hears the word voters the discussion collapses into complaints about real electoral systems. I want the example to be a single agent aggregating several complete rankings over the same alternatives — a hospital committee ranking treatments by different criteria, a research group merging reviewers' orderings of papers, something of that shape — and I want you to tell me which example to use, why it carries the theorem better than an electoral one, and what it must have (complete orderings over a fixed finite set, at least three alternatives, genuine disagreement among the rankers) and must lack (cardinal information, a natural common scale, any obvious correct answer) for the impossibility to bite rather than to look like an artifact.

Then map the misconceptions in the order this audience will actually form them and tell me where in the sequence to head each one off. I expect at least these: that the theorem says no voting system is fair, or that all aggregation is impossible; that the dictator is an ordinary tyrant rather than a single individual whose strict preference always decides; that the result is about strategic behavior or manipulation, which is a different theorem; that it forbids aggregating utilities or scores; and that independence of irrelevant alternatives is either trivially true or plainly false. Finally, give me one question to pose at the end that would reveal whether they have understood the theorem as a statement about the joint consistency of a set of conditions rather than memorized its name, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say openly that I am leaving it out.
