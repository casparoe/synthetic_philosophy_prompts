I'm co-running a session next week for a second-year "well-being and the good life" seminar — 16 students, 80 minutes, small room with movable chairs, and I'm allowed to bring whatever props I want as long as I clean up afterwards. The unit is on what makes a life go well, and I want to run an activity that makes the students *feel* a view I've been calling tranquilism, though let me state it in my own words since I doubt they've met it and I don't want to assume you'll recognize the label: the claim is that the only thing intrinsically good for a person is the absence of craving and of suffering — that pleasure, acheivement, ecstasy, even love's best moments are at most instrumentally good (good insofar as they don't drag craving in behind them), and that a flat, craving-free, mildly contented life beats a life of spectacular highs and lows, even if you add up all the highs and find them vastly outweighing the lows. That's a hard sell in a room of ambitious nineteen-year-olds, so I want the game to do the selling, not me.

Here's my half-formed idea and I'd like you to fix it, wreck it, or replace it entirely. I was thinking of a treadmill setup: each student starts with a craving card (things like "you want to be admired," "you want to be comfortable," "you want to win something"), each card carries a charge, and every round you can spend effort to knock the charge down, which earns you pleasure points that go up on the whiteboard for everyone to see — except the craving you satisfy comes back, or a fresh one gets dealt, so nobody ever actually reaches zero. Alongside that I'd have a separate pile of suffering tokens that just subtract from your total and can't be traded or worked off, so students can see that getting rid of suffering is a different operation from chasing pleasure. Then there's a one-way "settle" move: you can permanently stop drawing cravings and zero out your charge, but you earn no more pleasure points and your number on the leaderboard freezes for the rest of the game. The plan is that the visible score rewards chasing while the real score — which I'd only reveal at the very end — rewards tranquility, so they spend most of the session optimising the wrong number and then get hit with it. I'm not sure whether that's too cute or too rigged; I want the reveal to land as "oh no, that's what I actually did" rather than "you tricked us, and also your numbers were arbitrary."

So: rules simple enough to explain in five minutes, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline for the full 80 minutes including the debrief, and roles if you think a few students should be dealers or referees rather than players. What do I do about the ones who'll spot the hidden scoring by round three and either defect or start telling everyone? And what will definately go wrong the first time I run it — I'd rather know now than find out live. In the debrief I want to map specific moments of the game onto the tranquilist claim: the moment the treadmill becomes obvious, the moment someone resents having to give up the pleasures to settle, the moment someone notices the flat life was winning all along. And please be honest about what the activity *can't* reproduce. I already suspect it can't reproduce the full claim that pleasure has zero intrinsic value, since any scoring formula I write has to give pleasure some weight somewhere, so tell me how to be upfront about that gap instead of papering over it — and whether that gap is fatal to the whole exercise.
