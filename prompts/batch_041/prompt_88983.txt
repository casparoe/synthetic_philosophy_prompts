I retired from the physics department about three years ago and have spent most of the time since reading philosophy, which my old colleagues think is a joke at my expense and my daughter thinks is a phase. Anyway, I've been chewing on something and I need your help with a speech. My daughter and her partner are holding a naming ceremony for their first baby in a few weeks — no religious affilation, some poems, a lot of food — and they've asked me to stand up with a glass and do the main five minutes. The room is the problem. Her partner is a postdoc in philosophy of mind, so a third of the guests will be his colleagues and their students, and they will hear every single thing I say in a way I can't control; the other half is my late wife's family, practising Catholics, who will be watching me carefully for the moment I imply that the baby is a bundle of cells and a work in progress with nobody in there. And my daughter's only instruction was: do not tell my child she can be anything she wants, I will actually leave the room.

What I want the speech to be genuinely about is the thing I keep circling back to, Dennett's essay on the self as a center of narrative gravity. I like it partly because of the analogy, which as a physicist I find more than decorative: a center of gravity is real, you can compute torques about it and get the bridge to stand up, and yet there's no fact of the matter about exactly where it is, because you can do the sums about any point you like and the physics comes out the same; it's a useful abstraction rather than an object, and nobody thinks the bridge is haunted by it. Applied to a person, this seems to me to do strange things to everything one normally says on an occasion like this. I can't promise the baby that she will grow into the person she already secretly is, because on this view there isn't one of those waiting inside; but I also can't say anything that sounds like there's nobody home, which is what her grandmother will hear if I'm clumsy about it. I did wonder whether the analogy is too strong, and a friend in my reading group says it breaks down because a center of gravity can be pinned down to any precision you want whereas a life story can't — I don't know what to do with that and it may be worth a sentence. The part that feels most alive to me, though, and the most dangerous to say out loud, is that the thing is made out of stories other people tell about you at least as much as the ones you tell about yourself, so everyone in that room is already an author of her and will be whether they like it or not, including the ones who will tell her what she is before she can argue.

So: could you write the speech? It should be warm — these are people I love and it's a happy day, not a seminar — and it should be honest to the idea rather than using it as decoration, which means feeling the constraints and finding something else to do where the usual move is unavailable: no true-self rhetoric, no you're-perfect-as-you-are, no promise that we will always understand her. Say something real about the co-authorship, since that's the bit with teeth for a room containing both relatives and philosophers, and let it land differently for the two halves without losing either. I'd rather hear it in my own voice than have it name philosophers or explain the theory like a lecture; if you want to quote something, keep it to a phrase at most. About six hundred words, maybe a touch more.
