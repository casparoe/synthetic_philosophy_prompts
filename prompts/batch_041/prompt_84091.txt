You are a philosopher of mind with a specialist's command of Daniel Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his alternative, the multiple drafts model, and you are also a competent critic of narrative film. Think the problem through before you write: brainstorm the scenes, images, and recurring motifs of the film I am about to name, set out the readings each might support, then throw away everything that merely illustrates the doctrine and keep only the two or three elements that genuinely put pressure on it. Only after that sifting should you compose your answer.

The film is Pixar's Inside Out (2015). I am writing an essay on it for a collection on philosophy and film, and I do not want the stock reading — the control room is a Cartesian theater, therefore the film is muddled about consciousness. I want the reading that starts where that observation leaves off.

Work through at least the following, though you should follow the film rather than treat these as a checklist.

First, the console as Cartesian materialism rather than dualism. Dennett's target is not the ghost in the machine but the finish line: any point in the brain where the order of arrival equals the order of presentation, so that what happens there is what one is conscious of. Headquarters is exactly such a point, and it is entirely materialist. Does the film register that the homunculus problem survives the death of dualism — that the crew at the console are themselves subjects, so that somebody would have to be watching them? Notice that we are never shown their own experience. Is that an evasion, a joke, or the point?

Second, the distinction Dennett draws between what he calls Orwellian and Stalinesque explanations. His argument from the phi phenomenon is that any retrospective report can be explained either by the subject having had the experience and having it revised in memory afterwards, or by the content having been doctored before it ever reached the subject, and that nothing can adjudicate between the two — which is why he denies that there is a fact of the matter about when an experience became conscious. Inside Out supplies both: Sadness reaches back and saddens memories that had been joyful, and Dream Productions scripts and films Riley's dreams before she undergoes them. Does showing us both, from the inside, amount to the film claiming the vantage point Dennett says nobody occupies? And does the film think there is an answer to when Riley became sad on her first day at the new school?

Third, the distributed imagery. Memory orbs, the train of thought, the personality islands, the memory dump, and above all the Abstract Thought sequence, in which the characters are progressively stripped of their familiar representational format — do these add up to a genuinely distributed picture of the mind, or does the console re-centralize everything they might have shown?

Fourth, the self. The film locates Riley's self in her islands and core memories, which is close to Dennett's claim that the self is a center of narrative gravity rather than a thing in the brain. Where does the film endorse that, and where does it retreat to something that owns the console? You might consider whether the crew is better read as a society of mind, in Minsky's sense, than as one self.

Fifth, the medium. The dream sequence is literally a film shoot, with a director, a script, and a camera. Is the Cartesian theater just the internalization of the movie theater, and if so, is Inside Out diagnosing that or exhibiting it?

Then be honest where the film resists the reading. Its emotional arc seems to require the console — the ending turns on who is standing at it — and perhaps no film can tell this story without a place where it happens. Close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that the arguments alone do not; I suspect it has something to do with why the Cartesian theater is so hard to give up.

Do not reduce the film to an illustration of a theory, and do not bend Dennett to fit the film; where the honest reading has to concede that the film resists, say so plainly. I need no citations or reading list, and do not worry about reproducing dialogue accurately — I know the film.
