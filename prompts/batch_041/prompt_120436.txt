Okay, weird ask, but here goes. I'm a reporter — science and ideas beat, mostly, which is a fancy way of saying I write about consciousness research and the occasional philosophy fight — and for the past few years I've also chaired the board of a small nonprofit newsroom that does long investigations. Our editorial handbook has one rule I helped write and now sort of regret: we don't use the word "genocide" in our own voice, in a headline or a finding, unless a court or a UN body has already made that determination. Until then we say "mass atrocities" or "crimes against humanity" and we quote other people's use of the g-word. The rule was supposed to keep us from turning into an advocacy shop.

Now we've got a case that's going to break the rule or break the org. Our team has spent two years in one province of a country I'd rather not name here. A minority group there — call them the Hill People, which isn't their name either — has been on the receiving end of a lot of things over about eight years: killings by paramilitary units, a sterilization campaign run out of provincial clinics with actual quotas attached, a boarding-school program that's taken several thousand kids and forbidden the language, a ban on the group's ceremonies, and a dam that flooded the valley with the burial sites and two of the three shrines. There's a paper trail — a provincial planning memo calling the group's "growth" a problem to be managed, a school directive, a clinic memo with numbers on it. What there isn't is a court. The country isn't a party to the ICC, the domestic courts are captured, and the governor's family sits on the mining consortium that benefits from the dam. Nobody is ever getting indicted. The dam and the language ban are also the parts I can't make fit anywhere in the convention's list of acts, and I keep wondering whether that's the whole ballgame or a detail.

Our lead investigator has 40,000 words and wants "genocide" in the headline. The board is split down the middle and I have the deciding vote and I hate it. One side says hold the line: we aren't a court, we can't establish intent, and the second we start slapping the label on the cases we care most about we become exactly what our critics say we are. The other side says the rule is a coward's rule — it outsources our judgment to institutions that are never going to show up, and it's precisely the no-court cases that need the word. One board member keeps bringing up Lemkin, the guy who coined the term in 1944, noting that he treated the destruction of a group's culture and language as central to genocide, and that the drafters of the 1948 convention considered that and then dropped it, leaving us with "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" — so why are we treating the lawyers' narrower version as the real one, and what is "as such" even doing in there? I don't know how much that should move me. And there's the practical thing: if we use the word, our two local staff get deported or worse and the office closes.

I'm bringing a memo to Thursday's vote. Draft it. I need a clear recommendation on two things: whether we keep, amend, or scrap the handbook rule, and what actually goes in this report — the headline, the finding paragraph, and when we publish. Its going to be close either way, so if you think we should amend rather than scrap, tell me exactly what the new trigger is, because "we'll know it when we see it" is how we got here. Take the strongest version of whichever side you land against, since I'd rather lose an argument on paper than win it by not having it. American English, no citations, no history-of-the-idea padding — I've read enough of those to last a lifetime.
