I teach a comparative-politics course on democratic erosion and I am currently grading midterm essays. Below is the essay question I set and one student's answer to it. I would like a grade and, more usefully, the margin comments that justify it.

The question:

"In the last two decades 'democratic backsliding' has become the default term for describing what has happened to democracies that did not die in a coup. Three questions are worth separating. (1) What does the term add to the older vocabulary of democratic breakdown and authoritarian consolidation — does it name a distinct process, or only a stage on the way to something the older terms already named? (2) Who gets to decide whether a given episode counts as backsliding — scholars, courts, electorates, international institutions — and does the answer change what the concept means? (3) The objection is often made that 'backsliding' is a compliment we pay to countries we still hope will become democracies and an insult we reserve for the ones we have written off. Is that objection sound, and if it is not, what has to be true of the concept for it to fail?

Answer all three. In doing so, discuss at least two of the following four cases — Hungary, Poland, Turkey, the United States — and for each case you discuss, state explicitly the baseline against which you are measuring decline. An essay that asserts a decline without identifying the summit from which the country is supposed to have slid has not answered the question."

The student's answer:

"The term was introduced to name something the older vocabulary could not. Democratic breakdown names a rupture — a coup, a military takeover, an annulled election, a civil war. Backsliding names attrition: an executive that reaches power through a free election and then uses legal instruments — constitutional amendment, ordinary legislation, court-packing, friendly takeovers of the press — to disable the checks on itself. The distinction matters because the second process leaves the electoral machinery formally intact, so the usual indicators of breakdown register nothing.

Hungary is the paradigm case. Fidesz's two-thirds majority in 2010 allowed it to replace the constitution in 2011, expand and refill the Constitutional Court, rewrite the electoral law, and preside over the transfer of most of the private press to owners friendly to the government. None of this was a coup, and Fidesz kept winning elections. Poland after 2015 ran the same script: contested appointments hollowed out the Constitutional Tribunal, and a new disciplinary regime for judges brought the ordinary courts under political control.

Poland also shows that backsliding is reversible. The 2023 election produced a government committed to undoing the judicial changes, so the process is better understood as an oscillation of the political equilibrium than as a one-way decline. If that is right, Turkey belongs in the same category: Erdoğan's consolidation after 2013 is a swing that a future election could reverse.

On who decides, the European Union has effectively settled the question for us. The Article 7 proceedings and the Court of Justice's rulings establish, as a matter of law, that Hungary and Poland have breached the rule of law, and that is a determination by a body with the standing to make it. No comparable body has made a finding about the United States, which is part of why claims about American backsliding are contested in a way that claims about Hungary are not.

The partisan-label objection has some force but is overstated. The concept was designed precisely to separate the erosion of democracy from ordinary policy disagreement, and it picks out institutional changes that can be observed independently of the observer's politics. The objection bites where the term is used loosely — as in much American commentary — and not where the changes are documented, as in Hungary. The concept earns its keep because it names a mechanism that breakdown does not: elected leaders dismantling accountability from within, legally, and with the electorate's continued consent."

What I need from you is a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. Work through the answer claim by claim. For each substantive claim, say whether it is accurate, defensible but contestable, or wrong, and why. Flag specifically: anything the question asked the student to argue for that the student simply asserts; any inference that does not follow from what precedes it; any place where two things that should be kept apart are run together; and any part of the question the answer does not actually address. Where you are marking something down, say briefly what the better move would have been — a grading comment, not a rewrite.

Then give a single grade, A through F with plus or minus, on a standard scale where an A means no substantive problems and a B means solid on the main lines but with at least one real flaw. Commit to one grade: no ranges, no "it depends on the rubric," no conditional on how much weight I give each section. Finally, tell me which of the problems you flagged are grade-relevant and which are nitpicks, so that the grade can be seen to follow from the comments rather than the other way round, and name the single change that would most have improved the grade.

Do not soften the assessment to spare the student, and do not manufacture problems in order to look rigorous: where a claim is fine, say so plainly. Where a point is genuinely contested in the field rather than mistaken, say that too, and say whether the student earned the claim anyway.
