Ok, I'm writing a longish essay-ish thing about the German-vs-American thing — Ordnung, follow the rules, don't make a fuss on one side; the "I answer to my own conscience and nobody gets to tell me what to do" streak on the other — and every arguement I have about it dies on a one-liner. My brother-in-law, who is German, says "rules are rules" in a way that's supposed to settle the whole question. A friend says the American version is "I have to do what I think is right" and says it like that settles it too. Neither will go one step past the slogan, and honestly I can't tell whether they're even disagreeing.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict on the cultures. Take "rules are rules" and lay out the precise claims it could be making, from the boring to the loaded. Off the top of my head the candidates are something like:

- the weak one: rules should generally be followed, other things equal, and this particular rule is just one consideration among many;
- the one where the fact that it's a rule is itself a reason, independent of what the rule says or how dumb it is;
- the one where the rule doesn't add a reason so much as push the other reasons off the table — you don't get to weigh it against the harm it's doing, that's not your call;
- the fairness reading: if it bends for you it has to bend for everyone, so the point is equal treatment rather than authority;
- the coordination reading: what we care about is that everyone can predict everyone, and the content is almost beside the point;
- the self-binding reading: rules exist because people like us will rationalize anything, so the rule is a device against our own future cleverness;
- the epistemic reading: someone upstream already thought about this, your job is not to re-litigate it;
- the expressive/identity reading: following the rule isn't a means to anything, it's a way of being a certain kind of person, order for its own sake;
- the jurisdictional reading: it's not a claim about reasons at all, it's a claim about who decides — and the answer is "not you";
- the cynical reading: it's what you say when you've lost the argument but still have the power.

For each of those: is it a platitude, a real position someone could actually defend, something false, or something that doesn't even hang together? And which one is most likely what a person means when they drop it to end an arguement — I suspect it's usually two of them blurred together, and I want to know which.

Then do the same for the conscience line. Is it anti-authoritarianism as such, or a claim that I have better information about my own case, or an authenticity ideal, or a claim about where responsibility lands (only I can be blamed, so only I can judge), or just selfishness in a nicer outfit? Same treatment, and same question about what people usually mean.

Finally: once both slogans are pinned down, which pairing of readings makes this a real disagreement instead of two people talking past each other? And does "which one has the better of it" survive that, or does it split into a bunch of smaller questions where the answer is obviously "it depends" — deference is good here, individual judgment is good there? If it splits, tell me what the actual axes are.

Please don't tell me to go read anyone, I can't look anything up right now and a reading list won't help me at the dinner table. Just do the conceptual work. Also I'd rather you add a reading I haven't thought of than spend the space on the ones I listed.
