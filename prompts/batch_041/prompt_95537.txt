I'm putting together an episode for a small philosophy podcast, and the guest is someone who defends Kant's answer in the murderer-at-the-door case and thinks it gets a bad rap. Her line, as she's laid it out to me: the duty to be truthful isn't owed only to people who deserve the truth or who'll use it well, and it doesn't get cancelled because the man at the door is up to no good. When you lie to him you do two wrongs at once — you attack the whole institution of truthful speech that contracts and courts and testimony all sit on top of, so the damage isn't confined to the guy on your doorstep, and you also degrade the humanity in your own person by turning yourself into a mere instrument for producing a good outcome. So on her view the "obviously just lie" reaction is consequentialism sneaking in through the back door, and the critic she's answering (the one who said the duty to tell the truth only binds you toward someone who has a right to the truth) just doesn't get what kind of duty this is. I want eight to ten questions that don't let her restate any of that, and that go for the seams. Specifically I need something on (a) the imputation-of-consequences move — she holds that if you lie and the murderer then goes and kills somebody else, that death is on you, and I want to know whether that's a moral claim or a legal doctrine about blame getting smuggled into ethics; (b) the loophole where you just decline to answer, or answer truthfully in a way that helps no one, which makes the whole position look like a rule about words rather than about harm; and (c) the pressure from Kant's own formula of humanity, since the rigorist line seems to treat the intended victim's life as a means to keeping truthfulness intact. For each of those three, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stuck if she says the obvious thing. Also work in a case I've been chewing on: you never assert anything false, but you deliberately leave the murderer with a false impression — is that a lie on her account, and does her answer to that commit her on misleading-by-implicature more generally? And I'd like one question about what happens when the person at the door is a lawful official rather than a random murderer, since I suspect the legal standing of whoever's asking is doing quiet work in her argument. No biography stuff, no "what drew you to Kant."
