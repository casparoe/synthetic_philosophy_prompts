I fix industrial sewing machines for a living and read alot of philosophy at night, so forgive me if the vocabulary is off. I've ended up holding a position and I want you to try to break it. Here it is. Take any formal system that's strong enough to do arithmetic and that's consistent. Gödel says there's a sentence in its language that it can't prove, but that the sentence is true anyway. I can see that it's true — I look at what the sentence is saying, notice that it's saying it isn't provable in the system, and go, right, and that's exactly why it's true, so the system can't prove it and it's true regardless. The system can't make that move about itself. So I'm not that system. And it doesn't matter which system you hand me, the same trick works on that one too. So I'm not any formal system at all — I'm not a machine. The way I put it to myself is: I can always take whatever system I'm reasoning in and stand one level above it, and no system can stand above itself. I know Lucas wrote about this a long time back and Penrose got a whole book (or two) out of it later, so I'm not claiming it's mine — I just cant find the hole. There's also the other theorem, the one about a system not being able to prove its own consistency, and I gather that's where the standard reply comes from.

What I actually want from you is a map of what this position is risking. What observations, formal results, thought experiments, or plain arguments would count against it — and which of those are fatal versus merely awkward? Don't just list the objections; tell me which ones the people who hold this view would themselves admit are weak spots, as opposed to the ones critics like to bang on about that the holders just shrug off. In particular, press on the move I'm most attached to, the standing-one-level-above thing: is there a way for that to be true of me and still have me be a machine — a machine that keeps extending itself, say — and if so, does that kill the argument or just change what it proves? I'd also like to know whether the position needs the strong version, the one where the mind is supposed to be running on physics that isn't computable (I think that's where Penrose ends up), or whether it can survive on the logic alone; and if the strong version is where the real exposure is, tell me what would count against that specifically. Last thing, be straight with me about whether this is the kind of view that can't lose — where any human mistake is just the machine part and any human insight is the non-machine part — and if it is, whether that's disqualifying for a view like this or just the normal price of doing philosophy. What would move me most is someone showing that my stepping up a level is really just me being handed the lower system's consistency by somebody else, and that I never actually do it on my own.
