I am a high school student writing an essay for an advanced statistics class on whether the replication crisis shows that something is wrong with the scientific method itself or only with how the method is practised, and my teacher's preferred illustration is one I cannot get past. She says a research field is like a screening programme: most of what gets tested is not the real thing, the test is imperfect in both directions, and so most of the results that come back flagged are wrong, just as most positives are wrong when the disease being screened for is rare. Because the test cannot be made perfect while the thing being looked for is uncommon, the sensible response is to improve the test, which she takes to mean stricter thresholds, larger samples, and studies registered in advance. A classmate of mine objects that this cannot be the right lesson: the screening picture is a Bayesian calculation applied to a procedure that refuses to reason from prior probabilities, so what the analogy really shows is that the statistics should be done differently, not that a cutoff should be tightened. I would like an audit of the analogy and of what it can be made to support.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction and no conclusion. (1) State the correspondence explicitly, deciding what plays the role of the screened population, the disease, the test and its sensitivity and specificity, and the prevalence of the disease, and say which of these roles has to be assigned a number for the analogy to yield anything quantitative. (2) Say which parts of the analogy are genuinely apt, and what they explain about the replication crisis. (3) Say where it breaks, singling out the disanalogies that matter for my teacher's conclusion and explaining why the rest do not. (4) Say what the analogy quietly imports from the screening case that the research case lacks, and which of those imports are doing the persuasive work. (5) Say which conclusion, if either, the analogy can carry, and what the smallest change to it would be that would make it a better guide.
