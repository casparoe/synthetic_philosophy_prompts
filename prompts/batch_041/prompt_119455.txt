Okay, so here's my situation and I need you to just write the thing for me. I'm a philosophy professor — I teach a seminar on memory and history, that's my whole deal — and the town where I grew up has asked me to give the address at its sesquicentennial in June. Wren's Falls, about 4,000 people, up in the hills, you've never heard of it. They want me because I'm the only professor they've ever produced, which tells you alot about the town. The centerpiece of the day is the rededication of the statue of Miss Adeline Pruitt, the schoolteacher who, in the town's telling, went back into the Water Street fire of 1911 three times and carried out eleven children, and died of it a week later. There's a song about it. My aunt has the song cross-stitched. You get the picture.

The problem. My cousin Dana works at the county historical society, and she spent two years on that fire, and she came away fairly sure Adeline Pruitt was at her sister's in Marbury that whole week, and that the person who actually carried the children out was Eli Rusk — a drifter who mucked out the livery stable, and who, in the town's *other* famous story, is the man they ran out of town in 1913 on suspicion of stealing a horse (the horse turned up, but by then he was gone). Her evidence is good but not airtight: two recollections that contradict each other, an undertaker's ledger, and a letter that may or may not even be about the fire. She's not publishing. She doesn't want to be the one who does this to her own family, and she's told me she'll keep quiet at the ceremony either way. So it's on me. And note the awkward part: Adeline Pruitt was real, she taught in that school for thirty-one years, the statue is at least partly earned. It's one scene that's in question, not the woman.

I don't want to stand up in front of 400 people who buried their parents under that statue and either (a) repeat something I now think is probably false, or (b) do the routine where the visiting intellectual explains to the locals that their beloved story is a lie and then drives home. There will be maybe five historians in the room and two reporters from the state paper, and they'll know exactly what I'm doing whichever way I go.

What I want to try out in the speech is a distinction I've been chewing on all semester, and I want to see if it survives contact with an actual audience. Call it the swap test. Take the load-bearing detail out of a community's story, drop the true detail in its place, and ask what the story is still saying about who we are. Some false details are scenery — swap them and nothing moves. Others are the whole point: the story was never really about the fire, it was about *that we are the kind of place where somebody goes back in*, and if the corrected version says something different about that, then you aren't fixing a fact, you're demolishing a self. My hunch — and I want the speech to test it rather than announce it — is that a commemorative claim is usually doing two jobs at once, reporting an event and constituting a community, and that the genuinely hard cases are the ones where the report is false but the constitution is fine, as against the ones where the false report is the only thing holding the constitution up. Run that test on Wren's Falls out loud, in the speech, without ever telling the audience that's what I'm doing.

So write me the address. Seven or eight minutes, call it 1,100 words, first person, spoken. No footnotes, no throat-clearing. If I'm going to say the difficult thing I have to say it in a way a room can actually hear. Constraints: don't assert anything the record doesn't support; don't call the story a legend or a myth as a way of smuggling a falsehood past people, because a story called a legend is still either true or false and this audience hears the difference; and don't flatter the town by implying the archive is just another story with equal standing — they'd smell it and Dana would never forgive me. Don't make me the hero of it; I'm the guy with the microphone, that's all. And don't end on a shrug. I need an actual last paragraph, and I need that last paragraph to give the statue something to mean for the next fifty years.

Last thing. I'd like the five historians to leave satisfied that nothing false was asserted, and the other 395 to leave feeling their teacher was honored. If those two can't both be had, then after the speech, in a couple of lines, tell me which one I gave up and why.
