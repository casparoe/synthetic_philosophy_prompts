So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy backround at all, and something about how we do retros has been bugging me and I genuinely can't tell if its a real problem or I'm just being pedantic.

Here's the setup. When something breaks in prod we run a blameless postmortem and the format is basically a causal chain. A config was wrong, the deploy pipeline didn't catch it, the alert that should have fired didn't because someone muted it during a migration, the oncall followed a runbook nobody had updated in a year. Nobody would ever accept "the team is just careless" as a root cause. You'd get asked for the actual mechanism, and "we don't know yet" is a perfectly respectable answer.

But then we do a quarterly review on why one team shipped their thing in six weeks and another team, same stack, same headcount, roughly the same roadmap, took two quarters and then it got cancelled. And the explanation everyone nods along to is "culture". Culture of ownership. Culture of shipping. Culture of fear. And nobody asks the follow up we would 100% ask in an incident review, which is: what is the thing, and how did it produce that result.

I've also noticed we cheerfully say things like "the team didn't want to ship it" or "the org wasn't ready", which is strange if the team is forty people who presumably wanted forty different things.

So what I want to know is what philosophical commitments this whole practice is quietly carrying around. Specifically:

- When we explain an outcome by culture, are we claiming culture is a cause in the same way the bad config was a cause? Or is it a different move, where the point isn't a mechanism pushing the outcome along but making the behavior intelligible to us? Is "because of the culture" the same kind of because as "because the disk filled up"? If it's a different kind, is that a dodge or a legitimately different form of explanation that we shouldn't force into the causal mold?
- Which rival views does the practice rule out? That culture is just a label we paste on an outcome after the fact? That the real causes were comp, headcount and who happened to be oncall, and culture talk is a polite way of not saying that? That a culture just is the sum of individual incentives and habits, with no extra thing there at all?
- Isn't it circular? We infer the culture from the exact behaviors we then explain by it. Two teams behaved differently, therefore different cultures, therefore the difference is explained. What would have to be true for that not to be circular?
- Is the practice even consistent with itself? The incident review demands mechanisms and is fine with "we don't know"; the org review accepts a single word and stops. Do the two halves presuppose different theories of what an explanation is?
- It also reminds me of that old Protestant work ethic story, Calvinists sweating over whether they're among the saved, and somehow the anxiety breeding a capitalist work ethic. Did the beliefs cause the behavior, or did the two just happen to fit together so well that the story feels explanatory? And is that a real distinction or am I making it up?

What I'm after: reconstruct the theory this practice implicitly assumes, separate what it strictly requires from what it merely fits nicely with, and say where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible views would license exactly the same meeting. Then tell me what would actually change if we swapped in a rival view, what our retros would have to start measuring, and what we'd have to stop saying. And no, I don't want a reading list, I want the actual answer.
