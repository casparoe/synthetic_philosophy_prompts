I am a philosopher working on population ethics and the ethics of reproduction, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper at the monthly colloquium of our medical school's bioethics center. The paper is a qualified defense of the procreation asymmetry, but not the standard one. I do not claim that existence is a harm or that non-existence is a benefit; I grant that there is no subject on the far side of the comparison to bear either. What I claim is that the asymmetry should be recast as a constraint on the terms of creation rather than on creation itself. Bringing someone into existence cannot wrong them merely by the fact that they exist, but it can wrong them by the terms on which they exist, specifically by fixing a load of suffering that they could not have consented to and, given who they are, could not have avoided. On this reading the asymmetry is person-affecting and consent-based; it carries no antinatalist implication; and its practical upshot is a pro tanto constraint against creating lives with a heavy unconsented suffering load. I put it to work on two cases: a fetus with a condition expected to involve chronic pain that cannot be palliated, and a fetus with a condition that is socially disabling but not itself painful.

The audience is not a population-ethics audience, and that is why I need help. The room will contain four groups. First, clinical ethicists who do consult work and think in terms of precedent, actionability, and what they can actually say to a family at the bedside. Second, two disability studies scholars who are skeptical of any argument that treats suffering as an intrinsic feature of a life rather than as a function of social arrangements, and who will hear in my unconsented suffering load a respectable-sounding way of counseling against disabled lives. Third, a health economist who works with QALYs, thinks in expected-utility terms, and will want to know what my constraint does under uncertainty. Fourth, a Kantian who is sympathetic to the consent language and will press hardest on whether it can bear the weight I place on it.

Draft my question-period prep sheet: eight to ten objections, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, with a separate note on how likely each is. I want the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones I would get at a population-ethics workshop, so do not include the standard charge that the asymmetry is ad hoc or that it rests on unreliable intuitions about hypothetical cases unless you judge that someone here will really say it, in which case tell me who and why. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it: the clinical ethicist's version of the non-identity problem does not sound like the philosopher's, and the disability scholar's version of the charge that I have smuggled the forbidden comparison back in sounds like neither. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is soft or concedes more than it appears to, and whether I should concede the point up front rather than fight it. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why I am hoping nobody asks it. Finally, tell me which single exchange has to go well for the paper to survive the hour, and identify the weakest part of my own position that I should be ready to defend rather than hope they miss. No reading list; assume I know the literature.
