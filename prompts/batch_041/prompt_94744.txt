I retired last year after thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics, and I have spent most of the first year of that retirement reading philosophy on my own — decision theory, and the parts of ethics that touch on it. In my old field we were trained to distrust any quantity whose average is set by a tail we will never sample: a formally divergent series in a perturbation expansion, an ensemble average dominated by configurations that carry absurdly small weight. So Pascal's mugging should have been familiar terrain, and instead I find that none of the standard replies bite. The replies I have collected — that the offered probability is too small to act on, that the mugger's promise is not credible, that infinite utilities are incoherent — all seem to me to answer a different question than the one the case poses, and I have come to suspect that the real difficulty is structural. Let me stipulate, to take the last reply off the table, that nothing infinite is on offer: the mugger may promise only finite improvements to the world, of any size he likes, at any probability he likes, since the size is his to name and the probability is his to assert. Here is the proposition I would like a number on: *Pascal's mugging is defeated not by anything about infinite value or about small probabilities, but by the fact that the expected-value reasoning it invites is self-undermining — if that reasoning licenses complying with this mugger, it equally licenses complying with an unbounded class of incompatible muggers (for any action available to me, some admissible offer recommends it), and a purported decision rule that returns contradictory recommendations across cases is not a reason for action at all, only a formula.* My own credence in that is about 0.55. A friend from the reading group, a formal epistemologist by training, puts it near 0.15; she thinks the self-defeat point is at best a practical inconvenience, and that the real work is done by some account of which probabilities an agent is entitled to ignore.

Please give me your credence in the proposition as stated, then decompose it. The terms I can see are: (i) that the class of competing offers is genuinely closed, in the sense that for any action some admissible offer recommends it; (ii) that contradictory recommendations across cases is a defect in a decision rule rather than a mere bookkeeping inconvenience — that a rule need not be action-guiding in that sense; (iii) that the self-defeat diagnosis is the *correct* one rather than the small-probability, credibility, or incoherence diagnoses, since the proposition could be true on one reading and false on another; and (iv) that the self-defeat point is not itself repaired by some tie-breaking or highest-offer-wins amendment, which would leave the expected-value core intact. If you think a term is missing or that one of mine is doing no work, say so and adjust. For each term give a probability and one line of justification, show how the terms combine into your overall number, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most — a re-reading of the case, or a piece of conceptual work you would have to do first. I would also like to know which of the three of us, me at 0.55, she at 0.15, or you, is most likely to be wrong about a specific sub-claim rather than about the whole thing. End with a final line of exactly this form and nothing after it: \answer{<your credence>; <most sensitive term, four words or fewer>; <that term's own credence>}.
