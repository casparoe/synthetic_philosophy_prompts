You are an epistemologist specializing in the iterativity of knowledge and the epistemology of introspection, and I need your help preparing for a recorded interview. My guest next week is a philosopher who has spent roughly a decade defending a position she calls bounded KK. The core of it is this: knowledge does iterate — you can genuinely know that you know — but only up to a limited depth, with the ceiling fixed by the reliability of the faculty that produced the first-order belief; so the KK principle is not simply false at the first step, as the standard anti-luminosity line would have it, but true up to some finite order and silent thereafter. She couples this with a second claim, which she treats as the more important one: that the epistemically fundamental status is not knowledge but being in a position to know, and that while iterated knowledge is bounded, the iterated "in a position to know" status is unrestricted.

I want questions that turn on the actual machinery of this view, not invitations for her to restate it. Before you draft anything, think carefully and at some length about the structure of the position: what work bounded KK is meant to do that plain KK and plain anti-luminosity each fail to do; what could fix the bound in a way that is not simply stipulated; how the bound interacts with the margin-for-error reasoning that drives the anti-luminosity argument in the first place; and what the switch to "in a position to know" is really purchasing her — whether it is a genuine repair of KK or a change of subject.

Then draft eight to ten interview questions. They should include, at minimum: one that forces her to say what the bound is a function of and why it is not gerrymandered to fit the cases; one that presses the margin-for-error machinery against the bounded claim, asking whether the same sorites-style slide that kills unrestricted KK does not simply relocate and eat the bound from above; one on whether a bounded KK can still ground the kind of first-person authority that motivated KK in the first place; and one on whether the position, once the bound is in place, collapses into a form of skepticism about higher-order knowledge that she would not accept. For every question, supply a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; where she is likely to deflect with a stock move — for instance, treating the bound as a research program rather than a commitment, or re-describing a failure of iterated knowledge as merely a failure of iterated "position to know" — make the follow-up harder to deflect with than the question it follows.

Keep the tone collegial but genuinely pressing, avoid generic biographical or warm-up questions, and make sure that every question is answerable from the content of the position itself rather than from any particular text or citation.
