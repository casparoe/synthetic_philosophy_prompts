I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to write a short piece for a lit mag about Aristotle's sea battle problem and fiction. I've got a rough argument and I'd like you to tell me where it's wrong, not just where its pretty.

Here's the gist. In a novel there are no future contingents. Before I write page one, the ending is settled, by me. So every sentence about what happens later — she leaves him in the spring, the shop burns down, the dog comes back — is already true or already false when the reader meets it, even though it's cast in the future tense. Which makes fiction a world where bivalence holds for future-tense claims without any of the usual costs: nobody's freedom is threatened, because the characters aren't real. Fiction is the one place where the sea battle problem just dissolves.

But then suspense is a puzzle. If the outcome was fixed before the reader arrived, why does she bite her nails? My answer: she isn't uncertain about the fictional future, she's uncertain about which kind of book she's in — a book where the lover dies or one where he doesn't. Her uncertainty is about conventions and my intentions, not about a fact inside the story. The story's future was never open; her model of me was.

The part I'm least sure of: I have a vague memory of a position in the branching-time literature, Belnap maybe, that says the future is genuinely open and there are seperate real branches, but only one of them is the actual future — a single line running through the tree — so statements about tomorrow are true or false after all, just not knowable in advance. That's the shape I want for my outline. Does that view actually exist in that form, and have I got it right? And does my analogy survive it, or am I just saying authors are gods, which is trivially true and therefore not worth an essay?

Comments on accuracy especially, but also tell me if the thesis is boring. And if the framing is confused from the ground up — say, if you think fictional truth is just authorial decree and the comparison to real future contingents is a category error — say so straight out, I'd rather know than be flattered.
