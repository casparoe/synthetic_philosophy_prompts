I'm a PhD student in philosophy, currently in a seminar on logical positivism and, on the side, writing about the epistemology of first-person reports of anomalous experience, and I keep running into something that looks to me like a clean reductio. Ayer built his reputation on the claim that talk of life after death is not merely false but meaningless, that sentences about surviving death lack cognitive content altogether; and then, after his own cardiac arrest near the end of his life, he reported an experience involving a red light and what he took to be figures attending to him, and by his own account the episode left him shaken and, on his own description, weakened his atheism even while he insisted his philosophical position was untouched. It seems to me that if the man who spent decades telling us that afterlife claims are nonsense then treats his own glimpse of the other side as a live hypothesis worth agonising over, the verification principle is refuted by its own author's psychology, and the experience is a datum the positivist simply cannot absorb. A colleague in the seminar has muddied this further by claiming that Ayer, in his later work on personal identity and survival, actually argued that survival of bodily death is an intelligible and non-self-contradictory hypothesis rather than a piece of nonsense, which, if true, seems to me to give away even more. So I'd like you to sort this out: what Ayer actually held, at the different stages of his career, about whether claims about surviving death are meaningful, whether they are intelligible, and whether they are evidentially supported, and whether those three questions can come apart in the way I have been running them together; what the near-death episode can and cannot count as evidence for; and whether his reaction to it tells us anything at all about the truth or the tenability of the verification principle, or only about the man.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way one philosopher writes a long letter to another: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And I'd like you to be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and to single out the point you are least sure of, rather than presenting everything in the same register of assurance.
