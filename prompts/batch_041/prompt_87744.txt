I am a doctoral candidate in jurisprudence, and I am writing to ask you to referee an argument that broke out in my department's law-and-philosophy reading group. The session had been on the Hart–Devlin exchange over the legal enforcement of morality, and two other members — neither of them me, and I honestly could not tell which of them had the better of it — spent the second hour in a sustained back-and-forth. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it was actually conducted, not a summary verdict on the debate at large.

By way of background, so that nothing turns on your recalling the details: Devlin's position, as our group reconstructed it, is that a society is held together in part by a shared morality, that a society may therefore use the criminal law to enforce that morality, and that the proper limit on this power is the intolerance, indignation, and disgust of the ordinary person. Hart's reply, again as we reconstructed it, is that this conflates genuine harm to others with mere offence, that the claim that moral deviation causes social disintegration is an unsupported empirical prediction, and that the state's warrant to coerce extends, with narrow exceptions, only to the prevention of harm.

The exchange ran roughly as follows.

Move 1 (the first member): Devlin's argument is at bottom a prediction — stop enforcing a shared morality and the society will come apart — and Hart's decisive objection is that there is no evidence for the prediction. Hart wins.

Move 2 (the second member): That is a caricature. The disintegration claim need not be a causal prediction about decay; it can be a conceptual point about morality as a single fabric, so that one strand cannot be pulled without loosening the rest. And because the law is itself a source of moral instruction, inconsistent or half-hearted law corrodes a norm's authority quite apart from any contagion effect. On that reading, Hart's demand for sociological evidence simply misses the argument.

Move 3 (the first member): Recast it that way and you have abandoned what made legal moralism distinctive. A purely conceptual fabric claim shows only that one has reason to disapprove; it does not show why the state has a reason to act that is not merely a reason to disapprove. And the law-as-teacher point is one Hart can absorb, since he already grants that law shapes positive morality while denying that this licenses enforcing it. So Devlin either stands on the empirical claim, which is unsupported, or retreats to ground Hart already holds.

Move 4 (the second member): There is a third option. Treat a society's shared morality as a threshold public good — like a general norm of civic honesty or a shared vocabulary of mutual restraint — whose value does not decline smoothly but collapses once participation falls below a critical mass, and where each individual defection is, taken alone, harmless and unobjectionable. On that model the disintegration thesis is a clumsy proxy for something real: cumulative, non-additive erosion. And it gives Devlin a reason the state may act even where no one is harmed and even without a prediction of decay, because the marginal act may be the one that tips the threshold.

Move 5 (the first member): Even granting the model, it proves far too much. Any norm whatsoever can be redescribed as a fragile commons, so the argument hands the majority a licence to enforce whatever it happens to cherish, with no principled stopping point. Devlin at least offered a limit — the ordinary person's intolerance, indignation, and disgust — and you have quietly discarded it. And if the harm principle can be stretched to cover cumulative harms, then your commons argument is not a rival to Hart at all but a special case of it.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me which specific claims each party is right about, whether the third option introduced in Move 4 genuinely escapes the dilemma posed in Move 3, and what, if anything, both of them are failing to see.
