Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology and ethics, both fully competent, who disagree about whether we should intervene in wild nature to reduce animal suffering. Neither should be a strawman. One defends a broadly interventionist view: the amount of suffering in the wild is enormous and largely invisible to us, and the mere fact that a harm is "natural" gives us no reason to leave it alone. The other defends a broadly non-interventionist view, but not by appeal to the "balance of nature" or the wisdom of ecosystems -- that argument is too easy to knock down and I don't want it in the dialogue. Instead, have this character argue from something more interesting and harder to dismiss: that our knowledge of wild animal welfare is so theory-laden and so poor that large-scale intervention is a form of hubris with a real chance of making things worse, and that the concept of "suffering" being exported into ecology is doing more work than anyone admits.

Let me give you some specific material to build on, so the exchange doesn't collapse into generic points. The interventionist should press the following: (a) that most wild animals die in ways that would be horrifying if they happened to a pet, and that the sheer number of lives makes the aggregate suffering hard to wave away; (b) that "we can't know" is an argument against certainty, not against action under uncertainty, and we routinely act under uncertainty in medicine and conservation; (c) that the appeal to nature is doing covert work in the non-interventionist's position. The non-interventionist should press: (a) that reports of wild suffering are extrapolated from a handful of species and a handful of proxies, and that the inference from nociception to suffering is not free; (b) that ecological interventions have a track record of unintended consequences, and the burden of proof should scale with the size of the intervention; (c) that the interventionist's own framework covertly assumes a hedonic common currency across species that may not exist, so the "aggregate" being maximized may be an artifact of the arithmetic rather than a feature of the world.

Have them respond to each other's actual points, concede where a point lands, and identify at least one place where they discover their disagreement is really about something upstream -- for instance, whether welfare admits of cross-species comparison at all -- rather than about intervention as such. End the dialogue without resolving it, but with each character having shifted slightly in what they take the central issue to be.
