I'm the host of a public interview series run out of our department's ethics centre, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade defending a thesis much of our audience will find perverse: that shame, not guilt, is the moral emotion a society should be organized around, and that the question of whether a culture can choose its moral emotions is not merely hard but malformed. Her position has three moving parts, and I want the interview to live or die on the joints between them. First, she holds that guilt is ledger-shaped: it presents a wrong as a debt and finds its natural completion in discharge — apology offered, restitution made, sentence served, account settled — so that an agent can be thoroughly guilt-driven while treating morality as a schedule of prices. Second, she holds that shame is identity-shaped: it presents a wrong as evidence of what one is, cannot be discharged by payment, and therefore resists the pricing of morality in a way guilt cannot. Third, and this is where I expect trouble, she holds that shame is nevertheless inescapably audience-dependent — constituted by the regard of someone, actual or imagined, whose contempt would matter to the agent — while insisting that this does not make shame heteronomous, because the relevant audience can be the self one is trying to become. Her standing move, whenever anyone raises the cruelty of shame — the shamed victim, the honour code, the pilloried teenager — is to say that this is shame in the service of a standard not worth having, and that the objection is to the standard rather than to the emotion. I want the interview to make that move harder to reach each time she uses it.

Please draft ten to twelve questions for me, in the order I should ask them, phrased the way I would actually say them rather than as topic headings, going after the content of the position instead of inviting her to restate it. I need at least one question on each of the following, and I'd like the hardest four to come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, plus, for two of them, a second follow-up that lands if she takes the other horn: (i) whether the ledger/identity contrast is a genuine structural difference between the two emotions or a redescription of the familiar thought that guilt is about acts and shame about the self, and what would show it is structural; (ii) the split between shame's occurrence and its warrant — the person ashamed of something she does not believe wrong, and unashamed of a cruelty no one knows about — and whether her account can call a shame warranted without also endorsing the contempt of the audience that would produce it; (iii) whether a shame-centred moral economy trades resentment for contempt, and what it costs to answer a wrongdoer with an attitude that demotes her from fellow participant in a shared norm to someone beneath it; (iv) the psychology of shame- and guilt-proneness, where the behavioural signatures — withdrawal and hiding versus approach and repair — appear to favour guilt, and what her account predicts that would let us tell the two apart; and (v) the scaffolding claim behind 'can a culture choose?' — whether emotions can be chosen at all, as opposed to being by-products of institutions, pedagogy, and the amount of privacy a society affords — and, if she says they cannot, how she can still hold that one repertoire is better, since a verdict nothing can act on looks inert.

Then two cases to put to her directly, since cases will do more work here than formulations. The first is a school that abandons detention-and-apology for a circle in which the offender must say what her conduct reveals about the kind of person she is; I want her to say which procedure her view recommends, what she would count as the circle going wrong, and a follow-up for the answer that the circle has the right shape but is badly run. The second is a community in which a young woman is shamed for refusing the marriage her family has arranged; there her standard-versus-emotion distinction is under maximum strain, and I want the question that forces her either to concede that shame's structure, and not only the local standard, is doing some of the harm, or to accept that her thesis has no content beyond the truism that good standards are better than bad ones. Where a question rests on an empirical claim neither of us can settle on stage, phrase it so that it asks her to state a prediction or a commitment rather than to concede a finding. Skip anything that would let her summarize the book, anything about how she came to the topic, and anything a hostile audience member could ask just as well without having read her.
