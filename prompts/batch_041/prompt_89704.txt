I am a novelist at work on a book in which a wildlife veterinarian comes to believe that the suffering of wild animals is the largest unaddressed moral problem there is, and that her profession's ethic of non-interference is a kind of cowardice. To keep myself honest about the arguments my character will have to make, I have invited a philosopher to sit for one of the long-form interviews I host, and I want the questions in hand well before she arrives.

Her position is a deliberately narrow one, and the narrowness is what I want to press. She holds that we have duties toward wild animals and should act on them, but she does not ground those duties in the badness of suffering as such; she thinks that route stalls against the scale of the problem and the impossibility of any calculus of wild welfare. Her ground is authorship. Human activity — habitat fragmentation, climate, roads, the reshaping of rivers, the movement of species — has already remade the conditions in which wild animals live, so we are not choosing between intervening and leaving nature alone. We are choosing which of our effects they will bear, and to call the present arrangement neutral is to hide a choice. From this she derives a triage rule: intervene where the intervention is targeted, reversible, and unlikely to produce a population that cannot survive without us; leave alone whatever falls outside that rule. She is drawn to fertility reduction — contraceptives, sterilants, supplementary feeding — as the intervention that best satisfies it, on the grounds that lowering the number of animals born into a violent life is more tractable than trying to make that life gentler.

Draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I need at least one question on the authorship premise itself: why human causation should matter, given that the suffering is the same either way, and what she says about the animals in places we have not touched. I need one on the epistemic burden her position carries — what we would have to know about whether a given animal's life is worth living, and what she would have us do while we do not know it. I need one that tests her triage rule against predation, where the targeted, reversible intervention may be to let a predator starve, and one that draws out where her interest in fertility reduction leads: whether she is committed to the view that coming into existence can be a harm, and what that implies for the animals already alive. I want one on who decides — what institution she imagines doing this work, and how she answers the charge that a programme of managing other species for their own good recapitulates, in a new key, the habits of improvement and tutelage that conservation inherited from its imperial beginnings. And I want one on the objection she must feel most, since she is asking readers to stop finding wildness beautiful: what she does with the thought that a world with fewer animals in it, or without predation, would be a lesser world, and whether that thought is an argument or a taste.

Keep the questions on her argument rather than her career; do not ask her to survey the field or to recommend reading, and do not let any question be answerable by restating the thesis. Close with the strongest case for doing nothing, and ask her which of her own commitments she would surrender first if that case turned out to be stronger than she thinks.
