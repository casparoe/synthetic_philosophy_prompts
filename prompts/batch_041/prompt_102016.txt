Question: Hacking draws a line between indifferent kinds and interactive kinds, and says that classifying people can produce a looping effect. (a) Using a made-up example, say a brand new diagnosis called low-stimulation syndrome for kids who zone out in class, explain what the looping effect is and how it's different from just saying that labels have social effects on people. (b) Does the looping effect show that something like low-stimulation syndrome isn't a real disorder? Give your view and say where Hacking would agree or disagree.

Their answer: Hacking's big move is that when you classify people, unlike classifying rocks, the classification can reach back and change the thing you classified. Rocks don't care what geologists call them, but kids labeled with low-stimulation syndrome will read about it online, meet other kids with the label, and start reading their own daydreaming through it, so they end up acting more like the stereotype. That's the looping effect: the label shapes the people, and then the people shape the label, because once enough of them start presenting differently the clinicians have to go back and tweak the criteria. So the category and the people are always feeding back into each other. The upshot is that the category isn't real the way a chemical element is. Since the loop means the disorder is partly produced by the act of diagnosing it, low-stimulation syndrome is a social construction and not a natural kind, and there's no fact of the matter about which kids really have it. That's Hacking's whole point, he thinks psychiatric kinds get made up by the experts who name them, which is why he talks about making up people.
