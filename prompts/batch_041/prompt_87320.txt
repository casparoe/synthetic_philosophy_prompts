I have no training in biology or in philosophy — I am a birdwatcher and a reader of popular science — but for the past two years I have been keeping a notebook of cases that seem to break every account of what a species is, and I have reached the point where I want the specification before I look at any more theories. Please write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of species must deliver. I mean the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the findings from biology it must not contradict, each with the reason behind it — why it is a genuine demand on the theory rather than the prejudice of one school. I care because I want to know whether the lines in a field guide correspond to anything in the world, and because conservation law and medicine seem to hang on those lines, so the purpose I have in mind is a theory good enough to tell me when I am entitled to say two organisms are the same kind and what I have thereby claimed.

Here is what has driven me to this. There are chains of populations arranged around a mountain range in which each population breeds with its neighbour, but the two populations at the far ends of the chain meet without breeding; there is no non-arbitrary place to cut the chain into kinds. There are pairs of organisms that look identical to me and to most experts and yet never interbreed, so that an entire kind can hide inside what looks like a single one. There are pairs that look obviously different and live differently and yet interbreed freely and leave fertile offspring, so that looking different cannot be the test. There are lineages with no sex at all, where the whole notion of interbreeding has nothing to bite on, and lineages such as bacteria that pass genes sideways between organisms that are not parent and child, so that ancestry is not a single branching tree. There are hybrids that have become a stable kind of their own while both parent kinds still exist, which leaves me unable to say whether that is one species, three, or some other number. There is the fossil record, where a lineage changes gradually enough that any line between one kind and the next is drawn by the person drawing it, and where the ancestor is by definition not a separate kind from its descendant, or so I am told. And there is the plain fact that the same word is used to different standards in a field guide, in an endangered-species statute, and in an evolutionary textbook, and nobody treats this as a scandal.

From these I want the list of requirements, and I want you to say for each whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp. I also want to know whether some subset of the requirements cannot be satisfied together, and if so which subset, and what that impossibility itself shows — I suspect that is the most interesting part. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the theories people actually defend, and whether a theory is obliged to say that species are real features of the world at all, or whether it may treat them as our convenient divisions, and whether that is a requirement on the theory or a separate question smuggled in. I am not asking you to score the theories one by one against the list; the standard itself is what I want. Please do not give me a reading list or citations, since I have no way to look anything up. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables — just an ordinary long letter.
