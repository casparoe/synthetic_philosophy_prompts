hey, I'm in a constitutional theory seminar and theres a question thats been bothering me since last week's session.

We spent a whole class on Ulysses contracts — the guy tying himself to the mast so he can hear the sirens without steering into the rocks — and how political theorists use that to justify constitutions that bind future generations. I get the basic shape of the idea. What I dont get is how the analogy is supposed to survive contact with the actual case.

In the story it works because Ulysses is still Ulysses. Same guy, same desires, he's just temporarily unable to act on them, and at the end he's glad the ropes held. So the binding is legitimate because the bound party endorses it — he just can't get himself to act on that endorsement in the moment. Fine. Now take a constitution. A constitution is supposed to hold a majority down at the exact moment that majority doesn't want to be held down, otherwise you wouldn't need one. And the majority isn't enchanted or drunk. It's just a different majority with different views. So it looks like I have to pick one: either the constitution only constrains people who are temporarily irrational and would come around (in which case a court striking down a popular law is just calling the voters drunk and promising they'll thank us later), or the voters are clear-headed and simply disagree, in which case the constitution has no legitimate claim on them and constitutionalism collapses into plain majority rule.

Both options seem bad, and my professor clearly accepts neither, so I'm missing something. Two things that make it worse for me:

1. The crew. In the story the same crew ties him up and unties him. In a real constitution the people who wrote it are dead, and the people bound by it never agreed to anything. Doesn't that turn a self-binding into one group binding another group? That seems like a completely different moral situation.

2. Eternity clauses — the kind of provision that's supposed to be unamendable, so the later self literally can't untie itself no matter how clear-headed it gets. Is that still a Ulysses contract, or is it just a dead hand? And does it maybe show that the precommitment story was never really about the bound party's endorsement in the first place?

Concrete version if it helps: imagine a constitution that entrenches a balanced-budget rule, and then a well-informed majority wants to repeal it during a recession. Is that the same kind of thing as Ulysses, or not? And if not, what actually makes a constitution binding on people who disagree with it?

Please answer in American English, and skip the reading list and citations — just walk me through where the analogy holds, where it breaks, and what the real options are. thanks!
