I belong to an interdisciplinary reading group — half economic historians, half development economists — and we keep circling the same question: why some former colonies grew rich and others did not. At our last session a visiting economist ended a long argument about colonial institutions by saying that geography is destiny, the thought being that tropical disease burdens, soil and crop endowments, and access to sea routes fixed the fates of whole regions long before anyone wrote a constitution, so our talk of institutional legacies was chasing a shadow. I suspect the phrase is doing several jobs at once, some of them close to platitudes and others very strong empirical claims, and that he was trading on the ambiguity. Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be asserting — a disambiguation rather than a verdict — and for each reading spell out what exactly is claimed about geography's causal role (necessity, sufficiency, a binding constraint, a raised probability, a source of lock-in or path dependence), which outcome it is meant to explain (income levels today, growth rates, the timing of industrialization, the quality of institutions themselves, or merely the durability of whatever arrangement happens to exist), and over what time horizon?

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, contentious, or outright false; whether it is even testable given that we have a single historical record and no way to rerun the experiment, and if so what kind of evidence would count for or against it; and finally which reading was most plausibly the one doing the work when he used the phrase to shut down the institutions discussion, and whether that reading actually entails his conclusion or merely sounds as though it does. Please keep the answer to the theses and their assessment — no reading list, no citations, and no country-by-country tour — because what I need to carry back to the group is a clear statement of what the slogan commits you to under each reading, and of where the argument actually turns.
