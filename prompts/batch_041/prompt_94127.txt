I'm putting together a little zine of poems about philosophical thought experiments and I need one for the extended mind. Pantoum, please — the proper kind, four or five quatrains, where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next one, and the last stanza loops round so that the poem's opening line is also its closing line.

The speaker should be Otto, the guy with Alzheimer's and the notebook from the Clark and Chalmers paper — the museum trip is fine as the scene. But the point is I want the form carrying the argument instead of the lines explaining it. In a pantoum a line keeps its words and changes its job, and thats exactly the parity claim: the same bit of information doing the same work whether it's sat in a notebook or in a head. So by the end the reader should geniunely not be able to tell which lines are Otto's and which are the notebooks. Let that happen gradually, don't announce it.

Two things to work in. First, the objection that this proves far too much — if the notebook counts as memory then so does my phone, so does the internet, so does my friend who remembers my appointments for me. I want that as a protest somewhere in the middle, and then the same line coming back later in a spot where it reads as a plain description of how things are rather than a complaint. Second, the detail everyone forgets from the paper: he doesn't double check the notebook, he just takes what's in it, and the notebook ends up mattering to him roughly the way a body part would. Make the first feel like trust rather than credulity and the second feel like that without spelling either out.

Dont let it resolve. And please don't use the terms extended mind or coupled system anywhere in the poem — if the form is doing its job we shouldn't need them. Someone who's read the paper should find it right; someone who hasn't should still come away with a good poem.
