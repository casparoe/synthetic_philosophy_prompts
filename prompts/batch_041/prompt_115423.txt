I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Hume's first Enquiry and I have argued myself into a reading of the chapter on miracles that I suspect is indefensible but that I can no longer see my way out of, so I would rather you diagnose it than reassure me. The reading is this. Hume's argument is a single a priori demonstration whose conclusion is that no testimony could ever make a miracle credible. A miracle is by definition a violation of a law of nature; a law of nature is established by the fullest possible evidence, namely the uniform testimony of experience; and since no testimony can outweigh evidence of that strength, the maxim that belief should be proportioned to evidence delivers a verdict without our having to examine a single witness. On this reading the conclusion is not about what anyone has in fact reported — it is a conceptual truth — and what it really establishes is that miracles are not the sort of thing one may rationally believe, and perhaps not the sort of thing that happens at all.

Two things bother me about it, and I have not been able to make them lie down. First, the reading makes the maxim self-defeating: if belief must be proportioned to evidence, and the maxim is precisely what tells us how to weigh evidence, then a maxim that instructs us to reach a verdict before weighing anything is not a maxim of evidence at all. Second — and this is the case I have been using to bait the seminar — take a consortium of laboratories with continuous, tamper-proof instrumentation reporting that a conserved quantity is reliably violated under conditions of extreme scrutiny, replicated across a dozen independent sites, with no error or fraud discoverable. On my reading we are required to reject the report out of hand, and we are required to do so for reasons that have nothing whatever to do with religion, which I had always taken to be Hume's real target. The same treatment would evidently fall on the first reports of meteorites, or of a previously unknown disease, which seems to me the reductio.

So what I want is a clean diagnosis. Where exactly does my framing go wrong? Is there a way of stating Hume's conclusion that leaves it genuinely dependent on the evidence rather than antecedent to it? And if the argument in fact turns on claims about the actual historical record of testimony rather than on the definition of a miracle, what remains of it once those claims are separated out and assessed on their own? I would rather have the mistake located precisely than be told that the standard reading is otherwise.
