I am presenting a paper at a small joint workshop next term and need a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defense of the paper itself.

My thesis is that Avicenna's flying man has been misread by both its friends and its enemies. The case as he sets it up is a person created in a single instant, complete and intact, suspended in the air with his eyes veiled and his limbs held apart so that they do not touch one another or feel the resistance of anything; such a person, Avicenna says, would affirm that he exists while affirming nothing at all about his body or its parts. I argue that this is not an argument for the soul's immateriality. It is an argument that there is a mode of self-awareness that does not rest on any awareness of the body, and that claim is neutral about what the mind is made of. Read this way the flying man escapes the familiar objections, in particular that it moves illegitimately from conceivability to possibility and that it presupposes what it sets out to prove. The cost is that it stops doing the metaphysical work Avicenna wanted from it, and I say so in the paper.

The audience is three groups. First, historians of Arabic philosophy, who know the corpus far better than I do and will object that I have detached the passage from its argumentative context and from Avicenna's own faculty psychology. Second, phenomenologists, who will say I have reinvented the lived body and the cogito badly, and that the case is incoherent because a self-awareness with no bodily horizon is not a possible experience at all. Third, analytic philosophers of mind, mostly physicalists, several of whom work on the question of which first-person judgments are immune to error through misidentification — roughly, which ones cannot be wrong about who it is that they are about; they will press on whether the absence of bodily awareness in the case is a phenomenological claim or an epistemic one.

Give me the prep sheet. I want the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each phrased the way the group that raises it would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Add a short list of what I should concede up front before the questioning turns hostile, and finish with the one objection I am hoping nobody raises. Where an objector would ordinarily rest on a passage, put the point in the objector's own words rather than quoting: I will not be quoting in the session, and I would rather not have wording in front of me that I cannot check.
