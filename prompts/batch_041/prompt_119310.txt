I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I somehow got voluntold into running a monthly "reading group" at work. It's basically an excuse for 12 of us (all engineers, mostly infra/ML, nobody's read any philosophy) to argue in a conference room for an hour and a half. I want to do a session on whether a reinforcement learning agent can actually suffer, and specifically the idea that a reward signal is like a little squirt of pleasure or pain. I don't want to just talk about it, I want them to feel the thing.

So: design me an in-room activity, 75-90 min, ~12 people, no special gear beyond paper, pens, and a whiteboard. The idea should come out of what happens, not out of me lecturing. I'm thinking they should basically build and run a tiny reinforcement learning loop by hand, and the twist is that I want the setup to make them genuinely unsure whether what they're watching (or doing) is suffering, in a way that doesn't just collapse into "obviously it's just numbers."

Some stuff I want baked in, because I think this is where it gets interesting and where they won't have memorized an answer:

- A round where the negative reward is just a step cost on the way to a big payoff (they have to walk through the bad squares to get the good one), and a round where the negative reward ends the episode right there. I want them to notice that one of those feels much more like pain than the other, and to have to say why.
- A round where the reward signal is lying: a square that's secretly the best move but reads as negative, and a square that reads positive but leads nowhere. I want the difference between the number and the thing the number is supposed to be about to become visible to them.
- A role-swap where one person is the agent and the other person is the reward function, and then a version where the numbers come from a printed lookup table instead of a human. Same map, same behavior. I want the room to feel the difference between "a person is doing this to me" and "this is just happening," and then have to admit the agent can't tell the difference.

Deliverable: the rules and materials, the exact sequence of rounds with a rough timeline, how points/tokens work (no real money, but make the negative stuff mildly and safely unpleasant for the person playing the agent, like a buzzer or having to stand up for a bit, so the flinch is real), and then a debrief that maps each moment back to the actual philosophical question of whether a reward signal is valence. In the debrief, I need the two obvious deflationary replies to be on the table (a chess engine avoids losing too and nobody thinks it's suffering; and reward is just a number the optimizer climbs, not a feeling) and the strong reply to them, and I want you to be honest about which side the activity nudges people toward and whether that's fair.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My guesses are that some loud person says "it's just numbers" in minute three and kills the room, and that everyone gets competitive and forgets to introspect, but I'm sure there's more. And be straight with me about the one thing the activity absolutely cannot reproduce and that I'll have to just tell them myself.
