QUESTION 3 (10 marks)
Walzer is standardly read as holding that combatants on both sides of a war are moral equals — that what a soldier may do to an enemy soldier does not depend on whether his own side's cause is just. (a) State as precisely as you can what this claim asserts, and distinguish it from at least two claims it is often confused with. (6 marks) (b) Explain the main objection McMahan presses against it, and say what does and does not follow from that objection about how soldiers fighting for an unjust cause should be treated, morally and under the law. (4 marks)

STUDENT ANSWER
The moral equality of combatants says that soldiers on opposite sides of a war have the same moral rights and duties in the fighting itself, whatever the justice of their respective causes. Walzer's defence of it turns on two things. First, ordinary soldiers aren't the authors of the war: they don't decide on it and they usually can't know the full story about whether it's justified, so it's unfair to make their rights in battle turn on a question they have no way of answering. Second, the rules of war only do their job if they're the same for everyone — the war convention has to be symmetrical, otherwise it just becomes another weapon the stronger side can use. Putting these together, the doctrine says that both sides' causes are equally just (or at least that we should treat them as such when it comes to fighting), which is why an unjust combatant doesn't wrong a just combatant by trying to kill him.

McMahan denies this. On his view, whether you may kill someone in war depends on whether they're liable to be killed, and liability tracks threat: whoever poses an imminent threat to others is liable to be killed in defence of those others. Since it's the unjust combatants who are doing the threatening, they're the ones who are liable, and the soldiers resisting them are not. So there's an asymmetry — the just side may kill, the unjust side may not — and the symmetry of the war convention is a fiction that mainly benefits aggressors. He does think most ordinary soldiers aren't culpable in any deep sense, since they're often ignorant or coerced or both, but that goes to blameworthiness rather than to what they may do.

My objection is that this proves too much. If unjust combatants really aren't permitted to kill anyone, they should be prosecuted as murderers, and every soldier in an army fighting an unjust war is in the same position as a murderer. That can't be right: it isn't how the law of armed conflict works, and an army that believed it was fighting for a just cause would have no reason to restrain itself. So I'd defend Walzer here, though I think the moral equality of combatants is probably better understood as a legal convention than as a deep moral truth.
