[20 marks] Appiah argues that moral revolutions—the end of duelling in Britain, the abolition of slavery in the British Empire—are driven not by moral argument but by honour. Eric Williams's thesis about abolition says something different again. Explain what each account claims, assess how far they compete, and say which you find more convincing for the end of duelling. Keep the causal question (what made the change happen?) separate from the justificatory question (what, if anything, made it right?), and say which question each theorist is answering.

Student's answer:

'Appiah's central claim is that moral argument is basically powerless on its own; people don't stop doing something because they are convinced it's wrong. They stop because it becomes dishonourable. In the duelling case, the old aristocratic honour code had made it a mark of courage to accept a challenge; Appiah shows that the practice died when the code shifted and duellists came to be seen as ridiculous or cowardly rather than brave. The same structure explains abolition: Wilberforce and the abolitionists didn't win by proving slavery was unjust—plenty of people already knew that—they won by making slaveholding a source of national shame and by appealing to Britain's honour as a Christian nation. So for Appiah, honour is the engine; argument is at best the lubricant. Notice that this is a purely causal story: honour is a motive, not a justification. Appiah isn't telling us why abolition was right, only why it happened.

Eric Williams's thesis is usually read as the opposite: economic interests were the primary cause, and the humanitarian rhetoric was at best secondary. Once the West Indian sugar economy declined and free trade became more profitable than the old colonial monopoly, the British ruling class lost its stake in slavery and abolished it. On this view, the moral argument was not the engine; the changing profitability of the plantation economy was. Williams is therefore a materialist critic of Appiah's idealism, and he is answering the same causal question as Appiah.

I think the two are less opposed than they look. Williams can explain why abolition became politically possible when it did, while Appiah explains what motivated the people who campaigned for it. But for duelling, Appiah is clearly stronger: there was no economic interest in duelling, so Williams-style explanations have nothing to say. The only real driver there was the changing honour code. Where I'd qualify Appiah is that arguments can help to redefine what honour demands, so they aren't purely epiphenomenal. So my verdict is that Appiah gives the better general account of moral revolutions, with Williams as a useful corrective on timing and interests.'
