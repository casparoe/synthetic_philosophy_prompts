I've been writing fiction for about fifteen years — four novels, the last two in close third — and I've gotten stuck in a way that has turned into a philosophy problem, which is annoying, because I don't have the vocabulary for it. The setup in the new book: my protagonist spends the first hundred pages quietly doing the exact thing she later tears into her brother about across a dinner table. The reader has watched her do it. He hasn't. I know the scene should land, and I know exactly why it curdles when it doesn't, but every note I make to myself is craft-talk — the reader's ledger versus the other characters' ledger, unreliable narration, dramatic irony, the difference between a character being right and the book agreeing with her, whether we've seen her pay for the same sin, how much forgiveness budget she has left, the whole you-of-all-people beat. Then I read a couple of things by philosophers about what I gather is called standing to blame — the idea that you can only hold someone to a standard if you're not in the same violation yourself, that a hypocrite's rebuke can be completely true and still somehow out of order — and it felt like people arguing in a dialect adjacent to mine about the same dinner table.

So here's what I want. Restate that whole debate — the forfeiture line (you did it yourself, so the right is gone), the complicity line (you're in it up to your neck), the it's-not-your-business line (nobody asked you), the difference between blaming someone and merely saying something true about them, whether the hypocrite's blame is impermissible or just inert, and where the third party who's watching from the couch fits in — entirely in my vocabulary, the one above, as faithfully as you can. One by one: what translates cleanly, what only survives with bending, what has no expression in it at all. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Is what's left over evidence that my vocabulary is missing something real, or evidence that the philosophers were leaning on a picture that dissolves the second you have to stage it with two people at a table and a reader watching? Be strict about not smuggling — don't just rename the philosophy with film-school words and call that a translation. I don't want plot advice and I don't want to be told what my character should do. Cap it at 800 words, and open with the one-sentence version of what survives the trip and what doesnt.
