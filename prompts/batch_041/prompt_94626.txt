Background: In a reading group, the discussion turned to the classic Molyneux question. Suppose a person has been blind from birth and can distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch. Suppose she suddenly gains sight and is shown the two objects. Would she immediately know, by sight alone, which is the cube and which is the sphere? Two graduate students, Alex and Bea, disagreed.

Alex: Yes, she would. She already has the concepts cube and sphere, and those are spatial concepts. Vision is just another way of presenting the same spatial properties. If she can apply the concept to the tactile presentation, she can apply it to the visual presentation, because the property is the same.

Bea: No, she would not. Having the concept does not mean she can apply it in a new sensory format. Tactile and visual representations are format-specific. She would have to learn the correlation between how a cube looks and how it feels. Until she learns that, the visual experience would be a meaningless pattern.

Alex: But consider a person who has used a sensory substitution device for years. The device converts a camera image into a vibrotactile pattern on her back. She can already tell a cube from a sphere using the device, though she has never seen. If she then gains sight, would she not recognize the cube immediately? The device has already given her a spatial representation that tracks the same property.

Bea: The device case is different. She has learned to interpret the device's patterns. That is exactly the kind of learning I say is needed. The device does not show that vision alone would suffice; it shows that with training, a new sensory signal can be mapped onto spatial concepts. And the device's output is not visual, so it does not tell us whether the first visual experience would be intelligible.

Alex: But the device's output is spatial: she feels edges, corners, and depth. If she can transfer from touch to the device, why can she not transfer from the device to vision? Both are spatial formats. You are making a very strong claim: that no amount of prior spatial knowledge can bridge the gap between modalities.

Bea: I am not saying no amount. I am saying the original Molyneux case does not establish immediate recognition. The device user has had training with a spatial code. The real question is whether the first visual experience, without any training, would be sufficient. And I think the answer is no, because the visual system has to learn to extract the relevant invariants. The device might help her learn faster, but that is not the same as immediate recognition.

Alex: Then you are treating the visual system as a blank slate. But if she already knows the cube by touch, and she sees a cube, the object itself is causing both experiences. Why would she not recognize the same object?

Bea: Because recognition requires more than the object causing an experience. It requires the experience to present the object as the same kind of thing she already knows. And that requires the visual experience to be structured in a way that matches her existing spatial concepts. That matching may be innate, but it may also be learned. The Molyneux case is precisely the test of whether it is innate.
