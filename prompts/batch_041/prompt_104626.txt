Me and my friend got into a two-hour fight last night about whether a person's "I" can be spread across more than one brain, and I need someone to actually sort it out for me.

His view is basically Hofstadter's: the self isn't a thing, it's a pattern — a strange loop that a brain happens to be running — so it's not welded to one skull. I said ok, but then that just sounds like Parfit, and Parfit also says the self isn't a further fact over and above psychological continuity — yet he seems to draw almost the opposite conclusion about what death means and what we should care about.

So what I want is a real comparison of Hofstadter's distributed-self picture and Parfit's reductionism, not two summaries stapled together. Where do they actually come apart, given that they both deny the self is some indivisible extra thing sitting behind the pattern? Does Hofstadter's strange loop do any work that Parfit's continuity and connectedness don't already do, or is it just Parfit with a nicer metaphore? And on the dead-loved-one case, do they really disagree about whether anything of the person survives, or only about whether it's rational to care about it?

Then the case that wrecked us: a couple together forty years, their loops deeply tangled, and one of them gets replaced bit by bit over years — Theseus's ship style — until the new version is indistinguishable from the old. Hofstadter: the pattern is what counts. Parfit: what counts is continuity and connectedness. Do they end up saying the same thing about whether the original person is still there, and about whether the other partner is right to feel they're still with them? Finish by actually picking a side on the distributed-I question and tell me which bullet each view has to bite.
