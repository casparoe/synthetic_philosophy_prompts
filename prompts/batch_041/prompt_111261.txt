Last night a friend and I spent about three hours arguing about Descartes and animals after a seminar, and I came out of it way less confident than I went in. I'd always treated the animals-are-just-machines thing as one of those views you're allowed to roll your eyes at, but he wouldn't let me, and every time I tried a comeback he had a sharper version of the position ready. He kept insisting it isn't the claim that a dog is a wind-up toy, it's a claim about what the evidence could ever show: he says we can explain everything a dog does — the yelping when it's hurt, the tail between the legs, the way it works out how to get the food off the shelf — without positing that there's anything it's like to be the dog, so the burden is on me to name the observation that would force the extra ingredient in. Then he had three tests he kept running me through: that no animal has ever used signs to tell us anything, or to say something about what isn't right there in front of it, whereas a human who can't speak at all will invent a language of gestures; that animal cleverness is always a narrow trick, a whole repertoire tuned to one situation, while human reason is a general-purpose instrument you can turn on anything; and that a mind is one and indivisible, one per body, so if animals had minds you'd have to say what happens to the mind when you cut a worm in half. There was also a distinction he kept drawing that I could never hold onto, between an animal having sensation and its having thought — like it could feel but couldn't be aware that it feels — and he claimed Descartes himself makes some version of that concession somewhere in his letters, which I half suspect he made up.

What I want is a dialogue between two people who are both actually good at this, so I can watch the whole thing play out instead of just losing an arguement in my kitchen. One is a defender of the Cartesian line in roughly the shape my friend gave it — the burden-of-proof move, the language test, the narrow-trick-versus-general-instrument point, the one-mind-per-body point, the sensation-without-thought distinction — and he should be the strongest version of that view, not a cartoon. The other is a critic who thinks the whole thing is a machine for protecting a conclusion, and he needs equally specific ammunition: that we infer other human minds from exactly the same kind of behavioural evidence and nobody calls that inference illegitimate, so the Cartesian owes an account of why the dog case is different that doesn't just assume the answer; that the language test may be measuring whether anyone ever bothered to teach the animal, or whether it has the right kind of throat rather than the right kind of mind; that a mechanical explanation of pain behaviour is only complete if you've already decided the inner side does no work; and that the indivisibility of the mind is either something smuggled in from theology or a claim about what we can imagine rather than about what's true.

Keep them on each other's actual points — when one lands a blow the other has to answer it rather than sliding to a new topic — and let each concede whatever he'd honestly have to concede, including the Cartesian when he's cornered; don't have the narrator rescue him. Don't resolve it tidily, since I don't think it resolves, but at the end have them say as precisely as they can what the one disagreement left underneath all the others is, and why it doesn't look like more evidence would settle it. Two people talking, no essay framing, nothing I'd have to go look up, and don't let the critic win just because he's the one I already agree with.
