I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years as a field ecologist — salmonid population work, mostly, if you can believe that paid the bills — and now that the knees have given out I've been reading philosophy in the evenings and occasionally inflicting it on dinner guests. Which brings me to my problem. An old roommate of mine ended up in behavioral genetics, and my neighbour across the street is a sociologist who is convinced the whole heritability business is a racket dressed up in algebra, and ever since they met at our new years thing they've been at it over dinner — same argument, different night, and by now I honestly can't tell whether they disagree with each other or just use the words differently. I want to hand them both a pen and make them write the document their argument should have produced. So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the camp that says a heritability coefficient is a real, estimable, causally meaningful parameter of a population and the camp that says it's descriptive variance-accounting with no stable causal content.

What I want in it: a numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign — what h-squared is a ratio of; what population and what range of environments it's relative to; what it does and doesn't imply about any individual's trait value, about immutability, about group differences, and about the gap between estimates built out of within-family comparisons (siblings, adoptions) and estimates built out of between-family comparisons; and whether they both accept that a high heritability is fully compatible with the trait moving a great deal if you change the environment. Then the precise points of disagreement, each written in wording the side holding it would accept as fair rather than as a strawman. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody — and for each crux a concrete case, design, or finding that both of them agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction for it written down in advance. Two things I'd especially like them pinned on, because I suspect that's where the real fight is hiding: first, whether there's any sense in which the genetic share of the variance is a fact about the trait that survives changing the distribution of environments, and if so what sense; second, whether an estimate has to be invariant under interventions that leave the variance alone but shift the mean — or the reverse — before it's worth calling causal at all. And please flag which parts of their fight are just verbal: if they turn out to mean different things by "genetic", or by "explains", or by "environment", say which sense each is using and whether anything actually turns on it.

Format it as the agreed-claims list, then a disagreements table (claim / camp A's wording / camp B's wording / what would settle it), and if there's a piece they'll never agree on, put it at the bottom under a heading that says so plainly instead of smoothing it over. No citations or reading lists needed, neither of them reads anything anyway.
