I'm a doctoral candidate in epistemology, and next term I'm running the graduate seminar that covers the paradoxes of belief. I've been trying to settle how the lottery paradox and the preface paradox should be presented, and I've reached a working view that I'd like you to interrogate rather than simply endorse. It seems to me that the two are at bottom a single paradox in different clothing: in each case a set of beliefs that are individually impeccable turn out to be jointly inconsistent, and in each case the culprit is the assumption that a belief can be adequately justified by something short of certainty. In the lottery, every ticket is overwhelmingly likely to lose, so we feel entitled to believe of each one that it loses — yet we also believe that some ticket wins. In the preface, an author believes each of her claims and, being honest about her own fallibility, also believes that at least one of them is mistaken. On my reading the author is simply being inconsistent and ought to be told to withhold belief from the conjunction, just as the lottery player ought to withhold belief from any single ticket.

If that is right, the cleanest resolution is a single rule covering both cases: never admit a merely probable proposition into your belief set. I've been calling it the "no statistical beliefs" rule, and I propose to teach the lottery as the fundamental paradox, with the preface as a corollary of it — the author's awareness of her own fallibility being, on this account, just a statistical belief about herself. So my question is whether this is the right way to frame the seminar, and, if it is, how I should motivate the rule to students who will object that it is absurd to say they may not believe their flight will land safely or that their kettle has boiled.

I should add that I'm aware I might be forcing the two paradoxes together for the sake of a tidy syllabus, and that the proposed rule may be doing more work than either paradox actually licenses. If I have misdescribed either paradox, or if the framing is confused in a way that makes this the wrong question to ask in the first place, I would far rather you told me so plainly than produced a fluent answer to a question that should not be put in that form.
