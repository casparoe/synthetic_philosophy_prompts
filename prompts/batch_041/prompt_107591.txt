I have come to accept the view that the correct target for aligning a very powerful artificial intelligence — and, more deeply, the correct account of what we have most reason to want — is the coherent extrapolated volition of the relevant population: roughly, what we would want if we knew far more, thought far better, were more the people we wish we were, and had grown up together long enough for our wishes to cohere rather than collide. The proposal is Eliezer Yudkowsky's (2004), and Nick Bostrom treats it in Superintelligence as a serious candidate whose parameters remain unresolved. I want it stress-tested, not defended.

To keep the criticism aimed, I propose to split it into three commitments, and you should tell me if that carving is wrong — in which case, recarve it and proceed:

1. The idealization commitment: the authoritative object is a function of idealized rather than actual preferences.
2. The coherence commitment: the idealized preferences must be brought to a fixed point of reflection, made consistent with one another, rather than left as the conflictual mess they are.
3. The authority commitment: the output of that process has genuine normative authority over what we now want, and is what a benevolent agent would be right to implement.

For each commitment, what would count against it? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results that should move me, tagged with which of the three commitments each one hits and with what would have to be true for the view to survive it. Sort them by how decisive they are: what would be fatal, what serious, what merely awkward. Where a defeater is not an objection but a consequence — something the view is committed to that turns out to be unacceptable, a case it cannot describe, a result showing that it collapses into a rival — say so.

Two objections I cannot place, and would like you to place for me. The indeterminacy objection: there are many admissible idealization procedures, each internally coherent, and they do not converge on one another, so the target is not determinate and the view quietly reduces to whatever idealization function its designer happened to write down — a collapse into arbitrary fiat. The estrangement objection: if extrapolation is thorough enough to produce coherence, the resulting preference set may be one that no living person would recognize as their own, and then the authority commitment owes us an account of why that stranger's wishes bind us, an account that seems to require either that we identify with the extrapolated self (which the estrangement case denies) or that we defer to it for reasons independent of our present selves (which is a rival theory of value in disguise). Are these real defeaters or artifacts of how I have set the view up?

I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualification — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a regulative ideal.

Structure your answer in five sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

"The position, restated" (~150 words): the strongest version of the view, including what it claims that its main rivals — actual-preference aggregation, a fixed objective morality, and straightforward moral enhancement of the designers — do not.

"Defeaters, from fatal to awkward" (~700 words): the ranked map, each item tagged by which commitment it targets and by the kind of consideration it is.

"What the defender concedes" (~250 words): which of these a committed proponent should accept rather than deflect, and why conceding them is or is not costly.

"Where the view is insulated" (~250 words): your honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and what that does or does not show.

"Verdict, confidence, and residual doubt" (~200 words): your verdict on the view as I have stated it, how confident you are in each main claim you have made, and the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
