Here's what I'm defending. The whole Orwellian vs. Stalinesque framing of the color phi phenomenon -- did the brain revise the experience before presenting it to consciousness, or did consciousness happen in real time and memory get rewritten afterward -- is a false dichotomy, because both options quietly assume that an experience has one determinate time of occurrence. What color phi actually exposes, I want to argue, is that we've been conflating two different things: the time at which a representation is *tokened* in the brain, and the time its *content is about*. The mid-flight color change can be tokened at, say, 200 ms while its content is about a change that occurred at 100 ms, and once you separate those, "when did the subject experience the change" has no single answer -- not because it's epistemically hidden behind memory, but because the question asks for a property the experience simply doesn't have. It's a category mistake dressed up as an empirical puzzle.

To give them something concrete to shoot at, I'm proposing my own probe. Instead of asking subjects what color the moving dot was -- which obviously forces a retrospective judgment and is the first thing they'll attack -- I want a probe-detection task: at various delays after the second dot, flash a low-contrast probe in one of the two colors and measure detection threshold. If a representation of the mid-flight color is tokened, it should transiently bias detection toward that color, and the onset of that bias dates the token. If the bias for the second color only starts after the second dot is physically on the screen, then you've dated the token; and if the content is about an earlier time than the token, my distinction is doing real work. It's my design, so don't worry about whether someone's already run it -- I want the objections, not the literature.
