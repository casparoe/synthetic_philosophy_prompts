For the past year I have been writing a novel whose spine is a coroner's inquest: twelve men in a shuttered room deciding what happened to a girl, with the reader seated among them while they decide it. The book has pushed me toward a question I am not equipped to settle on my own. I have read, in the way a layman reads, about the argument — the Condorcet jury theorem, as I gather it is called — that if each member of a group is even slightly better than a coin toss at answering a yes-or-no question, then a sufficiently large group will be almost certainly right; and I have read enough about the long quarrel over the assumptions behind that result to know that the quarrel is where the interest lies. I understand the claim well enough to be gripped by it, and not well enough either to defend it or to attack it.

I am a novelist by trade. My analytical vocabulary is the vocabulary of craft: point of view and focalization, the reliable and the unreliable narrator, free indirect discourse, subtext, dramatic irony, the distinction between showing and telling, the implied author, the gap between what a character says and what a scene establishes. That is the vocabulary into which I would like you to translate the theorem and the dispute over its assumptions, as faithfully as the translation can honestly be carried off.

Take the theorem's moving parts one at a time: the proposition the group is deciding, which in a novel is rarely as tidy as a yes or a no; the individual's competence; the independence of the individual judgments; the sincerity with which each judgment is expressed; the aggregation rule; and the conclusion about the group's probability of being right. For each, tell me what it becomes in my terms, or that it becomes nothing at all. Then give me the residue report. Which parts survive the crossing intact? Which could be carried across only by quietly changing the subject, so that what arrives on my side is no longer what left yours? Which have no counterpart whatsoever in the way a novel works? Be scrupulous about the smuggling: if the translation succeeds only because independence has been redefined as characters who do not talk to one another, or competence as the reader's trust in the narrator, say so, and say whether the redefinition is fair or is sleight of hand.

I am equally interested in which way the residue cuts. If narrative has no way to say that a group is 0.94 likely to be right, is that a hole in my vocabulary, a real feature of collective belief that novels cannot render, or evidence that the theorem is doing something with numbers that has no analogue in the thing I am trying to depict — or, for that matter, that the claim is less substantive than its mathematical dress suggests? And consider the objections rather than the theorem itself: that actual groups fail the independence condition because they share a culture and correct one another's errors; that deliberation manufactures agreement without manufacturing accuracy; that a confident majority can be confidently and unanimously wrong. Do those cross the border more easily than the theorem does? A novel, after all, is largely a machine for demonstrating correlated error, and I suspect my craft has more to say about a room full of people being wrong together than about a room full of people being right.

Close with the briefest answer you can give to the question I actually care about: does any of this correct how I should imagine twelve men in a shuttered room arriving at a verdict? Not plotting advice — a correction to the picture.
