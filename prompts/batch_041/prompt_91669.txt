I teach a comparative ethics seminar — 16 undergrads, 80 minutes, no prep they'll actually do. I want to run Confucian role ethics as something they experience rather than a lecture about the Analects. The claim I'm after isn't the usual "Confucians care about family and ritual." It's two sharper things. First, the self isn't something that has roles, the way a person has hats; the roles are all there is, and there's no leftover individual behind them to consult when two of them pull apart. Second, a role isn't owned by one person — what my role asks of me depends on how you're playing yours, so if you're failing as a son, my obligations as your father change. Which makes the usual seminar reflex — step back, find a neutral standpoint, apply a principle — exactly the thing that's supposed to be off the table.

Design the activity so they walk into that. I'm imagining roles assigned as directed ties between actual people in the room (nobody gets a free-standing role like "virtuous person"), a small contested pot of something — the family's land, say — and a few forced decisions. Give me the rules, materials, the order of rounds, and how to handle the moment where a counterpart drops out: does the role still make demands once there's nobody on the other end?

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto those two claims, tells me what'll predictably go wrong the first time (I expect they'll treat the roles as costumes they can take off and start appealing to universal principles anyway) and how to keep that from killing it, and is honest about which part of the view the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
