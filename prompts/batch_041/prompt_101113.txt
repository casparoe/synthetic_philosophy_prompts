In my history class we've been studying Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and my teacher illustrated it with an image I've seen credited to Weber: material interests are the engine that drives the train of history, while a society's ideas are the switchmen who decide which track the train takes. The point of the image is to show that culture can be a real cause and not merely a reflection of economic life — a switchman genuinely determines where the train ends up, so ideas are not just smoke. I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than take it on trust. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the engine, the fuel, the tracks, the switch, the switchman, and the destination — and then say where the mapping breaks. Which disanalogies actually matter for the work the image is being asked to do, and which are harmless? What does the train picture quietly presuppose — for instance, that the tracks are already laid, or that motive power and direction can be cleanly separated? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that culture is a cause survive on its own, or was the analogy carrying it all along? Throughout, separate what is settled among people who know the Weber literature from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
