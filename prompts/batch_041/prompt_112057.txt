For a few years now a friend of mine and I have been reading about archaeology as a hobby — neither of us has any training in it — and he has become extremely opinionated about what can and cannot be concluded from what comes out of the ground. I have been keeping a mental list of the verdicts he gives on finds we read about, and I would like you to work out what he will say about the case we are currently arguing about, rather than simply guess at it.

His verdicts so far, as well as I can remember them. Forty nearly identical clay figurines found in a pit sealed beneath a house floor: evidence of a recurring household ritual, and one of the few cases where you can say something about what people believed, because the things were made over and over and buried under the floor instead of used. A single finely carved bone pendant found in a rubbish heap: tells us nothing, since one object could have been lost or thrown out by somebody. A hoard of bronze axes and a cauldron in a peat bog a long walk from the nearest settlement, with dozens of similar deposits known from other bogs: an offering, and about as safe as this kind of claim ever gets. Swords in a river, deliberately bent and snapped: an offering, because somebody took the trouble to ruin something valuable on purpose. A very finely decorated pot in an ordinary house: just a pot, because decoration is not religion. Three hundred identical shell beads in a household rubbish heap: workshop debris, not a deposit, because rubbish is where lost and discarded things end up in bulk. A figurine with its head snapped off, set upright against the inside of a doorway: deliberate, because that is a placement and not a loss. A stone with a groove that looks like a face, picked up loose on the surface: a stone, because people see faces in stones. At one settlement, a single house twice the size of the others and full of goods from far away, with the same pattern at several other settlements: a ranked society, a real institution rather than one successful farmer.

The case we are arguing about. At a settlement currently being excavated, eleven of the fourteen houses have a shallow niche in the wall of the main room. Ten of those niches contain a single small unworked pebble about the size of a thumbnail; the pebbles are all different stones and none of them is worked or valuable; seven of the niches have a smear of red ochre on the back wall; and nothing else was found in them. No other site like this is known, and the excavators have started calling the pebbles votive.

What I want is an argument, not a verdict. First, set out the rules he could be running that fit every one of the rulings above, including at least two that fit all of them equally well and any that I have missed, and say what each of those rules predicts about the new case. Second, tell me which rule you think he is actually running and why, and which of his earlier verdicts he would have to give up or reinterpret if he turned out to be running the other one instead. Third, name a further case, different from this one, that would force those rules apart, and say what each rule would have to say about it. Finally, tell me which of his rulings are really claims about what people did and which are claims about what they believed, and whether he has any right to the second kind at all.
