I teach a 90-minute decision theory session to roughly 20 upper-level undergraduates and graduate students, and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the Ellsberg pattern rather than just hear about it. Have them bet points rather than money on a known-composition urn and an unknown-composition one; then elicit from each of them the minimum winning chance at which they would be indifferent between the ambiguous bet and a lottery on a known chance, and put the group's two elicited numbers, for red and for black, side by side on the board so that the shortfall from 1 is something they compute themselves. Then run two further rounds: in one, the ambiguous urn's composition was itself fixed by a coin flip they did not see, and in the other it was fixed by a previous participant who was told to make their lives hard. Nothing should be named until the debrief. Give me the rules, the materials, and the order of the rounds, plus a debrief that maps what happened onto the sure-thing principle and onto the main families of ways decision theorists have tried to make these choices coherent. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot show, that is, what I will still have to argue for rather than let them see. Write in American English.
