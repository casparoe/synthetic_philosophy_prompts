Rich countries report large sums as development aid that never leave the donor country at all — the cost of hosting refugees, cancelled debts, the overhead of their own aid agencies — while much of what does leave is tied to purchases from the donor's own firms or conditioned on policy changes the recipient government would not otherwise make. The enterprise remains discretionary throughout: budgets rise and fall with donor politics, and no poor country has an enforceable claim on a single dollar of it, even as the same governments describe aid as the discharge of a moral duty and endorse the thought that poor countries have a right to develop. What theory of obligation does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that assistance to the poor is a matter of beneficence — generous, optional, and up to the giver — or of justice, owed as a matter of right? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the accounting rules and the conditions strictly require from what they merely fit comfortably with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of what the rich owe the poor would equally license it. Finally, if the practice presupposed a duty of justice rather than a gift, what would have to change?
