I was at a departmental seminar on democratic theory last week, and a visiting speaker closed a long argument about whether rational-choice models can explain turnout by saying, flatly, 'Every vote counts.' A colleague of mine, who works on social choice, rolled his eyes and muttered that the claim is statistically false, since the chance that any single vote is pivotal in a national election is vanishingly small; another friend, who works on the philosophy of action, insisted that the speaker was obviously not making a statistical claim at all. I have been left with the uncomfortable feeling that everyone was using the same four words to mean quite different things, and that the real disagreement was less about democracy than about what kind of claim a slogan like that can even make.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a defence of any one reading, the precise theses that 'Every vote counts' could be expressing? I would like the readings ranged from the trivial to the radical: the literal arithmetical claim about ties and margins; the probabilistic claim about pivotality, including the distinction between the probability of being pivotal and the probability of being decisive given that the election is close; the expected-utility claim about instrumental efficacy; the act- versus rule-consequentialist and causal versus evidential decision-theoretic readings; the expressive or identity-based reading on which the point concerns the meaning of the act rather than its causal upshot; the Kantian or team-reasoning reading on which one votes as a member of a collective that can secure an outcome; the assurance-game and civic-republican readings; and any others you think are live. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false, and say which reading would actually support the speaker's use of the slogan to close down the turnout debate—and which, if any, would vindicate my colleague's eye-roll. Please also note where the phrase 'the paradox of voting' is itself ambiguous between the majority-cycling sense and the turnout sense, since I suspect that ambiguity is doing some of the work here. I do not need a reading list or citations; I want the conceptual map. Write in British English.
