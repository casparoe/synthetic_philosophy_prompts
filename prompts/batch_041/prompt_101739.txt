I need help prepping for something and I've been up arguing about it with my friend Priya till like 1am, which has honestly made me more nervous, not less. Next Thursday I'm giving a workshop talk to our department's ethics-and-public-policy group where I defend a broadly Scanlonian contractualist approach to how a public health body should set priorities. Basically the claim is that the right question isn't "which policy maximizes total health" but "which policy could no one reasonably reject," and that this survives the standard objection that contractualism can't handle numbers -- one person facing a catastrophic loss versus a million people each facing a tiny one, the whole "you can't add up complaints, so the view can't see the million" thing. My line is that the numbers problem is really a problem about the baseline we're measuring complaints against: a complaint is always a complaint relative to some specified default, and once you're explicit about the default, a lot of the scary cases either dissolve or get worse in interesting ways. E.g. a rule that lets a plant impose a barely-perceptible extra cancer risk on a million people to avoid one identifiable worker's death looks aggregative on one baseline and not on another, and I want to argue the contractualist can say something principled about which baseline is the right one to use. I also want to lean on the idea that some complaints are "losses from a status quo" and some are "failures to be helped," and that only the first kind even get to be aggregated -- which I think is doing real work but Priya kept telling me it's just utilitarianism with extra steps and now I'm worried she's right.

The problem is the audience is definately not friendly to this. It's mostly health economists who think in QALYs and cost-effectiveness ratios and will see contractualism as a fancy way of refusing to do the math; two or three clinicians who keep invoking the "rule of rescue" (the instinct that you always save the identifiable patient in front of you) and will want me to say that's contractualist, which I'm not sure I can; one Kantian who I'm pretty sure is going to say that if I'm measuring complaints against a baseline and letting some get aggregated, I've quietly stopped being a contractualist at all; and one decision theorist who always asks about ex ante vs ex post and risk and whether my complaints are being evaluated behind a veil or not, and I never have a good answer. I've got maybe twenty-five minutes and then a long Q&A, so I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from *this* room -- not the ones I'd get from a Scanlon seminar -- ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a short reply I can give in two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft.
