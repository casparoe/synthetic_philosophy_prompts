I should say up front that I have no academic background in philosophy — my degree is in accounting and I read this literature on my own, mostly in the evenings — so I'm asking for help with the parts of a talk that training would normally cover. A friend who teaches in the philosophy department of a nearby university has invited me to present a paper to her interdisciplinary reading group on shared agency next month, and rather than polishing the paper I want to spend the time working out what the twelve or so people in that room will actually throw at me. The room, as she describes it: two of them are Searle-and-Bratman people who will defend the irreducibility of we-intentions and think the whole game is to get the first-person plural mode of presentation right; one is a developmental psychologist who studies shared intentionality in toddlers and will insist that jointness is in place long before any mutual reasoning about contributions; one is an economist who works on team reasoning and will want to know what my account predicts in a stag hunt that orthodox game theory does not; one is a legal scholar who cares about complicity and joint criminal enterprise and will read everything I say as a claim about when people can be held answerable for one another; and the rest are graduate students who mostly care whether any of this bears on collective responsibility.

The position I'll be defending is deliberately deflationary. I want to argue that nothing is added by positing a distinctively plural intention: agents are acting together when each treats the others' contributions as a premise in their own practical reasoning and each has reason to believe the others are doing likewise, where that mutual knowledge is usually produced by cheap observable commitment signals — showing up, taking one end of the table, starting to sing — rather than by any special mental state. I also want to say that jointness comes in degrees, and I propose to measure the degree by how much each participant's plan would have to be revised if the other's contribution were removed: the more the removal unravels, the more joint the action. My test cases are a pair of movers carrying a piano down a staircase, two strangers who wordlessly form a queue at a broken ticket machine, and a case I've been calling the silent duet: two musicians who have agreed on a score and a tempo, and who then perform it in separate soundproof booths, each hearing only a click track and neither hearing the other, so that nothing passes between them during the performance at all. I think the silent duet is genuinely a case of playing together, only a thin one, and I take that to be evidence for my account rather than against it, though I expect at least two people in the room to disagree violently.

Please act as an expert in the philosophy of action and social ontology, and before writing anything, think carefully through the space of objections this particular room could raise — objections that grow out of their starting points, not the generic ones from an introductory textbook. Then give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so questions I will actually be asked, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person most likely to ask it, with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I would be bluffing. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. No reading list, please — I have no library access and would rather have the arguments. Finish by telling me what would change your mind about your ranking: which consideration, counterexample, or piece of empirical work would lead you to order the objections differently, and where your own reasoning about this room is most likely to be wrong.
