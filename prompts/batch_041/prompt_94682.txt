Question: Ian Hacking's work on transient mental illness and looping effects is often read as a challenge to the idea that psychiatric categories are natural kinds. Explain what Hacking means by a transient mental illness, distinguish the looping effect from a simple self-fulfilling prophecy, and assess whether transient mental illnesses give us reason to deny that psychiatric categories are natural kinds. In your answer, use Hacking's distinction between interactive and indifferent kinds, give one example of a transient mental illness, and consider one serious objection to the claim that looping effects undermine psychiatric natural kinds.

Student's answer:

Hacking's idea of a transient mental illness is that some mental illnesses are not timeless natural kinds but appear in specific historical and social conditions, flourish for a while, and then fade away. The classic case is fugue, the compulsion to wander, which was diagnosed in late nineteenth-century France and then largely disappeared. Hacking thinks such illnesses are real for those who have them, but their reality is not the reality of a natural kind like gold. They are made possible by a niche—a set of social institutions, expectations, and medical practices—and they loop: the diagnosis shapes the experience, which shapes the diagnosis.

This is not just a self-fulfilling prophecy. In a self-fulfilling prophecy, the prediction causes the outcome it predicts. In a looping effect, the classification and the classified interact in a more open-ended way. People can resist, reinterpret, or perform the label; the category can change direction or collapse. Hacking's dynamic nominalism says that the kind and the people in it come into being together. He contrasts interactive kinds with indifferent kinds. Indifferent kinds do not respond to being classified; interactive kinds do, because the people classified can reflect on the classification. Psychiatric categories are interactive, whereas chemical elements are indifferent. Transient mental illnesses are a species of culture-bound syndrome, like koro or amok.

Do transient mental illnesses show that psychiatric categories cannot be natural kinds? I think Hacking's answer is yes. If a category is transient—if it exists only in a particular niche and disappears when the niche changes—then it cannot be a natural kind in the strict sense. Natural kinds are supposed to be stable, discovered rather than made, and independent of our descriptions. Hacking sometimes suggests that all psychiatric categories are transient or interactive, since they all classify people who can reflect on the classification. That would mean psychiatry is not a natural science in the same way as chemistry. But this does not show that psychiatric categories are arbitrary or that they do not track anything real. It shows that they are human kinds, historically situated and normatively loaded. The looping effect is thus an argument for a cautious, historically informed realism, not for anti-psychiatry.

A possible objection is that many psychiatric categories, such as schizophrenia, have been stable across cultures and times, so they are not transient. Hacking could reply that stability across cultures is not the same as being a natural kind, because the category may still loop through local practices. Or he could say that schizophrenia is not a single kind but a cluster of looping kinds. Either way, the objection shows that the looping account is not meant to apply uniformly to every psychiatric category.
