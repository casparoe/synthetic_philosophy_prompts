Please compare two accounts of how a Bayesian agent should respond to evidence she is not certain about, and let the comparison turn on a worked case rather than on generalities.

The first account is Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics: the agent's new probabilities over a partition {E1, ..., En} are stipulated, and she updates by holding her conditional probabilities P(X | Ei) fixed and reweighting them by the new probabilities. The second is the likelihood-ratio, or virtual-evidence, treatment: the uncertain observation is modeled as a reading produced by a channel, the agent conditions on the reading, and the update multiplies each state's prior by a fixed likelihood.

The case I want worked through: three mutually exclusive and exhaustive states A, B, C with priors 0.4, 0.35, and 0.25. The agent receives two pieces of uncertain evidence. The first, taken alone, would justify moving her credence in A to 0.6, on the partition {A, ¬A}. The second, taken alone, would justify moving her credence in B to 0.3, on the partition {B, ¬B}.

Please compute (i) the result of applying the two Jeffrey shifts sequentially with the A-shift first; (ii) the result with the B-shift first; and (iii) the result of the likelihood treatment when the two readings are conditionally independent given the state and each channel's likelihood ratio is the one that reproduces the corresponding single-observation shift.

Then tell me what the comparison establishes. Is one of the three answers correct and the others mistaken, or are they answers to different questions? If the two sequential orders disagree, is that a reductio of probability kinematics, or a symptom of feeding it inputs that fail to determine a unique model? Is every Jeffrey shift on a partition equivalent to ordinary conditioning on an auxiliary variable in an enlarged space, and if so, does the likelihood treatment turn out to be the more general account rather than a rival to it? What exactly does rigidity demand at the second step of each ordering, and is that demand stable once the partitions cut across one another? Does the comparison turn on a distinction I should be drawing between uncertainty in the evidence and uncertainty about what the evidence is evidence for, or between an agent whose doxastic state is unsettled and an agent receiving a noisy measurement? And what happens when the two single-observation targets are jointly unsatisfiable, say because they sum to more than one — does that possibility tell against one account rather than the other? I would rather have the carving than the verdict: I want to know what each account is committed to, which of those commitments are optional, and which of the three numbers survives once the optional commitments are stripped away.
