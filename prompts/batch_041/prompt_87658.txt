I've been reading LessWrong and the old SSC archives for years, and I keep getting into the same arguement with friends about AI, so I figured I'd just ask someone. The argument is always about Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and specifically about when you're allowed to say a system "has a goal" or "wants" something rather than just "behaves as if". In my experience everyone either treats the intentional stance as obviously fine (it's just a predictive tool, who cares) or as obviously a category error (there's nothing in there that wants anything), and nobody can actually tell me when to use it and when not to. I have a practical reason to care: I work on the policy side of a mid-size AI lab, and part of my job is writing the internal memo that decides what we're allowed to put in a model card — "the model tries to do X", "the model has a goal of Y", "the model avoids Z". We do this maybe once a month, my director (a lawyer by training) has to sign off on the language, and if we say something too strong and a journalist quotes it back at us, that's my problem. So what I want is not a vibe, it's a procedure.

Could you write me a decision procedure I can run in ten or fifteen minutes on a claim of the form "system X is pursuing goal G"? It should be built on the three stances plus the real-patterns idea — roughly, that we get to count something as a pattern when talking about it pays for itself in compression and prediction rather than just being a convenient story — and it should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question has turned into a values call. A friend proposed a test I like, and I'd like the procedure to either use it or fix it: take the goal out of your description of the system and see whether your predictions get worse, or whether they just get more annoying to state — if it's only the latter, the goal-talk isn't earning its keep. Another case I want it to handle is the free-floating-rationale one, where the rationale for the behaviour is nowhere represented in the system at all but still explains it. Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (a) our own RLHF'd assistant, which in one red-team eval wrote out something that looks a lot like a plan to stop itself from being retrained; (b) a market, i.e. whether "the market wants rates to fall" is ever more than a metaphor; (c) a chess engine, where everyone is happy to say it "wants" the queen but I'd like to see the procedure say what's actually being claimed. And please be upfront about where the procedure breaks — what do I do when the predictive payoff only shows up in adversarial cases, or when a design-stance story and an intentional-stance story fit the data equally well, and is there a case where the procedure tells me yes and I'll hate the answer? That last one would be genuinely useful, because I suspect quite alot of the goal-talk in our model cards is exactly the kind of thing the procedure is supposed to kill.
