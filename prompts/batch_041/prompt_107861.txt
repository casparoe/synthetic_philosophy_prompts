I teach a upper-level seminar on concepts and induction -- cog sci and phil of mind mostly, a couple of CS students -- and I want to close the term with one essay question on Bongard problems. Every year I get the same two paragraphs about fluid concepts and nothing that engages the thing I actually care about, which is this: the twelve boxes never tell you which rule is the rule. Any finite left set and right set is consistent with infinitely many rules, and in the problems I draw myself there is usually at least one alternative that fits all twelve perfectly and that I would still mark wrong. I've been calling those stowaways and the students pick the word up in a week.

So please write the question (40 minutes, 20 marks) plus a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme.

A few things I need from it:

- Put a concrete problem in front of them; don't just ask them to hold forth about Bongard problems in general. The exam is on paper, so describe the boxes in prose, one sentence each, six left and six right. A shape problem if you can make it work in words; if that gets too fiddly, strings of digits or symbols are fine. And don't reuse one of the originals -- they've seen those. The model answer has to show box by box that the intended rule fits and that the stowaway fits too; no hand-waving there.

- The crux is that fitting the twelve and being the rule are two different achievements, and the twelve boxes deliver only the first. They should have to say what does the rest of the work -- the drawer's intention, a shared sense of which descriptions are simple or natural, the way a description's content depends on how it would be applied beyond the twelve -- and what that means for whether the problem has a determinate answer at all.

- I want the stowaway to come apart from the intended rule on a thirteenth box. Show them that box and ask what the problem itself licenses them to conclude about it. (My own view, which they don't have to share: strictly nothing, and the fact that it feels like something is the interesting datum.)

- Work in the other half of why these problems are hard: the rule may need a description that wasn't in the solver's vocabulary when they started -- something about relations among the shapes in a box rather than properties of any one shape -- which is why a classifier with a fixed feature set can nail the twelve and still have no idea what it's doing.

- Don't let the model answer slide into total underdetermination. That's both boring and false; the real puzzle is that there's a robust, shared sense of which descriptions are live, and where that comes from.

On the epistemic side: have the model answer be explicit about which of its claims are settled and which are contested among people who actually work on this. "The data underdetermine the rule" is arithmetic, not a position. "The drawer's intention is what makes one rule the correct one" is a position, and so is denying it. Don't let either pass as the consensus, and have the marking scheme reward students who keep them apart.

Marking scheme: four or five must-hit points, marks totaling 20, and for each one a line on how students usually blow it. Then a short paragraph on where the A/B line falls -- I want it to be about whether they can say what licenses the intended rule over the stowaway, not about how much they've read. No citations, no names required, and don't give marks for dropping names.
