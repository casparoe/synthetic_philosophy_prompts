Exam question (15 points):

In the drug case, you can save either one person, David, or five other people, but not all six. (a) Explain Taurek's argument for the claim that the numbers do not count in this case. (b) State what he thinks should be done, and why. (c) Explain how he would respond to the objection that saving the five saves more lives, so the numbers do count after all.

Student answer (written in fifty minutes, without notes):

Taurek asks us to imagine that we have a supply of a drug that will save either David, whom we know, or five strangers, but not all six. The utilitarian says we should save the five, because five lives are more than one. Taurek denies this. His central argument is that the losses at stake do not add up. When David dies, David loses his life; when one of the five dies, that person loses his life. These are separate losses, each suffered by a different person, and there is no further loss — the loss of five lives — that anyone undergoes. So we cannot say that the loss of the five is five times greater than the loss of David. Each of the six stands to lose the same thing, his life, and each therefore has an equal claim to be saved. From this Taurek concludes that the numbers do not give the five a stronger claim, and that saving the five is not required merely because they are more numerous.

What he thinks should be done follows from this. Since each person's claim is equally strong, each person should get an equal chance. Taurek proposes a lottery: each of the six names goes into a hat, and whoever is drawn determines the outcome, so that if the draw picks David we save David, and if it picks one of the five we save the five. Each of the six has a one-in-six chance of survival, which is what equal respect for each person requires. The five, taken together, end up with a five-in-six chance, which Taurek accepts as a by-product rather than as a reason.

Taurek's deeper premise is that a human life has infinite value. Since no finite number of lives can outweigh an infinite value, no amount of counting can show that the five have a stronger claim than David, and this is why the numbers cannot count in the way the utilitarian needs them to.

The utilitarian will object that saving the five has a higher expected number of lives saved — five rather than one — so the numbers do count after all. Taurek's reply is that this objection assumes what is at issue, namely that the value of lives can be summed across persons. Expected-value reasoning treats the five as one larger quantity of good, but the good at stake is not a quantity of that kind. And since the numbers do not count, the choice between David and the five is not one that reason can settle: any procedure that treats the six alike is as defensible as any other, and Taurek's lottery is simply the procedure he happens to prefer.
