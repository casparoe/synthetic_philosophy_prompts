I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, sixteen students, one ninety-minute session. The session I am planning is on original versus derived intentionality: Dennett's two-bitser and its descendants on one side, Searle's Chinese room and the systems reply on the other. The topic has become personally urgent in a way I did not expect. Last month I used a phone application that identifies mushrooms from a photograph. It returned a confident identification and a confident verdict on edibility, and I acted on it for about ten minutes before checking. The identification was wrong in a way that mattered: the species it named is one whose members are sometimes harmless and sometimes deadly, and the application had no way to tell me which I was holding. What has stayed with me is not the error but the ten minutes in which I was treating the application as though it knew.

Design a ninety-minute classroom activity that makes my students feel that pull and then see what it is pulling on. I want to be able to run it with a partition or a screen, a deck of cards, and two or three prepared confederates, without computers or internet in the room. What they should come away having felt is, first, that two systems can be behaviorally indistinguishable across every test the group can devise while we describe one as knowing and the other as merely computing, and second, that as soon as the group tries to say what licenses that difference — the origin of the procedure, the causal history, the presence of consciousness, the intrinsic character of the internal states — every candidate either fails to separate the cases or commits them to something costly. The two-bitser is the lever I want to use: a machine whose internal states are a duplicate of those of a system we are content to call a knower. Searle is the counterweight: a person who runs a procedure correctly and understands nothing.

Then lay out the activity in full: rules, roles, materials, and a scoring scheme that needs no money and yields a visible group score; the phases with timings; the point at which the group must commit in writing to a criterion of understanding, so that everything revealed later can be measured against what they wrote; the reveal that the machine was executing a written decision tree; the further reveal that the expert's judgments admit of being written out as a tree of the same kind; and a third system whose procedure was not written by anyone but grown by selection, which I want used to test whether origin does any work at all.

Then the debrief: map each phase onto the parts of the distinction, say where the two-bitser and the Chinese room pull in opposite directions, and tell me frankly which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in exposition.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle each failure.
