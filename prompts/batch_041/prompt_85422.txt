I'm teaching a stats-for-journalists night class and I keep getting the same question: does Simpson's paradox mean the pooled number is always the one lying to you? I want a case to hand out that breaks that reflex. Build me one scenario where an exposure — a drug, a school program, whatever's realistic — looks protective within every subgroup but harmful overall, and where the textbook fix (stop pooling, stratify) is actually the wrong move, because the thing you'd stratify on is partly caused by the exposure itself and partly by some unmeasured risk factor, so slicing on it manufactures the reversal instead of removing a confounder. Ordinary setting please, hospital or school district or insurance claims, and the scenario itself in one paragraph. Then do the check: state the causal model in plain words, give the three comparisons — pooled, each stratum, and what you'd actually want to estimate — say which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I swap that stratifier for a real common cause of exposure and outcome, and how a sharp skeptic who thinks you should always stratify would come after the case. If the numbers can't be made to hang together, say so instead of fudging them.
