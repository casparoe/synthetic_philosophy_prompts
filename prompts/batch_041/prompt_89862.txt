I am a high school student in a philosophy club, and I have spent the last few weeks trying to write up an argument that our adviser sketched for us about moral particularism, the view that there are no true moral principles because the same feature of an action can count in favor of it in one case, count against it in another, and count for nothing at all in a third. I have set the argument out in numbered steps and then extended it with two steps of my own, and the result now appears to prove vastly more than the position it began from. I would like a diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked innocent, and what each side has to pay.

(1) A moral principle would have to say that some feature of an action, such as that it breaks a promise, or causes pain, or gives someone pleasure, makes the same moral difference wherever it turns up, so that the feature is always and everywhere a reason for or against acting that way.

(2) No feature behaves like that. Our adviser's illustrations: that an act causes someone pain is normally a reason against it, but the pain a bully feels when he is stopped from hurting a smaller kid is not a reason against stopping him at all; that an act gives someone pleasure is normally a reason in its favor, but the pleasure a bully takes in humiliating a classmate is no reason in favor of the humiliation.

(3) So no feature carries a fixed moral valence.

(4) So no moral principle of the kind described in (1) can be true.

(5) And since no moral principle can be true, a good moral judge never needs one: judging is seeing what matters here, not applying a rule.

(6) I then noticed that the phenomenon in (2) is not peculiar to morality. That a witness sounds confident is a reason to believe her, but not when she is the sort of person who is confident about everything. That a move threatens my queen is a reason to answer the threat, but not when answering it is exactly what loses the game. That a study found an effect is a reason to believe the effect is real, but not when the study was small, unblinded, and the result barely cleared significance.

(7) So if (2) licenses (3) and (4), then (6) licenses the same conclusion about reasons in general, and there are no true general principles of evidence, inference, or strategy either, no rule of inference that holds in every case and no method that works in every context.

(8) That cannot be right. A rule of inference that failed in some situations would not be a rule of inference, and I do not think modus ponens or the law of non-contradiction are context-dependent in the way the pain case is.

I would much rather keep (2), (3), and (5) than (8), but I want to know what keeping them costs. Which step should I give up? If it is (1), and I have simply drawn the notion of a principle too tightly, then please say exactly what a generalist's principle looks like once it has been loosened enough to survive (2), and whether anything still recognizable as a principle is left. If it is the move from (3) to (4), or from (6) to (7), then please name precisely what goes wrong in that move and why the step looked so natural. And if the argument is in fact sound and (8) is the step that has to go, then say what principle can still mean.
