ok so I've been reading rationalist blogs for like a decade now — SSC, LessWrong, the old sequences, the whole thing — and there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a name for. Curious whether it has one, or if not whether you can give me a good one.

The shape of it: someone defines a kind by what it does rather than what it's made of. 'A gene is whatever gets reliably inherited and makes a difference to the organism.' 'Pain is whatever state is caused by damage and makes you flinch and avoid and moan.' 'A belief is whatever plays the belief-ish role of guiding action.' The pitch is that the definition is wide open — anything that does the job counts, no snobbery about what it's built from.

Then someone brings a weird candidate that does the job: a silicon chip, an ant colony, a committee, a thermostat, a book on a shelf, a look-up table. And instead of going 'huh, ok, so that counts too,' the person says 'no, not like that.' Which is fine, maybe. But the thing I keep noticing is what happens when you push on the 'why not.' It's never another feature of the job. It's always some property the original examples just happened to have and the weird one doesn't — same stuff it's made of, same way it came about, it's inside a head, it's the sort of thing we already agreed counts. So the 'whatever does the job' clause turns out to have been doing no work at all, and the real rule was 'relevantly like the examples I started with,' which nobody ever said out loud. The open-sounding definition is a dressed-up list of the paradigm cases.

I've seen it with genes (a prion? a methylation mark?), with pain, with 'memory' (is a written record a memory? a database?), with 'belief' (does a chatbot believe anything?). The tell is always the same: the definition is definately sold as open but the acceptance test is closed, and you can basically guess the verdict without even hearing the job description — just ask 'is it like the paradigm cases?'

So: (1) does this have an established name — a by-what-it-does definition that's secretly a disguised enumeration? (2) if not, coin me one, but I want the carving, not a label: how is this different from (a) legitimately leaning on background conditions that were never part of the stated job, (b) family-resemblance or cluster concepts, where the openness is the whole point, and (c) just being too vague to test? (3) where's the line — when is adding a 'but not like that' clause a genuine refinement of the job and when is it smuggling a paradigm back in? And (4) does this recurring mess mean there's something wrong with defining kinds by what they do in the first place, or is it just people doing it sloppily?

Also be honest with me: is this just the old dilemma about what-it-does definitions — either so loose they let in everything or so tight they shut out the interesting cases — wearing a hat? If it is, I'd rather find out I've reinvented a wheel than get a shiny new word for an old thing.
