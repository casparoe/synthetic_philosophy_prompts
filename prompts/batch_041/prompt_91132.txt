You are a philosopher of science specializing in the metaphysics of reduction and emergence, with a working knowledge of statistical physics and complex-systems modelling. Think carefully before answering, and open with a short brainstorming section listing the candidate concepts you consider and why you set each aside; only then give your verdict.

I am a condensed-matter experimentalist who has drifted into reading philosophy of science, and I keep meeting a pattern I cannot name. It arrives as a package of two claims that are almost always asserted together, as though the second were a corollary of the first.

First claim: the higher-level feature is nothing over and above the lower-level facts. Fix the microstate and you have fixed the macrostate; there is no extra ingredient, no downward force, no leftover residue. Second claim: the higher-level description is nevertheless where the science is. The dependable generalizations are stated at that level, and they survive wholesale variation in the microdetails — which is exactly why they look like laws rather than like summaries of accidents.

Three places I meet it:

1. Near a critical point, a fluid and a magnet show the same critical exponents, though their microphysics has almost nothing in common. Tinker with the microphysics and the macro-regularity does not budge. Nobody concludes that fluids and magnets are the same stuff; they conclude that one higher-level regularity has two utterly alien micro-realizations.
2. The backwards-travelling waves that form on a crowded motorway turn up in models built on quite different assumptions about how drivers behave.
3. A molecular-dynamics simulation reproduces the ideal-gas law, and someone says this shows the gas law is nothing over and above the molecules — even though the simulation had to be set up around the very macro-quantity whose status was in question.

I have been told this is multiple realizability. That seems to cover only the middle of the story, and by itself it is too cheap: plenty of predicates are realizable in many ways and carry no laws at all — being worth exactly one euro, being a piece of furniture — so realizable-in-many-ways cannot be what makes a macro-generalization dependable. I have also been told it is emergence, but the definitions I find are about unpredictability or about novel causal powers, and I am claiming neither: my point is not that we cannot compute the macro-behaviour, but that the micro-description is not a substitute for the macro-generalization even in principle, since you cannot recover it from the microdetails without already knowing what you are looking for.

What I am after is one configuration with four parts: (i) the macro is fully determined by the micro; (ii) the macro-generalization is projectible and stable under micro-perturbation; (iii) the micro-story does not displace the macro-generalization as a way of stating the regularity; (iv) no macro-entity does causal work over and above the micro-causes.

So: does (i)–(iv) taken together have an established name? If the literature splits it into two or three separately named pieces, tell me how the usual divisions run, which piece my pattern really is, and which of the four parts each candidate concept captures and which it misses. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it precisely, and give me boundary cases — at least one case that satisfies some but not all of (i)–(iv) and that you would exclude, and one borderline case you would include. Finally, tell me what turns on the answer: is the difference between calling the macro a convenient bookkeeping device and calling it a genuine level of law a substantive metaphysical difference, or only a difference in our epistemic situation? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the joints located.
