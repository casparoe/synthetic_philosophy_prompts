I'm teaching a seminar on social ontology next term and I want to do a unit on whether generations are real. Before we read anything I want the students to have a spec in hand: what would a theory of generations have to do to earn the claim that generations are real kinds, and not just labels we slap on cohorts for marketing decks and trend pieces. Write me that desiderata list.

Heres what I want it to cover, and some things I'm not sure belong on it. The boundary problem: why does the boomer cohort end in 1964 and not 1963, and is any answer to that principled or is it all convention? The fact that any difference between cohorts can be re-described as a difference in age or a difference in period -- the age-period-cohort identification problem -- and whether that makes generational explanations unfalsifiable or just hard to test. The difference between a cohort (everyone born in a window) and a generation in the thicker sense (a cohort that shares a formative experience and a bit of self-understanding), and whether the thicker sense is doing any explanatory work or just rhetorical work. The problem of within-cohort variation: if a generation is supposed to share values, what do we say about the members who dont, and does the theory have to treat them as exceptions or as evidence the kind isn't real? And the reference-group point: people can identify with a generation that isn't theirs, or disidentify with their own -- does the theory have to accommodate that, and if it does, is the kind still a kind?

Then: do generations have to be causes (does being a boomer make you vote a certain way), or can they be effects (does a shared formative experience produce a shared outlook), or is it enough that they're useful summaries? I want a criterion for what would count as evidence that a generation is a real kind rather than a useful label, not just a list of studies.

Finally, tell me which of these requirements cant all be met at once. My suspicion is that the thick sense of generation and the causal/explanatory sense pull apart, and that the moment you solve the boundary problem in a principled way you lose the shared-experience claim. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular accounts, and whether any account of generations survives the whole list or whether the honest answer is that generations are useful fictions. A table is fine if it helps.
