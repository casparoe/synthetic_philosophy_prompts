I chair the reproductive ethics committee at our academic medical center, and since last month I have also been one of the clinic's patients: my partner and I are midway through an IVF cycle and have just received a preimplantation genetic report on our own embryos. I would rather not be drafting this at all, but the committee has to settle a policy question at its next meeting, and I have told the vice chair I will recuse from the vote, so the memo needs to stand on its own for colleagues who do not share my situation.

The question arises from something new in our lab. Alongside the screening we have always done for chromosomal abnormalities and single-gene conditions, we have begun returning a polygenic report that scores each embryo on a small number of traits that fall within the normal range. Our medical director wants a rule — call it best first — requiring that when several usable embryos are available, the one with the highest score be transferred first unless the parents give a reason the clinic accepts. He argues that if selecting the child with the best chance of the best life is a genuine obligation, the clinic cannot knowingly let a couple pick the lower-scoring embryo, and that we already do precisely this whenever we select against a serious disease. On the agenda is a couple with two unaffected embryos whose scores differ by a modest amount; they want to transfer the lower-scoring one. They offer two reasons: they believe the score is close to noise, and one of them, who is short, does not want her child raised under a clinic policy that implies her own life was the worse outcome.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should land on one of three positions — best first made mandatory, selection left entirely to the parents, or a halt to returning non-medical scores at all — with conditions if you recommend conditions, and with a timeline tied to our next cycle start, roughly six weeks out. I want the reasoning to be substantive rather than gestural, and I specifically want it to work through the following:

- whether selecting against a serious disease and selecting on the basis of a normal-range trait are the same kind of decision, and if they are not, what exactly does the work in distinguishing them;
- the fact that the lower-scoring embryo is not made worse off by being brought into existence instead of the other one, and what that does to the medical director's argument;
- whether the obligation, if there is one, falls on the parents or on the clinic, and what follows for a clinic asked to transfer an embryo it believes is not the best available;
- a complication our genetics consultant raised: these scores were calibrated largely on cohorts of European ancestry and predict substantially less well for patients of other ancestries, so a best-first rule would bind different families unequally;
- whether a middle option — for instance, letting parents choose freely among the top-scoring few but not the bottom — is coherent or merely a way of avoiding the question;
- what the policy would say to people who already exist and would score low, and whether that is a reason to abandon the policy or only to word it carefully;
- the strongest version of the medical director's case, stated so that he would recognize it as his own.

No citations, please; the committee knows the literature, and I need something they can argue with rather than a reading list. Keep it to about three pages, and end with a motion I can put on the table verbatim.
