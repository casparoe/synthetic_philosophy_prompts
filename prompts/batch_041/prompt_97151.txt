I cover science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am at work on a piece about Douglas Hofstadter's thought experiment of the careenium: an arena packed with tiny magnetic marbles that bounce about and clump into larger, semi-stable clusters he calls simmballs, the puzzle being whether the marbles shove the clusters around or the clusters shove the marbles. A popular-science newsletter a colleague forwarded to me summarises the lesson as follows:

'Picture an arena full of countless tiny magnetic marbles. Left to themselves they simply bounce and stick, and the clusters they form — Hofstadter calls them simmballs — in turn knock one another about and rearrange the marbles inside them. So which level does the pushing? Do the marbles shove the clusters, or the clusters the marbles? Hofstadter's answer is the striking one: the clusters. The marble-level story is complete but tells you nothing about what is really going on; the real causal work happens one level up, among the patterns, and the marbles are merely carried along. This is his picture of the mind in miniature. Neurons are the marbles; thoughts, and the self that has them, are the clusters; and it is the clusters that cause our behaviour. The moral is that consciousness is no illusion spun off by the brain — it is a cause in its own right, with powers that the physics underneath cannot account for, which is why Hofstadter insists that a complete physical account of the brain would leave out the very thing doing the work.'

Before I build a paragraph of my own on that, I should like an accuracy audit. Taking the claims in turn, tell me what a careful reader of Hofstadter ought to accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what would make him wince or object outright; and tell me specifically whether the conclusion the newsletter draws — that the higher level has causal powers of its own which the underlying physics cannot account for — follows from anything in the experiment as it is usually set out, or whether it is doing work the thought experiment does not license. I am inclined to think the newsletter is broadly right, since I have always taken Hofstadter to be a friend of the higher level, someone who thinks the mind's business cannot be transacted in the language of neurons; but a philosopher friend tells me the newsletter gets him almost exactly backwards, and I am not equipped to adjudicate. Finally, please say where your own verdict is most likely to be wrong: which consideration, or which way of reading the thought experiment, would lead you to a different assessment. British English, please.
