I'm a TA for an intro cognitive science course and I'm marking the long-answer section of the final. One of the questions (worth 10 points) was:

Does language shape thought? Set out the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, distinguish the versions it's usually split into, and assess the empirical evidence for each. Also say whether any effects that have been found are best described as effects on perception, on memory, or just on how people handle the task, and what evidence would show that a linguistic difference causes a cognitive difference rather than merely going along with it.

And one student answered:

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis says that the language you speak determines how you think. It gets split into a strong version, linguistic determinism, where language fully determines thought, and a weak version, linguistic relativity, where language biases thought in particular tasks. The strong version is basically dead -- babies and animals think without any language at all, so thought clearly doesn't depend on language, and that also means language can't be doing much of the shaping. Since the strong version fails, the only defensible reading left is the weak one, which is pretty much what Sapir and Whorf meant anyway. The weak version does have support. Russian speakers discriminate shades of blue faster because Russian has seperate basic terms for light and dark blue, which shows they perceive the difference more finely. Speakers of languages that use compass directions instead of left and right are much better at dead reckoning. And the classic case of Inuit languages having dozens of words for snow shows how a language carves up a domain and makes its speakers notice distinctions other people miss. So the hypothesis is false in its strong form but Whorf's main idea survives, and the effects are probably mostly on perception.
