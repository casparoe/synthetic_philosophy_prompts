I have been invited to give the commencement address at the graduation of a master's program in global development. The graduates are heading into jobs at aid agencies, finance ministries, and NGOs, most of them reasonably confident that they are about to do measurable good in the world, and the dean's only instruction to me was that the address should leave them feeling that the work matters. My difficulty is that after fifteen years spent evaluating development interventions I have become convinced of what philosophers call consequentialist cluelessness: that the causal ripples of any substantial action run so far and tangle so thoroughly that a confident estimate of its net effect on the world — for good or for ill — is close to worthless, and that this is not a gap that better evidence will one day close but something structural about how effects propagate and how they would have to be aggregated. I cannot stand up and promise these graduates that their careers will leave the world better than they found it; I am not even sure I can promise them that anyone could ever find out.

Please write the address for me — six to eight minutes as spoken. It has to be honest in that sense and still work as a send-off. Keep the room in mind: a handful of faculty who know the literature on cluelessness and will wince at any hand-waving, and several hundred family members who have come to watch a first-generation graduate cross the stage and who will not enjoy being lectured about the limits of expected-value reasoning. One further wrinkle: the class has chosen a motto for its banner, to the effect that we plant trees whose shade we will never sit in, and the dean has asked me to say a few words about it. I would like to do that honestly — the motto already half-admits that we will not see the result, but it assumes the result will be shade — without quietly redefining it into something safer than it is.

Two temptations to avoid. Do not resolve the tension by suggesting that even if the good cannot be measured, we may trust that doing our best will somehow turn out for the best; that is precisely the claim the view denies. And do not let the address slide into a general sermon on the unknowability of everything, which would be both false to the view and useless to the people in the chairs.
