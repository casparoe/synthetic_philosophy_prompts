I do probabilistic risk assessment for a living — fault trees, event trees, initiating events, frequency times consequence, core damage frequency, expected fatalities per year, aleatory versus epistemic uncertainty, de minimis risk, that whole world. I have zero philosophy training and I keep bumping into a thought experiment people call Pascal's mugging (no idea who came up with it). The version I've got is: a stranger says that if you hand over your wallet he'll give you something absurd — in one telling, a quadrillion happy days of life, in another he threatens you with something horrible if you refuse — and even if the odds he can actually deliver are minuscule, the expected value of paying up is astronomical, so a strict expected-value maximizer has to pay. And if you fall for it once you can be taken again and again, forever. My gut says this is a real problem for anyone who treats expected value as the decision rule.

What I want is that whole thing restated in the vocabulary I actually use. Not dumbed down — translated. Assume I'm fluent in PRA and a total beginner in philosophy; I know probability fine, I just dont know the philosophical literature.

Give it to me in four parts, in this order:

1. "Ground rules" — about 150 words. Which of my terms you're going to lean on, and what you'll do when the mugging needs something I have no word for.

2. "The translation" — 400 to 600 words. The mugging restated clause by clause as if it were a hazard report sitting on my desk: what the hazard is, what the initiating event is, what the frequency is, what the consequence is, who's exposed, what the decision is. As faithful as you can make it — dont smuggle in a PRA assumption and call it a translation.

3. "What didn't survive" — 300 to 400 words. What got distorted on the way over, what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and where you had to fudge.

4. "Verdict" — 200 words or so. Is the residue a hole in PRA, or a sign that the philosophers were never saying anything precise in the first place?

And one thing answered straight, because it's the reason I'm asking. My instinct is that the mugger is just a hazard with no fault tree behind him — no mechanism, no initiating event, no data, nothing to model — so he never makes it into the assessment and that's the end of it. Is that a principled reason to ignore him or a bureaucratic one? Here's the part that actually worries me: the rule we work to isnt expected-value maximization at all. It's that you keep driving a risk down until the cost of reducing it further is grossly disproportionate to the benefit. If that's PRA's real answer to the mugger, tell me whether it's a principle or just a habit — and whether it still holds when the mugger knows that's how we work. And please dont just recite the usual fixes at me (cap the utility, be more Bayesian about his odds, penalize weird priors); I want to know which of those my own trade already does under another name, and where the version I have is just a rule of thumb wearing a lab coat.
