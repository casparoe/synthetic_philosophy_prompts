I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a young Cambridge mathematician who dies at twenty-six, and who spends his last months writing a handful of pages about the meaning of life. I have read the short epilogue that Ramsey wrote at the very end of his life, the one printed after his death alongside his logical papers, and I want to use it not as a doctrine to be summarized but as a hinge for a larger instrument. As I understand him, Ramsey refuses to be humbled by the size of the heavens, insists that his picture of the world is drawn in perspective with human beings in the foreground, and treats the meaning of life as something that can only be settled from inside a human standpoint, if it can be settled at all.

Please build me a taxonomy of the positions on the meaning of life in the form of a decision tree, designed so that a reader of my novel — and I, for my character notes — can be sorted by what we actually believe rather than by the labels we prefer. The branches must be labeled with the question that splits them, and each question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the positions, so no node may ask whether one accepts cosmic purpose, expressivism, or the like in those terms.

The distinctions that matter for my purposes are these: whether the universe as a whole can carry a meaning at all, or whether meaning attaches only to lives and things within it; whether importance is a fact about an object or a relation to a perspective; whether scale — size, duration, sheer number of beings — has any bearing on importance; and whether the question of life's meaning is a genuine question, a confused one, or one that admits no answer yet still demands a response.

The leaves should include, at minimum, the view that the cosmos has a purpose in which human lives participate; the view that the cosmos is meaningless while particular lives and things carry real, non-illusory meaning; the view that importance is entirely projected and meaning-talk is expressive rather than descriptive; the view that the question is not a genuine question and should be dissolved rather than answered; the view that there is no meaning yet one must live in defiance of that fact; and the view, which I take to be Ramsey's, that the question is answerable only within the human perspective, so that cosmic scale is simply beside the point. Leave a leaf open for a position I have not named.

Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then place the positions in it. For each leaf, give the position a name, use a proper name only where the view is unintelligible without one, and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note which combinations of answers go unoccupied and diagnose why, whether because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say plainly whether the tree can distinguish Ramsey's position from the nearest one to it. Use concrete cases as the diagnostic material — the eventual heat death of the universe weighed against a child's death today; a life well lived that leaves no trace and is forgotten; the last living person on earth; an unpublished novel no one will ever read; a pill that makes one content with an empty routine — and phrase each as a question a lay reader could answer about herself.

No citations, no reading list, no quotations from Ramsey; paraphrase or describe where you need to refer to him. Write in American English.
