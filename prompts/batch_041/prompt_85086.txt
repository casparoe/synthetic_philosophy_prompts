My friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about capital punishment, and we finished where such arguments tend to finish: he holds that a murderer's death is owed to him as a matter of desert, that a state which will not exact it is indulging its own fastidiousness at the expense of the victim, and that the risk of executing the innocent argues for better procedure rather than for abolition; I hold that the practice cannot be justified at all. What struck me afterwards is that his reasons and mine never met — his are about what is owed, mine about what we can know and what we become by doing it — and that we were conducting the whole dispute in a vocabulary neither of us had chosen.

So I have been rereading Beccaria — *On Crimes and Punishments*, 1764, the book that more or less started this argument — and what I want from you is not a survey of the contemporary debate but a prediction about the man. He died in 1794, and everything that gives the present controversy its shape postdates him: DNA and the exonerations it produced, life without parole as a genuine substitute for execution, decades-long waits on death row, a human-rights regime in which abolition is an obligation rather than a policy preference, and a jurisprudence that measures the practice by its arbitrariness and its unreliability rather than by its severity.

Here is my reconstruction of him; correct me where it is wrong. Punishment is legitimate only as a defence of the social compact, and its measure is the harm a crime does to society. Because the compact is assembled out of the smallest portions of each person's liberty, no one can have surrendered his life through it, and so no right to kill the condemned exists: the scaffold is "a war of a whole nation against a citizen" — his phrase, from the chapter on the punishment of death. Even if such a right were granted, the practice would remain unjustified, because its deterrent power is weak: death is a terrible but momentary spectacle, whereas the continuing example of a man condemned to hard labour for life works on the mind by repetition; and the execution itself breeds compassion and indignation in the crowd rather than the salutary terror the law intends. Yet he allows one exception — where a man, though deprived of his liberty, still has the power and the connections to endanger the state, and even then only when the state is on the verge of losing or recovering its liberty.

Granting all that, predict what he would say about the following, showing your reasoning:

1. The innocence argument. Is it continuous with his insistence that certainty, not severity, is what deters, or does it quietly concede the retentionist's premise that the penalty is legitimate in principle and merely misapplied in fact? Would he treat a demonstrated execution of an innocent person as a refutation or as an accident of procedure?

2. Substitution. The world has supplied, in life without parole, the alternative he recommended, and on a scale he could not have imagined; European human-rights law now treats irreducible whole-life detention as itself degrading. Does that vindicate his recommendation, embarrass it, or force him to say that the substitute is a lesser evil only, and not a solution?

3. The exception. Modern terrorism and the prisoner who directs violence from outside his cell are offered as the cases that reactivate his exception. Can he grant the exception without abandoning the contractarian premise that does all the work in his argument, or is the exception already a fissure in it?

4. Dignity. The argument that now prevails in much of the world is not consequentialist at all: it says that the deliberate killing of a defenceless person in custody violates human dignity whatever the effects. Is that argument available to him, alien to him, or a better statement of something he was groping towards?

5. Deterrence. He offered a psychological thesis — certainty and duration outweigh severity — which is now an empirical question, and the evidence is contested, with some studies claiming a marginal deterrent effect and others claiming that executions increase homicide. Would he welcome the empirical turn, or does it leave his argument hostage to findings he cannot control? Note that the "brutalization" hypothesis is his own claim about the crowd's hardening, put in statistical dress.

6. The politics of it. In the United States the practice has contracted into a few states and, within them, a few counties; much of the retrenchment has come through courts, clemency, and quiet non-use rather than through legislation. What is he to make of abolition that the public whose compact he invoked has never ratified? Does he read that pattern as an argument against the practice, on grounds of arbitrariness, or as evidence that the practice is dying of its own contradictions?

Finally: tell me where the record genuinely underdetermines the prediction and you are extrapolating, and where he would have to give up a premise rather than merely extend one — that is, whether the man who emerges is an abolitionist of principle or one whose abolitionism is contingent on facts about deterrence and the fallibility of courts. I am not asking for a literature review or for citations, and please do not put words in his mouth that he never wrote. And if you can, say whose case he would have thought rested on the better kind of reason, setting aside whose conclusion he would have endorsed.
