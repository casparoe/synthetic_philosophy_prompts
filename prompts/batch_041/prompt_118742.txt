The exam question (one of four short essays; suggested time 40 minutes):

State the Principal Principle (PP) as precisely as you can. Be explicit about whether PP constrains conditional or unconditional credence, and about the role that admissibility plays in your formulation. Then apply PP to two cases.

Case 1: An agent regards it as equally likely that a coin's objective chance of landing heads is 0.5 and that it is 0.7. She has no other information relevant to the outcome. What credence in heads does PP recommend?

Case 2: An agent knows that the objective chance of heads is 0.5. She also knows that a predictor whose announcements about such tosses have been correct 90% of the time has announced heads. The predictor's accuracy is a stable fact about the predictor and is not itself a matter of chance. Is the predictor's announcement admissible evidence? What credence in heads does PP recommend?

Finally: does PP merely record something about our concept of chance, or does it place a substantive constraint on any acceptable philosophical account of what chance is? Explain how your answer bears on the Humean best-system analysis of chance.

The student's answer:

The Principal Principle (PP) is the principle that connects subjective credence to objective chance. In its precise form, where ch_t(A) = x is the proposition that the objective chance of A at time t is x, and where E is the agent's total evidence at t, PP states that a rational agent's credence in A satisfies

C(A | ch_t(A) = x ∧ E) = x,

provided that E is admissible. Put informally: chance is the guide to credence, and PP says that rational credence follows the chance.

The admissibility clause is essential. If the agent's evidence already settles whether A is true — if it entails A or entails its negation — then the agent cannot defer to the chance, because the chance has nothing left to tell her. Evidence of that kind is inadmissible, and PP does not apply to it. Admissible evidence, by contrast, is evidence that leaves the outcome open. The admissibility clause therefore restricts PP to cases in which the agent's evidence does not preempt the chance.

PP is a normative bridge principle, not a definition. It does not tell us what chance is; it tells us how chance and credence must be related if the concept of chance is to do its work. Because of this, PP constrains philosophical accounts of chance: an account on which the chances are not the quantities that rational credence should track has not succeeded in giving an account of chance at all. On the Humean best-system analysis, chance is the probability function that appears in the best systematization of the total history of the world, where the best system is the one that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit. PP connects this analysis to rational credence: the probabilities of the best system are the ones that credence should defer to, so the analysis tells us what chance is and PP tells us why chance matters.

Case 1. The agent regards it as equally likely that the chance is 0.5 and that it is 0.7. Here PP cannot tell her what to think, because PP is a principle of deference to a particular chance, and the agent does not know which chance obtains. She must first settle her credence about the chance; only then can PP guide her. So PP is silent in this case, and her credence in heads is constrained only by the requirement that it be a coherent function of her credences about the chance.

Case 2. The agent knows that the chance is 0.5, and she knows that the predictor, who is right 90% of the time, has announced heads. Is the announcement admissible? The announcement does not entail that the coin lands heads, since the predictor is fallible. So the announcement leaves the outcome open, and it is therefore admissible. PP applies, and it tells us that the agent's credence in heads should be 0.5. This may seem to conflict with the predictor's reliability, but there is no conflict: the predictor's accuracy is a fact about the predictor, not about the coin, and it does not change the chance. An agent who raised her credence to 0.9 on the strength of the announcement would be treating the predictor's track record as information about the chance, which it is not.

PP is not a report about how we happen to use the word chance; it is a constraint that any account of chance must satisfy. This is why it bears on the best-system analysis: the analysis must deliver probabilities that can play the credence-guiding role, or it has not delivered chance. PP and the best-system analysis therefore support one another, with the analysis supplying the metaphysics and PP supplying the epistemology.
