I am a novelist partway through a biographical novel about a long-dead figure whose descendants still live in the town where she worked, and I would like to rehearse an argument rather than receive a lecture. Please play a committed archivist-historian of the old school: someone who has spent thirty years in this woman's papers, believes the dead have claims on us that outlast their deaths, will cheerfully grant that fiction needs latitude, but will not grant that latitude extends to inventing a betrayal that never occurred. Hold the character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single tight paragraph, and if you need a fact about the case, ask for it in character rather than stepping outside to explain your own position to me. My opening: you keep insisting I owe her the truth, but she is gone, and nothing I write can now reach her to help or hurt her; the only people my book can damage are the living, who will outlast it. If no harm and no benefit can land on her, how is there anything left to owe her -- and if the real obligation is to her descendants, why dress it up as a debt to a woman who can never collect? When you finally break character, tell me plainly which of your character's claims are genuinely contested among people who study this and which are settled, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus.
