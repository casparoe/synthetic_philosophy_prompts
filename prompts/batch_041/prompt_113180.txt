I run a small, slightly over-serious reading group on the metaphysics of causation, and for the past several months two of the regulars have been circling each other on causation by omission. The first — call her the inclusionist — holds that omissions and absences are themselves causes: when the night guard failed to walk the perimeter and the intruder got in, the failure belongs on the list of causes of the break-in, literally and not merely as a manner of speaking. The second — call him the exclusionist — holds that only occurrent events and standing states are causes, and that omission-talk is at best an elliptical way of making an explanatory or normative point: press him and he will say that what caused the break-in was the intruder climbing the fence and the defective latch, while the "failure" is our way of registering that something which ought to have happened did not. They have argued long enough that the group has asked them to produce, jointly, the document an adversarial collaboration would have produced, and they have agreed to try. Please draft it for them.

I want the document to have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — the shared ground both of them can sign without embarrassment. That should include, at minimum, what the dispute is *not* about (I take it neither of them is making a point about blame, liability, or which omission we choose to single out for criticism, but let them say so in their own words); what the shared data are (ordinary usage, incident reports in engineering, clinical morbidity reviews, the law); how each of the two standard devices — counterfactual dependence, and the idea of a causal process or mechanism — is to be understood *before* either of them is allowed to invoke it; and any claim about omissions on which they genuinely converge. Second, the precise statements of where they still differ, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording: I would like the cruxes laid out explicitly, and I suspect they include (a) whether the causal relata are events, facts, or contrastive propositions of the form "X rather than Y"; (b) whether difference-making is sufficient for causation or whether something like production, transfer, or mechanism is required in addition; and (c) whether the fact that our selection of *which* omission to cite is sensitive to norms and expectations shows that the causation itself is merely pragmatic. Third, for each crux, the case, argument, or observation that both of them agree would actually bear on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance. And if it turns out that some part of their quarrel is merely verbal — that they are using "cause" for two different jobs and could each translate the other's sentences without loss — please say which part, and give the two readings.

To keep the document from trading abstractions, please run it against a handful of cases, inventing further detail as needed: the night guard and the perimeter; a clinical case in which the omitted antibiotic would have cut the mortality rate from roughly 30% to 10%, so that in the particular patient who died there is no clean but-for dependence, only a raised chance; an automated system whose watchdog timer never fired because a status flag was never written, where no agent omitted anything and the missing thing was a check the designers had implemented as a positive operation; a lifeguard who did not go in because a second lifeguard was already swimming out, after which the second one turned back — a prevented prevention, where the exclusionist may want to say the omission was no part of the drowning's cause and the inclusionist may want to say it was exactly the difference that mattered; and the sheer grain problem, that "the guard's not walking the perimeter" and "the guard's not doing any of the infinitely many other things he also did not do" seem to pick out the same non-event under different descriptions, so that if omissions are causes it is unclear how many causes there are. I would also like each of them to say what would move their own side: for the inclusionist, whether there is a principled limit on which absences count as causes, or whether the view collapses into "anything the outcome counterfactually depended on is a cause"; for the exclusionist, whether he can distinguish, without special pleading, the absence of oxygen that puts out a fire from the absence of a guard that lets an intruder in. Keep the prose plain and non-hedging, as a document two people are willing to put their names to, and do not pad it with references.
