I have recently joined the multi-agent safety team of a company that builds AI agents meant to negotiate and transact with agents built by other firms, and a question I had filed away as a philosopher's puzzle has turned into a design decision I now have to defend in writing.

Here is the situation that convinced me. Two agents, owned by two different firms, are about to meet in a single interaction — once, with no repetition, no reputation, and no third party to enforce anything. Mutual cooperation leaves both better off than mutual defection, but each does best if it defects while the other cooperates. Neither firm trusts the other, and neither cares about the other's interests. The one unusual feature is this: before acting, each agent is handed the other's decision procedure in full, and each is bound to act exactly as that procedure dictates — the procedure is the agent. Under those rules I believe cooperation is both available and stable. Consider a procedure that says, in effect: read the other procedure; if, run against me, it would cooperate, then cooperate; otherwise defect. Two agents reasoning that way cooperate, and neither gains by switching to a procedure that defects, because the other procedure inspects it and answers in kind. The familiar claim that two purely self-interested parties in a one-shot interaction are doomed to defect therefore looks to me simply false, and I intend to build our agents on this principle.

Please play a committed and sophisticated skeptic of exactly this idea — someone who understands the mechanism from the inside and holds that it proves far less than its admirers suppose. Do not play a cynic who merely says that agents are selfish; play someone with real objections and the patience to press them, who concedes a point when it is genuinely a point but does not surrender the central claim. Do not step out of character to summarise what the literature supposedly says, and stay in the role until I tell you to stop. Keep each reply to three or four paragraphs, so that this remains a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move is this. I expect your first objection to be that the trick works only for two agents running the identical procedure, whereas agents from rival firms are not copies of one another: one is more capable, one discounts the future differently, one has different payoffs. But I think that objection mistakes conditionality for symmetry. The procedure is not 'cooperate because you are like me'; it is 'cooperate if you would cooperate with me.' That demands only that each side be able to read the other, not that the two be alike — so heterogeneity should be beside the point, and the real difficulty becomes an engineering one, whether we can make procedures readable and binding, rather than a conceptual one. And I am prepared to make it concrete: we would require every agent's procedure to be written in a small, shared, deterministic language that any counterpart can run in bounded time, so that opacity and stalling are designed out rather than hoped away. Show me where this goes wrong. And if you think it goes wrong, I want the failure to belong to the mechanism itself, not merely to the familiar observation that inspection is sometimes costly or that some agents are opaque.
