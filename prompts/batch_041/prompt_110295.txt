I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've fallen into a long-running argument between two camps about what makes a letterform the letter it is. The first camp — call it the skeleton camp — holds that an 'a' is an 'a' because its parts stand in the right structural relations to one another: a closed counter, a right-hand stem, some kind of terminal. On this view a style is a consistent deformation of a shared skeleton, and the skeleton is what every 'a' has in common. The second camp — the resemblance camp — holds that there is no skeleton at all: 'a'-hood is graded similarity to a cloud of remembered examples, sorted out by a community of readers, with no part that has to be present. These two have been talking past each other for years.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. I want the claims they agree on, including about cases that look like they should split them; each remaining disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things would actually move one side; and, for each crux, the concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Please work through at least these: the single-story 'a' and the double-story 'a'; the '@' sign, which seems to contain an 'a'; a solid, filled-in 'a' silhouette; the same shape serving as 'a' in one writing system and as a different character in another; an 'a' whose bowl is left open at the top, one stroke short of a 'u'; and an ambiguous shape that reads as 'a' in one word and 'o' in another. Also tell me which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, and where the two sides reach the same verdict for different reasons. No references, please — I want the arguments, not the literature. Format it as a numbered list of agreed claims followed by a table of disagreements: the point at issue, each camp's wording of its own view, the crux, and what would move each side.
