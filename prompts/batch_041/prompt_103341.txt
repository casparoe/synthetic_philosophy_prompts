I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am putting together a talk and a companion blog post for a technically minded audience on a question I keep running into whenever global business or development comes up: what exactly makes an act corrupt, and whether the word as used by international institutions quietly encodes a specifically Western or liberal set of standards. I would like you to build me a decision tree that takes a reader from a small number of starting commitments to a named position in this dispute. The purpose is to sort people by what they actually believe about concrete cases rather than by the label they would apply to themselves, so no node may simply ask the reader whether corruption is culturally relative or which camp they are in.

Please construct the tree from the logical space outward: first identify the questions that genuinely divide the positions, then set out which combinations of answers are coherent, and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Each internal node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should carry the name of the position (or unnamed, if nobody plausibly defends it) together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

I want the following distinctions to do the branching work, though you should add others if the space requires them. First, the source of the standard: does the reader think there is a culture-independent fact about whether an exchange is corrupt, or that the operative standard is the written rule of the institution involved, or the prevailing norms of the society, or something a society could be wrong about? Second, where the wrongness is located: in the violation of a role or a trust, or in the harm the act causes, or in neither, the wrong being merely conventional. Third, whether the agent's motive and the form of the transfer change the classification — a gift given in gratitude after the fact, an obligation recognized in advance, an expected return, a payment disguised as something else. Fourth, whether the explanation of widespread corruption is individual deviance or a stable collective equilibrium in which no one could unilaterally do better. Fifth, whether calling a practice corrupt is itself a political act that imposes one group's standards on another, and whether that observation is a reason to stop using the word or only a reason to use it carefully.

The nodes should be anchored in specific cases, and I would like these included: a customs officer paid a customary fee to process a shipment that would otherwise sit for weeks; a manager who hires a qualified cousin over a marginally better-qualified stranger; a patient's family giving a surgeon a gift after a successful operation; a firm that pays a local official's nephew's tuition to be shortlisted for a contract; a legislator who directs public funds to his own district; a company that obeys the local law where it operates but violates its home country's anti-bribery statute; a ceremonial or religious gift that functions as an entry fee to a market; and a tip that the recipient has come to expect. You may add cases if they separate positions more cleanly.

I need the following positions to appear as leaves: a universalist view on which there is a single moral standard that can indict a society's own norms; a legalist or formalist view on which corruption is defined by the rules of the institution; a conventionalist view on which the society's norms are the last word; a principal-agent view centered on the betrayal of an entrusted role; a collective-action or systemic view on which the individual moral frame is the wrong unit of analysis; a functionalist view on which some such payments are efficiency-enhancing and condemning them is ethnocentric; a virtue or character view; and, if it is distinct, a two-level view that separates the definitional question from the question of blame. Note explicitly which combinations in your space are unoccupied and say briefly why — because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, for instance a legalist and a conventionalist in a society whose laws already track its norms, or a principal-agent and a collective-action view once the principal is itself the problem.

Aim for roughly eight to twelve internal nodes and seven to nine leaves, and give me the result as an indented tree with the questions on the branches and the positions at the tips. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations; reason it out from the structure of the disagreement, and where you need to describe a well-known definition or slogan, paraphrase it rather than quoting it.
