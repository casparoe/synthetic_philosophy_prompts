For the better part of a year a close friend and I have been having the same argument, and after a long weekend of it I have decided that I would rather understand what kind of argument it is than win it. He is a classical utilitarian of the ordinary total-summing sort: pleasure and pain lie on a single scale, and the only question worth asking about a state of affairs is where the sum falls. I am drawn to something else — I hesitate to call it negative utilitarianism, because I do not think suffering is the only thing that matters, but I do think the claims of suffering carry a priority that the summing picture cannot express, so that preventing a given quantity of pain is not simply to be traded off against producing a comparable quantity of pleasure. The infuriating thing is how much we agree about. We agree that suffering is bad for the sufferer and not merely bad impersonally; we agree about the empirical facts of which creatures are sentient and roughly how badly they fare; we agree that the world contains far more suffering than anyone should be willing to accept; and on nearly every concrete case — factory farming, the suffering of wild animals, whether to have children — we converge once we have talked long enough. Yet we cannot state our disagreement without it dissolving. He asks whether there is any quantity of mild contentment that would compensate for an hour of agony, and I want to say no and cannot say why, and he finds that answer arbitrary; he tells me that when I insist on priority I am merely stipulating a peculiar shape for the value function, a shape for which I have produced no argument, and I tell him that when he adds, he has already assumed the commensurability that is the whole question. He then offers the familiar reductio — that on my view a painless extinction of all sentient life would be a positive good if it foreclosed the possibility of future suffering — and I genuinely cannot tell whether I am meant to deny the premise, deny the inference, or deny that the conclusion is absurd. When I press him on the asymmetry that seems to me the most obvious thing in the world — that it can be wrong to bring a sufferer into existence and yet not wrong to refrain from bringing a happy being into existence — he replies that this is person-affecting residue, a piece of grammar mistaken for an insight, and that if we had separate words for pleasure and for pain we would simply stop arguing; I think that is precisely backwards, since the dispute is about whether one word will do. The same exchange resurfaces in our reading group whenever the subject comes up, and nobody there has moved either.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement in axiology, a verbal disagreement about how the word 'better' ought to be used, a disagreement about the epistemology of moral intuition — about which intuitions count as data and which as confusions to be explained away — or two different questions asked under a single heading, namely how to rank states of affairs against one another and how to weigh the claims of those who exist against the claims of those who might? I have a hypothesis of my own, which I should like you to test rather than adopt: that my friend and I do not in fact disagree about value at all, and that everything turns on whether the procreation asymmetry is a datum to be explained or an artefact to be explained away. If that hypothesis is right, say what would move the argument and what would count as progress; if it is wrong, say where the real fault line lies and why it is not the one I have identified.

Argue from the positions as I have described them rather than surveying who has said what: I have no library to hand, and a reading list or a set of citations would be of no use to me. Tell me plainly what kind of disagreement this is, what a resolution would even look like given that diagnosis — a distinction, a worked case, a formal result, or a change of question — and give me a straight answer about whether anything could shift someone who begins on the other side, or whether this is the sort of dispute that should be expected to sit there permanently, and if so why that is not simply a failure on both our parts.
