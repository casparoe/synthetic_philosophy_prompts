Consider the following claim: for a continuous quantity, the principle of indifference fails to determine a unique credence function, and the failure is genuine underdetermination rather than a defect that a better formulation of the principle could repair. Give me your credence in that claim, decomposed.

I want the sub-questions the number rests on, each with its own rough probability and one line of justification: (i) whether the classical circle-and-chord case, with its three parameterizations — uniformly random endpoints, uniformly random radius then a uniform point along it, uniformly random midpoint in the disk — exhibits a real inconsistency in the principle or only an equivocation over what a random chord is; (ii) whether some symmetry or invariance requirement on the prior, that the answer not depend on arbitrary features of how we describe the space, actually singles out a unique prior rather than merely pruning the candidates; (iii) whether the ambiguity dissolves once a physical generating procedure is specified, and if it does, whether that shows the principle is doing any work beyond the instruction to state your experiment; (iv) whether the trouble is confined to the continuum, so that indifference remains unproblematic for finite partitions and the real subject is the metaphysics of continuous chance rather than indifference itself; and (v) whether the disagreement among the candidate priors survives translation into action — if the payoffs are coarse enough, do the competing priors actually prescribe different bets, or does the ambiguity wash out?

Then combine these into your overall number, name the sub-question it is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move it most. I put myself at roughly 0.7. Finally, if a colleague insists the paradox shows only that a random chord is an ill-formed description, at what credence on the target claim would you place them, and which of the five terms do you think they weight differently from you?
