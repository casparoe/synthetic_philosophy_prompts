I run a 90-minute session for sixteen upper-level undergraduates in an ethics and philosophy-of-law seminar. They have all encountered the claim that consent is valid only if it is not coerced, not obtained by deception, and given by someone competent; they can recite the three conditions and they treat them as a checklist. I want them to feel that the checklist is neither necessary nor sufficient, and to feel it in what happens rather than in what I tell them afterward. Design the activity.

Constraints. Sixteen participants, 90 minutes, a room with movable chairs, no real money, no real stakes, and nothing that requires anyone to disclose anything personal. Participants should not be told in advance what is being tested, and the words coercion, deception, and competence should not be spoken until the debrief.

Build the session around four movements.

1. The Deal, about 20 minutes. Participants work in triads: a Requester who wants a signature, a Signer whose signature it is, and a Recorder. Each Requester receives a private card listing a fixed menu of moves and must use exactly the moves on the card; the Requester is not choosing tactics, and the group should be told this, so that no one is assigned blame by the fiction. The menu should include at least these: an accurate statement of the facts; a material omission; an outright falsehood about a matter the Signer will never consider and that makes no difference to the Signer's choice; an offer that leaves the Signer strictly better off than their alternative but that is withdrawn unless the Signer signs; a move that works by waiting the Signer out under time pressure; and a move that invokes a personal relationship to make the Signer feel obliged. Each Signer receives a private card stating what happens if they decline, but only partially: the card should make part of the Signer's alternative genuinely unknown to the Signer, so that the baseline against which any pressure would be measured is unavailable even to the person whose consent it is. Assign two triads the same Requester card but different Signer cards, so that a split verdict is guaranteed later.

2. The Slip, about 10 minutes. When the Signer says yes, the yes is recorded on a slip in a fixed form: one sentence naming the act consented to. No reasons, no states of mind, no record of what was said. The Recorder keeps a hidden record of which moves were used.

3. The Audit, about 20 minutes. Collect and shuffle the slips and redistribute them to readers who took no part in the transaction. Each reader marks the slip valid or invalid and gives one reason. Have readers commit to verdicts privately before any public poll; if you want the convergence to carry a price, award a point for each verdict matching the group's majority.

4. The Reveal and the Debrief, about 40 minutes. Return each slip to its triad and disclose the move records. Structure the debrief so that the group discovers, in order: that the readers' verdicts diverged; that the divergence tracked how well the deal went for the Signer rather than which moves were used; that identical moves drew opposite verdicts across triads because the baselines differed; that the slips drawing the most invalid verdicts include transactions with neither a lie nor a threat; and that the one transaction containing an outright lie drew almost no invalid verdicts, because the lie was harmless. Only then should the facilitator name what the group has already run into: that a threat is coercive only relative to a baseline, and that if the baseline is what the party was entitled to, the test becomes circular; the difference between consent as an act and consent as a state; the difference between a consent that is defective and a consent that is valid but overridden; and the difference between consent and mere compliance. If time allows, add a fifth round in which one Signer signs on behalf of a third party who cannot consent, and let the group discover that validating the proxy's consent requires a further authorization whose own validity is now in question.

Deliverables: the rules and materials, including the move cards, the Signer cards, the slip template, and the Recorder's record sheet; a minute-by-minute timeline with the facilitator's cues; the debrief mapped moment by moment onto the concepts listed above; what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, including participants who break character to negotiate fairly, readers who complain that they lack the information needed to judge and treat the exercise as a guessing game, and the flatness of a reveal that arrives when verdicts happen to be unanimous; and a frank account of which features of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I must therefore supply myself.
