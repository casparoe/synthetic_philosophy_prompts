I am a high school junior, and I am writing a paper for a health sciences elective in which we have spent most of the term on the vocabulary of epidemiology: case definitions, incidence and prevalence, the sensitivity and specificity of a surveillance system, ascertainment and reporting bias, denominators and age-standardization, confounding, and the distinction between a descriptive report and a causal claim. In the same term my history class has been arguing about the claim, which I keep meeting in popular writing, that violence has declined over the long run, and I have realized that I can only follow that argument when I stop translating it into the terms I actually use. I would like you to do the translation systematically. Restate the position that violence has declined — together with the standard objections that it depends on what we count as violence, that per capita and absolute figures may point in different directions, and that it is unclear whether there is a fact of the matter about any of this — in the vocabulary of epidemiology and public health surveillance, as faithfully as you can. Treat violence as the outcome under surveillance, and tell me what plays the role of the case definition, the case-finding mechanism, the denominator, the confounder, and the reporting artifact.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the historical debate translate cleanly, and which require you to distort the epidemiology — for instance, by treating a case definition as something discovered rather than stipulated, or by pretending that a rate is a fact rather than the product of a counting convention? Which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? I suspect that the moral status of violence — that some of it is justified and some of it is not even an event but an arrangement, so that there may be no victim to count — is one such part, but I do not trust my own suspicion and would like you to test it. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to a defect in the epidemiological framework, a defect in the historical argument, or merely a difference of purpose between the two, and whether the question of whether there is a fact of the matter has any meaning at all in a discipline that treats its categories as instruments for counting rather than as descriptions of nature.
