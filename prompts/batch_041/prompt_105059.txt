I was a geophysicist for 35 years and only got into philosophy of probabilty after I retired, so forgive me if I've got the labels a bit mixed up. I'd like you to write me a short story, maybe 2000 words, that drops the three main ways of thinking about chance into a concrete mess instead of a seminar room: the frequency view (a probability is a long-run relative frequency), the subjectivist view (a probability is a degree of belief, ideally a well-calibrated one), and the propencity view (a probability is a real disposition in the physical setup, not in our heads and not in the statistics).

Here's the setting I want. A small island runs its whole insurance market through a government office called the Bureau of Chances. The Bureau has one licensed randomizing device, a chance-forge: a machine with a dial you set between 0 and 1 and a lever you pull, and it's supposed to produce outcomes at roughly that rate. But make it strange on purpose: the forge has a memory, so the propensity of the next draw depends partly on the run of draws before it, and the dial only pins down the long-run rate in a statistical sense, never any single draw. Then throw in the case the Bureau can't dodge: a policy on a one-off event, say whether a dormant volcano on the island erupts in the next decade, where there's no ensemble to be frequent about and it either erupts once or never.

For characters, give me a frequentist actuary, a Bayesian underwriter, and a retired physicist who insists the dial setting is a real property of the machine the way charge or mass is a real property, and who thinks the volcano has a genuine disposition to erupt too. Let them actually fight over the volcano policy. I want at least one scene where the disagreement changes what someone does — a premium they set, a hedge they refuse, a clause they sneak into a contract — and at least one scene where the subjectivist gets accused of being able to believe any number he likes, tries to defend himself with calibration, and someone points out that calibration is itself a frequentist idea.

Also build in one wrinkle I've been turning over. The Bureau has a robustness rule: a probability only counts as legitimate if it stays roughly the same when you redraw the boundary of the reference class in small ways (which decade, which volcano, which class of machine). Use that rule as the thing that makes the propensity view and the frequency view collide, since the propensity theorist wants the chance to be a feature of this particular setup while the robustness rule keeps dragging other setups in.

A couple of things to avoid: nobody should win by quoting some famous philosopher, and don't end with a tidy lecture explaining which interpretation is right. I'd rather the ending leave me feeling the fight is at least partly about what words like 'real' and 'chance' are doing, and that the three of them aren't only disagreeing about facts. If one of them changes his mind, fine, but make it feel like a person changing, not a proof landing.
