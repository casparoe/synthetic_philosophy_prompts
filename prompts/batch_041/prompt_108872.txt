I've been trying to get clear on what Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals actually show, and I keep running into a gap between the informal summaries and the structure of the argument. The usual summary is something like: if you assume that the probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability, you get a collapse — either the conditional turns out to be equivalent to the material conditional, or the probability function turns out to be trivial, assigning only 0s and 1s. But I'm not sure which of those is the real result, or whether they are two different results resting on different assumptions. More importantly, I'm confused about which assumption is doing the work. Is it the assumption that conditionals are propositions with truth values? Is it the assumption that they are Boolean — that they can be embedded under negation, conjunction, and disjunction, and that the probability function is defined on the resulting compounds? Or is it something about the probability function itself, like the assumption that it is a classical probability function? I have seen people say the result shows conditionals cannot be propositions, but that seems too strong, because the result appears to rule out only a uniform propositional treatment that works across all probability functions, not a propositional treatment tailored to a particular probability function.

So what I would like is an explanation that walks through the structure of the argument — not a formal proof, but a clear account of what is being assumed at each step and where the pressure points are. In particular, I want to understand whether the result is best read as an impossibility result about conditionals, or as an impossibility result about a certain kind of probability function, or as something else entirely. I am also curious whether the result has a local version and a global version: does it show that no single probability function can have a conditional whose probability tracks the corresponding conditional probability, or only that no single conditional can do this across all probability functions? And if the latter, does that leave room for a view on which the probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability in some restricted class of cases — say, when the antecedent is epistemically independent of the consequent, or when the conditional is not itself embedded in further logical structure — or does the argument rule that out too? I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I just want the conceptual geography of the result, including which assumptions are essential and which are mere conveniences, and what the most defensible diagnosis is of why the triviality arises.
