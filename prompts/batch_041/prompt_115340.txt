I am a PhD student working on the foundations of bounded rationality, and I have been trying to make precise the claim that HCH -- the recursive human-oracle construction in which a root question is answered by a human who may pose subquestions to copies of herself -- is an idealization of deliberation. I keep reconstructing an argument that seems to establish something much stronger than anyone intends, and I cannot tell whether the argument is invalid or whether its conclusion is true and merely uncomfortable. Here is the argument as I have it.

(1) For any deliberative process, we can ask what verdict it would reach given unlimited computation, unlimited time, and perfect honesty. Call this the process's ideal verdict.

(2) The ideal verdict is a function of the question and the evidence available to the deliberator.

(3) Any such function can be represented as a decision tree: at each node the deliberator considers a subquestion, and the answer determines which branch is taken.

(4) If the deliberator may consult copies of herself at each node, the decision tree becomes a recursion: each node's subquestion is answered by a sub-deliberation that returns an answer, and the root combines the answers.

(5) This recursive structure is exactly HCH. So HCH is the canonical idealization of any deliberative process.

(6) In HCH, each sub-deliberation is a function of the subquestion it is given. It does not depend on the global state of the whole deliberation, except insofar as that state is encoded in the subquestion.

(7) Therefore, in the ideal limit, deliberation is decomposable into local sub-deliberations: the answer to any subquestion is determined by that subquestion and its children, and not by the order of sibling questions or by the global context.

(8) This proves too much. It implies that any two ideal deliberators who decompose a question differently but agree on all local subquestions must reach the same verdict. It also implies that holistic, context-sensitive reasoning -- the kind that seems to resist decomposition -- is either an illusion or a symptom of bounded rationality.

I want to keep (1) and (2); I think there is an ideal verdict, and I think it is a function of the question and the evidence. I also want to keep (6); a sub-deliberation should not be able to peek at the global state. But (8) feels wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give up one of (3), (4), (5), or (7), which is least costly, and what would I have to replace it with to keep the idealization idea alive?
