Me and my cofounder are finally writing the operating agreement for our two-person company — equal partners, no investors, both of us have other options if this thing blows up — and a friend of mine who's midway through a game theory PhD keeps telling me we should be looking for safe Pareto improvements instead of just splitting everything down the middle. The way she put it: a safe Pareto improvement isn't a deal that's good for both sides on the assumption that both sides behave sensibly. It's a change to the setup itself that leaves every player at least as well off no matter how the others actually end up playing. You don't need to trust anyone, and you don't need any beliefs at all about what they'll do; all you need is some assumption about how the outcomes of related games line up, like that the same structure played twice gets played the same way, or that nobody bothers with an option that's obviously dominated. She also said a couple of things I only half followed: that there's a ceiling on what a safe deal can promise you and it sits below what you could get if you could actually count on each other, and that even once you've got a few safe options, saying which one is better is harder than it sounds.

I don't want a explainer on any of this. What I want is the most important implications of the idea for how we should actually write the agreement and run the partnership day to day — the things that change about what we put in writing, what we refuse to put in writing, and what we should expect to happen when we disagree.

- basis — which bit of the safe-Pareto-improvement idea it falls out of (the certainty/guarantee requirement, the need for assumptions about how different games relate to each other, the fact that you need no beliefs about how anyone plays, the ceiling on what can be guaranteed, the difficulty of comparing two safe options, or whatever else)
- what it means for us — the concrete drafting or operational consequence for two equal partners
