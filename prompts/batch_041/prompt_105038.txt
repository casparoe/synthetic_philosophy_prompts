Last night a friend and I argued past midnight about Kavka's toxin puzzle, and we each convinced the other for about five minutes at a stretch — which is itself some kind of evidence about the puzzle. I want to send her a poem that does what our argument could not: hold both halves of the thing in one frame without letting either dissolve, so I'm asking you to write it.

Work from the standard set-up. An eccentric billionaire will wire a million dollars into your account in the morning if, at midnight tonight, you sincerely intend to drink a vial of toxin at noon tomorrow — a toxin that will make you wretchedly ill for a day but will not kill you or leave any lasting harm. The money is yours hours before the drinking is due; nothing forces you to go through with it, and everyone knows you are free to change your mind once you are paid. The trouble is that the intention must be genuine at midnight to earn the money, and by noon the only thing that made drinking worth it — the payment — is already in the past. Kavka's claim is that you cannot actually form the intention, because intending is not predicting your own future behavior: it is a practical commitment that requires you to see the act as one you have reason to perform, and you know you will have no such reason when the hour comes. Gauthier's reply is that this misorders deliberation: once you have genuinely committed, the question at noon is not whether drinking pays but what someone who has so committed may rationally do, and the answer is to drink. I want a poem that keeps that disagreement live rather than handing it to either man.

Write it as a terza rima of at least six tercets, closing on a single line left deliberately without a rhyme partner. Let the interlocking chain do structural work: each tercet should hand its sound forward to the next the way the reason for intending is handed forward from midnight to noon and never cashed, and the closing unpaired line should be the drinking itself, standing alone because by then it has nothing left to rhyme with. Speak in the voice of the person at midnight, alone with the vial and the offer, turning the thing over — perhaps addressing the friend, perhaps the billionaire, perhaps themselves. A reader who knows the puzzle should be able to tell which tercets voice the intention and which voice the reason, should hear Kavka and Gauthier in tension rather than in sequence, and should feel why the last line stands by itself. Get the small asymmetries right: that merely predicting your own behavior would be cheap and would not earn the money, that what is demanded is closer to intending to intend, and that the whole difficulty lives in the gap between the confidence you can muster at midnight and the reasons actually available to you at noon. Someone who works on intention for a living should recognize the puzzle rather than wince at it, and someone who has never heard of it should still find a good poem.

Write in American English. After the poem, add a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "form" (a string naming the meter and rhyme scheme you used and how the closing line departs from it); "voice" (a string identifying whose perspective the poem speaks from); "recurring_rhymes" (an array of strings, one per recurring rhyme sound, each saying what that sound stands for in the puzzle); "kavka_position" (a string saying how the poem renders the impossibility claim); "gauthier_position" (a string saying how it renders the opposing claim); "why_the_last_line_stands_alone" (a string); and "left_open" (a string naming the tension the poem deliberately does not resolve).
