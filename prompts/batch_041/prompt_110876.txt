Me and two friends are doing a joint presentation for a philosophy of biology seminar on what is life, and we've built an argument that we now think is a reductio of every definition we've tried, except none of us agrees on which step is the bad one and we present Tuesday. Here it is, numbered the way it's on the whiteboard:

(1) A definition of life has to sort things the way working biologists sort them -- paradigm organisms in, rocks and crystals and fires out -- and it has to do that by naming some property they all and only share.
(2) A mule is alive and a mule is sterile, so reproducing can't be necessary for being alive.
(3) A bacterial spore or a dry seed is alive with basically no metabolism running, so an active metabolism can't be necessary either.
(4) Subtract reproduction and metabolism and you're left with being made of cells. But then 'alive' just means 'cellular', which relocates the whole question to what a cell is -- and the first living things presumably weren't cells.
(5) So there's no single property that all and only living things share.
(6) If there's no such property, there's no fact of the matter about the borderline cases -- viruses, prions, whatever's happening in the RNA world, whatever comes back in a Mars sample.
(7) And if there's no fact of the matter about whether the first replicators were alive, then 'the origin of life' isn't a research question with a determinate subject matter, it's just the origin of something-or-other.
(8) Origins-of-life research is not confused about its own subject matter. So (5) is false.

I'm not willing to give up (2) or (3), they just seem true, and past that I'll pay whatever the fix costs, but I can't shake the feeling that something in the middle is doing an equivocation and I can't find it. Our TA said something cryptic about cluster concepts on the way out the door and that didn't help. So: is this argument broken, or is it valid and we just have to pay up?
