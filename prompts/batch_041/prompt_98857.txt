I've somehow been asked to give the toast at the annual gala for a small arts foundation that funds Black artists — they want me because I've written about the Harlem Renaissance for years and, I'm assuming, because I'm cheap. It's a tricky room: painters and curators who bristle at anything that sounds like a sermon, donors who'd quietly like a little uplift, and a couple of professors who've actually read their Du Bois and will notice if I get him wrong. The honoree is a painter in her sixties who has spent forty years insisting her work isn't political — she paints light and bodies and grief, and she'll tell you that if you want a message you can go read a pamphlet.

Here's my problem. I keep circling Du Bois's old provocation from the twenties, the one where he insists that every work of art is propaganda whether or not it admits to being, that the dream of art floating free of any purpose is a luxury told by people who can afford it, and that an artist who claims to be just painting is either fooling herself or producing the most effective propaganda of all — and meanwhile half the field has spent a century pushing back on him, saying that's a recipe for bad art and worse politics. I don't want to ambush her at her own party or turn a toast into a seminar, but I also can't stand up there and pretend the question isn't sitting in the room. So write me the toast — five minutes, warm, funny where it can be — that honors her and her refusal while taking Du Bois's challenge seriously rather than just name-dropping it, and that definately lands for both the donors and the painters without either half feeling lectured at. If you think the honest move is to side with her against Du Bois, do that, but then actually show me why his objection doesn't land; if you side with Du Bois, don't make it a scold. And don't quote anything at me that you're not sure of the wording on — paraphrase if you have to.
