I have recently joined the classification committee of an independent democracy index, which each year places every country in one of four tiers — Democracy, Flawed Democracy, Hybrid, Authoritarian. Each country is scored from 0 to 10 on four indicators: electoral integrity, media and information pluralism, institutional checks, and a turnover record, where 0 means no alternation of governing power in a quarter-century and 10 means alternation has become routine and repeated. The overall verdict is produced by a decision rule that has never been written down; it has simply been applied consistently, and I am now expected to apply it without being able to say what it is. The disputes that dog every index of this kind — whether one word should cover a country where the incumbent actually loses and a country where it merely could, in principle — are sharpened here by the arguments over how Hungary and Turkey ought to be described, but I want my answer to come out of our own record rather than out of those debates. Here is that record, with indicator profiles and the verdicts we reached:

Aurelia — electoral integrity 9, media 9, checks 9, turnover 9 — Democracy
Valoria — 6, 6, 7, 7 — Flawed Democracy
Erevia — 5, 4, 5, 4 — Hybrid
Solmara — 4, 3, 4, 8 — Hybrid
Karst — 2, 2, 3, 1 — Authoritarian

The case now before us is Fjordia, and its profile is unusually clean. Electoral integrity 9, media 8, checks 9, turnover 2. By our own coding, its elections are genuinely free and fair, its press is plural, its courts are independent, and its opposition operates without harassment. Yet the same party has governed for thirty years and has never been turned out. Two facts complicate the picture. The main opposition is legal, well funded, and unmolested, and its vote share has risen at each of the last two national elections; and at a recent regional election it took control of the country's largest province for the first time, so that a national alternation now looks possible within a cycle or two. Nothing in the past several years has degraded Fjordia's institutions; the only movement is the opposition's ascent.

I want you to predict how the committee will code Fjordia, and, more than that, to reconstruct the rule we are implicitly running. The question I care most about is whether the turnover record functions as one graded factor among four or as a necessary condition that caps the verdict regardless of the other three — and whether, if it is a condition, we are applying it retrospectively (has the incumbent ever lost?) or prospectively (could the incumbent lose now?).

Structure your reply under exactly these five headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

The rule we are actually running (about 250 words). State the decision rule that best fits the full record, and say explicitly whether turnover is operating as a graded factor or as a veto, and retrospectively or prospectively.

Rival rules that fit the record equally well (about 300 words). Give at least two alternatives that reproduce every verdict above, and say what each predicts for Fjordia. I am less interested in which is true than in how much of our past behavior is underdetermined by it.

My prediction for Fjordia (about 250 words). Name the tier you expect, the rule you are betting we are running, your confidence, and the reason for preferring that rule over the rivals.

The case that would settle it (about 200 words). Specify one further case — its indicator profile and the outcome it would produce — that would best separate the two leading rules, and explain why that case discriminates where the existing ones do not.

What the committee would be committing to (about 150 words). If we code Fjordia as you predict, what substantive position about the meaning of the word democracy are we taking on, and what would we be implicitly asserting by coding it the other way?

Do not cite any literature or supply references; I want the reasoning from the record alone.
