ok so I'm a backend dev, never taken a single philosophy class in my life, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on Benacerraf's dilemma after hearing it name-dropped on a podcast. My rough grip on it, and correct me if I've got it wrong: you want a story about mathematical truth that does two jobs at once. Job one, say what makes something like "7 is prime" actually true -- so there's some object out there the sentence is about, and truth is correspondence to it. Job two, say how we could ever come to know it. And the standard worry is that whatever story gives you job one (numbers are abstract, non-spatial, causally inert objects) makes job two impossible, since how do you get epistemic grip on something you can't causally touch; while whatever story gives you job two (math is symbol-pushing, derivations from axioms) loses job one, because then the axioms are just rules and there's no fact of the matter about which ones are right. That's the dilemma as I understand it. I don't want a reading list or citations or anything like that, just the analysis.

What I want is for you to take one real historical episode -- the 19th-century discovery of non-Euclidean geometry -- and run it through that dilemma, in both directions. The facts as I have them, so you don't have to retell the history to me: for something like 2000 years the parallel postulate was treated as a self-evident truth about space, and generation after generation tried to derive it from the other Euclidean axioms; Saccheri and others tried and it backfired on them; then Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky worked out geometries where the postulate fails and no contradiction shows up; later people built actual models (pseudospheres, disk models) showing those weird systems are consistent if the Euclidean one is; and then in the 20th century general relativity came along and physical spacetime turned out not to be Euclidean anyway. That's the shape of it.

So, first direction: which horn of the dilemma were the geometers actually standing on, before and after? Pre-independence, "Euclid is true of space" looks like the semantic horn -- the objects are points and lines in real space, and the triangle-angle claim is true because that's how space is -- and the epistemic horn was handled by "it's just self-evident," which is an epistemology that only works if the objects are the physical ones. Once independence lands, my guess is the whole thing flips to the combinatorial horn: the theorems become truths about what follows from a given axiom set, and "which geometry is true" either becomes an empirical question that physics answers or gets quietly dropped. I want to know whether that flip is really as clean as I think it is, whether anyone was doing if-thenism or structuralism before those labels existed, and whether the move the geometers made -- we don't know which geometry is true of space, but we do know the implications -- is a genuine escape from the dilemma or just relocating the problem somewhere else.

Second direction, and this is the part I actually care about: what does the episode do to the dilemma? My hunch is mixed. On one hand it's a great illustration of the pressure, because here's a domain whose objects were supposedly real and knowable, and it turned out the "self-evident" access route was just a projection of local experience, so the epistemology was never doing the work it claimed to do. On the other hand geometry had an exit that arithmetic doesn't: you can point at a physical space and ask which geometry it satisfies, whereas there's no lab test for whether numbers are really out there, so the dilemma might be at its sharpest exactly where this episode is least relevant. Tell me whether that asymmetry is real or whether I'm cheating -- is the geometry case even a case of the dilemma, given that its objects (pre-independence) were physical and causally reachable? If the honest answer is that it only looks like a case because we retro-fit it, say so, and say what a real test case would have to look like instead.

Third, the part I keep going back and forth on: the structuralist line, that a theory is about a structure rather than some special collection of objects, is supposedly the standard escape. Does the geometry story support that or undercut it? Because it looks to me like the geometers found that two different axiom sets describe two different structures and both are fine, which is structuralism's happy ending, except Benacerraf's own worry seems to be that structuralism still needs the structures to exist out there somewhere, which drags the semantic horn right back in. Walk me through whether the episode is evidence for structuralism, and where the dilemma still bites after you've gone structuralist.
