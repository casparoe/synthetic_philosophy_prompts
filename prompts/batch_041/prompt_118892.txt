Q3 (20 marks). A lamp is off at t = 0. Its switch is flicked at t = 1/2 min (on), t = 3/4 min (off), t = 7/8 min (on), and so on, so that the n-th flick happens at t = 1 − 2^−n minutes and infinitely many flicks happen before t = 1 minute.

(a) Set out the problem this is supposed to pose for the possibility of supertasks. [4]
(b) Set out, as forcefully as you can, the reply that the example shows only that the description of the case is incomplete, and so does not show supertasks to be impossible. [6]
(c) Variant: at step n (at t = 1 − 2^−n) we put balls numbered 10n − 9 through 10n into an urn and take out the ball numbered n. How many balls are in the urn at t = 1? What does this case show about supertask descriptions and their outcomes? [5]
(d) Now take a dial with a pointer that moves continuously, whose reading at t = 1 − 2^−n minutes is 90 − 2^−n degrees. Does this case belong with the lamp or with the urn, and why? Does the lamp example establish that supertasks are impossible? Argue for your answer. [5]

And here's what they wrote:

(a) The lamp is off at 0 and flicked at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ..., so before t = 1 it's been flicked infinitely many times and there is no last flick. After an even number of flicks it's off and after an odd number it's on, so the states go on, off, on, off, ... forever. The problem is that this never settles: there's no 'state after all the flicks' to read off, so neither on nor off seems to be forced. And each answer looks refutable — it can't be on, because every time it was turned on it was turned off again at the next flick, and it can't be off, because every time it was off it was turned on again at the next flick. Yet it has to be one or the other. So the case looks like it shows that infinitely many steps can't be got through in a finite time.

(b) The strongest reply is that the description is simply incomplete. It tells us the lamp's state at every time strictly before 1, and says nothing about t = 1 itself. So 'on or off at t = 1?' has no answer as the case is set up — not because the answer would be contradictory, but because nothing in the story fixes one. We're free to add a clause: 'and at t = 1 it is on', or 'and at t = 1 it is off', and neither addition contradicts anything already in the story. The apparent paradox in (a) comes from treating the state at t = 1 as though it had to be the state left behind by the last flick; since there is no last flick, that's an extra assumption smuggled in and not part of the case. So the example doesn't show supertasks are impossible, it shows that this description underdetermines the outcome. This is Thomson's own diagnosis — he invented the example to make exactly this point — while Benacerraf's later paper argued that a contradiction really does follow and concluded that supertasks are impossible.

(c) Zero. Ball k goes in at step ⌈k/10⌉ and comes out at step k, so every ball is out by some time before t = 1 and never returns, and nothing is added at t = 1 itself. So the urn is empty at t = 1. This shows that a supertask can have a fully determinate outcome: the description pins the answer down and we can prove what it is.

(d) The dial belongs with the urn. The readings are 89.5, 89.75, 89.875, ..., converging to 90, and since the pointer moves continuously the reading at t = 1 is 90. Nothing is left open. The lamp is the odd one out because on, off, on, off, ... doesn't converge: at t = 1 the lamp is neither on nor off. That's a contradiction — a lamp must be either on or off — so the lamp description is inconsistent, and a supertask that keeps flipping a discrete state like that is impossible.
