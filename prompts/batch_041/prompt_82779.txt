I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections for close to fifteen years, and one thing that keeps nagging at me is that my reactions to paternalism and nudging do not match any of the standard labels. Soft versus hard, means versus ends, libertarian versus coercive -- none of them predicts my gut. So here are my verdicts on a spread of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, generates them, what it says about cases I have not listed, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

My verdicts:

1. Produce at eye level, candy on the bottom shelf: permissible, barely worth a comment.
2. Organ donation as the default, with an easy opt-out: permissible.
3. Opting out of organ donation requiring a notarized form and a fee: not permissible, even though it produces the same registrations as (2).
4. Graphic health warnings covering most of a cigarette pack: permissible.
5. Cigarettes sold only from behind a counter, so that buying them means asking a clerk: permissible, but I feel worse about it than about (4), and I cannot say why.
6. Automatic enrollment of new hires in a retirement plan at a default contribution rate, withdrawable at any time: permissible.
7. Automatic enrollment of new hires in a monthly donation to the employer's preferred charity, with the same ease of opting out: not permissible, and I want to say more firmly not permissible than I want to say (2) is permissible.
8. A website that pre-checks a box so that users donate to a political cause unless they uncheck it: not permissible.
9. A doctor saying that a treatment is what she would want for her own mother, which moves the patient without conveying any new fact: permissible when the treatment is clearly indicated, not permissible when it is a genuinely close call.
10. A tax form pre-filled with an estimate of your income, which raises honest reporting partly because people assume the government already knows: permissible.
11. A city that makes the healthy option the default in its cafeterias and quietly pays a bonus to cashiers who mention it: not permissible, even though the effect is the same as (1) plus (2).

The pair I cannot square is (6) and (7). Both are defaults, both are trivially easy to reverse, both work through inertia, and in both cases the employee would, on reflection, probably endorse having given the money. If the principle is that a nudge is fine when it steers you toward what you yourself would choose under reflection, then (7) should be as fine as (6). If the principle is that a nudge is fine when it leaves you free to opt out, then (7) is as fine as (6) again. Something about the second case being other-regarding, or expressive, or enlisting you in someone else's project seems to be doing work that neither standard story mentions, and I want to know whether that distinction is principled or just squeamishness.

And here is the case I keep going back and forth on: a state that nudges people toward having children -- parental-leave defaults, housing lotteries weighted for families, a default that opts new parents into a child benefit -- all reversible, all aimed at the agent's own long-run good as most people conceive it. Sometimes I think this is (6) with babies; other times I think the state has no business shaping something that load-bearing for a person's identity, and that the difference is not the mechanism but what is being aimed at.

I have a tentative principle of my own, and I would like it tested and then repaired or replaced. Roughly: an intervention is permissible when it makes the agent more likely to do what she would choose if she were thinking clearly and knew everything relevant, and impermissible when it substitutes the intervenor's judgment for hers. It handles (1) through (5) and (9), I think. It does not obviously handle (7), and it says nothing about the children case.

Two further things I want the principle to answer. First, I have been trying to name a kind of intervention I will call self-effacing: one that works only as long as the target does not know it is operating, and that would stop working if it were explained. Case (10) is arguably one. Is a self-effacing nudge ever permissible, and if so, does the answer turn on whether the target would consent to it in advance if asked? Second, does the principle extend from states to private parties -- an employer, a platform, a friend -- or does the same intervention change its verdict with the actor, and why?

Give me the principle or the small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to apply, with the clause each case forces and the verdicts it delivers on cases I have not considered. Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
