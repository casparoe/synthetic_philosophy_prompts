I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral epistemology, and a few weeks ago a classmate offered what struck everyone in the room as a very tidy summary of the literature on moral testimony. I've reconstructed it as faithfully as I can: "Ordinary testimony transmits knowledge: if a friend tells me that Canberra is the capital of Australia, I can simply come to know it on her say-so. Moral testimony cannot do that. If she tells me that eating meat is wrong, I can at most believe her, or act as though I believed her; I cannot come to know it, because moral knowledge requires seeing the reasons for myself. That is why the philosophers who write about this say that someone who is vegetarian only because her partner is, or who opposes a war because her priest does, has no genuine moral knowledge at all — she is merely deferring, and deference is not a route to moral truth. The real lesson of the debate is that moral testimony is impossible, which is why morality turns out to be more like taste than like science: there is nothing there to be handed from one person to another."

I nodded along at the time, largely because she is a much better student than I am, but two things nag at me. The first is a vague memory that the people who are pessimistic about moral testimony are often realists of a fairly robust kind, not relativists or expressivists, so the closing line about taste does not sound like where they are going. The second is that the step from "you cannot get moral understanding this way" to "you cannot get moral knowledge this way" seems to be carrying a great deal of hidden weight, and I cannot tell whether the second claim is meant to follow from the first or is simply being assumed.

Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what someone working on this topic would accept as fair, what is a defensible but contested reading, and what is simply not something anyone in the debate holds? I do not need a reading list or citations — I need to know which parts of this I can repeat in seminar without embarrassing myself, and in particular whether the conclusion about morality being like taste is supposed to follow from the premise about deference or has been smuggled in from somewhere else.
