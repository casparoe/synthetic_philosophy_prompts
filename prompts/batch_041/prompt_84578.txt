I'm designing the famine unit for an undergraduate course in political economy, and I want to settle the framing before I write the lectures, since I think I've landed on a clean thesis and I want to know whether it will survive contact with students.

The thesis is this. Once one takes Sen's entitlement approach seriously, natural disaster ceases to be a useful category for famine. Famines, on that view, are failures of people's command over food rather than failures of the food supply; and since command over food is fixed by endowments, wages, prices, and the legal order, a famine is always something a society does to some of its members. Every famine is therefore at bottom a political act, a crime rather than a calamity. Wherever people starve, someone has chosen not to prevent it.

I'd like help turning this into a lecture that won't embarrass me. Two points in particular.

First, the inference. If the entitlement approach is right that a famine can occur without any absolute shortage, does it follow that in an actual famine the food was present and being withheld, so that the remedy is always redistribution rather than relief? I want to state that as a clean implication, but I suspect I'm skipping a step and cannot see where.

Second, the classification. I intend to teach the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward famine as three instances of one kind, politically produced starvation, with the blight, the drought, or the failed harvest treated as at most a trigger. Students reliably raise harvest failures, and I want a principled way to show that this is a distraction from the analysis rather than a qualification of it.

I have also been using a test of my own and would like your view of it. Call a famine political if, holding the food supply fixed, the deaths would not have occurred. It seems to me that in every real case the test comes out political, which I take to confirm the thesis. Tell me whether the test is doing what I think it is.
