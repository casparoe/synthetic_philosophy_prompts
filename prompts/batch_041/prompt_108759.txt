I've been working through Indian epistemology on my own, and the thing I keep tripping over is the Nyāya claim that there are four independent sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison or analogy (upamāna), and testimony — rather than just perception and inference with the rest reducible to them. Take the textbook comparison case: you're told that a gavaya is like a cow, and then out in the forest you see an animal resembling a cow and recognize it as the gavaya you'd been told about. Nyāya treats that recognition as a distinct kind of knowing, not just perception plus the memory of what you were told. Here's the test I've been trying to apply: call a source independent only if there's some truth it could put in your hands that no combination of the other sources, however cleverly deployed, could deliver — the standard many people use to argue that testimony is really just inference in disguise. Please give me your credence that upamāna passes that test, with the reasoning broken out: how much rides on what "independent source of knowledge" should mean (does it demand genuinely new propositional content, or is a new word-to-world link enough?), how much on whether the recognition case actually delivers anything perception and testimony together couldn't, how much on whether the whole question is well-posed rather than a verbal dispute about how to count the sources, and any further term you think belongs — a number and a line of justification for each, then how they combine. Then tell me which of those terms my number should be most sensitive to, and what argument or case would move it most.
