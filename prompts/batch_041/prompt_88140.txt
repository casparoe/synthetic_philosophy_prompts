I'm a third-year student in a seminar on the ethics of wildlife and conservation, and I've been trying to pin down the rule my instructor is actually running. She uses a settled accounting convention in every case we discuss: the net welfare of a site over a decade is the sum, across every animal that lives there during that period, of that animal's own lifetime welfare, counted from its own point of view; animals that are never born contribute zero; and death itself carries no separate penalty or bonus, so an animal that dies young simply contributes the (negative) total of the short life it actually had. For the purposes of the seminar she also treats it as established that a typical small-bodied wild animal's life is net negative, since the overwhelming majority of offspring die before reproducing, usually of hunger, exposure, or predation. Please take both of those as fixed for this exercise; I want the rule she is using, not an assessment of her premises.

Her verdicts so far, from our case studies:

1. A winter feeding programme for a deer herd. The herd roughly doubles over the decade, and the individual deer are worse off: crowding, a chronic disease that spreads at the feeding stations, and heavier winter starvation per head once the population overshoots the food supply. Her verdict: the programme increases net wild-animal suffering.

2. Reintroducing an apex predator into a reserve. Prey numbers fall by roughly half over the decade, while the surviving prey are in measurably better condition: higher body weight, fewer parasites, fewer starvation deaths. Predator numbers rise, but the added predator lives are a small fraction of the prey lives removed. Her verdict: net suffering falls.

3. Closing an open landfill that had subsidised an enormous gull and rat population. Their numbers fall by about eighty per cent, and the survivors are in better condition than they were on the landfill diet. Her verdict: net suffering falls.

4. A fish-processing plant whose discards supported a gull colony that grew to many times its former size. The gulls were in worse condition throughout (disease, aggression, malnourished chicks), and the colony crashed when the plant closed. Her verdict: over the whole period, net suffering rises.

One remark of hers I keep turning over: when I asked, in case 2, why the improvement in the survivors' condition did not outweigh the loss of lives, she said that in these cases 'the arithmetic is mostly in the number of lives'. I could not tell whether that was a claim about that case or her general rule.

Next week's case, which she has not seen: a conservation trust proposes to convert a forty-hectare exhausted gravel quarry into a constructed wetland. The quarry today supports few animals, and those in poor condition, with little cover, frequent desiccation, and high starvation. The ecology team's projections are much higher primary productivity; roughly an order of magnitude more individual animals per hectare, mostly invertebrates such as midges, water fleas, and worms, but also several times as many amphibians, small mammals, and waterfowl; and better average condition across the board, with lower juvenile mortality, fewer starvation deaths, and better body condition, because food and cover are abundant. The construction phase will kill or displace most of the quarry's current inhabitants; treat that as a one-off pulse that is small relative to the decade's totals.

Please act as an expert in environmental ethics and population ecology, and think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming the candidate rules her verdicts might be implementing, then commit to a verdict.

Predict what she will say about the quarry-to-wetland proposal: would she judge that it reduces or increases net wild-animal suffering per hectare over the decade? Lay out the two or three most plausible general rules that fit all four earlier verdicts equally well, say what each predicts here, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would most cleanly tell the remaining rules apart, including a case that separates any two that are still live after the new one. If her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly and identify what would resolve it.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, the predicted verdict, and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
