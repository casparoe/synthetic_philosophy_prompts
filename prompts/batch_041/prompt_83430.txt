I am a retired geophysicist who has spent the past three years reading philosophy on my own, and I have run into a disagreement I cannot settle. I would like you to take it up as an expert in rational choice and the mathematics of risk. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin with a brief brainstorm in which you set out what each of the two positions below is actually committed to; only then give me your advice.

My neighbour across the road plays poker for a living. His counsel is twofold. First: never stake more than a small slice of my savings on any wager, even one that plainly favours me, because the players he has watched go broke were not unlucky but overbet — past some stake, ruin becomes a matter of time rather than chance. Second: I should buy the extended warranty on the car and the travel insurance for our trip, both of which I have priced out and both of which cost more than they can possibly pay back on average. My other neighbour, a retired economist, says the reverse of both: stake as much as possible on anything with positive expected value, and never buy insurance priced above its expected payout.

Neither man can give me a principle, only a habit. Here is the case that makes me want one. A friend has a coin he assures me is biased sixty-forty in my favour, and offers a single wager at even money — once only, no repeats — and asks me to name the stake. The economist says the expected gain is twenty per cent of whatever I put up, and that I should therefore put up everything. The poker player will say only that the stake should be small, without saying how small. Is there a principled answer, and what is it? What fraction would you actually name, and why that fraction rather than one twice or half as large? Does the answer change if the same coin is offered to me a hundred times and I reinvest as I go? And how can the same reasoning that restrains me on the coin tell me to buy insurance that is worth less than it costs?

Give me the general rule, be explicit about the assumptions it rests on, and tell me where it fails — if, say, my taste for money over the relevant range really is linear, or if ruin would not be final because I could earn it all back. I am not afraid of mathematics, and I would rather have your reasoning than a reading list.
