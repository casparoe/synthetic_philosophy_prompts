I'm in a seminar on the history of ethics and I've ended up writing on the part of Bentham nobody bothers with — his views on sex and the criminal law, not the Greatest Happiness stuff. Here's what's bugging me. The standard story of how Britain decriminalised male homosexual acts is a Mill story: the Wolfenden commitee reported in 1957 that homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a crime, and it says in its own words that "It is not, in our view, the function of the law to intervene in the private life of citizens, or to seek to enforce any particular pattern of behaviour"; then Lord Devlin pushes back that a society has every right to enforce its shared morality because that morality is what holds it together, Hart answers him with the harm principle, and eventually you get the Sexual Offences Act. All very Mill. But Bentham had already made the whole argument in an essay he never published, well over a century earlier: the only thing that can justify punishing an act is the mischief it does, these acts do no mischief to anyone who isn't consenting, the "unatural" objection dissolves on inspection, and the real suffering is produced by the law's own machinery — blackmail, prosecution, ruin. So I want to run the episode the other way round, through Bentham rather than Mill.

What I need: first, map his actual framework onto the case. What would a Benthamite calculus say the committee and Parliament should have done, and where does it match what they did — the private/consenting-adults line, the refusal to call it a disease, the age of consent they settled on? Then tell me honestly where the theory goes silent or gets the case wrong. I'm suspicious of a few spots. He treats it as a harmless taste and has no notion of it as an identity, or of a minority that needs protection from the majority rather than just from the criminal law. He can't fix an age of consent at any particular number without importing a convention the hedonism doesn't supply. And his hedonism looks like it hands Devlin a weapon, because if the disgust of the majority counts as pain in the ledger, the same calculus can be made to come out against reform — so does his framework actually resist Devlin, or does Devlin beat him at his own game? Then the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory — is Wolfenden a vindication of Bentham, or did it happen for reasons he didn't have, and does it matter that the reform came when it did and not when his argument was sitting in a drawer? Keep the "should have done" honest: where the theory is silent, say so instead of ventriloquising a Benthamite answer. No citations needed, I just want the mapping and where it breaks.
