I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about John Maynard Keynes's conception of the good life, and I would like your help working out what it actually implies. The dispute began with Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities of our grandchildren, in which he predicted that, absent major wars and major population growth, the economic problem — the struggle for subsistence that has absorbed most of human history — could be solved within about a century, that living standards in the progressive countries might rise several-fold, and that his grandchildren's generation might work something like fifteen hours a week and confront, for the first time, the real question of how to use freedom from pressing economic care.

My friend pressed two objections. First, incoherence: Keynes himself conceded that a second, relative class of needs — the ones satisfied by superiority over others — is insatiable, so the economic problem cannot be solved by productivity alone; and his macroeconomics depends on acquisitive and precautionary motives that he elsewhere described, roughly, as semi-criminal and semi-pathological propensities we would eventually need to discard, along with the love of money as a possession rather than as a means to the enjoyments and realities of life. Second, emptiness: the injunction to live wisely and agreeably and well supplies no content unless one smuggles in the aestheticism of his early Cambridge circle, which took certain states of mind — the enjoyment of beautiful objects, the pleasures of human intercourse — as the ultimate goods, and which may be an ethic for a small elite rather than for a whole society.

Please set out the most important implications of Keynes's conception of the good life, taking both objections seriously. What does it imply for the relation between growth and the good life — is growth a means that can be completed, or a treadmill whose completion is psychologically impossible? What does it imply for working time, income support, and the social valuation of care work and of employment whose value is doubtful? What does it imply for an individual tempted by this vision about ambition, saving, career, and money? And how stable is Keynes's position internally, given that his ethical ideal descends from a pre-economic milieu while his economics presumes continuing acquisitiveness? Be concrete and analytical, distinguish what Keynes actually claimed from what has been read into him, and say where the position is genuinely unstable rather than merely vague.
