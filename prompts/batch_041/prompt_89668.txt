I am a final-year computer science student taking a political theory course as an outside option, and I am writing a seminar paper on the following question. My entire working vocabulary comes from systems security: subjects and objects, access control, capabilities, threat models, trusted computing bases. I would like you to translate the tripartite division we have been working with in the course — negative liberty as the absence of interference, positive liberty as self-mastery or self-realisation, and the republican conception of freedom as non-domination, which is supposed to be genuinely distinct from non-interference — into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then to tell me what does not survive the translation.

To be clear about my terms, since I want the translation to be exact rather than evocative: I understand a subject to hold 'ambient authority' when it can act on an object by virtue of what or who it is, rather than by holding a specific unforgeable token for that object; the object-capability discipline is the design in which authority is ambient-free and must be delegated explicitly; 'authorisation' is the checking of an action against a policy; and a 'trusted computing base' is whatever must be correct for the policy to hold at all. I am fluent in all of this and in the threat-model reasoning that goes with it. I am not fluent in the philosophical vocabulary, so please do not presuppose it on my behalf; the point of the exercise is to see how far my own terms carry.

Then I want the residue report. For each of the three views, say which of its claims translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion — and exactly what gets distorted — and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Two matters I especially want addressed. First, the standard case of someone who is never in fact interfered with but remains dependent on another's goodwill: is the republican claim that such a person is unfree anything more than the security engineer's observation that an unexercised capability is still attack surface, a claim about risk rather than about freedom? Or does it require something my vocabulary cannot supply, such as a notion of arbitrariness defined relative to the subject's own interests rather than to a policy? Second, positive liberty's structure of a higher and a lower self: is there an honest counterpart in layered or specification-governed architectures, or would I merely be projecting?

Finally, a verdict. Where the translation fails, is the failure a defect of my vocabulary — something security has no way to say, and perhaps should not pretend to — or is it evidence that the philosophical distinction was doing less work than it appeared to? Please keep the translation honest: where my framework forces a choice that the philosophical debate leaves open, flag it rather than choosing silently, and do not resolve tensions in the source by quietly importing security's own assumptions, for instance treating any authority that is properly authorised as thereby legitimate. Note also that my three targets are usually presented as rival interpretations of a single contested concept, whereas in my vocabulary they would be three independently measurable properties; say what that shift costs.
