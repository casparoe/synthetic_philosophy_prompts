I have no training in philosophy, but I read widely and argue with friends more than is good for me, and I keep circling a pattern I cannot find a proper name for. I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it, and if there is not, what it ought to be called.

The pattern concerns disagreements in which the two sides agree about every fact there is to agree about, and about every practical consequence of the answer, and yet neither is willing to say that the quarrel was only about words.

A small case first. A friend and I spent an evening on whether a plant-based patty counts as a burger. We agreed on the ingredients, the cooking, the way it is served, the history of the word, and even on which restaurants would and would not put it on the menu under that name. No discovery could have changed either of our minds, because we already agreed on everything discoverable. When I finally said that we must simply mean different things by the word, he said that this was exactly his point, and that my usage was the misuse. That seemed to me the sort of thing I have heard called a verbal dispute, and I thought we were finished.

Then a second argument, and this is the one that puzzles me. It was about whether a certain organisation's practice should count as a religious ritual. The stakes were real — tax treatment, who was admitted — but again every observable fact was common ground. Here the same move failed. When I said that we were using the word differently and so there was nothing left to fight about, I was told that I had not dissolved the disagreement but dodged it: that the real question was which of the two uses ought to govern, that this was a genuine question even though no fact would settle it, and further that one side's way of speaking could not even express what the other side was asking. That last claim is what I cannot get a handle on. If everything is common ground, what is left over that one vocabulary can say and the other cannot?

I have noticed the same shape in arguments about whether numbers exist, or whether a cloud is one object or many, where the disputants seem to agree on all the physics and all the arithmetic and still think the other is missing something.

So what I am after is not simply that some disputes turn on words. It is the line between two kinds of case: those where showing that the parties use a word differently really does end the matter, and those where it does not, because the parties then disagree about which use should win, or about whether the other side has managed to say anything at all. I have heard the phrase 'verbal dispute,' and I suspect the answer lies near it, but that phrase seems to cover only the first kind, and the second is the one I keep running into.

What I want is not a bare label. Is there an established term for the second kind of case, and if there is no single one, what is the best name, defined so that it is clearly distinguished from an ordinary verbal dispute, from a disagreement about the facts, and from a mere difference in taste? Is the thought that something is lost when one side's vocabulary is translated into the other's a legitimate idea or a confusion? And what marks the boundary — what would have to be true of a dispute for the reply that we mean different things to be the whole truth about it, rather than a dodge? A case on each side of that line would help me more than a definition.
