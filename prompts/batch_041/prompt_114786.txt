I am a novelist, and my current book has reached the stage where the scenes are written but the assumptions beneath them are not. The book turns on a question my craft cannot settle: whether one person can be brought to see that something is beautiful by another person's word alone. One of my characters is told weeks in advance that a particular recording will undo her, and it does; another reads a score at the piano and is moved before she has heard a note; a third buys a canvas on a dealer's description and looks at it properly only afterwards; a fourth has the ending of a novel spoiled for her and finds, to her surprise, that the spoiling deepens the thing. Each of those scenes is only as honest as my unexamined assumptions about what testimony can transmit in the aesthetic case, so I would rather have the specification before I commit to the draft.

Please write the desiderata for a theory of aesthetic testimony — for an account of what passes from one person to another when one says that a thing is beautiful, or moving, or worth your evening. What must any adequate theory of this deliver? Set out the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must remain consistent with, and for each requirement name the case or consideration that puts it on the list. Mark, for each, whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the commitment of one camp wearing the costume of a requirement. My purpose is not philosophical tidiness but a standard I can write by: the theory should tell me when a character may honestly be moved by a report, when her deference to a better ear is rational rather than cowardly, and when she is only pretending to have seen for herself.

I expect the list to have to deal with at least the following, and I would like you to say in each case whether it belongs on the list and why: the gulf between a work's non-aesthetic properties — attribution, date, technique, what happens in chapter nine — and its aesthetic ones, since the former travel by report without complaint; the difference between knowing that a work is good and being able to appreciate it; the difference between a reason to believe and a reason to go; the line between the evaluative and the merely descriptive in criticism; the parallel case of moral testimony, which has the same shape and may or may not want the same solution; the fact of trusted critics who disagree, which forbids any account that makes taste arbitrary; the plain fact, not to be wished away, that testimony is a source of knowledge about nearly everything else; and the awkward fact that readers act on reviews, jacket copy, and a friend's insistence all the time, so that no account may simply declare such deference worthless.

Then the part I most want: any pair among these requirements you suspect cannot be satisfied together. I would rather learn that the specification is inconsistent now than discover it in a third draft. Tell me also which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and finish with a short pass over the main families of answer — that testimony can transmit aesthetic knowledge; that it cannot, and that acquaintance is required; that it can transmit belief but never appreciation or the rationality of caring — and how each fares against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard I can hold in my head. I want the requirements, not a reading list.
