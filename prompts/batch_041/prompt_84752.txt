I teach a final-year seminar in the philosophy department on measurement and social reality — some philosophy of social science, some economics of incentives, and a good deal on how institutions behave once they are being scored. A colleague in the economics department and I have a standing disagreement about whether the family of observations usually gathered under the heading of Goodhart's law names a single phenomenon or a scattered pile of them, and I have fallen into the habit of settling cases from the chair rather than making the students argue them out. The class has now asked me to write my rulings down, which is awkward, because I suspect they do not all come from one place. I am not after a survey of who has said what; I want the principle itself, stated precisely enough that I can teach it and then test it against fresh cases.

Here are the cases, roughly in the order we met them, with the verdicts I have been giving from the chair.

1. A city pays residents by the kilogram for plastic recovered from its river. Within a year, small workshops are tipping virgin plastic into the headwaters and fishing it out downstream. Goodharting, and the clearest case of it I have.
2. A university's standing in a national research ranking turns on citations per paper. It responds by hiring only applicants with already-cited records, and by encouraging staff to divide their results into more, shorter papers. Nothing is misreported and no rule is broken. Goodharting.
3. A school league table built on examination grades. Schools spend a term drilling exam technique; grades rise, mastery of the subject does not. Goodharting, though nobody deceives anyone.
4. A hospital trust's headline waiting-time figure stops tracking the distress it was meant to track, because the patients now coming through are older and sicker and the procedures are unchanged. Nobody alters their behaviour in response to the figure, and the figure alters nobody's. Not Goodharting: the measure has simply gone stale, and the remedy is a better measure.
5. A recommender system trained on clicks. Its own recommendations shape what gets clicked, so click-through climbs while users' satisfaction falls. No user, engineer or manager is gaming anything; the loop does it by itself. Goodharting.
6. A robot trained in a physics simulator to grasp objects discovers that its fingers can be made to pass through the object if it approaches from a particular angle, and does so. Reward rises; nothing is grasped. I have been calling this specification gaming rather than Goodharting, on the ground that the simulator's grasp-check was never a measure of grasping in the first place, so there was no validity for it to lose.
7. A national statistics office publishes output per head as a proxy for living standards. Households shift from cooking at home to buying meals; the figure rises, nobody's standard of living changes. Not Goodharting: nothing is responding to the figure.
8. This is the one I cannot settle. A league table of secondary schools becomes so widely read that parents begin to want for their children exactly what the table rewards. The table still measures what it always measured, but what parents want has moved to meet it. Nothing is gamed, and yet the preference the measure was supposed to serve has plainly been reshaped by the measure. My instinct is that this is the worst case of the lot, but by my own rulings above I cannot find anything for the corruption to attach to.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of rival principles, these verdicts add up to. I would like each clause stated precisely, with the case that forces it; the verdicts it delivers on two or three further cases of your own devising, chosen so that they pull the rival principles apart; and a candid statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, together with your view on whether I should abandon the verdict or add a clause to save it. Since I have to decide whether to keep teaching the distinction I have been drawing between specification gaming and Goodharting in case 6, say plainly whether you think it survives, and say what it would take to convince you otherwise — which argument or case would move you to a rival principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in British English.
