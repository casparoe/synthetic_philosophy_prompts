I keep bumping into the same little ritual in cause-prioritisation circles and its started to annoy me. You take a cause — biosecurity, factory farming, lead exposure in middle-income countries, whatever — and score it on three things: importance (how much good solving it would do), tractability (how much extra progress one more person or one more pound actually buys), and neglectedness (how few people are already on it). Then you multiply the three together into a single number, rank the causes by it, and that ranking is meant to tell you where to put your career or your donations.

The bit that gets me is that the same people will then fund a whole portfolio of causes that score terribly on their own rubric, on the grounds that they might be wrong about which moral view is the right one and want to hedge across worldviews. So the rubric ranks, and the practice then partly ignores the ranking, and nobody seems bothered by that.

What philosophical commitments does the whole thing presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed: what view of value, of reasons, of what makes a cause worth doing does this scoring practice only make sense under, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Tell me which parts are really load-bearing — does the arithmetic presuppose that the three dimensions are independent and commensurable, or is that just a convenience? Is the portfolio carve-out consistent with the rubric or does it concede that the rubric was never doing the work it pretends to? And separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits nicely with, since I suspect several quite different theories would license the same spreadsheet. Finally, what would have to change if you swapped the underlying view for value pluralism, where goods can't be traded off against each other, or for a prioritarian view that cares who gets helped rather than how many?

Write it as continuous prose please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just an ordinary email-style answer.
