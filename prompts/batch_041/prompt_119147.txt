I'm a third-year student in a seminar on classical social theory, and I'm applying for a one-year undergraduate research fellowship whose application asks for a two-page proposal; I'd like you to write that proposal for me. Begin directly with the proposal itself — no preface, no restating of my request, no meta-commentary about what you're doing. My half-formed idea, which I've been arguing about with two friends from the seminar, is this: the standard objection to Weber's Protestant ethic thesis — that values or ideas cannot be causes, so the thesis must collapse into either disguised materialism or mysticism — seems to me to rest on a conflation of two different causal notions that Weber keeps separate but never fully reconciles. The first is the one he takes over from von Kries's work on the foundations of probability, where a cause counts as adequate when varying it counterfactually shows that the outcome would not otherwise have occurred; this notion is built for imputing a singular historical outcome to one factor among many, and it is deliberately not a regularity or law. The second is closer to what he gestures at with elective affinity: a value orientation and a set of practices fit, reinforce, and stabilize one another, and the explanatory work is done by the fit rather than by anything pushing. My suspicion is that the Protestant ethic thesis is carried almost entirely by the second notion, and that its apparently causal claim is really a claim about selection — the ethic does not cause capitalist conduct so much as it alters the environment in which conduct is retained, so that practices which would otherwise have remained sporadic become self-reproducing. I want the proposal to turn this into a question with a determinate answer rather than a survey: under what description, if any, can a value orientation count as a cause of an economic form on Weber's own methodology, and does the answer change depending on which of the two causal notions is in play?

For the approach, I'd like the proposal to argue that the question is best settled not by exegesis alone but by a two-part method. First, reconstruct the two notions as sharply as possible and state for each what would count as a value orientation failing to be a cause under it; I want to introduce a working distinction of my own between orientation causation (the value shapes conduct directly) and selection causation (the value shapes which conduct survives and spreads), and then ask which of these the historical thesis actually needs and whether Weber has the resources to license either. Second, test the distinction on a small case, ideally one I can specify myself rather than borrow, in which the two notions give opposite verdicts — for instance a practice that becomes durable because it is value-congruent and socially rewarded even though no one adopted it for the value's sake. The proposal should also confront the tension with value relevance: if the inquirer's values already select the object of study, does that selection leak into the causal claim, and can selection causation be stated without smuggling the value in as an efficient cause? I'd like the proposal to say honestly what would count as the project failing (if the two notions turn out to be intertranslatable, the whole distinction is idle, and I should say so up front), to give a realistic month-by-month plan for a one-year undergraduate scope, and to be explicit that a plausible result is a clarified negative one — that the thesis needs a causal notion Weber never supplies, and that this is a defect in the methodology rather than a defect in the historical claim.
