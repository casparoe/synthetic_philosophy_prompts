I'd like a dialogue about animal sacrifice in Maimonides. Not the usual "did he think sacrifice was primitive" thing — I want two people who both know the texts going at the actual disagreement, in some detail, and neither of them should be Maimonides or the Ramban themselves. Make them modern defenders of the two readings.

Character A holds the line from the Guide, III:32, where Maimonides explains the commandments: the sacrificial system was an accommodation. God took the modes of worship the Israelites were already used to from the surrounding idolatrous cults and transferred them to His own service, so the habit could be redirected and idolatry uprooted. On this reading the offerings have no value of their own; the whole rationale is historical and pedagogical — meeting a stiff-necked people where they were.

Character B holds something like the Ramban's line in his commentary on Leviticus, written against Maimonides: that the offerings do have worth of their own, that the details carry the meaning (which animal, the salt, the laying on of hands, the fire on the altar), and that God wouldn't have commanded a whole apparatus of ritual with nothing good in it just to humor a superstition.

Both of them should be sharp — no strawmen, no cheap points, and they should actually respond to what the other just said rather than delivering little speeches past each other. Some things I'd like them to get into, though not in this order:

- whether they're even disagreeing about the same thing. A may be giving a causal story about why the practice arose, while B is asking what makes it good. I want them to notice that and fight about whether Maimonides slides between the two.
- the details. If the specific animals and modes were borrowed from what the pagans did, why are they prescribed so exactly, and why would they be binding forever? B should press this hard, and A should have a real answer, not a shrug.
- the restoration problem: Maimonides' own legal code expects the messiah to rebuild the temple and bring the sacrifices back. If they were a concession to a situation that's long gone, what's the sense in that? I know some later readers treat this as a genuine tension, and I want it treated as a live problem rather than waved away.
- whether a concession reading makes God out to be manipulating people through a belief He doesn't share. A should say something honest here, not dodge.

Close with a short note — a paragraph at most — on where the dialogue leans on a contested reading of one of the sources, and name the one consideration that would most likely make you switch sides on who has the better of the arguement. I'm genuinely not sure which way I lean, so I don't want the note to just bless whatever conclusion the dialogue stumbles into.

Keep it under maybe 1200 words, and don't be polite about it — I want the disagreement sharp.
