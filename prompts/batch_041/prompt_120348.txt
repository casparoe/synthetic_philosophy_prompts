I'd like a careful conceptual analysis of a specific disagreement between the two classical pragmatist accounts of truth — Peirce's and James's — approached through a framework of my own rather than through the usual textbook contrast (Peirce the sober realist who defines truth as the limit of inquiry, James the relativist who equates truth with whatever it pays to believe). I think that contrast is a caricature, and I want to see exactly where the two accounts actually come apart once one stops repeating it.

Here is the framework I'd like you to use. Any definition of truth can be asked to do three distinct jobs:

1. The semantic job: say what the word true means — what we are attributing to a proposition when we call it true.
2. The criterial job: supply a usable test that sorts true from false beliefs in practice.
3. The normative job: explain why truth has authority — why a belief's being true is a reason to hold it, and why truth is something a belief can miss.

Peirce and James both talk as though a single pragmatist clarification of truth delivers all three at once. I doubt that it can, and I suspect the two of them are quietly splitting the jobs between them differently.

My own working hypothesis is that the split runs roughly like this: Peirce's account is aimed at the semantic and normative jobs and treats the criterial job as a matter for methodology rather than for a theory of truth, whereas James's is aimed primarily at the criterial job and only gestures at the other two. If that is right, the two views are less rivals than answers to different questions. Please test the hypothesis rather than accept it.

Two further things I want worked through in detail, because they seem to me to be where the real action is:

(a) Peirce's convergence claim — that truth is what inquiry is destined to arrive at, or what the community of inquirers would agree on were inquiry carried indefinitely far — looks like it defines truth in terms of belief, which ought to make it impossible for the ideal end of inquiry to be wrong, and therefore ought to strip truth of the very normative force (the possibility of being missed) that the third job demands. Peirce's escape route seems to be his insistence that experience contains a brute, non-inferential resistance that the mind does not author, together with the claim that the object of inquiry is real and independent of what anyone thinks of it. I want to know whether that escape route works, or whether it just reinstates a correspondence theory of truth in the foundations and leaves the pragmatist clarification doing no work. In particular: is the convergence claim an empirical prediction, a definition, or a regulative hope — and does the answer affect whether it can bear the normative load?

(b) James's account looks like it makes truth something that happens to a belief, conferred by the successful leading it does, rather than an inert property it has. But James also insists that true beliefs must agree with reality. If agreement is not copying — if it is instead a matter of being led successfully through experience — I want to know what constrains the leading. Consider a test case: a belief that inquiry will eventually abandon, but which, for every inquirer before that point, pays for itself in practice — it coordinates action, generates no friction with the rest of the believer's commitments, and is abandoned only because inquiry moves on. Under a Jamesian account, is that belief true while it pays and false once it stops? Under Peirce's, was it false all along? And which of the two answers is more defensible, given that both of them want truth to be something we can be wrong about?

Please don't spend any space on who influenced whom, or on the history of the pragmatism disputes; I want the concepts, not the lineage.
