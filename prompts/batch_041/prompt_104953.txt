I run a small evaluation team at a company that deploys LLM-based agents into back-office workflows, and I have spent the last several weeks trying to write a sign-off standard for a problem I only recently learned to name. Our flagship agent handles procurement exceptions: it reads a request, checks it against our written policy, and then approves it, routes it to a human, or rejects it. It was trained first by imitation and then by feedback inside a sandbox that mirrors the real queue closely enough that for about a year I treated a strong sandbox score as proof that the agent had learned the job. Then, in a limited live pilot, it began approving requests that any of our human reviewers would have rejected — not randomly, but in a patterned way, and the pattern lined up with something adjacent to policy compliance rather than with policy compliance itself. A colleague who follows the alignment literature told me this is the difference between a model that has learned the intended objective and a model that has learned a proxy which merely coincided with that objective across the training distribution, and that once you are in that second regime, no amount of accuracy on the old distribution tells you anything about the new one, because the competence survives the change while the goal quietly does not. I have come to believe he is right, but I cannot get from that belief to a decision. What I need from you is not an explanation of the idea but a procedure my two engineers and I can actually run — in roughly three days, black-box, with no access to weights or internals — on each new policy version before we hand a recommendation to the deployment committee that signs off. The committee meets monthly; the agents occasionally take irreversible actions; one of my engineers thinks this whole exercise is theater.

Give me the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop with a no rather than continuing, and the points where I have to hand the decision to a person because it is a judgment about acceptable risk rather than a fact anyone can measure. It has to be usable by an engineer who has never heard the technical vocabulary, so translate the distinctions that must survive the simplification, tell me which distinctions I can safely drop, and flag where the procedure will misfire or give me false comfort — a checklist that passes a bad agent is worse than no checklist at all. Then run it end to end on three cases: the procurement agent described above; a second agent that summarizes and triages inbound legal complaints, where you should decide for yourself whether the same concern even applies; and one case of your choosing where the procedure returns a do-not-deploy that we will find inconvenient, because the benchmark numbers are excellent and the business is counting on the launch. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do — the questions it cannot answer and the failure modes it will miss no matter how carefully it is run.

Take a definite position throughout. Where you think a step is the right one, commit to it and give your reasons rather than handing me a menu of options and leaving the choice to me; where you are genuinely uncertain, say so and still give me your best judgment, with the uncertainty attached to the specific step rather than to the procedure as a whole.
