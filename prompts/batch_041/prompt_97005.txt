I am a high school student writing an independent research paper on labor, and I would like help with the analytical core of it rather than with the historical narrative. My case is the West Virginia school employees' strike of 2018. I have assembled the record myself, and since I know you cannot look anything up, here is the version of the facts I am working from; please treat these as given rather than adding details of your own.

In February 2018, after the legislature passed a pay raise the teachers considered inadequate — two per cent, with one per cent more in each of the two following years — teachers and school service personnel across West Virginia stopped work. Roughly twenty thousand employees took part, and every public school in all fifty-five counties was closed for nine consecutive school days, affecting about a quarter of a million students; it was the first statewide school strike in the state since 1990. The stoppage was unlawful — public employees in West Virginia have no legal right to strike, and the state's attorney general issued an opinion to that effect and offered to back districts that enforced the ban — and it defied union leadership as well: on 27 February the two statewide unions announced a settlement with the governor and told members to return to work, the members refused, and the action continued as a wildcat strike until the legislature gave way. The settlement that ended it was a five per cent raise for all state employees, which the legislature had refused before the strike, plus a freeze on health-plan premiums but no durable fix to how the state employees' health plan is funded. Teacher pay in West Virginia was among the lowest in the country and the state was struggling to fill vacancies. The action is generally credited with helping set off the teacher walkouts in Oklahoma, Arizona, Colorado and elsewhere over the following year.

The piece of theory I want to run against this is a distinction I keep meeting in labor writing, between two sources of workers' leverage. Structural power is leverage that comes from a worker's position in the economy rather than from any organization, and it is usually split in two: workplace bargaining power, the capacity to disrupt the employer's operations from inside by withholding labor at a choke point, and marketplace bargaining power, the capacity to leave or to be hard to replace because one's skills are scarce or the labor market is tight. Associational power is leverage that comes from collective organization as such — numbers, membership, dues, the capacity to coordinate action and to make a call that members actually obey. The usual claim is that the two are complementary, so that a group with weak structural power can still win if its associations are strong.

What I would like from you:

1. A mapping of the episode onto that distinction. Who is the employer here, and does the framework even have the right entity in view, given that county boards formally employ the teachers while the legislature sets their pay? What, concretely, were the teachers' workplace and marketplace bargaining power, and how much of the outcome do they explain? And if the raise was won by the unions' associational power, when exactly did that happen, given that the settlement their leadership negotiated was repudiated by the very members it was supposed to bind?

2. An assessment in the other direction: what does this case show about the framework? Does it confirm it, embarrass it, or hand it a kind of case it was never built for — a public-sector, service-providing, largely female workforce with no legal right to strike, whose employer loses no revenue when the work stops?

3. A verdict on a hypothesis of my own. I suspect the episode needs a third kind of power, one that comes from being seen as right: the leverage that accrues when the public treats your cause as legitimate and the people you are disrupting side with you rather than against you. Is that a genuine third category, or does it collapse into one of the other two, or into something else again? Tell me what would settle the question rather than just adjudicating it by intuition.

4. An honest treatment of the framework's silence about rights. The strike was illegal and the teachers won anyway; the right to organize was, in this episode, neither a legal entitlement nor a bargaining chip but something the strikers simply asserted. Does a power-based account have anything to say about that, or is it silent in a way that matters?

5. Whatever the framework cannot reach. Say plainly where it goes quiet rather than forcing it to speak.
