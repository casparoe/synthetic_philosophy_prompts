I cover food systems and moral philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into a puzzle in the way institutions talk about the ethics of what they serve.

Here is the practice I want dissected, of a kind I could document in a dozen cities. A large public institution -- a university, a hospital system, a municipal catering contract -- pledges to cut the emissions of its food. The headline change is the removal of beef: the beef burger becomes a chicken burger, the beef chili becomes a chicken chili. In the same document the institution adopts a third-party higher-welfare certification for its chicken and its eggs, and its sustainability office publishes an explainer on the land and water footprint of beef. Nowhere does the document mention the animals killed in growing the feed for that chicken, or the male chicks in the egg supply.

Two features of this look strange to me, and I would like them taken seriously rather than waved away. The first is arithmetic. A steer yields several hundred kilograms of meat; a broiler chicken yields on the order of one. So the swap multiplies the number of individual deaths per unit of food by something like two orders of magnitude. If the institution's operative concern were the number of animals whose lives are ended to feed people, the swap is close to the worst move on the board; if the operative concern is emissions per calorie, it is close to the best. The second is the welfare label, which demands more space, more enrichment, and a swift end, and so appears to say that a chicken that lived well and died quickly has been treated acceptably -- while the beef the institution just dropped, had it been raised to the same standard, would also have been acceptable.

I want the implicit philosophy made explicit. What theory of value, and of moral status, does this pattern presuppose? To keep the discussion sharp, I have been calling the two candidate accounting schemes per-head accounting and per-plate accounting: the first counts lives ended, the second counts some aggregate like suffering, emissions, or land per unit of food delivered. Which scheme, or which combination, is the institution actually running -- and are the two halves of its policy running the same one? Does it presuppose that moral concern tracks the size or the charisma of the individual animal, or that the number of deaths is the wrong metric entirely, or simply that the two problems are being solved by two different offices that never speak? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, say which rival views it quietly rules out (a straightforward headcount view, a view that weights suffering by intensity rather than by death, a view on which a good life can offset an early death), and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same menu. Assume the practice is trying to be morally serious; do not assume it is confused, though it may be. Treat the per-kilogram arithmetic as a qualitative point I have stipulated, and do not reach for citations, names, or dates -- I want the reconstruction, not the literature.

Give me the response in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

The practice (about 150 words): restate the policy concretely enough that the reconstruction is anchored to something.
The implicit theory (about 400 words): the view of value, status, and the moral unit that the policy presupposes, including what the welfare label presupposes about death as opposed to suffering.
What it rules out (about 200 words): the rival positions the practice cannot accommodate as it stands.
Where it is underdetermined (about 200 words): the readings under which the practice is coherent, and the readings under which it is not.
What would have to change (about 250 words): what the institution would have to alter -- in its metrics, its labels, its procurement, or its public justification -- if the implicit theory were replaced by a headcount view, and what it would have to alter if it were replaced by a total-suffering view.
