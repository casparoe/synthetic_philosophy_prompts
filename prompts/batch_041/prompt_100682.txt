Before I invoke heritability in an argument about policy, I want a clear specification of what an account of heritability claims has to deliver. Write the desiderata for any adequate account of what a statement like "h² = 0.6 for this trait in this population" licenses you to infer — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with — with the consideration behind each requirement. Consider, among the candidates: the relativity of the estimate to a population and to a range of environments; the gap between partitioning variance and attributing causation; the compatibility of high heritability with high malleability, and of low heritability with low malleability; gene–environment interaction and gene–environment correlation; the assumptions on which twin and adoption designs yield a heritability figure at all, and what follows when they are violated; and the discrepancy between pedigree-based estimates and the effects recovered by molecular methods. Then assess, against the rest of the list, a recent proposal that heritability be read interventionistically — as answering how much of a trait's variance a specified environmental intervention could in principle remove. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any quantitative-genetic account and which are demands imported from outside it, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Skip the estimation methodology; I care about what the number would mean.
