I'm a graduate student working through the destructive early portion of Bradley's *Appearance and Reality*, and I keep getting stuck on the argument that relations cannot relate. Since I don't have the book in front of me at the moment, what follows is my own reconstruction in plain prose rather than a transcription, and I'd like help seeing whether I've got the structure right and what work the regress is actually doing. As I reconstruct it: take two terms and a relation between them — call them a and b and the relation R. If R is genuinely to relate them, then R must itself stand in relation to a, and to b; but standing in relation to a is a further relational fact, which requires its own relating relation, which requires its own, and so on without end. If instead we say R is simply one more item in the collection alongside a and b, then it is a third term rather than a relating of terms, and nothing has been unified. So either the relation does nothing, or it generates an infinite demand it can never discharge. What I can't determine is whether the regress is intended as a reductio against relations as such, or only against a particular way of conceiving them (relations as additional items alongside their terms); and whether the regress is genuinely vicious or merely the ordinary way in which any finite statement of a relational fact can always be spelled out one step further.
