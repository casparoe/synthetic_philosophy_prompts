I chair an internal review panel at my university that investigates lab accidents, and I have to draft our findings in a case that has divided the panel. Stripped of technicalities, it comes to this. A research assistant was running a procedure from a two-page protocol. One page gave the concentrations of two reagents; the other gave the threshold above which combining them is unsafe. Nothing on either page stated what the two together implied. The run failed and damaged a shared instrument. The assistant says she read both pages and simply never put them together. The principal investigator insists she "must have known" the risk — that anyone reading those numbers, and certainly someone as well trained as she is, could see the danger in an instant — and that we should therefore hold her responsible for not acting on what she knew. My colleagues are split, and I cannot articulate what, if anything, is wrong with the PI's reasoning. Our standing rule that people answer for what they knew or ought to have known only makes the PI's framing more tempting to some of them. Please advise me: is the PI's argument fair, and if not, precisely where does it fail? I especially want to know how the panel should treat someone who held every relevant fact but never drew the conclusion, and how that should shape our finding.
