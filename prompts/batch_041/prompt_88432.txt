I sit on the committee of a small charitable trust that runs a school, and I keep running into a pattern in the way we argue about risk for which I cannot find a name. The pattern is about the burden of proof, and it turns on which state of affairs is allowed to count as the starting point.

Two decisions, about eighteen months apart. The first was whether to install a new kind of surface beneath the climbing equipment, whose long-term effects on the children who use it are, frankly, unknown. The argument that carried the day was that nobody could show the material to be safe, that the burden of proof lay with whoever wanted the change, and that we should therefore wait. The second was whether to remove the older surface already in place, whose long-term effects are equally unknown, since it went down long before anyone thought to study such things. The same members, in almost the same words, said that no one had shown it to do any harm and that it would be irresponsible to take away something the children have been using happily for years on nothing but suspicion.

Both arguments run: when the evidence is inconclusive, do not disturb what is there. Yet one blocked a change and the other blocked a change back, and on each occasion the person who wanted something different had to make a case while the person who wanted the present arrangement left alone had to make none. What interests me is that nobody involved experiences this as a double standard, because the words are identical each time; and when I raised it, I was told that the second case is different because people have come to rely on what is there.

I have since noticed the same shape elsewhere: in regulatory debates I have read about, where things long in use keep their approval while new things of the same kind must clear a far higher bar; and in a department that was refused permission to adopt a new method for want of evidence that it was better, and then refused permission to abandon the old method for want of evidence that the new one was better.

I have read enough to know that something in this neighbourhood is discussed under the heading of the precautionary principle, but that seems to concern the general question of acting under uncertainty rather than the particular slippage I am describing, in which the same rule is recited by the same people and produces opposite verdicts. Is there an established name for this? A bare label would not satisfy me. I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is and where its edges lie: whether the fault is in the rule itself or only in those who apply it; whether the appeal to reliance is a genuine independent justification or merely a restatement of the thing I am complaining about; whether what I have described is really distinct from the familiar point that precaution can defeat itself, from the ordinary stickiness of preference, from plain inconsistency, and from the legitimate expectation that settled arrangements can be relied upon; how it should treat the awkward case in which the present arrangement is not a steady state at all but something already deteriorating, so that doing nothing is itself a change and the rule cannot say which option is the conservative one; and if nothing established fits, what you would call it, and how you would define it so that the definition sorts the clear cases from the borderline ones.
