I teach an upper-level seminar on medieval political thought — mixed bag, a few philosophy grad students and some history undergrads who are stronger on context than on argument — and I'm putting together the take-home essay for the unit on Aquinas and resistance to authority. Every year I get the same two papers. One says Aquinas straightforwardly permits killing tyrants and points at the commentary on the Sentences; the other says he forbids it and points at the treatise on kingship he wrote for the king of Cyprus; and neither notices that the two texts may be answering different questions. I want a question that makes that mistake impossible to make.

So: one essay question, answerable in about 90 minutes of writing, that puts those two texts in genuine tension and forces the student to say what exactly the disagreement is, if it is one. They have to deal with the fact that the early commentary treats freeing one's country by killing a tyrant as praiseworthy in at least some cases, while the later treatise warns against private presumption in killing even a bad ruler and seems to locate the remedy somewhere else — in public authority, in God's judgment, or in the sheer risk that the cure costs the community more than the disease. The crux I want them to hit is whether that restriction is moral (nobody may ever do this) or jurisdictional (this isn't yours to do, though it may still be someone's), and whether the distinction lines up with the difference between a usurper who never had title and a legitamate ruler who governs tyrannically. A strong answer will also pull in the sedition discussion from the Secunda Secundae, where Aquinas argues that the unity sedition violates is the unity of law and common good, and that disturbing a ruler whose rule isn't ordered to the common good doesn't count as sedition in the relevant sense — students who see that can say something sharp about why the later caution isn't just a reversal. And I'd like them to say something about what the community's role has to be for the remedy to be legitimate: does Aquinas need a standing public authority with the power to depose, and does the framework collapse where there isn't one?

Please give me: (1) the essay question, phrased so it can't be answered by summarizing either text; (2) a model answer of around 650–750 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 that names five must-hit moves, the marks for each, and, for each, a one-line note on the mistake students typically make there. Assume the students have no internet — don't ask them to cite editions, page numbers, or secondary literature, and I'd rather the question be self-contained about the texts than have them guessing at citations.
