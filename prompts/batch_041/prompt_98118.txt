I have been corresponding with a philosopher — call her R — who works on dispositions, and I am trying to reverse-engineer the rule behind her verdicts. In each of the following cases she is asked, at the moment before anything is triggered, whether the object possesses the disposition in question, and she returns a flat yes or no.

(1) A glass tumbler with the ordinary microstructure of glass. Is it fragile? Yes.

(2) A tumbler of that same microstructure, packed tightly in shock-absorbing foam; struck, it does not break. Is the tumbler fragile? Yes.

(3) A tumbler of that same microstructure over which a sorcerer has cast a spell: should the tumbler ever be struck, the spell will instantaneously suspend its brittleness, so that it does not break. Is the tumbler fragile? Yes.

(4) A cup of soft steel, with no brittleness in it at all, fitted with a device that shatters it whenever it is struck; struck, it shatters. Is the cup fragile? No.

(5) A steel cup over which a sorcerer has cast the opposite spell: should the cup ever be struck, the spell will instantaneously convert it to glass and it will shatter. Is the cup fragile? No.

(6) A sugar cube encased in a waterproof shell; placed in water, it does not dissolve. Is the sugar soluble? Yes.

Now the case I would like you to call. A rod of a material I will call sleeper alloy: as it sits it is not brittle in the least — you can bend it like copper wire. But its internal structure is metastable, and being struck is itself the trigger of an intrinsic transformation: within the instant of impact the rod's own structure rearranges into a glassy, brittle state, and it shatters. Nothing external acts on the rod; the transformation is simply part of what the rod intrinsically does when struck. Would R say the rod is fragile, before it is struck?

I care less about R's answer than about the rule she is running. Her six verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one rule — among others, an intrinsic-basis rule (a thing is fragile just in case it currently possesses the intrinsic property that, in the ordinary case, is responsible for breaking) and a counterfactual rule (a thing is fragile just in case it would break if struck, once external finks and masks are set aside); a naive conditional that ignores masking and finks altogether is ruled out by the data, but say so explicitly rather than assuming it. Lay out the rules that fit the six verdicts, say what each predicts about the sleeper rod, give your best guess as to which rule she is running and why the alternatives fit her data less well, and name one further case I could put to her next that would force the surviving rules apart.
