I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of war, and I keep noticing a pattern that our readings treat as three unrelated topics. Under the law of armed conflict, a combatant who kills enemy soldiers in accordance with the rules is not criminally liable for doing so, and that immunity holds whichever side was in the right — the aggressor's soldier who fights lawfully commits no crime merely by fighting. Yet the same body of law makes aggression a leadership crime: only those who shape a state's decision to go to war can be prosecuted for it, not the people who carry it out. And in the United States, conscientious objector status is available only to those who oppose war as such; a soldier who objects to one particular war as unjust, however sincerely, does not qualify, as the Supreme Court held in 1971. So the law appears to treat the individual soldier's judgment about whether the war should be fought as irrelevant both to what he may lawfully do and to what he may refuse to do.

What does this arrangement presuppose about the moral standing of the individual combatant? Does it commit the law to the moral equality of combatants, or could reciprocity, the humane treatment of prisoners, soldiers' limited access to information about the justice of the cause, or the state's authority to decide on war do the same work without that thesis? Which rival views does the arrangement actually rule out, and which does it merely fail to accommodate? And are its parts consistent with one another, or does the leadership-crime rule quietly concede something that combatant immunity denies?

Finally, consider a reform I have heard proposed: permit selective objection, but only where a recognized international body has declared the war illegal. If we adopted it, would we have replaced the presupposed view or simply relocated the same deference to authority? And if we accepted instead the view that a soldier fighting an unjust war is doing something seriously wrong, what in this arrangement would have to change?
