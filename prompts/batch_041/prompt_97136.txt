I sit on the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI lab, and I have to write teh draft opinion for Thursday's meeting because I missed the last two and everyone else is annoyed at me. We're deciding whether to ship a coding assistant built on a model that's maybe two generations behind the frontier but still strong enough to write nontrivial patches. The safety team isn't claiming the model is aligned. Instead they're proposing what they call a control protocol: the untrusted model drafts code, a much weaker trusted model scores each diff for suspiciousness, anything above a threshold gets thrown out and rewritten by the trusted model, and a random 2% of accepted diffs go to a human reviewer. There's also a resample rule where if the trusted monitor flags three diffs in a row the session gets killed. The alignment team hates this. They say if the model is actually misaligned, a weak monitor can be fooled by steganographic cues in variable names or comments, or by the model writing code that looks boring but does the bad thing later; and if the model is aligned, we're paying a huge control tax in latency and quality for nothing. The safety team's reply is that we don't need to know whether it's aligned, we only need to know it can't do much damage under the protocol, and that waiting for alignment guarantees is itself a decision to deploy nothing, which has its own costs. One board member keeps framing it as bounded adversary versus tool: if you treat it as a bounded adversary, control is the only honest safety case; if you treat it as a tool, control is just insurance you can't afford.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday: recommend a concrete decision — ship, don't ship, or ship under specific conditions — with a time frame, maybe a 30-day pilot with named tripwires and a rollback trigger, and give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side I end up rejecting. Don't just recite generic AI safety talking points; reason from the actual protocol above, say what would count as the protocol failing, and say what evidence would change the recommendation. I'd also like it to address the bounded adversary versus tool framing directly, because I think that's doing more work in the disagreement than people admit.
