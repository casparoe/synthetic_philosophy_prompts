In a graduate seminar on aesthetics, a fellow student repeatedly used the phrase "a variation on a theme" to settle disputes about originality: whenever someone claimed a work was genuinely new, she replied that it was merely a variation on themes already in circulation, and from this she drew the further conclusion that the pursuit of originality is pointless. I am persuaded that the phrase cannot bear that weight, but I cannot state precisely what it is claiming, nor where the argument goes wrong. Please set out, as a menu rather than as a single preferred reading, the distinct claims that "a variation on a theme" could be making when it is used this way — ranging from the nearly trivial to the strongly revisionary — and for each indicate whether it is true, defensible, or false, and whether it would actually license her conclusion about the futility of seeking originality.

Several distinctions seem worth respecting, and I would like the menu to mark them. Is the claim descriptive (creativity happens to proceed by variation) or prescriptive (one ought to vary rather than invent from nothing)? Is the relevant variation the recombination of pre-existing elements, movement along the parameters of a pre-existing representational space, or something else again? Is the thesis about the workings of an individual mind, about the history of a genre or tradition, or about thought as such? And how does each reading handle the awkward case of a work that appears to introduce a new dimension rather than a new value along an old one? Finally, tell me which of these readings the phrase is most likely meant to carry in ordinary argumentative settings such as the one I describe, since that is the reading I will actually have to answer.
