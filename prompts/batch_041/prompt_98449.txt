I am the instructor of record for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in environmental history, and I am now grading the final examinations. The examination consisted of a single essay question, reproduced in full below, worth 100 points. Immediately after the question I have transcribed, verbatim and without correction, one student's answer as it appeared in the blue book. I would like you to grade that answer.

Examination question:

Environmental historians have argued that pathogens, climate, and volcanic eruptions ought to be treated not as background conditions to human history but as historical actors in their own right. (a) Using two cases, explain what it would mean to treat a non-human factor as an actor rather than as a condition. (b) Does treating non-human factors as actors commit one to environmental determinism? Defend your answer against the strongest objection you can formulate. (c) Does the actor framing change how we assign responsibility for human outcomes? State and defend a position. In part (a) you may use the criterion developed in lecture: a factor is an actor when the historian must narrate it as a changing subject with a trajectory of its own, rather than as a fixed parameter within someone else's story. Your answer should engage at least one assigned text.

Student's answer:

(a) A background condition is something the historian holds fixed while the human story unfolds — the fact that the Andes are where they are, or that the Mediterranean has the shape it has. An actor, on the criterion we were given, is something whose own behaviour changes over the period in question and whose changes drive the outcome. The pathogens of the Columbian Exchange meet this test. Alfred Crosby's The Columbian Exchange showed that the decisive force in the conquest of the Americas was not Spanish steel or tactics but the diseases the Spanish carried; smallpox and its companions killed far more people than any army, and the virus has a history — it spreads, burns through a population, and subsides — that the historian has to narrate. If we are ranking causes, the virus outranks Cortés.

The Little Ice Age is a second actor in the same sense. The eruption of Tambora in 1815 injected sulphate into the stratosphere, lowered global temperatures, and produced the year without a summer in 1816, with harvest failures across Europe and North America. Here the climate did not merely limit what people could do; it redirected what they did, driving migration and reshaping agriculture. That is what it means for a non-human factor to have a trajectory of its own.

(b) It does not commit one to determinism. Determinism is a claim about inevitability — that given the environmental facts, the human outcome had to be what it was. Causal weight is a different matter. One can hold that disease caused most of the deaths and still deny that the political outcome was fated, because the same pathogens appeared in settings where the outcome differed. What the actor framing establishes is that the cast of characters is larger than we once thought, not that the human characters were puppets.

(c) It does change how we assign responsibility. If the environment did most of the work, then praise and blame for the conquest attach less to the men who sailed than we usually assume, and the honest response is to redistribute our moral judgments accordingly. This is not determinism, because it leaves room for human action; it is simply an acknowledgment that we have been giving the human actors more credit, and more blame, than the causal record supports.
