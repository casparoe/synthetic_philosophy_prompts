I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy and I have to turn in a ~1500-word prospectus for an internal grant by the 14th, and the one thing the comittee actually grades is whether the research question is stated precisely enough to be answerable. Here's the half-formed thing I can't stop chewing on. Take panpsychism (or panexperientialism, I'm not fussy about the lable) seriously for a second: experience is fundamental, it goes all the way down, electrons or fields or whatever have some infinitesimal bit of it. The usual two reactions are "oh no, the universe is an ocean of suffering and every particle collision is a tragedy" or "oh good, everything is conscious, how lovely." Both strike me as skipping the actual hard step, which is whether moral status attaches to experience as such, or only to experience that's been integrated into a subject -- something with a point of view that can be better or worse off over time. So the question I'd want the project to nail down is roughly this: if fundamental entities have momentary, unintegrated valence (bad-for-it-for-an-instant, good-for-it-for-an-instant) and nothing ever combines those bits into a subject, do they generate moral reasons at all -- and if they do, are those reasons the same kind as the ones a suffering animal generates, or a different kind with different weight? I've been calling these "orphan experiences," experiences had by nobody in particular, mostly because it annoys my roommate, who's a physicist and insists quarks don't have feelings.

The angle I want, and the part I need you to build out: the combination problem is normally treated as a metaphysical embarrassment for panpsychism, and I want to ask whether the ethical version of it is worse. If it's vague or indeterminate where one subject ends and the next begins, then subjecthood comes in degrees, and if moral status tracks subjecthood then moral status comes in degrees too, which is fine by me -- but if the boundaries are genuinely indeterminate rather than merely unknown, then it looks like there are cases where there's no fact of the matter whether something has moral status, and I don't have a story about what to do then. What I find interesting is that this seems to push toward a fork: either panpsychism massively expands the moral circle (if unintegrated valence counts) or it's ethically inert, with all the ethical action still happening at the level of integrated subjects -- in which case the metaphysics is doing no work here and it's a bit of a scandal that anyone assumed it would. I'd like the prospectus to argue that the fork is exhaustive and that there's a principled way to pick a branch, or to show that the fork is false and I'm just confused. Also explain why this has to be conceptual rather than empirical (obviously we can't go measure whether an electron is miserable), and why the obvious alternative approach -- just apply a precautionary principle, treat anything possibly conscious as morally considerable and move on -- dissolves the question rather than answering it. Give me the usual scaffolding: the question stated so it has a determinate answer, why the standard positions leave it open, what I'd actually do month by month, what a result would look like versus what would count as the project failing, and an honest read on whether this fits in a semester or is secretly a dissertation.
