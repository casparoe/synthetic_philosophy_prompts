I cover archaeology and the history of science for a general-interest magazine, and two or three times a month I file a story in which a period label is doing the heavy lifting in the first paragraph: a manuscript find described as the last flowering of the medieval world, a set of experiments billed as the birth of modern science, a technique credited to a Renaissance rediscovery. I know these labels were coined and hardened long after the centuries they name, and that they carry a story about decline and rebirth rather than simply marking time; what I lack is a way to act on that knowledge at four in the afternoon with an editor waiting. Write me a decision procedure I can run on my own draft in a few minutes: the questions to ask about any period term, in order; what counts as a clean answer to each; when the procedure says cut the label or replace it with a plain date range; and where it stops and hands the call to me or my editor, because what is at stake there is which story we want to tell and not a fact about the past. Keep the distinction between a label used as a time-stamp and the same label used as a claim about a rupture or a cause, since that is the one my copy keeps fudging, and make the tests work when the label arrives in a researcher's press release and when it is applied to centuries outside Europe. Then run it on three cases: the medieval-world find, the same centuries called early modern in a story about South Asia, and the birth-of-modern-science framing where the framing is what makes a modest result sound important. Tell me plainly which of the three the procedure refuses to settle, and where it would give an answer I will not like. No citations or reading lists; this has to run on what a working journalist can hold in their head.
