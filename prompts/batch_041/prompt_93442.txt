Im a law clerk at the federal courthouse here and I've somehow ended up as the keynote at our next naturalization ceremony -- about sixty people taking the oath, their families, two immigration judges, and a civics class from the high school that gets bused over. Eight minutes, nine if I'm lucky. I want to build it around Frederick Douglass's constitutional views, but not the obvious version. Everyone reaches for the Fourth of July oration and I'd rather not. What actually has me hooked is the reversal: he spent the first decade of his public life as a Garrisonian, arguing the Constitution was a pro-slavery document, a bargain with the slave power, and that anyone who swore an oath to it was implicated in it -- and then in the early 1850s he flipped and spent the rest of his life insisting that read properly it was an antislavery document instead. His argument was mostly a lawyer's argument about the text: the preamble, the fact that the words slave and slavery never once appear in the original document, that the three-fifths clause and the fugitive clause both talk about persons rather than property, and that Garrison and his crowd had handed the slaveholders a gift by conceding the thing. I've been arguing about this with my brother-in-law, who teaches history, and I've come around to thinking both readings are half right, which is the part I don't want smoothed over.

So: write the speech. I want the reversal doing real structural work -- these people are about to swear they'll defend the Constitution, and the speech has to make sense of that given that the same text was used to defend slavery for seventy years and that the courts of Douglass's own day read it the other way. Don't let it become a patriotic crescendo and don't let it become a seminar; it has to land for the two judges and the local bar association people who are coming, without losing the families and the sixteen-year-olds, some of whom are still learning English. No quoting the famous Douglass lines, everyone's heard them, and please don't turn him into a modern progressive -- he was a man of his century and I don't want to pretend otherwise. If the honest answer is that the text didn't free anybody by itself, say so.
