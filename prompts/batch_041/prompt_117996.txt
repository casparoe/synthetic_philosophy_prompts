I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm trying to get a piece on Parfit's hitchhiker off the ground — the desert one, where the driver will only stop if he believes you'll actually send the money afterwards, so the rational move seems to be to make yourself into the sort of person who would pay up. My argument is that the case has quietly gone stale. It's whole force comes from your disposition being the only currency you've got out there. Give me a signal, an escrow account, a reputation score, a smart contract, a prediction market I can bet on my own future behaviour in, and I don't have to become trustworthy — I can buy credibility at whatever the market price is. I've been calling this credibility plumbing, and my claim is that it doesn't just rescue the hitchhiker, it dissolves the interesting part of "rational irrationality": precommitment turns into a service with a price list, and the live questions become what the device costs, who can't afford it, and whether the poor-but-honest hitchhiker loses the seat to the rich one who simply posts collateral. I want to hold on to a residue of cases the plumbing can't reach — someone reading your face in the few seconds before they decide to stop. Then a seperate closing section on AI, where every commitment is auditable by construction, and trustworthiness might be an obsolete virtue rather than a load-bearing one.

Give me exactly five numbered comments on the argument, one or two sentences each, no intro or wrap-up: where it's weakest, what I'm leaving out, and whether my telling of the case and its point is even right.
