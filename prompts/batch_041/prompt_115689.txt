A mate of mine is a republican and keeps trotting out "the monarch reigns but does not rule" to argue the King is basically a ceremonial ornament, so we may as well bin the whole thing — but then the same guy turns around and says the Crown is our last backstop if a PM ever goes full autocrat. Those two can't both be resting on the same five words, so I think the slogan is doing several jobs at once and he doesn't know which one he's using. Can you lay out the different precise claims it could be making — legal, political, normative, whatever — and for each say whether it's trivially true, substantive and actually defensible, or just false, and which reading he must be leaning on each time? I'd like it tested against some concrete cases rather than in the abstract: royal assent (what actually happens if the King says no), a hung parliament where nobody can command a majority, and a governor-general sacking a prime minister (the Australian one in '75). Does the slogan even cover the monarch's representative, or only the person in the crown? And I keep hearing that the monarch really only has three rights — to be consulted, to encourage, to warn — is that a fifth reading of the slogan or a quiet admission that the slogan is false? Menu with verdicts please, not an essay, and no reading list — I just want to win the argument at the pub.
