I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and before I read any more proposed solutions to the problem of consequentialist cluelessness, I want the specification rather than the solutions. Give me the list of requirements that any theory of how a consequentialist should act under cluelessness would have to meet in order to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with your judgment about which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. I do not want names, dates, or a reading list; I cannot check attributions and I am not looking for a survey.

Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy is relative to a purpose: I want a theory usable by an agent who has to commit to an action now, with bounded time and attention, who wants to keep the distinctively consequentialist commitment that only consequences count, and who wants neither to be paralyzed by the thought of untraceable effects nor to be credulous about effects that nobody could have traced. If you think that brief is incoherent, say so in a sentence or two and then work with it anyway.

The requirements I have managed to formulate on my own are below. Sharpen them, correct them, or discard them if they are not genuine requirements, but treat them as the starting point rather than ignoring them.

1. Locate the cluelessness. The theory should say whether the trouble lies in our information about an act's causal upshots, in the value attached to those upshots, in how the act itself is individuated, or in the summation of effects over an unbounded future, because the remedy differs in each case. A theory that treats these as one problem will often prescribe gathering more information when the quantity being estimated is not well defined in the first place.

2. Distinguish grades of failure. Failing to rank two options, ranking them without any justified confidence, and ranking them in a way whose sign flips under a different description are three different failures, and a theory that runs them together will either overreact or underreact. In my own work the analogous distinctions are: the check does not run, the check passes and I do not believe it, and the result changes when the units change. I want the requirements stated so that these stay apart.

3. Survive re-description. One purchase of a coffee, one movement of a hand, and one further increment of participation in a global supply chain should not receive incompatible verdicts, or else the theory must say which description is canonical and why. A procedure whose output depends on which description it is handed is not a decision procedure.

4. Be neither dominated by the unestimable nor blind to it. If an arbitrarily unlikely, arbitrarily large effect can move the theory to any conclusion, it cannot guide action; if it ignores such effects by construction, it has given up on consequences. The same tension appears at the other end, with a certain small effect weighed against an unestimated large one.

5. Say what happens over an unbounded future. Either there is a principled stopping rule, or there is an argument that none is needed, or the instruction to maximize expected value is not well formed because the relevant sum diverges or is undefined.

6. Separate the theory's cluelessness from mine. The interesting claim is that the ranking itself is unsettled, not that I cannot compute it, so the requirements should be stated about the theory's verdicts rather than about my epistemic state; otherwise the theory is being blamed for my limits.

7. Be runnable by a bounded agent. A criterion satisfiable only by an ideal agent with unlimited time is not a standard for me, and a theory that tells me to act only when the verdict is determinate has handed the real work to a meta-procedure it has not supplied.

8. Keep the critical bite. A theory that dissolves cluelessness by declaring the options roughly equal, or by deferring to ordinary moral opinion, has stopped being a consequentialist theory; I want the requirement that forces it to rule some actions out and say why.

9. Fix the unit of evaluation. I cannot estimate the consequences of one coffee purchase, but I might estimate the consequences of a standing policy of buying coffee, or of a disposition to do so; the theory must say whether moving to policies, rules, or dispositions answers the problem or changes the subject.

10. Cope with being one of the things being evaluated. What I choose changes what future agents count as a consequence and how much weight they give it, as when I decide what to teach a junior colleague or release a library that many people will build on. The evaluation must not be ill-founded when its own future operation is among the effects.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, taking the plain expected-value maximizer, the indirect or rule-based consequentialist, and the view that says only that one should do as well as one can given one's evidence as the candidates to beat. And tell me whether any two or more of the requirements cannot be satisfied together. If the specification turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that is the result I most want, so do not dissolve the tension by quietly weakening a requirement.
