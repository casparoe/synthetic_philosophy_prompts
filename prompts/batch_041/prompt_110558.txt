I have no academic training, but for the last couple of years I've been reading about lost civilizations, alternative chronologies, and the fights that break out around them, and I've come to hold a set of gut verdicts that I cannot reduce to a rule. I'd like you to find the rule, if there is one, and to tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. A Russian mathematician's claim that most of ancient and medieval history was fabricated in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries by a small group of chronologers, so that many figures we treat as separated by centuries are really the same person. Pseudohistory. It makes no difference to me that he is a real mathematician using statistics.

2. Ancient astronauts: the great monuments of the world were built with extraterrestrial help. Pseudohistory.

3. A Nobel-winning biologist once suggested, in an ordinary scientific paper, that life on Earth might have been deliberately seeded by some earlier civilization. I do not think that is pseudoscience at all, even though it is as unverifiable as anything in (2), and it also says aliens were involved. It reads to me as an honest speculation that admits what it is.

4. A psychiatrist's book claiming that Venus was a comet ejected from Jupiter within human history, and that its passage caused the plagues and parted the seas. Pseudoscience. Yet the mainstream story that the Moon was made by a giant collision between the young Earth and another body, a catastrophe on a far greater scale, seems to me ordinary science.

5. A German meteorologist's 1912 proposal that the continents drifted apart from a single landmass: attacked for lacking a mechanism, largely dismissed for about forty years, now the foundation of the field. Not pseudoscience, and the consensus was simply wrong.

6. The 1989 claim of room-temperature fusion in a beaker. I want to split this one. The original announcement was bad science, not pseudoscience. The movement that grew around it afterwards, blaming the calorimeters and the referees for decades rather than the claim, is pseudoscience.

7. The old American belief that the earthworks and burial mounds of North America were built by a vanished race rather than by the Indians. Pseudohistory, and it was the orthodox view, taught as fact in its day, and used to justify dispossession. What troubles me most is that here the true answer was the one that kept being dismissed, and the people who dismissed it were not a conspiracy, just an entire society.

8. Flat earth, and the claim that the earth is six thousand years old. Pseudoscience, instantly. I notice my verdict here is much faster and much angrier than on any case above, which makes me wonder whether I am using the word descriptively or as an insult.

The rule I have been working with has three parts. (a) A claim turns pseudoscientific when its defenders explain away every failed test by adjusting something other than the claim itself: the instrument, the replication, the referee. (b) A claim turns pseudoscientific when it needs a keeper of the secret, someone with both the motive and the power to have hidden or destroyed the evidence, because ordinary scientific error does not need a villain. (c) A claim is not pseudoscientific merely for being speculative, unpopular, or later shown to be false.

I can already see trouble. Clause (b) does nothing for the mounds case, which had no secret-keeper, and it seems to convict the ulcer doctor who complained that the establishment was ignoring him, whom I take to be a hero. Clause (a) does not obviously separate the fusion crowd from the continental-drift people, who also spent decades defending a claim the evidence had not yet caught up with. I have also been told that the answer is falsifiability, but (3) is arguably unfalsifiable and I refuse to call it pseudoscience, while (5) was perfectly falsifiable and got rejected anyway. And nothing in my rule mentions what strikes me most about the cases I am sure of: the argument is always over the same old material, and nobody ever goes out and digs a new hole. Real researchers seem to spend their effort on evidence that does not exist yet; the others spend it re-reading what we already have.

Two further things I cannot sort out. First, some of my verdicts are about a claim and some are about a person or a group; (6) is entirely about people and (3) entirely about a claim. Which of those is pseudoscience really a property of? Second, the cases I am most certain about all carry a payload: they flatter or indict a nation, a religion, or a race. The ones I am least sure about do not. Is that a coincidence, or does the harm have to be written into the definition?

What I want from you: the principle, stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause; an honest statement of which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should surrender that one; and its verdict on a case I have not listed, namely the claim that the Sphinx is thousands of years older than the dynastic Egyptians, on the grounds that the erosion on its enclosure walls is water erosion from a wet climate, put forward by a working geologist who says the field refuses to listen to anyone outside it.
