Next term I am giving a guest lecture to a cohort of first-year PhD students in a decision theory seminar on risk-weighted expected utility. They know the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the standard expected utility representation cold, and most of them assume that any systematic departure from expected utility must be a reasoning error rather than a rival conception of rationality. Do not deliver the exposition; plan the lecture. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, from the separation of risk attitude from the curvature of the utility function through to the risk function itself; the single concrete choice problem that should carry the whole framework, and why the Allais paradox is the wrong vehicle for this audience; the misconceptions they will predictably form — in particular that the risk function is merely prospect theory's probability weighting under another name, or that it distorts belief rather than expressing a distinct attitude toward risk — and the exact point in the sequence at which to head each off; one question to pose at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped what is distinctive about the theory; and what I should explicitly set aside at this level and tell them I am setting aside.
