I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and a labmate and I have been going back and forth about whether it is legitimate to say that a large language model believes things. To pin the dispute down I wrote his position out as an argument, and I now suspect it proves too much, but I can't locate the fault line. I'd like you to help me find it.

Take on the role of an expert in the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of cognitive science, with a particular command of the literature on intentionality and the attribution of mental states to artifacts. Think carefully, and work through this step by step rather than giving me a verdict up front.

The argument, as I've reconstructed it:

(1) To attribute a belief to a system is to claim that the system's behavior is best predicted and explained by treating it as possessing that belief, together with the desires and other states that a believer would have.

(2) A belief attribution to a system is justified if and only if the intentional stance — treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — predicts and explains its behavior better than any competing stance does: the design stance, which predicts from the system's internal organization and purpose, or the physical stance, which predicts from its microphysical constitution.

(3) For a present-day language model, the intentional stance does this well. We correctly and informatively explain its outputs by saying that it misunderstood the prompt, assumed a false premise, was trying to be helpful, or thought the answer was such-and-such.

(4) The same is true of a chess engine, a thermostat, a colony of ants, and the process of natural selection: for some range of behaviors, the intentional stance predicts and explains what each of them does better than the alternatives.

(5) So, by (2), belief attributions to language models, chess engines, thermostats, ant colonies, and natural selection are all justified.

(6) But it is not justified to attribute beliefs to a thermostat, an ant colony, or natural selection. Whatever we are doing when we talk that way, we are not saying something true about their minds.

(7) Therefore either (2) is false, or (3) does not secure what I want, and we are no more entitled to say that a language model believes something than that a thermostat does.

I want to keep (3): I think there is a real and defensible sense in which a language model can be said to believe, or at least to represent, that such-and-such. I also want to keep (6), because I cannot bring myself to say that a thermostat or an ant colony has beliefs. I have no particular attachment to (2) as stated.

Here is what I keep running into. The phrase "better than any competing stance" in (2) is doing all the work, and I can read it two ways. Read strictly — the stance that would yield the most accurate predictions if we had unlimited time and computation — the intentional stance is never the best one for an artifact, because in principle the design stance (just run the code) or the physical stance always predicts at least as well; that would kill (3). Read loosely — the stance most useful to an interpreter with limited time and information — the intentional stance is best for the thermostat too, since it is less trouble to say it believes the room is cold than to trace its circuitry; that would kill (6). So the criterion seems to force me to surrender one of the two claims I care about.

Before you commit to a diagnosis, please brainstorm the candidate fault lines — at least three — and say which you can eliminate and why. Then address the following:

(a) Is the argument valid, or is there a step where something slips: a quantifier or scope shift, an equivocation, a slide between what justifies adopting a stance and what makes an attribution true? If there is such a step, name it and explain why it looked innocent.

(b) If the argument is valid, which of (2), (3), or (6) is least costly to give up, and what exactly does giving it up commit me to? I want the price of each option, not merely the one you prefer.

(c) Is there a reading of "better," or a replacement for (2), that lets me keep both (3) and (6) without cheating? If the honest answer is that belief talk is stance-dependent in a way that makes my attachment to (6) unmotivated, say so plainly and tell me what follows for how we should talk about language models.

Be concrete about the intermediate positions — instrumentalism, realism about the intentional stance, and the idea that what matters is whether the system's internal states track states of the world — and say which of them each step of the argument actually requires. Finish by stating clearly whether you think a language model can be said to believe anything, and on what grounds.
