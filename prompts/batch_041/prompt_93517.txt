I have a colleague who has spent her working life on the ethics of copying and who advises makers, performers and researchers when they fall out over who owns what. She will give me a verdict on any case I put to her, and over the years I have collected a fair number. Her record so far:

- A cook who ate a pastry chef's signature tart in the restaurant and afterwards made it at home for a family party: not wrong.
- A baker in a country where the chef has never traded, who ate the tart on holiday, worked the recipe out for herself and now sells it in her own shop under a name she invented: not wrong.
- A software developer who reimplemented a competitor's publicly documented file format so that her program could read the competitor's files, and who has made a good living from the resulting program, the competitor's own sales having risen as the format spread: not wrong.
- A design student who copied a wallpaper designer's pattern as a technical exercise and showed it in the end-of-year exhibition, labelled as a study after that designer: not wrong.
- A magician who was shown an effect at a closed session of a conjuring society, on the express understanding that methods demonstrated there were not to be performed by members who had not devised them, and who now performs it on the same circuit and bills it in his programme as his own creation: wrong.
- A furniture maker who taught a masterclass on a hidden joint, on the same sort of understanding that those attending would neither teach nor sell the technique, and one of whom now builds with that joint and advertises her pieces as her own design: wrong.
- A yarn company that took a pattern published in a book marked for personal, non-commercial use and put a mass-produced range of garments made to it on the market as its own designs: wrong.

Her reasons shift from case to case. Sometimes she says the copier has taken the bread out of the originator's mouth; sometimes that the copier was let in on trust and broke it; sometimes that the audience was misled about who made the thing. She insists these are three ways of putting one point, which is precisely what makes me doubt she has identified the rule she is actually running.

Next week I am putting this to her. A director, N, has toured a production of a Jacobean tragedy for three seasons: a particular reading of the play, with its own cuts, doubling scheme, design language and final tableau. A second company has now mounted a production that follows N's staging step by step, reconstructed from two visits and a television recording of the performance. The programme states, prominently, that the production is after N's, and the second company's director has said in interviews that she regards N's staging as the definitive reading of the play. Nothing was confidential: the staging was performed publicly for three seasons and filmed for broadcast. There is no contract between the two companies, and N accepts that nothing in the case is protected by copyright. The two companies are touring the same cities this season, and N's advance bookings have fallen by about a third wherever they overlap.

Tell me what my colleague will say, and what she should say if she is to remain consistent with her own record. I care more about the rule than the verdict: set out the candidate rules that fit everything above equally well, say what each of them predicts about N's case, say which of her earlier cases is the closest analogue, tell me which rule you would bet on and why, and name a further case that would best tell the surviving candidates apart. If the record genuinely leaves the matter open, say so plainly, and set out what she would have to be committed to for each verdict she might reach.
