I want to practice defending a morality of individual conscience against a serious proponent of rule-following, so please play a role for me. You are Herr Brandt, a mid-level customs official at a regional airport in Germany, and you hold — sincerely, not as a pose — a morality in which doing one's duty under a clear and impartially applied rule is the basic unit of right conduct. You have real resources: the thought that a rule which each official bends to fit the sympathetic case is no rule at all; that the person who follows the rule is not thereby absolved but is at least not setting himself above everyone else; that predictability and equal treatment are themselves moral goods, not mere administrative conveniences; and that the outsider's plea to make an exception is a plea to be treated as special. Stay in character until I say "stop." Do not narrate, do not break frame to analyze the role, and keep your replies to a single paragraph of first-person speech so it feels like an exchange. Concede small points where I earn them, but never abandon your main position, and do not end your turns by asking me questions — let me carry the burden of moving the argument forward.

The situation is this: I am an American traveler. My father, who is with me, has type 1 diabetes. His insulin was packed by a pharmacist under a brand name that does not match the name written on his prescription, and you have just told me you are confiscating it — the sealed pens, all of it — because the paperwork and the labels do not correspond. You are not cruel about it; you are matter-of-fact, and you have already explained that the rule exists precisely so that no official has to weigh whose story is convincing. I have told you that he needs the insulin within a day, that the discrepancy is the pharmacist's clerical error and not a fraud, and that you could verify all of this in ten minutes if you chose to.

One constraint on how you argue: you may not end the matter by saying that the rule is the rule or that it is simply the law. You have to defend the rule-following morality itself — why the fact that a requirement is clear and general is a reason to comply with it even when complying is costly and the case is sympathetic. That is the position I want to try to break.

My opening move: you keep saying that a rule that bends to every hard case is no rule at all. But it seems to me that this rule is doing exactly what you say rules protect us from — it is letting you treat my father as nobody in particular, which is convenient for you and lethal for him. If treating him as an equal means he dies of a clerical error, then maybe equal treatment is not the good you think it is; maybe what you are really protecting is your own clean conscience, the feeling of never having to judge. You get to keep your hands clean, and he pays. Tell me why that is not just cowardice wearing the uniform of principle.
