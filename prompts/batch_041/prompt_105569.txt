The story that started it: one hiker has a hunch the left fork is the right way and takes it, and it happens to be correct; another reads the map and takes the same fork. Both arrive. Call them Hunch and Map.

(1) Hunch: Map's belief is worth more — he *knows*, Hunch merely got lucky — and knowledge is just more valuable than true belief.
(2) Map: valuable how? Same trail, same outcome. If a true belief gets you there, where's the shortfall? You're admiring the process, not the outcome, and admiring a process isn't a claim about the belief's value.
(3) Hunch: the shortfall shows up when things change — the trail forks again and Map can re-derive while Hunch is just guessing anew. Knowledge is robust, and robustness is value.
(4) Map: then you're not saying knowledge is better, you're saying it's better *elsewhere* — which is just saying it's more useful, an instrumental claim, not the claim that knowledge beats true belief as such. And on your own view Hunch's belief was fine *here*.
(5) Hunch: forget robustness — Map *deserves* his true belief and Hunch doesn't; he earned it.
(6) Map: 'deserving' a belief is a moral notion smuggled into epistemology — a kid knows her parents' names without earning it, so deserving can't be what knowledge adds.
(7) Hunch: maybe what knowledge adds is calibration — a true belief held with confidence matched to how reliable the basis really is keeps steering you right, whereas Hunch's true belief carries no license for its confidence, and that mismatch is the loss.
(8) Map: but Hunch's confidence was vindicated, so the mismatch cost him nothing; and if you mean it costs him *in expectation*, we're back to usefulness in general, not to this belief being worth less.

Neither budged. Give me a verdict: who's right on each numbered move (clearly wrong / shaky / fine / clearly right), what each side is quietly assuming, what they're both missing, and who's more right overall. Don't hedge — I need to know who to tell they lost.
