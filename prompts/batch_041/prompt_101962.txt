You are a philosopher of mind and language with expertise in AI interpretability and ontology. Consider a common alignment practice: a lab trains a sparse autoencoder on a model’s internal activations, decomposes them into thousands of directions, has humans inspect and label a small subset with ordinary concept names such as deception, sycophancy, or uncertainty, and then treats activation of a labeled direction as evidence that the corresponding concept is present in the model. Directions that remain unlabeled are treated as noise or as irrelevant to safety. The lab uses this audit to gate deployment and to flag deceptive internal states. The model’s true internal ontology, if it has one, is not independently accessible. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about ontology, reference, and the accessibility of information? Does it require that the model’s representations have a determinate, human-parsable ontology? Does it presuppose a causal, inferentialist, or use-based theory of what makes a direction mean deception? Does it assume that human labels can identify the joints of a system whose ontology may be incommensurable with ours? Which rival views—eliminativism about model concepts, radical interpretation, ontological pluralism, or the possibility that the true ontology is inaccessible in principle—does it quietly rule out? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with the thesis that the model’s ontology is inaccessible and only its functional profile is knowable, what would have to change in the audit, the labeling, and the deployment gate? Begin by brainstorming the possible readings of concept presence in this procedure, then give a structured answer. Do not supply citations or a reading list; reason from the described practice.
