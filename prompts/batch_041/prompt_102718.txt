I'm the faculty rep on my uni's academic accomodations appeals panel and we've got a case this week that's splitting the room. A third-year student with a severe acquired memory impairment (encephalitis a few years back, he can't reliably hold onto new material for more than a few hours) already has extra time and a note-taker approved. Now he's asking to bring into a closed-book exam the setup he wears all day: a smartwatch paired to his phone, running an app that continuously records and transcribes, dumps everything into a personal knowledge base, and then surfaces relevant snippets and generates little summaries and practice questions on the fly. Here's the wrinkle that's got people going: his two closest study partners have write access to that same knowledge base, and he to theirs, so a decent chunk of what's in there is stuff the three of them contributed to each other, and the summaries are machine-generated, not just his own notes handed back to him. He says this isn't a crib sheet, it's his memory, it's as much a part of how he thinks as his own head is, and taking it away is like confiscating part of his brain. The exams office says closed-book means closed-book, full stop, and that if we let this in we have to let in everyone's phone and the whole category collapses. One of my colleagues half-remembers some criteria from a philosophy course — that a tool only really counts as part of your mind if it's always there when you need it, instantly accessible, automatically trusted rather than consulted, and endorsed without deliberation — and wants us to just test the case against those four boxes; I'm not sure that's the right test and I'd like your view folded in.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Friday's meeting. Recommend a concrete outcome — allow, deny, or allow with conditions — and if it's conditions, spell out exactly what they'd be and how we'd actually enforce them, in a way an exam invigilator could follow. Don't just fall back on the familiar Otto-and-his-notebook thought experiment; genuinely take his claim that the setup is part of his cognition seriously, and genuinely take the exams office's integrity and parity worry seriously, including whether the shared, machine-generated part of the knowledge base changes the picture compared to a private notebook. I want you to commit to a position and defend it rather than laying out options for us to pick from. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning, and skip the preamble and the restating of the question.
