Right, so this needs some context first, because the context is the whole point.

I teach a second-year rational choice course in a philosophy department — 18 students, one 75-minute session. They've done expected utility and Nash equilibrium in normal form, but they've never seen an extensive-form game in their lives. Last night a friend of mine who actually does decision theory and I spent two hours arguing about the centipede game and got no where. Her line: the game is a reductio. If both players are rational, and each knows the other one is, and so on up, backward induction says player one takes on move one — but the outcome where they both keep passing until near the end is better for both of them. So one of those assumptions has to give. My line: nothing gives. The argument is valid. Being rational and knowing the other guy is rational really does commit you to taking on move one; the reason people don't is that people aren't rational, and that's a fact about people, not a hole in the theory. We didn't settle it and I don't want to settle it in front of the class. I want the class to have the argument instead of me, and to have it from the inside.

So: design me an activity I can run on Thursday. Not a lecture with a game bolted on — I want the rules and the payoffs doing the teaching, and I want them to walk into it themselves. What they should come away with is the feeling of having said out loud that taking on move one is obviously right, and then not doing it.

Things I specifically want built in:

- They should have to work out and commit to the backward-induction reasoning before they play, so that when their play contradicts it, the contradiction is theirs and not mine. I'm pretty sure the obvious way is definately to have them play the last round of the chain first, agree about what's right there, and only then find out that the chain has been extended backwards. If you have a better way, use it.
- No real money. Sweets, or points on a visible scoreboard, something small and clean.
- A stage where the chain has no visible end: after each pass the game just continues with some probability and dies at random, so there's no move anyone can point to as the last one. Be straight in the debrief about what that does and doesn't change. I've heard people say it dissolves the whole problem and I've heard people say it changes nothing, and I can't tell who's right.
- If it fits, a variant where each of them writes down a rule before seeing the other's, and then we play the two rules against each other. My friend says that's the interesting case, because you can commit yourself to something you'd never actually do once you're sitting in it. Tell me whether that's the same problem as the original one or a different one.

The debrief is the part I care most about. It should take the things that actually happened in the room and hang the philosophy on them. I want it to lay out the escape routes — the assumptions you can drop, and what each one costs — and to say which one it thinks is least bad. Also, someone always says 'this is just the surprise quiz thing' (teacher announces a test next week, students reason it can't be any day). I'd like the debrief to say whether that's the same shape of problem or a different one.

And be honest with me about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do when it does, and which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll just have to tell them. No reading list, no citations — I want the thing I can run on Thursday.
