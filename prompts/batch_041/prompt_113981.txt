The Decoy Problem

There is a corner of decision theory where the most unsettling adversary is the one you can never reach. An acausal threat is a threat issued by an agent with no causal channel to you: it cannot bomb you, hack you, or pay you. What it can do, on this picture, is create simulations of you — copies — and make those copies suffer unless you comply with its demands. If you care about your copies, or if you accept a decision theory on which your copies' fates are bound up with your own, the threat has teeth even though nothing the threatener does ever touches your body.

A defense that circulates in these discussions is the surrogate goal. You deliberately adopt, and publicize, a goal you do not intrinsically care about — say, that a particular unremarkable sequence of digits is never printed. When the acausal threatener comes calling, it threatens the surrogate instead of your real goal. You capitulate on the decoy, the threatener is satisfied that its threat worked, and your true values are left untouched. The surrogate is a lightning rod, absorbing the strike that would otherwise land on what you actually care about.

I want to argue that this defense is unstable for a reason internal to it. The surrogate is supposed to be a goal you do not care about. But a threat only has leverage if the threatened loss would actually move you; a threatener who knows you are indifferent to the decoy has no reason to expect capitulation, and a threat that cannot produce capitulation is no threat at all. So the surrogate has to be something you genuinely mind losing — or it fails at its one job. But the moment it is something you genuinely mind losing, it is a terminal value, and you have not shielded your real goal: you have simply added a second item to the list of things an adversary can hold hostage. The defense either does nothing or makes you worse off. Call it the decoy dilemma.

The usual reply is that the surrogate need not be intrinsically valued; it need only be believed to be, or to be a value you can credibly commit to. But commitment is precisely what the acausal setup is built to see through: the threatener is assumed to model you accurately, and an accurate model of an agent who adopted a decoy instrumentally will predict that the agent abandons it whenever abandoning it is costless. The whole premise of the acausal case is that you cannot hide your true utility function — if you could, the original threat would never have landed.

There is a version of the view on which the surrogate is not a decoy but a genuine conversion: you really do come to care about the digit sequence, and that is the price of protection. But then the protection is bought with value corruption, and a threatener who understands this can target the conversion itself — threaten your real goal unless you adopt a surrogate, and you have been made to change what you care about by the very threat you were trying to defuse. At that point the line between giving in and being changed has collapsed.

What I'd most like to know: is the decoy dilemma a genuine bind, or an artifact of how I've set up the threatener's knowledge and the agent's commitment? If it's genuine, is there any way to be the kind of agent for whom a surrogate goal works — and if so, what does that agent have to be like? And if it's not genuine, where exactly does the argument cheat?
