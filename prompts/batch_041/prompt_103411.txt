At my school's philosophy club we have been arguing about whether it is possible to choose rationally when you have no reason to prefer either option, and I would like you to write a dialogue between two members who defend opposite answers. The first holds that in a case of perfect indifference — the ass set down exactly between two identical bales — there is simply no reason favoring either side, so any selection is arbitrary and an agent who wants a reason to act is stuck; the second holds that this conflates having no preference with having equal preferences, and that a merely indifferent agent can still act: by having adopted a tie-breaking rule in advance (say, always take the left bale), by letting the act of choosing itself generate a preference, or by noticing that some slight asymmetry is always present. Have each character reply to the other's strongest point rather than talk past it. In particular, I want them to press three things: whether a tie-breaking rule supplies a reason or only a procedure for acting without one; whether, if the options are identical down to the last atom, there is any fact the chooser could get right or wrong; and whether anything changes if the options are not tied but incomparable — two futures the person cannot rank at all — rather than exactly equal. Neither character should be a pushover; each should concede where the other lands a genuine point.
