Write a short story of roughly 2,500 words set inside a fictional municipal agency, whose name you should invent, that has adopted a single composite measure of its own performance. Specify that measure within the story itself, and make it novel enough that a reader with no prior exposure to Goodhart's law can follow exactly what becomes of it. The familiar observation that a measure degrades once people begin optimizing for it should be the premise of the story, understood from the first page, not its climax. What the narrative must actually dramatize is the next move: the agency's attempt to protect the measure by folding resistance to gaming directly into the score, so that each division is rated partly on how difficult it is to manipulate. Incorruptibility thereby becomes the quantity under optimization, and the integrity of the measure depends on a definition of corruption that is itself revisable.

Center the story on a mid-level analyst charged with maintaining the integrity of this self-referential score, who comes to see that the only divisions rated highly for incorruptibility are the ones that have quietly redefined what counts as corruption. Let the ending stay unresolved: the analyst should neither expose the system nor be broken by it, but arrive at a clear-eyed understanding of the trap that the reader is invited to share.

A few constraints. Do not name real economists or cite real work; the theory is to be enacted rather than discussed, and no character should deliver a lecture on it. Avoid the vocabulary of management where a concrete image will serve instead, and render the gaming through particular decisions — a form quietly redesigned, a definition narrowed by a subclause, a worthy act performed chiefly because it documents well. Keep the prose spare and observational, pitched closer to a case study than to satire, legible to a reader who knows nothing of the concept and rewarding to one who knows it well. Third person, past tense, approximately 2,500 words.
