I am a philosophy professor, and for the past year I have sat on an advisory committee for our university's art museum and special collections, which is midway through consolidating three legacy catalogues — a card catalogue, a 1990s accession database, and the current collections-management system — into one. The cataloguers are now de-duplicating, which means that several hundred times a year someone has to decide, from the records alone, whether two entries describe one object catalogued twice or two numerically distinct objects that happen to look alike. They have asked me for a procedure they can actually run, and I have had to tell them, a little to my own embarrassment, that the identity of indiscernibles is the wrong thing to hand them unexamined: the converse inference is safe, the principle itself is not, and the whole difficulty lies in what to do when the record shows no discernible difference at all. So what I want from you is that procedure, written for a cataloguer with no philosophical training, applied at a rate of twenty or thirty cases a week, ten minutes or less per case, on records that are often incomplete and sometimes contradict each other, where a merge is effectively irreversible and bears on loan eligibility and insurance valuation, and where any decision above a stated threshold of consequence has to go to the registrar for sign-off.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions with stopping rules: what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where a no ends the inquiry, and where it must hand the case to a person because the question is no longer a question of fact about the records. It has to say explicitly which differences the cataloguer may count as discriminating — material or intrinsic features, spatial location, provenance and causal history, relations to other objects in the collection — and it must block the trivializing move of treating being this very object as a discriminating property, which would make every pair distinct and the exercise pointless. It should be candid about the asymmetry: discernibility licenses an inference to distinctness, indiscernibility licenses nothing. And it has to say what to do with the museum's equivalent of Max Black's two spheres — two identical unmarked casts from the same mould, poured in the same run, whose provenance files burned, so that nothing in the surviving record distinguishes them, including, if you take the location question seriously, their position. Is the procedure entitled to say two objects there, or is that a stipulation the museum makes rather than a fact it discovers? Please do not hedge on this point. I want a definite, defended position on where the procedure draws the line between we have established that these are two and we have decided to treat them as two, and an argument for why the line falls there rather than somewhere else.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: the two casts; a photograph and a much later print struck from the same negative, both accessioned under one number, where the museum would prefer to keep them as a single object for loan purposes; and two byte-identical digital scans of the same page, ingested twice from two different vendors with different embedded metadata, where the cataloguer cannot tell whether the page was photographed twice or the same file was simply supplied twice. At least one of the three should come out in a way the museum will not like; say which, and why you are not adjusting the procedure to avoid it. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do — the cases in which the difference between one object and two is perfectly real but unreadable from any record, so that the answer turns on facts the cataloguer has no way to reach — and tell me where in the procedure judgment has merely been dressed up as a rule.
