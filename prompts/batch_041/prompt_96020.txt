I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years of low-temperature measurement behind me, the last decade spent reading moral philosophy as a serious amateur — and I have been asked to lead one ninety-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on science, technology, and values. The students are quantitatively trained, roughly half of them engineers, and none has taken a course in ethics. The convenor has billed the session as lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain. Work from the stipulation below rather than from whatever the phrase may mean in the literature, since I may be mangling it: suffering and enjoyment lie on one cardinal scale of hedonic magnitude; ordinary aggregation governs comparisons on each side of a critical level; but any suffering above that level is lexically prior to any quantity of suffering or enjoyment below it, so that no finite mass of sub-threshold misery can outweigh a single instance of supra-threshold misery.

I do not want the view explained to me. I want the teaching plan, built for this audience. Specifically:

First, the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency made explicit. Does the commensurability question have to be settled before the threshold can even be stated, or is the threshold what forces the question? What must the group already accept for "is this one scale?" to be a live question rather than a slogan?

Second, the single example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and lack. It should be small enough to hold in mind while a magnitude is scaled up and down, and it should return different verdicts under the lexical reading and under a merely heavily weighted reading. Tell me which obvious candidate to discard and why — I have in mind the case in which a very large number of barely noticeable irritations is weighed against one prolonged agony.

Third, the misconceptions this particular audience will form, and the point in the sequence at which each is to be headed off. My guesses: they will hear "threshold" as "a very large weight"; they will treat cardinal comparability as though it were thermometry; they will conclude that the view denies a common currency, whereas I take its actual oddity to be that it affirms one below the threshold and refuses it across; and they will cry line-drawing fallacy.

Fourth, one question to put at the end that distinguishes a group which has understood the structure of the view from a group which has merely recoiled from it.

Fifth, what I should refuse to open at this level, and say that I am refusing.

Keep it to a single sitting, and do not send me readings.
