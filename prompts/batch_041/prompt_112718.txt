I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and over the past year I've been reading my way into population ethics and longtermism on my own. I've now assembled a set of views that I find individually convincing and that I'd like to start arguing for in a reading group, but before I do that I want a stress test: I can't tell whether these are a coherent package or just a handful of intuitions I happen to like, and I'd rather find out now than in front of other people. Treat this as an audit of my position rather than a general survey of the field.

Here is the package. (1) Totalism: the moral value of a state of affairs is the sum of the welfare of everyone who ever lives, so a world with more good lives is, other things equal, a better world. (2) The universe is spatially infinite, or at any rate there will be infinitely many people across an infinite future; I don't think humanity is a finite cosmic one-off whose story simply ends. (3) Finite acts matter: spending a few hundred dollars on a genuinely effective charity, or sparing one animal a life of suffering, makes the world better in a way I'm not willing to give up. (4) Special urgency for the badly off: relieving someone's severe suffering counts for more, morally, than adding an equivalent quantity of happiness to someone who is already comfortable. (5) Agent-neutrality: whether an act is right doesn't depend on whose welfare is at stake or on when in history it occurs; the moral point of view is the view from nowhere.

What I want from you is a ranked audit. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs are only apparently in tension, order the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. I keep expecting (1) and (4) to collide — a flat sum over everyone versus extra weight for the worst off — but I've never been able to make the objection land, so if it dissolves I'd like to know why. Beyond that I don't have a good sense of where the fault lines are, so please don't stop at the first conflict you find; I'd rather be told that a commitment is not even well-formed than be reassured that everything fits.
