Here's the question I set on the exam:

"1) Explain the role that common knowledge of rationality plays in the backward-induction argument, using the finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma and the centipede game as your examples. 2) The conclusion of backward induction in these games strikes most people as absurd. Does that show that (a) common knowledge of rationality is an implausible assumption, (b) the backward-induction argument is invalid, or (c) something else? Defend your answer. 3) Finally: if each player attaches a small but positive probability to the other playing irrationally, does cooperation become sustainable? Explain."

And here's what the student wrote:

"Backward induction starts at the last decision node: the player moving there is rational, so she takes the action that maximizes her payoff given what's left of the game. Knowing this, the player at the previous node compares her payoff from stopping now against her payoff from continuing, given that the next player will then stop; she stops if stopping is better. Iterating back to the root gives the backward-induction outcome.

For this to work we need each player to be rational, to know the other is rational, to know that the other knows that she is rational, and so on. That infinite hierarchy is common knowledge of rationality. In the centipede game, if it's common knowledge that both are rational, the last player stops, so the second-to-last stops, and so on back, and the first player stops immediately, destroying the gains from cooperation. The finitely repeated PD unravels the same way: in the last round both defect, so in the round before that both defect, and so on down to the first round.

I'd answer (c). The backward-induction argument is valid, and common knowledge of rationality is actually a pretty weak assumption — it just says everyone is rational and that this is common ground, which is true of almost any interaction between sane adults. The real problem is empirical: people don't reason by backward induction. Experimental subjects cooperate in one-shot and finitely repeated PDs and pass early in centipede games, so what's wrong is the descriptive assumption that people maximize in the game-theoretic sense, not the logic.

On the small-probability-of-irrationality question: no, it doesn't help. Once there's any chance the other player is irrational, the induction has no secure base, and the whole thing unravels all the way back to the first node. So adding doubt makes cooperation less sustainable, not more.

The one thing I'd flag is that if you weaken common knowledge to mere mutual knowledge — each knows the other is rational, but not that the other knows it — backward induction can break down, which is why the case feels like it's about knowledge rather than about payoffs."
