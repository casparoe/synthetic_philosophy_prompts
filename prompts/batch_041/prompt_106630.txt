You are a political philosopher who also designs classroom simulations, and you are good at building activities whose rules, rather than whose closing lecture, teach the concept. Design a 90-minute in-class activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in democratic theory, 18 students, that makes them feel the force of sortition (selection by lot) and rotation in office, and also feel the force of the standard objections to both. I do not want a lecture up front and I do not want the terms of art introduced until the debrief.

The activity should run in at least two, preferably three, rounds in which the class must fill a small decision-making body and then live with what that body decides. Vary the selection procedure across rounds: at least one round by competitive election or open volunteering, and at least one by lot. Give the body a real but small stake it controls, such as the allocation of a limited pool of participation credit or the choice of the seminar's final topic, and give individual students private, conflicting preference cards so that representation actually matters. Build in a self-referential twist: at some point the seated body itself decides the selection rule for the next round, so the class runs into the regress of who selects the selectors. Add a rotation or term-limit constraint that forces turnover, so they discover what is lost when experienced members are removed and what is gained when entrenched ones are. Arrange the incentives so that the strategic, the confident, and the merely available are rewarded differently by the two procedures; the participants should notice that election and lot recruit different kinds of people before anyone names that fact.

Deliver: the rules and materials, the private preference cards or a scheme for generating them, the timeline minute by minute, the payoff scheme with no real money, the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto descriptive versus substantive representation, accountability, the selection regress, and the amateur-versus-professional legislator tension. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Finally, be honest about what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, for example the multi-decade timescale of a real political career and the infrastructure of parties and media.

Begin with a brief brainstorm of what you want the students to feel at each round, then give the full design. Think carefully about the incentive structure before writing it up, and write in American English.
