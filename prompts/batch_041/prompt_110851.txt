I have been trying to get clear on the fink/mask distinction in the metaphysics of dispositions, and I suspect I have been taught it backwards; before I build anything on it, I want to check my reconstruction.

The fink case, as I have it, is a live wire wired to a device that would make the wire dead the instant it is touched by a conductor. The wire is disposed to conduct when touched even though it would not conduct if touched, because the device would have removed the disposition before the manifestation was due. The mask case, as I have it, is a fragile glass packed in foam: the glass is disposed to break when struck even though it would not break if struck, because the foam absorbs the blow.

The two look to me like the same structure. In each, an intervener keeps the manifestation from occurring, and in each the object really does have the disposition before anything happens. The only difference I can find is that the device in the wire case is triggered by the very stimulus that would manifest the disposition, whereas the foam is simply there and is triggered by nothing in particular. If that is the whole of it, then the distinction is a distinction about the trigger of the intervener rather than about dispositions, and it ought to be irrelevant to whether a conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions succeeds.

And indeed, if we state the analysis as: x is disposed to M when C just in case, were C to occur and the disposition's intrinsic basis to remain in place throughout, x would M, then the wire comes out disposed, since its basis survives in exactly the scenario in which the device does not fire, and the glass comes out disposed as well, since the amended antecedent neutralizes the intervener by requiring the basis to persist and the glass's basis does persist. So both cases seem to be accommodated, and I cannot see why anyone would say that masks are the harder case and finks the easy one.

To test the taxonomy I imagined a third device: a glass fitted with a spring-loaded brace that snaps into place only at the instant of a blow, absorbs the blow, and then retracts. By the trigger criterion the brace makes this a fink, yet the glass has plainly not lost its brittleness.

So: which of these have I misclassified, and what actually distinguishes a fink from a mask? If the distinction was never about triggers, I would like to know what it is about, and whether the conditional analysis can be repaired along the lines I suggested.
