I am a novelist, and for three years now I have sat on the programming council of a literary festival of the kind that each year "focuses" on a single country, builds a strand of panels and translations around it, and asks a guest curator to write a short framing essay for the programme book. I have also written three novels set in a country that is not my own, which is presumably why I was given the seat and certainly why the matter now before us makes me uneasy. Our next edition focuses on a country whose name I am going to leave out of this note — not out of delicacy, but because the question, as I have come to see it, is about the form of a certain kind of claim rather than its content: whether a festival can present a literature by way of a national character, and if it can, under what description and on whose authority.

The guest curator is a translator from that country, long resident abroad. Her draft explains the literature largely by reference to what she calls the character of the people — their temperament, their relation to irony, their sense of time — and among its specific claims is that the novel in that country has never been at ease with plot. It is, I should say, a deliberately unexoticising essay; her aim is to overturn the picture our own publicity has traded on for a decade, and several of her character claims are plainly meant as corrections to it rather than as the received ideas they resemble. Two of the invited writers have written to object. The first says the character claims are simply false, and that the circumstances she herself invokes — a long period of censorship, a language with a thin market for translation — do not license them; she is right that the essay slides from the one kind of claim to the other as though the second followed from the first. The second objector does not deny that the claims may be true. She says that a foreign audience should not be handed them in that form, and that the difference between her making such remarks in her own essays and the curator making them in our programme is a difference of who is speaking to whom.

The council meets on Thursday and the programme copy goes to the printer the following week. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation — run the essay as it stands, run it with a specified revision, drop the framing note altogether, or print it alongside a commissioned response — with a workable deadline attached, and with reasons that the curator and both objecting writers could actually answer rather than merely resent. I would like it to say plainly whether any form of national-character framing is defensible in a document like this one, and if so which forms and subject to what conditions, and to leave the council with a rule we can apply to the editions that follow. Half the council will vote to print the essay unchanged, so the strongest argument for doing so should be stated fairly before it is answered. I am not asking for a survey of the scholarly literature; the essay, the two letters, and the demands of the format are the materials I need you to work with.
