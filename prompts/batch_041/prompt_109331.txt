I'm a science journalist — mostly I write about AI and the weird philosophical eddies around it — and for the last few months I've been sitting in on a reading group that's half alignment researchers and half philosophers of technology, where the same fight keeps breaking out: whether "low-impact" behavior, or "don't cause unnecessary side effects," can ever be cashed out as an actual formal objective, or whether every attempt quietly bakes in a picture of how the world is supposed to be. My editor thinks this is a story about regulation. It is definately not a story about regulation. Anyway, here's the thing I'd like a number on, stated as precisely as I can manage: There exists a formal measure M of an action's side effects such that (i) you could put it in an agent's objective alongside a task goal; (ii) it's defined without reference to any particular value specification or set of objectives — it's "agent-agnostic"; (iii) it's non-trivial, i.e. it doesn't forbid all change, rubber-stamp the status quo, or collapse into "do nothing"; and (iv) an agent optimizing task-goal-plus-M would actually avoid the side effects we'd pre-theoretically want avoided. What's your credence in that existence claim? I don't want "depends what you mean by side effect" as an answer — pick the most defensible reading, say which reading you picked, and give me the number.

Then the decomposition, please, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term: how likely is it that "side effect" has a privileged baseline at all (the counterfactual where the agent did nothing? some default policy? a "null action"?) rather than the baseline itself being an arbitrary choice; how likely is it that the formal proposals people actually wave around — the ones that penalize an agent for making future states or options unreachable, or for lowering the attainable utility of a broad range of possible goals — can be specified without a distance metric or a prior over goals that already encodes value; whether the regress really terminates, say at something like "preserve option value for every possible goal," and whether that's a genuine stopping point or just a dressed-up conservatism that punishes any change; and how much of this is even about impact measures at all, versus being better handled by corrigibility, or by the agent just staying uncertain about what we want and asking. Then combine the terms into a final number and tell me which one it's most sensitive to — I suspect it's the second, but I'd like to be wrong — and what single thing would move you most: a worked example of a measure that gets the classic cases right without a value spec, an impossibility result showing any such measure must be either trivial or value-laden, or just a seperate argument I haven't thought of.
