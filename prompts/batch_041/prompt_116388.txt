I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and the part of my job I think about most is the post-mortem: after an outage, we sit down and try to say what caused it. That practice has habits I have come to trust. We separate the trigger from the latent defect. We insist that a failure looking obvious in hindsight is not evidence that anyone saw it coming. And we have learned that if you ask only whether an operator made a mistake, you will get the same outage again, because the real causes usually sit in the design of the system and in what the organisation rewarded. I have been trying to apply those habits to an argument I keep having with friends, and I would like a serious essay on it.

The question is whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes. I know roughly where the positions sit: the older picture in which a famine is what happens when a population outruns its food supply; the entitlement picture associated with Amartya Sen, on which famines occur when people lose the ability to command food, which can happen even when food is available in the aggregate; and the harder claim that some modern famines — the Bengal famine, the Irish famine, the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward — were not misfortunes but the predictable output of deliberate policy, and so belong with crimes rather than with disasters.

Please write an essay that takes a side and defends it, and let the argument turn on a criterion rather than on anecdotes. Here is the criterion I have been trying out, and I am not sure I believe it:

A famine counts as a political crime rather than a natural disaster when (a) an alternative policy package existed that was feasible, and that was knowable at the time, by the standards of the day rather than with hindsight, to be sufficient to prevent most of the excess deaths; (b) the government knew its own policies were contributing to the shortfall; and (c) it kept those policies anyway because abandoning them would have cost it something it valued more than the lives at stake, whether a war, an ideological programme, or its hold on power.

Treat that criterion as the thing to be argued about. In particular:

Whether each clause is necessary, and whether the three together are sufficient. If a government kills through negligence or indifference rather than preference, does clause (c) excuse it, and should it?

Whether the natural versus political distinction is a real dichotomy at all. If hazards are partly socially produced — who farms the marginal land, who receives relief, who gets counted — does the distinction survive, or does it survive only as a way of assigning blame?

Whether clause (a) can ever be applied honestly, given that we always know more afterwards than the people deciding did. What would it take to apply it without smuggling hindsight in?

Two hard cases, which I would rather you resolve than dodge: first, a government whose policies cause a famine but which sincerely and not unreasonably believes those policies will improve food security; second, a government that could prevent a famine in a politically marginal region and does not, not from malice but because the region does not matter to it.

What follows from your verdict in practice: whether calling a famine a crime rather than a disaster changes how it is remembered, whether anyone owes anything to survivors, and whether the label makes future famines less likely.

I am not after a reading list, so do not spend words on citations. End with a definite verdict rather than a survey of the options. A qualified verdict is fine — for instance, that the crime versus disaster axis is the wrong one and should be replaced — as long as it is a position and you say what it commits you to.
