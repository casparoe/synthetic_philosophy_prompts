I'm a second-year student taking a seminar in ethical theory, and I've been asked to run the tutorial session on the demandingness objection to consequentialism. I'd rather hand out a decision tree than a list of positions, so that people work out which view they actually hold from their verdicts on cases rather than from the label they'd like to wear. Please write that tree.

Start from a single entry question, in plain language, that separates the views on which morality issues requirements governing what you may keep for yourself from the views on which it does no more than rank outcomes as better and worse. Everything else should hang off that split.

At every branch node put one question a first-year student can answer without knowing where the branch leads — ideally a concrete case, with the two or three answers a thoughtful person might genuinely give — and label the branch with the claim doing the splitting, phrased as a claim rather than a label (for example: a requirement must leave the agent a protected sphere of discretion; or: what is required must be something no one could reasonably object to bearing). Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or the word unnamed if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I want the branches to be driven by these distinctions, which are the ones my seminar keeps tripping over:

- the ceiling: is there any level of sacrifice beyond which morality stops asking, or is the demand always "as much as you can"?
- the demand profile: as the need in front of you grows, does the demand rise proportionally, jump at a threshold, or plateau?
- the enforcement line: does the ceiling on what is required coincide with the ceiling on what is blameworthy, or can a view hold that you ought to give more while denying that you are at fault for not doing so?
- the object of the demand: does it attach to individual acts, to the shape of a whole life, or to institutions and policies? A view can be maximally demanding act by act and lenient about a life, or the reverse.
- marginal impact: does the requirement survive when your contribution changes nothing detectable, or when almost no one else complies?
- the comparison baseline: is the demand indexed to what others are doing — each of us owes a fair share and no more — or is it absolute, so the shortfall of others lands on you?
- supererogation: does the view leave any room for the optional at all, or does "best available" crowd it out?

These positions must appear as leaves: an unlimited maximizing view that keeps nothing of comparable importance; a maximizing view with a cost-sensitive threshold; a two-level or rule-based view whose rules leave room for partiality; a satisficing view with a good-enough threshold; a hybrid that pairs a maximizing principle with a protected option to discount others' interests; a scalar view with no ought at all, only better and worse; a reasonable-rejection view on which the burden a principle places on the agent can block it; a view of duty with built-in latitude, where the agent sets the level; a threshold view on which constraints hold until catastrophe and then give way; a fair-share view on which no one must cover another's shortfall; the moral-saint critique, on which the demand is not merely large but corrosive of a good life; and the skeptic's view that demandingness carries no weight in choosing between theories.

Use at least these cases somewhere in the tree: a spare kidney and a stranger who will die without it; a restaurant dinner weighed against a donation; a choice between a lucrative career and a vocation that helps no one; partiality toward one's own child; a person who gives nothing, ever; a person who has already given a great deal and wonders whether the demand resets.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate by verdicts on cases, and say what would separate them instead. Note any combination of answers with no occupant, and diagnose the vacancy: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. No citations or reading suggestions, please — the handout has to stand on its own, and I can't verify references.

Aim for eight to ten branch nodes and twelve to fourteen leaves. Finish by telling me how confident you are that the tree is complete and correctly wired, and single out the one classification or branch you are least sure of, saying what would settle it.
