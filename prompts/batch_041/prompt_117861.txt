I teach a final-year undergraduate module on causal inference and philosophy of science, and I am drafting the final examination. I want one long essay question, worth 25 marks and intended for 60 minutes, on Simpson's paradox and the logic of adjustment. The question must not be answerable by reciting the definition of the paradox, nor by saying that one should always condition on subgroups or never condition on them. It should force students to use temporal and causal assumptions to decide which comparison answers which question. The question may be divided into labelled sub-parts, but it must read as a single question. Please use the following data, which I have constructed for the examination.

An observational cohort study compares two triage protocols, A and B, for a condition. There are 1,000 patients in each arm. The outcome is survival to discharge. Two further variables are recorded. Severity is assessed at triage, before the protocol is assigned. ICU transfer is recorded after the protocol has been in use for 24 hours. Protocol assignment was at the clinician's discretion. The data are:

Protocol A: overall survival 270/1,000 (27%). By severity: mild 90/100 (90%); severe 180/900 (20%). By ICU transfer: transferred 160/800 (20%); not transferred 110/200 (55%).

Protocol B: overall survival 730/1,000 (73%). By severity: mild 720/900 (80%); severe 10/100 (10%). By ICU transfer: transferred 120/200 (60%); not transferred 610/800 (76.25%).

The same 2,000 patients generate all of these summaries. The question should present the data and ask students to draw a directed acyclic graph consistent with the temporal information, to state which of the three comparisons (crude, severity-stratified, ICU-stratified) identifies the total causal effect of protocol on survival under that graph, to compute a directly standardised survival proportion for each protocol using the combined severity distribution as the standard, and to say what a policymaker should conclude about which protocol saves more lives. It should also ask students to explain what would change if severity were instead measured after treatment assignment, or if ICU transfer were a pre-treatment variable. The question should make clear that the data alone do not determine the answer; the causal assumptions do.

Then write a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should have five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 25), and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students that point. The must-hit points should include: the correct DAG and the roles of severity and ICU transfer; the distinction between a confounder and a mediator; the correct identification of the severity-stratified comparison as the total effect; the standardised calculation; and the policy conclusion. The marking scheme should also distinguish a first-class answer from an upper-second-class answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The model answer should not cite any literature, and the marking scheme should not require citations. Begin your response with the examination question itself, with no preamble; then give the model answer; then give the marking scheme. Do not restate my request.
