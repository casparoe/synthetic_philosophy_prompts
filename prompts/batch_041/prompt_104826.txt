I've been reporting on political theory for a few years now and there's one fight I keep bumping into that I still can't quite make sense of. On one side you've got the neo-Roman / republican camp — Pettit, Skinner, and the people who've built on them — who say freedom is non-domination: you're unfree whenever you live at the mercy of someone else's arbitrary power, even if that power is never actually used against you. On the other side is the more familiar negative-liberty line going back to Berlin, where freedom just means non-interference — nobody's blocking you, so you're free — and from where the republican thing looks like non-interference plus a bit of social psychology, or maybe a wish, dressed up as a third concept. I've sat through maybe four workshops on this now and the same moves happen everytime: the republicans wheel out the slave with the kind master who never interferes, the negative-liberty people say fine, that's just a case where interference is threatened or likely, everyone agrees the example is doing a lot of the work, and then nothing moves. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A real disagreement about what freedom is, or two camps answering different questions under one word — one asking when a person is unfree, the other asking when a person's options are open? Or is it verbal all the way down, a fight over whether 'freedom' should track a status or a set of options?

My own hunch, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong, is that the impasse isn't about any particular case but about what a theory of liberty is even for: the negative-liberty side wants something you can measure and compare across people and societies, and the republican side wants something that explains why certain relations are degrading even when they cost you nothing, and those two jobs just pull the analysis in different directions. To make that less abstract, here's a case I don't think I've seen anyone use. A private water company in a city has the legal power to cut off any household's supply at will, but it has signed a binding, externally enforced contract never to do it, and in fifty years it never has. The republican says those households are still dominated — the power is sitting right there, just unexercised — while the negative-liberty person says they're as free as anyone, since nothing is stopping them. Is the disagreement here factual (about what powers actually exist), evaluative (about what makes a relation degrading), or conceptual (about what the word picks out)? And whichever it is, is there anything that could genuinely settle it — a distinction, a case, a formal result — or is this the kind of debate where the two sides simply start from different intuitions and we should stop pretending a knockout argument is coming? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what.
