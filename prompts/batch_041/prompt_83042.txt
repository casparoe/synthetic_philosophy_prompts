I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the limits of Jeffrey conditionalization, and I want to use a real scientific episode as the pressure test. The episode is the OPERA anomaly, and I will state the facts I need you to assume rather than have you narrate them: in September 2011 the OPERA collaboration, running a neutrino beam from CERN to the Gran Sasso laboratory over a baseline of roughly 730 km, reported that the neutrinos arrived some sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would have, at about six sigma; the collaboration published while explicitly inviting the community to find the error; and by mid-2012 the discrepancy had been traced to two instrumentation faults, a fibre-optic link between a GPS receiver and the master clock that was not properly seated, and an oscillator running outside its specified frequency. Do not spend space retelling this, and do not give me a survey of the Jeffrey literature; I know both.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions. Begin from the framework as I hold it: Jeffrey's rule is designed for an agent whose evidence arrives not as a proposition she becomes certain of but as a new probability distribution over a partition of evidential states, so that the posterior is the prior conditionalized on each cell and then averaged under the new weights, and its advertised advantage is that the agent need never conditionalize on a proposition about her own evidential state. Then tell me, for this episode, who the updating agent is — the collaboration, the wider community, and an individual theorist are plainly not the same agent and do not face the same problem — what the partition would have to be, and whether the reported measurement is representable as Jeffrey evidence at all.

I have been developing a distinction that I want you to test rather than accept. Call measurement uncertainty the ordinary case in which the reported value is drawn from a distribution centred on the true value, so that the likelihood is a well-defined function of the parameter. Call provenance uncertainty the case in which what is in doubt is whether the reported number is the number it purports to be — whether the process that generated it has the causal history that would make it evidence about the parameter at all — so that the doubt attaches to the generation of the number rather than to its scatter. What I cannot settle is whether provenance uncertainty is expressible as a Jeffrey-style distribution over an evidential partition, or whether it is a different kind of uncertainty the framework cannot house; and if the latter, exactly which presupposition fails. If you think the distinction collapses — that provenance uncertainty is just measurement uncertainty with an extra hierarchical layer — say so and show me how the reduction goes.

Two further things. First, I am aware that Jeffrey updating is in general not commutative, so that the order in which uncertain evidence arrives can change the posterior; I want to know whether the actual sequence here, the announcement followed by internal rechecks and then by the two fault findings, makes that path-dependence visible, and whether the community's arriving at the right answer owed anything to the order. Second, I want your judgement on whether this is a fair test at all: if Jeffrey's rule was never meant to cover a case in which the doubt is about the provenance of the evidence rather than its value, tell me that plainly, and tell me what a fair test would look like.

Close by saying what the episode shows about the theory — confirmation, counterexample, or evidence that it was answering a different question — and whether some other apparatus does better: a hierarchical model over possible systematic errors, an explicit discount for the reliability of a report, a set-valued or robust treatment, or something I have not named. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
