Six weeks ago my neurologist started me on a medication whose most commonly reported side effect is unusually vivid dreaming, and the dreams I have been having are unlike anything I experienced before: long, coherent, emotionally violent, and continuous enough that I can sometimes trace them back to the moment I lost consciousness. I had assumed this was simply a window onto something that had been happening in my head all night, and that the drug had turned up the volume. Then, trying to understand what was happening to me, I ran into a philosophical position I had never taken seriously — the view, associated with Norman Malcolm's book Dreaming and, in a different form, with Daniel Dennett's cassette theory, that dreams are not experiences at all: that nothing is felt during sleep, and that a dream is something the waking brain assembles and inserts into memory in the moments around waking. I have been going back and forth on this for two weeks, and I would like a number rather than an orientation.

Here is the proposition I want your credence in, stated so that it has a truth value:

When a sleeper later reports a dream — the tiger, the falling, the conversation — the phenomenal events described in that report were actually experienced by the sleeper at the time of the sleep episode, rather than being generated at, or after, the moment of waking.

Please give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed. At minimum, I want a number and a one-line justification for each of the following, plus any term you think belongs and I have missed:

1. The base rate at which retrospective reports of events are accurate records of experiences occurring at the reported time, given what is known about confabulation and memory reconstruction outside the dream case.
2. The probability that the neurophysiological evidence specific to dreaming — REM-stage activation, the correspondence between reported dream content and recorded eye movements, dream enactment in REM sleep behavior disorder, and the recent work in which sleeping participants signaled with prearranged eye movements and answered simple questions in real time — favors genuine online experience rather than post hoc construction.
3. The probability that the strongest version of the post hoc view survives that evidence: that is, whether real-time signaling from a sleeping person is best explained as experience occurring during sleep or as something the theory can accommodate without granting experience.
4. The probability that the conceptual and linguistic considerations — that a first-person report of a dream is verified only retrospectively, that the grammar of the dream report does not by itself entail that anything was undergone, and that the first-person authority we grant to dream reports is of a different kind from the authority we grant to waking perceptual reports — actually bear on the metaphysical question rather than only on how we talk.
5. The probability that the term experience is determinate enough, in this context, for the proposition to have a single truth value at all, as opposed to splitting into several questions that the two sides answer differently.

Then combine them, showing the arithmetic or the qualitative weighting you used, and give me the resulting credence in the proposition above.

After the number, do three things. First, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what evidence or argument would move it most — I want something specific enough that I could in principle go look for it, and I would rather hear about a study design, a clinical population, or a conceptual distinction than a general appeal to future science. Second, tell me whether you think the Malcolm-Dennett dispute is a genuine disagreement about what happens during sleep, a verbal dispute about the word experience, or two different questions — one about the metaphysics of sleep and one about the epistemology of dream reports — being argued under a single heading, and how that diagnosis should affect the number you gave me. Third, tell me which way my own case should push me: whether a drug-induced dream that feels continuous with the moment of falling asleep is better evidence for the online-experience view or is exactly the kind of vivid, well-formed narrative that the post hoc view predicts, and why.
