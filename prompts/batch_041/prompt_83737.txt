I am planning three years of work on money-pump arguments and diachronic rationality, and I want to know which open questions in that area are actually worth the time. Assume a background in decision theory and formal epistemology, no allegiance to any particular framework, and an audience of philosophers plus theoretically minded economists.

To keep this from collapsing into a survey of the obvious, take the following rough taxonomy as your starting point. Some money-pump arguments need only a synchronic preference cycle plus a willingness to make the pairwise trades, and the temporal sequence of offers is merely the device that collects the loss. Others are specifically diachronic: the agent is vulnerable only because it treats each choice point in isolation, so the argument bites against an agent whose preferences never change but whose choice rule is myopic in some way. A third family runs the pump through the agent's own future selves, exploiting a predictable drift in preferences or credences, so the present self loses to a sequence of its successors. A fourth requires the agent to be unaware that the sequence of trades is a pump at all, which raises the question whether vulnerability to being pumped counts as a defect when the agent never registers it.

Given that taxonomy, which open questions matter most? I want a defended ordering rather than a list. For each candidate, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is with the tools philosophers actually have, and what would move it up or down the ranking, whether a counterexample, a formal result, or a shift in how we connect money pumps to the ordinary canons of rationality. State explicitly what the ranking is optimizing for and where the criteria conflict. Finally, tell me which of the four families is a distraction, and why. I would rather have two or three questions defended properly than ten gestured at.

Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write it in an email.
