Alright, I need an essay and I want it to actually land somewhere, not just survey the terrain.

Background: a friend of mine keeps saying she can't take consequentialism seriously because its demands are insane -- you'd have to give away basically everything, never spend money on anything fun, treat your own plans and relationships as worthless unless they happen to be the most good-maximizing thing you could be doing at that moment. She thinks that's a reductio. I think thats way too fast, and I want you to write the essay that either backs me up or tells me I'm wrong.

So: does the demandingness objection actually sink act-consequentialism, or is it weaker than it looks?

Don't just hand me the standard replies (the "our intuitions are the thing that's broken" line, or the "make it a rule / allow satisficing" line, or the "switch to a scalar view with no sharp line between right and wrong" line). Use them where they're relevant, but I want you to push on four specific things:

1. Demands that scale with capacity. Most versions of the view seem to say you owe more the more you're able to do -- so someone with a fortune, or rare skills, has almost their whole life spoken for, while someone with very little to give gets off comparatively easy. Is that proportionality a fair answer to the objection, or does it make things worse, since it means the theory asks the most of exactly the people who have the most to lose?

2. The scalar move. If you drop the idea that there's a line between permissible and impermissible and just say some actions are better than others, is "too demanding" even a coherent complaint anymore? Or does that reply just give up the thing that made the view worth defending in the first place?

3. The mirror objection. Consequentialism seems to demand the world of your time and money while asking almost nothing of your character -- you can stay selfish, petty, and cold as long as the numbers come out right. Does that asymmetry strengthen the demandingness objection (it's asking for the wrong thing) or is it really a totally separate complaint that doesn't belong under the same heading?

4. Ought implies can. If the view genuinely requires more than a human being could sustain, what follows? That the theory is false, or that we've mis-described the demands -- e.g. that burning out makes you a worse instrument, so resting is actually the optimific choice? And if that escape hatch is always sitting there, isn't it suspiciously convenient?

Make it concrete at least once -- pick a person (say, a mid-career professional who's decently paid, has a family, and one thing they love doing that does no good for anyone else) and say what the theory demands of them, then contrast with someone with far more capacity. Use that to test your claims rather than just decorating them.

End with a verdict. Not "it depends" and not "both sides have a point" -- tell me whether the objection lands, and if it only partially lands, say exactly which version of consequentialism survives it and which doesn't. And tell me what the objection really shows if it doesn't sink the theory: is the problem our psychology, or is it definately just that the world is bad enough that a demanding morality is the correct response to it?

No reading list or anything, just argue it out. Somewhere between 5 and 8 paragraphs is fine.
