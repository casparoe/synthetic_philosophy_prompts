Question 3: "The Dutch book argument is standardly presented as the canonical argument for probabilism. Explain how the argument works, and then say precisely what it establishes and what it does not: (a) does it deliver the probability axioms in full? (b) does it extend to conditionalisation? (c) what is the most serious objection to it, and can it be met?"

Student's answer:

"The argument starts from the idea that your degree of belief in a proposition is just the betting quotient at which you'd be indifferent between betting for and against it. Suppose your credences violate the probability calculus — say you're 0.6 confident it will rain tomorrow and 0.6 confident it won't. A bookie who knows your credences can offer you two bets, each of which you take to be fair: pay 0.6 for a ticket paying 1 if it rains, and pay 0.6 for a ticket paying 1 if it doesn't. If it rains you're down 0.2; if it doesn't you're down 0.2. You've been Dutch booked. De Finetti's theorem generalises this: any set of betting quotients that violates the axioms is vulnerable to a sure loss, and any set that satisfies them is immune. So coherence — conformity to the axioms — is necessary and sufficient for avoiding a sure loss, and since it's irrational to accept a package of bets you're guaranteed to lose, incoherent credences are irrational.

Two things follow. First, the argument delivers the full Kolmogorov axiomatisation, countable additivity included, which means de Finetti's own finitely additive version of probability was simply a mistake — his theorem refutes him. Second, the same style of arguement runs diachronically: Lewis's diachronic Dutch book catches anyone who fails to conditionalise, so probabilism and conditionalisation really stand or fall together.

The standard objection is that all this shows is that an incoherent agent can be exploited by a bookie who knows their credences, which isn't the same as the agent being irrational or inconsistent. But the objection misses the point: since coherence is necessary and sufficient for avoiding a sure loss, an incoherent agent's credences are defective in exactly the sense the objection denies. So the argument stands, though of course it tells us nothing about which priors we should have."
