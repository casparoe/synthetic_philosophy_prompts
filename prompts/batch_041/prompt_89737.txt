I am a high school student, and I am writing an essay for my school's philosophy club on a question that came up in our psychology unit and that I have not been able to settle on my own. I would be grateful if you would write the essay for me. I am less interested in a survey of what various authors have said than in a sustained piece of reasoning that ends with a definite verdict, so please argue from the case described below rather than from a list of sources.

Our teacher asked us to imagine the following. A committee revising a major psychiatric diagnostic manual is considering whether to add a new category, which I will call Sustained Cognitive Overload Syndrome. The proposed criteria are: (1) a persistent sense of mental exhaustion that sleep does not relieve; (2) difficulty beginning tasks even when the person wants to begin them; (3) a feeling of distance from one's own thoughts and memories; (4) unusual sensitivity to noise and light; and (5) a sense that one has not returned to one's earlier level of functioning. A diagnosis requires any three of the five, together with distress or impairment. There is no laboratory test, and no single mechanism is known; the committee's working assumption is that several different causes may produce the same cluster.

The evidence the committee has collected is what makes the case hard. In the region where the category was first introduced, referrals rose more than sixfold within three years, and patients began describing their difficulties in the manual's exact vocabulary. Clinicians reported that patients who took up the label often felt relieved and less self-blaming, but also became less willing to attempt work or study, on the grounds that they had the condition. Separately, a research group found that the label predicted who would respond to a particular course of therapy better than any existing diagnostic category did. In a second region, where the same difficulties were presented to patients as a difference in cognitive style rather than as a disorder, the reported symptoms were milder and did not cluster in the same way.

The question I want the essay to answer is whether the committee should adopt the category. Our teacher mentioned in passing that a philosopher named Ian Hacking argued that classifications of people are unlike classifications of rocks, because people can react to being classified; he called human classifications interactive for this reason. I already grasp the general shape of that debate, and it does not by itself tell me what to do here, so please do not simply restate it. Instead I want the essay to work through the following. First, distinguish two ways a category might reshape the people it classifies: it might change only how they describe and conduct themselves, or it might change the underlying condition itself, so that later members of the category are not the same sort of thing as earlier ones. Say which is happening in this case, and explain why the difference matters for whether the category picks out something real. Second, consider whether a category can be real without a single shared mechanism, and whether the generalizations the committee wants to make about the condition survive if the condition shifts in response to being named. Third, say whether the data from the second region counts against the category's validity or only against a particular way of presenting it. Fourth, consider whether the verdict should depend on what the category is for, since explanation, prediction, clinical care, and the self-understanding of patients may pull in different directions. Finally, state the strongest objection to your own verdict and answer it.

Please organize the response under the following headings, in this order, with roughly the stated lengths:

1. The proposal and the evidence, in plain terms — about 250 words.
2. The case for adopting the category — about 450 words.
3. The case against adopting it — about 450 words.
4. Can a moving target still be a real kind? — about 400 words.
5. The verdict I would defend — about 350 words.

The verdict should be definite and should say what would have to be true of the evidence for you to change your mind. Please write in continuous prose, without lists or tables, and without citing anyone.
