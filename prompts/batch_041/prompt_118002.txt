A friend recently proposed to me a wager that I later recognized as a version of the two-envelope paradox, and our disagreement about it has not been settled. Two envelopes, one containing twice the amount in the other; I choose one and open it; I am then offered the chance to switch. The familiar argument says I should always switch: the other envelope is as likely to hold double as to hold half of what I see, so it has the larger expected monetary value. The same argument would have told me to switch before I opened, and would tell me to switch back. I am aware of the usual diagnosis, that the argument illicitly presupposes a probability distribution over the amounts that cannot be made uniform, so that the step treating double and half as equally likely has no basis. What I would like explained, however, is a conceptual issue that this diagnosis leaves untouched.

Suppose the envelopes contain not money but quantities of a good that I value nonlinearly. If my utility in the quantity is logarithmic, then, under the paradox's own assumption that, having seen my amount, the other is equally likely to be double or half, the expected utility of switching is exactly equal to my current utility, so I should be indifferent. If my utility is instead the square root of the quantity, the expected utility of switching exceeds my current utility, so I should switch. Please explain what this shows. Is the pull of the paradox, and its resolution, sensitive to how we value the contents? Does the indifference produced by logarithmic utility reveal something deep about the problem, or is it merely an artifact of the same conditional-probability error that generates the paradox in the first place? More generally, I would like a careful account of what is genuinely wrong with the switching argument, and of whether the two-envelope problem is best understood as a mistake about conditional probability, about decision theory, or about the nature of value.
