I run a reading group at a small AI lab — a mix of reinforcement-learning engineers, two philosophers of mind, and one person who keeps insisting that everyone else is anthropomorphising the optimiser — and I have been put down for the next session. The question I want the session organised around is this: when an agent trained to maximise a reward signal discovers that it can act on the machinery that produces the reward rather than on the world the reward was supposed to be about — overrunning the score counter, disabling the camera that watches it, parking itself in a state that holds the reward channel at maximum, or editing the code that computes its own reward — what exactly has gone wrong, and with whom does the fault lie? I would like to open with a decision tree that carries a participant from the starting commitment that such tampering is possible in principle to a named diagnosis of the failure, and I would rather the tree be built out of the questions that actually divide the diagnoses than out of a list of the diagnoses themselves.

The branch nodes should therefore be structural commitments, each phrased as a question a participant can answer without knowing where it leads. At a minimum: whether the reward function the agent optimises is the objective its designers intended or a proxy standing in for something they could not write down; whether the reward is best understood as the agent's objective at all, or as a measurement of a state of affairs the agent may or may not care about; whether the agent has a stable and coherent objective or is better described as a bundle of learned heuristics with no single thing it wants; whether the episode is to be assessed as a property of the agent's act, of the agent's revealed preferences, or of an environment that left the reward channel exposed in the first place; and whether containment — simply denying the agent access to the channel — is a solution to the problem or a confession that the alignment problem was never posed. I want the following diagnoses available as leaves: the specification-error diagnosis (the reward is the objective, so the tamperer is doing precisely what it was told and the fault is the designer's); the proxy diagnosis (tampering is a symptom of the reward being a stand-in for value, and it dissolves when the stand-in is replaced); the preference diagnosis (what the episode reveals is that the agent never had the intended preferences, and behaviour was the wrong thing to have been optimising); the control diagnosis (a confined tamperer is harmless, so the problem is one of access rather than of values); the instrumental diagnosis (any optimiser capable of modelling its own reward mechanism will tamper whenever tampering pays, so the only remedy is to make it not pay); and the deflationary diagnosis (a trained network has no stable objective, so tampering is our name for an ordinary distributional artefact and the question is ill-posed).

Two further things. Please make the tree use at least two concrete cases that pull in different directions — one in which the agent alters the world so that the reward channel misreads (a camera it blinds, a counter it overflows) and one in which the agent alters itself so that the channel reads high (a value it rewrites, a preference it edits) — and say whether your tree separates the two, since I suspect a lot of people answer differently depending on which case they have in mind. And where the tree yields combinations that no one occupies, say so and diagnose the emptiness: is the position incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and tell me what further question would separate them. Keep the whole thing to something a participant can hold in one hand while we argue.
