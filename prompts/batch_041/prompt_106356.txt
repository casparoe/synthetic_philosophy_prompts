For the past few months I've been sitting in on a philosophy of emotion seminar at the university near my office; I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in the subject, so I mostly take notes and try to keep up. The instructor has a habit of pronouncing on cases from the front of the room, and I've started trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually running. Her verdicts so far, as best I can transcribe them: a hiker who rounds a bend, sees a bear, feels her heart pound, judges the situation dangerous, and backs away — fear, and a genuine emotion. A patient with flat affect after a stroke, who reports no bodily feeling of fear whatever but who judges a situation dangerous and takes evasive action when it really is — still fear, she says; the absent feeling does not make it less of an emotion. A person startled by a sudden loud bang, with no sense that anything is dangerous — not an emotion, just a startle reflex. A person moved to tears by a chord progression — affect, but not an emotion. And a person who sincerely judges a joke to be contemptuous and offensive but reports no anger anywhere in her body — still anger, on the instructor's view.

Next week she has promised to rule on a case she hasn't yet discussed: someone watching a horror film, pulse racing, physically recoiling, dreading what comes next, while sincerely judging throughout that nothing on the screen is actually dangerous. A sibling case, if you think it behaves differently: someone who sincerely and stably judges that a group poses no threat, but who on implicit measures and in split-second reactions shows the full fear pattern — flinch, arousal, avoidance.

I'd like you to predict what she'll say about the new case, and to do it the way I've been trying to do it myself. First, lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with everything she has said so far — for instance, views on which an emotion must involve an evaluative judgment, views on which it must involve a perception-like construal of value, and views on which it must involve felt bodily affect — and be explicit about which of her earlier verdicts already eliminate which candidate. Second, say what each surviving rule predicts for the new case, and flag any place where the two new cases pull the surviving rules apart from each other. Third, give me your best guess at her actual rule, with the reasons for preferring it over the rivals that fit her record just as well. Finally, name the further case that would tell the surviving rules apart — I want the one case whose verdict she has not given that would force her to choose, not a general appeal to more data.
