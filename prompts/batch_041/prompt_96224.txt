I retired a few years ago from an experimental physics laboratory and have spent much of the time since reading my way into logic and the philosophy of mind, mostly on my own and mostly in the evenings, which means I am liable to get hold of an idea firmly and then find I have grasped it slightly wrong. I have lately been worrying at a small puzzle of the kind that this literature likes to use for illustration, and what I want from you is an explanation of the conceptual issue it is supposed to raise — worked through, not reported. I have no library within reach and no interest in being told who has written what about it; I would rather have the problem itself thought about carefully.

The puzzle is this. One begins with the string MI, where the letters are simply marks with no meaning attached, and one is allowed four operations: (1) if a string ends in I, append U to it; (2) if a string begins with M, double everything that follows the M, so that Mx becomes Mxx; (3) replace any occurrence of III by U; (4) delete any occurrence of UU. The question is whether the string MU can be reached. It cannot, and the customary reason is that one looks not at individual derivations but at a quantity preserved by the rules: the number of I's in any string so produced is never a multiple of three, since MI has one I, the first and fourth rules leave the count untouched, the second doubles it and doubling a number not divisible by three keeps it so, and the third lowers it by exactly three. MU has no I at all, and zero is divisible by three; so the target is unreachable. This is offered as a case of stepping outside a system: rather than search the space of derivations, one notices a property of the system as a whole and the impossibility drops out at once. My difficulty is that I cannot see what is supposed to be outside of what. In my own field we do this continually — a conservation law or a symmetry argument will tell you that a process is impossible without anyone's having to simulate it — and I would have said the symmetry argument is simply more physics, not an escape from physics. Likewise the argument about the I-count reads to me as ordinary arithmetic about strings, a theorem of some perfectly ordinary formal system, checkable line by line.

So what I would like explained is the following, and I would rather have it argued than asserted. First, is stepping outside a system anything more than switching to a different and richer system in which the first can be described and reasoned about? If it is more, what is the something more, and how should I recognise it in a concrete case rather than in a slogan? Second, if the move is at bottom a matter of hitting on a compact invariant rather than running a long search, is the distinction one of complexity and of representation rather than of logic — and if so, does the metaphor of an outside survive at all, or is it a picturesque way of talking about a better encoding? Third, is there any principled account of which systems a given agent can step out of, and does anything of philosophical weight depend on the fact that the step is taken by an agent who had until then been working inside? I would also be glad to know just where the analogy with my conservation laws breaks down, and whether the unreachability of MU is genuinely of a piece with the self-referential results that this literature usually has in mind when it speaks of a system's being unable to establish something about itself, or whether it is only a homely illustration of the same slogan.
