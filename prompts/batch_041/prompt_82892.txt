I'm trying to figure out my gut verdicts about how much citizens should be allowed to hide from the state, and whether there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just doing folk sociology about the country I grew up in. Here's where I land, and some of it obviously doesn't hang together:

- The state knows my income and my address. Fine. Nobody thinks that's tyranny.
- Germany makes you register your address within a couple weeks of moving and expects you to have ID on you. Fine.
- But a mandatory national ID you have to carry at all times and produce on demand — I feel like that's not fine, even though it's basically the same info as the last one. I can't defend the line.
- License plate readers logging every car everywhere: not fine, and "the streets are public" doesn't rescue it.
- Cops buying my phone location from a data broker with no warrant: not fine, and "you clicked agree on the app" doesn't rescue it.
- The state publishes the salaries of public employees: fine. The state publishes the names of everyone on welfare: not fine. Both are about public money! This one bugs me.
- A nasty infectious disease gets reported to public health: fine. My insurer or my employer finding out my HIV status: not fine. Same fact, different recipient, and I think that's doing real work.
- A census that counts how many people live here: fine. A census that records my religion next to my name: not fine, even though I'd happily tell a pollster my religion.
- Employers or landlords asking for my social media passwords: not fine.
- The state reading my email with a warrant: fine. The state keeping a searchable pile of everyone who ever emailed a protest organizer, "just in case": not fine.
- Credit scores: I sort of accept them. Chinese social credit: I don't. I cannot articulate the difference cleanly and it's embarrassing.

My working theory, which I don't trust: the state may know facts about me, but it may not assemble the kind of knowledge that lets it anticipate and pre-empt what I'm about to do before I've decided it — because at that point I'm not the one deciding anymore. A German friend insists there's a whole constitutional doctrine like this, some census case from the 80s, "informational self-determination" or something, and I'd like to know whether that idea actually does any work or whether it's just a nice phrase.
