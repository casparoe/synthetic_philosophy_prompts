Some friends of mine got into it at a meetup last week and I'm still chewing on it. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for like a decade, so I know all the vocabulary, which is maybe the problem: I could follow every move they made and still not tell you who was right. I was just standing there holding a drink.

Here's the shape of it. Marcus says 'knows' depends on what's at stake. His example was the bus. You check your phone, see the 7:15, and if you're just going to a movie you'd happily say you know the bus comes at 7:15. If you're trying to make a flight -- same phone, same schedule, same eyes -- you'd say you don't really know and you should double check. Nothing about your evidence changed. So either the stakes changed whether you know, or the stakes changed the word. He says it's the former: pragmatic encroachment, the real deal, not just a fact about how we talk.

Dana says that's backwards. Your evidence is your evidence. If the schedule is reliable you know it, if it isn't you don't, and stakes can't reach in and flip that. All stakes change is whether it's polite or useful to *say* 'I know.' Low stakes, you're allowed to speak loosely and nobody bothers you; high stakes, someone will jump down your throat. But you knew or didn't know the whole time. She also said that if he's right about anything, it's really a story about the word 'know' having different standards in different conversations -- which she called 'contextualism with extra steps.' I'm not sure that's a fair characterization, and I'm not sure it's wrong either.

Marcus came back with: if it's only about words, why does it track stakes so neatly, in every case we can construct? And anyway the 'just words' move can't do the work, because the thing that tells you when to stop gathering evidence is exactly what's at stake, and knowledge is the thing that licenses you to stop. That felt like his strongest move to me but I couldn't tell you why.

Then Dana tried a reductio. She said: fine, take you seriously. Then knowledge is for sale. Buy the travel insurance, and suddenly you know you'll make the flight; hire a driver and you know the route. Rich people know more than poor people, not because they have better evidence, but because they can afford to make being wrong cheap. Nobody thinks knowledge works like that.

Marcus didn't fold. He said money already buys evidence, so why is it shocking that it buys stake-reduction, and if a backup plan means being wrong doesn't hurt, maybe you really do know more with the backup plan in hand. Dana said no -- that just shows he switched the claim. With insurance, what you know is 'I'll get there either way,' which is a different, easier proposition than 'the bus comes at 7:15.' She says he keeps sliding to an easier target and calling it the same knowledge. Marcus said if that objection counts, it counts against her too, because in the movie case the target is the same bus-time fact and she'd still let him say he knows it. They went around like that for another twenty minutes. I genuinely couldnt referee, and my gut says half of this might be a verbal dispute and half might not, but I can't tell which half is which.
