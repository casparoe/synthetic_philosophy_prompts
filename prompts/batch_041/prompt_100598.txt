I cover science and ideas for an online magazine, and every couple weeks I hit a story where two things I actually care about pull in opposite directions and I have no way to weigh them. Not the easy stuff — the easy stuff is where one option is just worse on every axis. I mean the ones where I can feel that there's no common scale: run the piece and you burn a source, spike it and you sit on something the public has a right to know; give the heterodox researcher a hearing and you lend credibility to something you think is wrong, refuse and you're the reason nobody ever hears it. Philosophers have a name for this (incommensurability, the Ruth Chang thing where the options are 'on a par' rather than one being better or worse or exactly equal) and I want that turned into something I can run at my desk in a couple hours before deadline. My editor has final sign-off, but I'm the one who has to bring her a recommendation.

Write me the procedure. Steps in order, and for each step say what counts as a sufficient answer and what to do when I can't get one. Say where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it hands off to a human because the question is a value call and not a fact — be explicit about which steps are judgment dressed up as method. I want the distinctions that have to survive the simplification (I don't need the full theory, I need the parts that change what I do) and an honest list of where this thing will misfire.

A few moves I want in it, and you can tell me if they're wrong or if I've got the philosophy backwards: a check for whether both options are really answers to a single question (if they are, I'm on a scale after all and can stop); a check for domination, plus a warning that most domination claims smuggle in a hidden scale; a test for whether something is being traded off in small amounts or sacrificed wholesale, since I have things I won't sink no matter how big the other side is; a written ledger of what I'd regret under each option, sorted by kind of regret, on the theory that regrets of the same kind mean there's a scale and regrets of different kinds mean the choice is genuinely hard; a move where I try to make the conflict dissolve by getting more specific about what the value actually demands (truth-telling about X to the people who need it, not publishing everything); a good-enough threshold I could defend in public; and then, if nothing has settled it, whatever it is that's left when you just have to pick and own it. Name the steps so I can say them out loud in a meeting.

Then run it on three stories I'm actually sitting on. One: a researcher whose result got a lot of press may have made an honest data error, and running it probably ends a grad student's career. Two: a piece explaining how a widely used piece of industrial software can be made to fail dangerously, which is a real public-interest story and also a how-to. Three: a claim I think is mostly wrong but not crazy, from someone who's been frozen out, where the story is really about the freezing-out. I want at least one of the three to come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to flag whichever one the procedure can't actually settle.
