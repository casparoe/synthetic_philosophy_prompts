I am a geologist by training — my dissertation was on chronostratigraphy, so I am at home with GSSPs and boundary stratotypes, stage versus age, biozones and magnetozones, event stratigraphy, and the distinction between a material rock unit and an abstract time unit — and I have lately been sitting in on a digital-humanities seminar on the historiography of periodization. The historians there keep saying that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not natural kinds but retrospective, value-laden impositions on a continuous record, and I want to see what that claim looks like when it is forced to speak my language. Acting as a philosopher of history who is genuinely fluent in stratigraphy, and thinking this through carefully before you write, restate the strongest version of that anti-realist position — and, where it helps to mark the contrast, the strongest realist reply — in the vocabulary of chronostratigraphy and event stratigraphy. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the translation itself. Begin by brainstorming the candidate mappings and laying out the target vocabulary and the rules you will follow to keep the translation honest, then proceed unit by unit: which historiographical claims become claims about chronostratigraphic units, which become claims about material units, which become claims about diachronous boundaries or time-transgressive markers, and so on.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. What does not survive the translation? In stratigraphic terms, what would such a marker even be locating, and what would be lost compared with the historians' ordinary use of the term?
