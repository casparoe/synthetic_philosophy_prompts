I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying, without much success, to get an intuitive grip on a cluster of arguments about how to reason from the bare fact that one exists. The two ideas I keep encountering are the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, along with the objection that the second of these permits a presumptuous-philosopher style of inference. I would like you to explain the underlying conceptual issue rather than survey the literature; please do not give me reading lists or citations, since I have no way to check them.

My rough understanding is this. The self-sampling assumption asks me to reason as if I were a randomly selected observer drawn from all the observers that a given hypothesis says exist, so that my evidence is more likely under hypotheses in which observers like me form a larger fraction of the whole. The self-indication assumption instead treats my existence itself as evidence, so that hypotheses positing more observers are favoured, on the grounds that more observers means more places for something like me to have turned up. The part I cannot follow is the charge that this second assumption lets someone conclude, on almost no empirical evidence, that the universe is enormous — or, in the doomsday literature, that it makes an early extinction much less likely than it first appears.

To make this concrete, here is a scenario I invented, and it is where my intuitions stall. A machine will run exactly one of two programs, selected by a fair coin. Program A creates a single observer, who then undergoes a particular experience, which I shall call X. Program B creates one observer who undergoes X and also creates a thousand further observers, whose experiences are entirely different from X and from one another. I wake up, and my experience is X. My tentative reading is that the self-sampling assumption should make me favour A, since under B I am only one of a thousand and one observers and the odds of being the one with experience X are correspondingly small; whereas the self-indication assumption should be neutral here, because the extra observers in B do not share my evidence X, so they lend no boost to B. If that is right, then the presumptuous-philosopher inference would seem to depend on the extra observers being duplicates of me in some relevant sense, rather than merely being additional. I would like to know whether this reading is correct, and what exactly the self-indication assumption is counting when it counts observers.
