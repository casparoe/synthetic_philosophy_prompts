So I've somehow gotten roped into running next week's session of our department's "Evidence and Inference" reading group, and the topic is Simpson's paradox — which I've taught twice before and both times I lost the room about twenty minutes in. I dont want you to explain the paradox to me; I want a plan for how to teach it. The crowd is mixed: a couple of cognitive-science grad students who've had a real stats course, the rest philosophers who have not, seventy-five minutes, whiteboard, and theres alot of ground and not much time. I want the whole session to be about one thing and I want it to land: that the flip itself is the least interesting part, and that whether the pooled number or the stratified numbers is the honest one is a question about what caused what, not about arithmetic.

Here's the example I want to build it on, and I want you to tell me if it's actually the right carrying example or if it's missing something. Two treatments for the same condition. Treatment X: mild cases 9/10 recover (90%), severe cases 11/100 recover (11%), so 20/110 overall (18%). Treatment Y: mild cases 85/100 (85%), severe cases 1/10 (10%), so 86/110 overall (78%). So Y crushes X overall while X beats Y in both the mild and the severe stratum — the textbook flip. But the point I want them to chew on is that this tells you almost nothing until you say what severity *is*: if severity is fixed before treatment is assigned (treatment can't move you between groups), the strata are the honest comparison and the pooled number is the confounded one; but if the treatment itself shuffles people between severity groups, then conditioning on severity is the error and the pooled number is closer to the truth. I want them to leave feeling that "just control for everything" is a trap and not a cure, and that a picture of what causes what is doing all the real work.

What misconceptions should I expect from that room, and the precise moment in the sequence to head each one off — I'm already expecting "so statistics can prove anything," "so you should always split into subgroups," "so the overall number is always the lie," and "this is a rare gotcha," but tell me the ones I'm not expecting.
