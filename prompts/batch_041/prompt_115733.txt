The reading group I sit in on has spent the better part of a year on the extended mind thesis — the claim that a notebook, a diary, a phone, or any external resource that a person consults without hesitation, trusts without checking, and would be lost without may count as part of her cognitive system, so that what the resource holds is not merely information she uses but something closer to what she remembers and believes. We keep coming back to one unresolved question: what marks the boundary between a resource that is part of a mind and one that is merely useful? Availability, reliability, the absence of verification, the history of how the resource came to be set up — each candidate criterion admits too much or too little, and we have not settled on anything. I would like a short story that puts pressure on precisely the part of the question the standard examples leave alone.

Here is the seed, and I would like you to build the story on it. A woman of about seventy has kept a memory ledger since she was twelve: a thick bound book in which the significant events of her life have been entered, week by week. She has never written in it herself — she mistrusts her own hand and has always preferred to dictate — so the entries were made first by her mother, then by her husband for three decades, and for the past twenty years by a paid amanuensis who comes on Sundays. He writes down what she tells him, but he also prunes, silently corrects her dates, supplies the names of people she has half forgotten, and now and then enters something she mentioned in passing and thought nothing of. She reads the book daily, believes it without checking, and cannot manage a week without it. Now other things are failing: her heart, her balance. Her daughter wants her in a care home. The ledger contains, in her own dictated words from years ago, a flat refusal ever to leave the house, and the family is treating that entry as her considered wish. Everything turns on whether the ledger is part of her mind or a document about her. If it is part of her mind, then what is written there is what she thinks and remembers, and the family is bound by it — including the parts the amanuensis wrote for her. If it is a document, then it is hearsay about a person, and those who love her may decide for her; which would mean that for twenty years a hired man has been quietly authoring the self they are now asked to obey.

I want the crisis of the story to turn on the difference between two answers to that boundary question, and I want both answers to have real force. One answer is functional: the ledger does the work of memory for her — it is consulted without hesitation, it settles what happened, it is the thing she thinks with — and therefore it is her memory, whoever's hand made the marks. The other answer is genetic, or authorial: a memory is something a person lays down herself, and what the amanuensis writes, edits, and supplies is not her memory but a story about her that she has been living inside. On that view the ledger is not a mind extended but a mind replaced, and its authority over her is exactly the problem rather than the solution. Let the story show why each answer attracts and what each costs. In particular, I want at least one scene in which the question stops being academic — where what the family does next depends on which answer is true, and where the person who must act cannot simply ask the woman, because she will consult the book.

Constraints. No character may name a philosopher, a thesis, or a thought experiment, and no one may lecture; the philosophical problem has to be carried by events, objects, and the way people talk around it. Include a scene in which an entry is read aloud and changes what she takes herself to have done; a scene in which the amanuensis's pruning is discovered, and his defense of it is not contemptible; and a scene in which two people who both love her disagree about what would be a kindness. The apparatus of the story should be paper, ink, and habit — no devices, no invented technology, no clinical or science-fictional framing. End on a concrete choice rather than a verdict: something a person does that only makes sense if one of the two answers is right, with the other answer left standing. Do not append an epigraph, an author's note, or a closing paragraph that explains the thesis.

Around two thousand to twenty-eight hundred words. Plain, unhurried prose, in a third person that stays close to whichever character is least able to see the whole situation.
