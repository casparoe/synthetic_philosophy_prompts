I chair the grant-making committee of a mid-sized private foundation — about $40 million a year, historically divided among four near-term programs (a scholarship fund, a vaccination-logistics partnership, a legal-aid network, and an archive-digitization project), each of which we can evaluate within roughly five years. At our last meeting one trustee circulated a memo proposing that we shift about two-thirds of the coming cycle to a single project: an attempt to build a durable international institution that, if it took hold, would in her estimate improve the long-run trajectory of the next several centuries by something on the order of a factor of a trillion, and which she puts at perhaps one chance in ten million of succeeding — while conceding something like a one-in-a-hundred chance that it entrenches an authoritarian order instead. Her argument is a plain expected-value one: one in ten million times a trillion is still a hundred thousand times the value of anything else on our list, so a committee that declines is choosing a worse world on purpose. Two other trustees have offered stopping rules rather than counter-arguments. The first would forbid any allocation resting on a probability below one in a thousand, on the grounds that our estimates below that threshold are noise and that only a hard floor protects us from being talked into any gamble whatsoever. The second would retain expected-value reasoning but require that the mechanism be independently verifiable within fifty years, so that we can learn something from failure.

I've been asked to circulate the committee's decision memo before Thursday's vote. Please draft it. It should recommend a concrete allocation for the coming cycle, propose a standing rule (or an explicit decision to have none) for 'astronomical stakes' proposals going forward, specify when that rule should be revisited and what evidence would overturn it, and be written so that trustees who disagree can respond to the reasoning rather than to a verdict. Take seriously the strongest case on the other side from my own: I'll say plainly that I find the anti-fanatic position's line-drawing hard to defend — I cannot see what makes one in a thousand principled and one in ten million absurd, and I am suspicious of any rule whose chief virtue is that it shields us from arguments we dislike — but I want a memo that argues with me rather than one that agrees, and I want the trustees' floor proposals answered on their merits. Please do not give me a survey of decision theory under uncertainty or a taxonomy of positions; give me a document we can act on, in which the recommendation follows from reasons a trustee on the losing side would have to grant are at least relevant.
