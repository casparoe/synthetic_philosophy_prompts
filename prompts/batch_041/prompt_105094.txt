I'm in a little reading group that's been chewing through philosophy of mind stuff, and last Tuesday two of the other members — Dara and Miles — went at each other for like forty minutes about epiphenomenalism while the rest of us ate chips. The view on the table was the blunt version: your experiences are caused by brain states but don't cause anything themselves. The throbbing of a headache is a side effect of the brain state that also makes you wince and reach for ibuprofen; the feeling itself does no work. I couldn't referee it, and honestly I lost the thread alot, so I'm writing down the exchange as best I can.

Miles opened: the view refutes itself. If experiences cause nothing, then the experience of being persuaded by an argument can't be what makes you say "I'm persuaded" — the brain state does that, and it would have done it whether or not any experience came along. So when an epiphenomenalist announces "epiphenomenalism seems right to me," that sentence was churned out by machinery that's indifferent to whether there's any experience in there. His endorsement is no better evidence for his own view than a parrot's squawk is for parrot theory. And the same goes for every belief about experience — none of them are actually about experience. Dara said that's way too fast: a thermometer doesn't cause the temperature, and its reading is still about the temperature, because the reading tracks it and is built to track it. Same deal — the brain state that produces "I see red" tracks the red-experience, since both flow from the same underlying state, so the report can be about the experience without the experience causing anything. Aboutness is tracking plus function, not the object causing the report. Miles came back: fine, but designed by what? If it's evolution or learning, then what got selected was the physical state's grip on behavior, and behavior is caused by the physical state, never by the experience. So the tracking the report actually has is tracking of the physical state — you've just relocated the aboutness over to the physical side and left the experience out. The thermometer reads the mercury and you're insisting on calling the mercury "the temperature." Dara: but the experience supervenes on the physical state — tracking the physical state just *is* tracking the experience, one fact described twice. You keep demanding a seperate causal link running to the experience and then acting shocked that there isn't one; the whole point of supervenience is that there doesn't need to be one. Miles: then your tracking is doing no work at all. If the experience is guaranteed to tag along with the physical state, the report's content can be fixed entirely on the physical side, and the experience is a passenger again — you haven't shown the report is about the experience, only that the experience is in the vicinity. But if you say instead that it's the experience that makes the report about red rather than about some physical state P, then you've handed the experience a job — fixing content — and now it isn't epiphenomenal anymore. Pick one. (Dara tried a move here about the experience being the vehicle of the content rather than its object, like the difference between what a sentence means and the ink it's printed in, but Miles said that's just relabeling and they went in circles.)

Can you go through the exchange and adjudicate it step by step — who's got the better of each round, what's right and what's wrong in each move — and then say who's more right overall? And whatever your verdict, tell me what both of them are missing, because I have a feeling the whole fight is being fought on a board neither of them drew correctly.
