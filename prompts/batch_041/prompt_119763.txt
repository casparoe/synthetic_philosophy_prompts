I am a retired physicist — forty years in statistical mechanics, with a late turn into quantum information — and since retiring I have been teaching myself philosophy from the ground up. I have got as far as Parfit on personal identity: the teletransporter, the branching cases, the conclusion that identity is not what matters. I understand the arguments well enough in their own terms. What I cannot do is see their shape in mine, and that is what I am asking you for.

Restate Parfit's case, and the claims he draws from it, in the vocabulary I actually think in. That vocabulary is the physicist's: states and observables, trajectories through a state space, ensembles, conservation laws and symmetries, correlation and entanglement, information and its copying, decoherence, records, irreversibility. I am fluent in all of it. Do the translation properly and claim by claim, not in generalities. Then give me the residue report: what carries across cleanly, what carries across only under distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — with your judgement, in each case, about which side is at fault.

To be concrete about the claims I want handled:

1. The basic case. My brain and body are destroyed here, and elsewhere a molecule-for-molecule replica is assembled, psychologically indistinguishable from me. Is the replica me, and does that matter? Give me Parfit's answer in my terms.

2. The branching case. The original survives and a replica is made as well; or two replicas are made. Parfit treats this as the crux. Say what he concludes, and whether my vocabulary can even mark the difference between one continuation and two.

3. Reductionism. The claim that facts about personal identity are just facts about brains, bodies, and psychological continuity, with nothing over and above them. Give me the nearest analogue in my vocabulary, then tell me honestly whether the analogy is doing any work or is merely decorative.

4. The positive thesis. Identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity of some sort, whatever its cause. Replace connectedness and continuity with whatever my vocabulary offers — overlap of records, correlation between states, fidelity of a copy, whatever fits — and tell me where the replacement stops being a translation and becomes the substitution of a different question.

5. The indifference to cause. This is the claim I find hardest to credit, because in physics a mechanism is never idle: a copy produced by a lawful dynamical process and a copy that appears by miracle are different physical situations, and I want to know which conservation laws, if any, the second violates. Does Parfit's indifference to the cause have a counterpart in my vocabulary, or is this precisely the sort of thing that fails to survive translation?

For each of the five, and then in a short general verdict, give me the residue report. Where a claim has no counterpart, say which way the failure runs: is my vocabulary missing something — an indexical fact, a first-person fact, a normative fact, a fact about what there is reason to care about — or is the original claim less determinate than it appeared, so that there was less there to translate than the philosophers suppose?

Two constraints. First, do not smuggle the assumptions of my own vocabulary into the translation and then present them as successes; if sameness of trajectory, or sameness of information, does not capture what Parfit is after, say so rather than declaring the problem solved. Second, do not treat the residue as an embarrassment to be explained away; it is the reason I am asking. I would rather have a short, exact answer that concedes three things cannot be carried across than a comprehensive one that pretends everything can. Two pages at most. I have no need of references — I want the argument, not the literature — and I would rather you flag a place where you have quietly redefined a philosophical term to make the translation work than let it pass.
