In a discussion of the moral status of insects, someone brought the exchange to a close with the line "no brain, no pain." The context was a disagreement about the insects killed in ordinary agricultural practice. One participant had invoked what is often called the scale argument: because the number of insects affected is astronomical, even a modest probability that they are sentient could make their interests dominate the expected moral value of the practice. The slogan was offered to defuse that argument, on the thought that insects, lacking a brain of the relevant kind, have no experience for the argument to weigh.

I want a careful inventory of what that one-liner could actually be claiming, and an assessment of whether any of the readings does the work it was asked to do. Set out the distinct theses it might express, ordering them from the nearly trivial to the genuinely substantive, and for each say what it commits you to, whether it is plausible, and whether it would by itself license setting the question aside. I would particularly like these questions kept apart: whether the claim is definitional or empirical; whether it asserts impossibility or only improbability; what the word "brain" is doing, given that insects do have brains, though not vertebrate ones; whether it depends on a distinction between mere nociception and felt pain; and whether it is in the end a claim about what we may assume under uncertainty rather than about what is the case.

Then relate each reading to the scale argument. That argument seems to require the probability of insect sentience to be low but nonzero: say which readings are compatible with it, which would undermine it, and which turn out to be premises of it rather than objections to it. My suspicion is that the slogan does different work in different mouths, and I would like to be able to identify which work it is doing when it is used to end the conversation.
