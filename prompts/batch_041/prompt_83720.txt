I have no academic background. I write the on-screen text, the spoken prompts, and the printed quick-start card for a home battery-backup system made by a small company, and for two years I have been turning over a distinction I met in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: every message carries three things at once. There is the inner message, what it actually says. There is the outer message, what it says about itself — that it is a warning rather than a status update, that it was written by a manufacturer rather than a neighbor, that it is addressed to an electrician rather than to me. And there is the frame message, the thing that tells you how to decode the message at all, which as far as I can tell almost never sits inside the message it governs.

I want that distinction turned into a procedure I can run on a string before I hand it to the localization agency. A batch is twenty strings, I am the only writer, and I have about two hours per batch. The strings then go out to a vendor and come back in sixteen languages; each must fit a forty-character display; each is also read aloud by a synthesized voice; and for three markets we ship a pictogram-only card, because the installers there read little of any written language. Every string also ends up in our web help center, where people arrive by search with none of the surrounding context left intact. One further complication: our automated quality tool compares each translation to the English and flags differences in length and in glossary terms. As best I can tell it only ever inspects the inner message, and I do not know what to make of that.

Please give me the procedure: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point where the answer is simply no — where a string cannot be translated and has to be rebuilt as a pictogram or a tone instead — and the point where the question stops being factual and has to go to our regulatory sign-off, who handles anything touching electric shock and has no patience for theory. Tell me which parts of the three-level distinction must survive into the checklist and which I can throw away, and be honest about the steps that are judgment dressed up as procedure.

I also have a half-invented fourth level for you to adjudicate: the message about who is being addressed and what that person is assumed to be allowed to do. Tell me whether that is genuinely distinct from the outer message or only the outer message seen from another angle, and if it is distinct, where it belongs in the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three of my strings — Battery at 20 percent; non-essential circuits will be shut off soon; Error 41, contact your installer; and a card showing a figure opening the equipment enclosure with a bar through it — and include at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I do not want to do. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, and which of my decisions it only appears to settle.
