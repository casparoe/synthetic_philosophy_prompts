I am trying to work out what I actually believe about karma as a theory of moral desert, and I want to know whether a single principle can be made to fit the verdicts I keep giving. Here are the cases and my reactions to them.

1. A man embezzles the pension fund of the firm he runs and lives well on it for twenty years. A con artist then takes everything he has. My verdict: deserved, and deserved specifically as a repayment — the loss answers the theft.

2. The same man is never defrauded but develops a slow, painful illness in old age. My verdict: still deserved, though it feels less like repayment and more like a bill being settled by other means. The kind of harm seems not to matter, only its size.

3. A second embezzler, who has confessed, repaid every cent, and spent his remaining years doing penance, is later defrauded in the same way. My verdict: not deserved — the account is closed, and his past should not be entered on it.

4. A newborn with a painful congenital illness. My verdict: not deserved, full stop; nobody has done anything to be repaid.

5. A man who has done nothing wrong is struck by a misfortune that happens to mirror a wrong committed by his father. My verdict: not deserved — the father's debt is not his to pay.

6. A driver who paralyses a pedestrian while drunk is himself paralysed years later in an unrelated fall. My verdict: deserved, and the mismatch in kind between what he did and what he suffers does not trouble me at all.

7. A hiker deliberately steps into a crevasse so that the others can cross a collapsing bridge. My verdict: his suffering is not deserved, but it is not undeserved either; it is chosen.

The principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: suffering is deserved just in case it is a fitting return for a wrong the sufferer himself committed, proportionate in amount, and not already discharged by repair. But I can see that the fitting-return clause pulls in two directions — in case 6 I ignore fittingness of kind entirely, while in case 3 I let repair wipe the slate clean, which is a very different way of cancelling a debt. I also cannot tell whether what matters in case 3 is that the debt was objectively repaid or that the man is now genuinely sorry; and if it is only the objective repayment, then a thief who returns everything but feels nothing would still owe something, which I am not sure I believe. And I have not said what work, if any, the idea of rebirth is doing: if the sufferer must be the very person who did the wrong, cases 4 and 5 fall out immediately, but then I owe an account of what makes an adult the same person as the child or the past-life self who incurred the debt.

Please tell me what principle, or small family of principles, actually captures the pattern, which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it implies for two cases I have not listed: a person who commits a great wrong and is rewarded with a long, untroubled, happy life; and a wrongdoer whose suffering, when it comes, falls not on him but on his children.
