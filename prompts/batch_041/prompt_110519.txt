Question 2 (10 marks, ~600 words)

In the alarm clock case, the same person plans to get up early and then, at the moment of choice, stays in bed. We looked at two ways of describing this: as weakness of will, and as a case of time-inconsistent preferences. Someone in the seminar claimed that the hyperbolic discounting model gives us a formal vindication of the traditional notion of akrasia, on the grounds that akrasia just is preference reversal.

(a) Set out what hyperbolic discounting says about how the value of a future reward decays, and explain why the model generates reversals of preference. (4)

(b) Assess the vindication claim: does the discounting model capture genuine weakness of will, or does it explain the phenomenon away? Argue for a position rather than just surveying the options. (4)

(c) What follows practically, if anything, for someone who wants to stop hitting snooze? (2)

Student answer

(a) The exponential model says that a reward of size V arriving t periods from now has present value V/(1+r)^t, with r fixed. Because r is the same at every delay, the ranking of two options is stable: if I prefer the larger later reward when both are a year off, I'll still prefer it when one of them is a week away. Discounting of that kind is time-consistent, which is what you get if you assume a single utility function maximised over the whole of a life.

Hyperbolic discounting replaces this with something like V/(1+kt). The crucial difference is that the implied discount rate falls as the delay grows: the rate I apply to the next hour is much steeper than the rate I apply to an hour that is a year away. So when both options are distant the larger one wins, but as the smaller one approaches, the steep part of the curve starts to bite and the ranking flips. That's preference reversal, and it happens with no new information coming in: I rank A over B at t1 and B over A at t2, and both rankings are mine. The b-d (present bias) version makes the same point in a different functional form — everything is discounted exponentially except the present, which takes an extra hit — and that's the version most of the empirical work uses, since it's easier to estimate.

(b) I think the model does vindicate the traditional notion. The traditional notion is that the weak-willed agent judges that she should do X and then does Y anyway. The alarm case has exactly that structure: the night before she judges that getting up is what she has most reason to do, sets the alarm, and means it; at 6am she turns it off. The model tells us why the second judgement is unstable. And it isn't that she's changed her mind about the value of the exam, or forgotten her reasons — she'd still endorse the plan if you asked her about it from a distance. It's that the steep discounting of anything delayed makes the bed look better from inside the moment. Note also that the reversal only happens when the sooner option is available right now; that's the whole content of present bias. So we get a precise, quantitative version of the old idea that the will is dragged around by what's near at hand.

One thing the model is silent about is regret, the sting of having gone against yourself. That may just be because it's a model of choice rather than of feeling, but it's worth flagging.

(c) The practical upshot is the commitment device. If I know my future self has a steeper discount curve, I arrange things so that future self has no choice: alarm clock across the room, an app that donates my money if I don't get up, a friend who calls. A naive agent, who doesn't predict her own reversal, will keep setting alarms and keep failing; a sophisticated agent predicts it and precommits. So the model has real advice to give, and it's basically Ulysses tying himself to the mast.
