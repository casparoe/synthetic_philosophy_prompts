I am preparing a handout for the opening meeting of a graduate metaphysics seminar on material constitution. My students will have read about the statue and the lump of clay and will arrive able to recite the puzzle; what they lack is a map of the terrain. I want them to locate themselves on that map before I name a single position. So please build me a decision tree rather than an essay: a classification instrument, not a survey.

1. Work from the logical space outward. Identify the independent questions on which positions actually differ, order them so that each branch is a question a student can answer about the cases without knowing which label it leads to, and only then place the positions at the leaves. Every node must be phrased in the material mode — about statues, lumps, squashing, carving, counting — never in the vocabulary of the positions it separates. No node may ask, in effect, whether one accepts a named doctrine, and no node may simply ask whether the statue is identical with the clay; ask instead what a student thinks happened to the things on the pedestal, and let identity fall out of the answer.

2. The following distinctions should appear as branch nodes somewhere, though tell me if any of them is not really independent of the others: whether the question of how many objects are present has one substantive answer, no answer until a sortal is supplied, or only answers relative to a chosen language or conceptual scheme; whether the statue and the clay are one thing or two; if two, whether they are related by a special non-identity relation or by ordinary co-location; whether the material side of the pair is an object at all or a portion of stuff; whether the modal difference (the clay survives squashing, the statue does not) is a fact about the things, a fact about our descriptions, or an artifact of how we count across possibilities; whether being a statue consists in form imposed on matter, in the intentions of a maker or a community, or in nothing beyond the arrangement of the matter; and whether the number of things present could be vague.

3. Tie each diagnostic question to at least one of these cases, and say which leaves each case prunes: (a) the familiar lump molded into a statue and then squashed; (b) a statue carved in place out of bedrock, so that no separately existing lump ever preceded it; (c) a found object — a piece of driftwood displayed unmodified as a sculpture — where no material was worked and no form imposed; (d) a single portion of bronze that is at once a statue, a lamp base, and an investment, so that more than two candidates coincide; (e) the statue's material replaced one chip at a time.

4. The leaves should include, at minimum, the identity view; constitution without identity; two coincident objects; hylomorphism; sortal-relative counting; four-dimensionalism; eliminativism in both directions (no statues, and no lumps); the view that the material item is a portion of stuff rather than an object; verbalism or quantifier variance; ontic indeterminacy; and one leaf for the view that there is a single thing which is neither the statue nor the lump but is described under two sortals. Where a leaf is a family, say what splits it further. Mark any leaf nobody has actually defended, and diagnose whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

5. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate with these cases, and say what additional case or question would pull them apart — or whether they are notational variants of one another.

6. Finish with the classification key: each answer pattern, the leaf it lands on, and one sentence naming the commitment that should embarrass the student who lands there.

Keep the whole thing under about two thousand words. Do not include a reading list, and avoid attributing views to named philosophers unless you are confident of the attribution; labels for positions are welcome, but the instrument has to work for a student who has never heard them. Write in American English.
