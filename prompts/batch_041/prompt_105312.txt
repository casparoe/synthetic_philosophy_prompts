I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who has waited tables for years, and I came away unsure whether we were disagreeing about a fact or about a concept. She holds that leaving nothing is simply wrong, not merely stingy, and that anyone eating in an American restaurant owes the server a tip whether or not they care for the custom; I said a tip is by definition voluntary, that a charge you are expected to pay is just a price, and that if servers are underpaid that is the employer's fault and not the diner's. The maddening part is that my own behaviour sides with her: I have never once left nothing, and I privately think worse of people who do. What I want from you is an account of what the institution of tipping in the United States presupposes about obligation.

Look at how the custom actually works, because its details seem to contradict one another. At a coffee counter the payment screen asks for a tip before the drink has been made, so the money cannot be gratitude for service received or an incentive for service to come; it looks more like a fee for belonging to the place. In a full-service restaurant the bill arrives with a blank line and the tip is nominally discretionary, yet a party of six or more often finds a service charge already added, so the same establishment treats the same act as optional for two diners and mandatory for eight. And the norm is pressed on visitors who never tip at home, whom we expect to conform the moment they land. I am struck that the restaurant converts the convention into a contract precisely where it expects the convention to fail, which suggests the custom's authority is weaker than the indignation it provokes.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory. What does the practice presuppose about where the obligation comes from -- an implicit term of the transaction, a duty owed to a dependent worker, or a social convention whose force is merely that everyone expects it? Does it presuppose that the customer, rather than the employer, is the party responsible for the server's wage, and does it presuppose a territorial view on which local conventions bind visitors as such? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to be surrendered if we adopted the rival view that the listed price is the whole price, that the tip is purely supererogatory, and that the wage is the employer's business -- which parts of the practice would have to change, and whether the change would be an improvement.
