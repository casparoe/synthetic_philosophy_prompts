Ok, I want a real dialogue, not two people trading bumper stickers, and I want it to end somewhere honest rather than with a tidy handshake.

Set it up like this. Two philosophers, call them R and S. Both of them are in the suffering-focused camp — neither is a cartoon utilitarian or a cartoon Kantian, and neither is a strawman for the other. They agree that suffering is the thing ethics has to get right. They disagree about what follows.

R is a straight negative utilitarian and a totalist: the only thing that matters morally is the total amount of suffering in the world, and she doesn't care how it's distributed, only how much of it there is. If she could shave a hair off the suffering of ten billion people at the cost of leaving one person in unthinkable agony, she'd want the exchange rate and nothing else.

S thinks suffering is the priority too, but he thinks R's view has a hole. He thinks distribution matters — that it's worse when the same total of suffering is concentrated in a few people than spread thin across many — and he's not sure suffering is even the kind of thing you can sum across persons like it's one quantity. He's the one who wants to put a case at the center of the whole conversation.

Here's the case. Suppose there's a device, a transfer box, that moves suffering from one person to another but can't destroy it. Whatever it takes off one person it puts onto someone else, one for one, no loss and no gain. The box also has a dial that trades intensity against duration: it can stretch a minute of agony into a year of dull ache, or compress a year of mild misery into one bad afternoon, at a fixed rate. Now picture a world with two people. One is in agony, the other is perfectly fine; call it 100 units of suffering total, all of it on one person. The box can move 50 units off the agonized person onto the fine one, so now each of them sits at 50. Same total, less concentration, nobody's suffering reduced by even a sliver.

R should be completely indifferent to whether you press the button — it's a wash by her lights. S wants to say you should press it, or at least that the second world is better, and he owes an account of why, if suffering is what matters and the total didn't budge. And R gets to press him on the leveling-down problem: if you can make things better without making anyone suffer less — while actually making one person suffer more — then what is your theory measuring? Is equal suffering a good in itself, and if so, isn't that a strange commitment for someone who says suffering is the whole story? S should get to fire back, and he shouldn't fold just because the objection has a name.

I want them to really dig in: aggregation versus distribution, whether suffering is commensurable across people at all, whether R's view can condemn cruelty that leaves the total untouched, whether S is smuggling in a non-suffering value like equality and whether that's fatal or fine. Then let it spill into the neighboring stuff — the asymmetry between creating a happy person and creating a suffering one, whether R has to bite the bullet on a painless instant end to all life and whether that's a reductio or just a bullet she can chew, and whether S's view even deserves the label suffering-focused anymore if it comes with side constraints and distributional commitments.

Nobody wins by being dumb. If S lands a good point R should visibly feel it, and the reverse. And when they hit a wall, I want them to say they've hit a wall instead of inventing a clean resolution. No citations, no name-dropping papers or authors — just the argument itself. Dont worry about being brief, but don't pad it either.
