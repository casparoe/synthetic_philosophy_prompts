Consider two philosophers, Vela and Orin, who disagree over whether institutional entities — promises, borders, money, the rules of a game — exist. Vela holds that they exist in the same sense in which rocks do. Orin holds that they do not exist, and that talk of them is a useful fiction. Take the following as stipulated.

(i) Vela and Orin agree on every question that can be posed without the vocabulary of existence-commitment: which sentences of our best social theories are true, which practices work, what explains what, and what follows normatively.

(ii) Their disagreement is confined to whether the entities those theories quantify over exist. Neither attributes confusion to the other; each can state the other's position accurately and has done so in print.

(iii) Each nonetheless regards the other's use of 'exist' as incorrect rather than merely different, and each can defend that verdict with an argument the other does not simply dismiss as question-begging.

(iv) A survey of competent speakers finds both uses widespread and stable under reflection, and finds neither use correlated with any independent error or ignorance.

(v) Asked about a world differing from ours only in that the relevant practices were never accepted, Vela judges that the entities exist there and Orin judges that they do not.

Now define a dispute as merely verbal just in case (a) the disputants attach different meanings to at least one of the disputed expressions, and (b) once those expressions are regimented so as to mean the same thing for both parties, no proposition remains that one affirms and the other denies.

Give me your credence that the Vela–Orin dispute is merely verbal in that sense. I want a number rather than a verdict, and the decomposition rather than the number alone. Assign a rough probability to each of the following, and to whatever further terms you think the decomposition requires: that Vela and Orin mean different things by 'exist'; that their counterfactual divergence reflects a difference in concepts rather than a disagreement about a proposition; that there is a fact of the matter about the counterfactual cases; that what survives regimentation is a normative question about which usage to adopt rather than a factual one; and that a normative question of that sort is itself substantive. Give each term one line of justification, say how the terms combine, and name the one your answer is most sensitive to. If you think the stipulated definition is defective, give the number under it regardless, and then a second number under the definition you would substitute.

Then tell me which argument or piece of evidence would move that term most. My own credence is about 0.45; if yours differs sharply, say which term we most likely disagree about. Finally, say whether you would expect the number to be higher or lower for a dispute with the same structure but a different subject matter, and why.
