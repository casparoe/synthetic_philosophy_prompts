I have been arguing about money pumps with two people in my department since the spring, and I have come to think the pump itself is the least interesting part of the disagreement. The setup is not in dispute. Take an agent whose preferences cycle: she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Start her with C. Because she prefers B to C she will pay a small sum to trade C for B; because she prefers A to B she will pay again to trade B for A; and because she prefers C to A she will pay a third time to trade A for C. She is back where she began, three coins poorer, and the standard moral is that her preferences are irrational, since a rational agent cannot be milked in this way. What my two colleagues dispute is what the sequence has shown, and neither will move. One of them reads it as a synchronic argument: at every stage the agent prefers the alternative on offer to what she holds, so a rational agent accepts each trade, and the fact that she ends up worse off simply reveals that the ranking she was choosing from was defective. The other reads it as at best a diachronic prediction about a myopic agent: an agent who sees the whole sequence coming can decline the first trade or buy a commitment device, and nothing about her being awkward to live with shows that the ranking itself was irrational. Each of them then says the other's reading is the one that assumes what it should prove, and we go round again. I have noticed, for what it is worth, that the case seems to turn entirely on whether the agent is allowed foresight and on whether her preferences at each stage are independent of the path she took to get there, and that we never seem to argue about those two things directly.

My hypothesis — please treat it as a hypothesis and not as an answer I am fishing for — is that this is not a disagreement about money pumps at all but about where in time the thing being assessed is located. The first colleague assesses a preference structure: a ranking is rational or not, and the pump is a test that uncovers a defect already present. The second assesses a choice situation: an agent facing offers at particular times, and the question is whether she is exploitable given her foresight, her options for commitment, and the offers actually made. If that is right, then the word 'irrational' is doing two jobs — being an incoherent ranking and being an exploitable chooser — and no further pump examples will settle anything. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me whether the impasse is verbal, substantive, or two questions wearing one name; say what each side is actually committed to, in particular whether either of them needs the principle that a rational agent cannot be made worse off by trades she herself prefers, and whether that principle is meant synchronically or diachronically; and tell me what would count as progress. Would a formal result help — something that separates being pumpable from being exploitable under explicit assumptions about foresight and about path-independence — or is the real move to stop asking whether the agent is irrational and start asking what a theory of rationality is for? And if my hypothesis is wrong, say so, and tell me what single thing the two sides are contradicting each other about.
