I spent most of last evening in a long argument with a friend about whether markets can know anything, and we came away stuck on a claim of hers that I could neither accept nor refute. Her case was this: the no-trade results in the economics of information establish that agents who share a common prior, and whose current allocation is already ex ante Pareto efficient, will never trade on the strength of private information, because the very willingness to trade is itself a signal about what the counterparty knows. From this she concluded, first, that in a genuinely efficient market trading would cease, so that the enormous volume we observe daily is evidence against the market's informational efficiency, and second, that those who keep trading must be either irrational or trading on noise rather than on information.

I was half-persuaded and could not say where the trouble lies, which is why I am asking. Is her argument sound? Does the no-trade result really imply that a market which prices information well would be a market in which almost nobody trades, so that heavy volume counts against the claim that prices aggregate dispersed knowledge? If it does, I would like to understand what the epistemically serious participants in those markets could possibly be doing; and if it does not, I would like to know exactly what the argument gets wrong, and what the result actually does tell us about the epistemic life of a market.
