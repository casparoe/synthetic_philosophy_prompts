A colleague of mine in the history faculty has been arguing that the cyclical and the linear views of history are not symmetrical options, and that the cyclical view is in a special kind of trouble. His argument runs as follows.

(1) A cyclical theory of history holds that political forms, and on some versions whole cultures, pass through a determinate sequence: Polybius's rotation of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun's rise and decay of dynastic solidarity, Spengler's cultures as organisms with a fixed life span, Toynbee's alternation of growth and breakdown. On any of these views the sequence is driven by processes that operate whether or not anyone grasps them.

(2) If such a theory is true, then the reception of any doctrine, the theory itself included, is itself a phase phenomenon. A society arrives at the cyclical view at the point in the cycle that makes it visible to it, and the view's currency is a symptom of where that society stands rather than evidence that it has discovered the shape of history.

(3) A doctrine whose acceptance is fully explained by the position of its holders in a process the doctrine itself describes cannot be accepted on its merits; the explanation of the belief displaces the evidence for it.

(4) Therefore, if the cyclical theory is true, its acceptance is no reason to believe it.

(5) A linear theory is not in this position. It holds that history has a direction, so that later understanding can correct earlier understanding, and it can be accepted as the outcome of cumulative inquiry rather than as the symptom of a phase.

(6) Therefore the two views are not on a par. A cyclical theory is self-undermining in a way a linear theory is not, and this asymmetry is by itself a reason to prefer the linear view.

I find (6) unacceptable, and I suspect the argument of some sleight of hand, but I cannot locate it. My instinct is that the trouble, if there is any, lies at (3); yet (3) strikes me as the least controversial step in the whole chain, since a belief that is a predictable output of the cycle is surely not thereby justified. And if I do give up (6), I appear committed to saying that a theory may be believed on its merits even when the theory itself predicts that the belief is a phase effect, which is the one thing the cyclical view is famous for asserting. Tell me which step fails, why it looked fine, and what it costs to give that step up. I would sooner surrender (3) or (5) than (1) or (2), but I want to know what I am buying, and whether the linear view really escapes the same treatment or only appears to. State how confident you are in your diagnosis, and identify the point in your own answer about which you are least confident.
