Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about money pumps, and I want to settle it by writing the argument up as a joke and sending it to him. I'd like the joke to actually work for someone who knows this literature, so let me set out exactly where we got stuck.

My position was that the money pump is the strongest argument we have that time-inconsistent preferences are irrational. His was that the whole thing is a shell game: it only bites against a chooser too dim to see the sequence coming. A chooser who notices that accepting the first trade sets up a chain of trades ending with them a dollar poorer and back where they started will simply decline the first offer, and so the pump tells us nothing about what rationality requires. I said that declining is itself a diachronic policy — a commitment that has to be held across time — and that whether the chooser can hold it is precisely the thing in dispute. He said that was circular, since the capacity to stick to a policy just is the diachronic coherence whose rationality we were trying to establish in the first place.

What we kept tripping over, and the reason I want the joke, is that two versions of the pump slide into each other. In the synchronic version a single chooser with a genuine cycle in their preferences — they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — is pumped by an outside trader who walks them around the loop for a fee. In the diachronic version there need be no cycle at any single time. The person's preferences are perfectly orderly at every moment; they simply change between moments, and the exploiter does nothing more sinister than arbitrage the disagreement between two time-slices of the same person, buying from the earlier slice what the later slice will pay more to have. If the second version is the real target, then "a smart chooser would just refuse" has no purchase, because there is no single smart chooser across time to do the refusing — only a succession of choosers, each being offered a trade that is a strict improvement by their own lights at the moment of choosing. I think that is the interesting version, and that the pump's sting comes from nothing but the fact that the same person's valuations disagree across time, with no deception and no confusion anywhere in the story. He thinks that is a sleight of hand: a person is not a series of strangers, and the moment you let the time-slices be separate agents, the argument stops showing anything about a person's rationality at all.

Please write the joke. Some requirements:

- It should be a joke, with a setup and a punchline — not an essay wearing a comic frame.
- The punchline has to turn on the actual content of the money-pump argument, not on generic material about economists, philosophers, or weakness of will. Someone who knows the synchronic/diachronic distinction and the "why not simply refuse?" objection should find it funny because it lands on a real feature of the debate.
- It should exploit the disagreement above, ideally the arbitrage framing: an exploiter who deceives no one, breaks no agreement, and does nothing that any single time-slice of the victim objects to.
- It should be short enough to send in a text message, and it should not depend on the reader having read any particular paper.

After the joke, add one or two sentences naming the specific feature of the debate the punchline turns on, so that I can point to it when he claims he doesn't get it.
