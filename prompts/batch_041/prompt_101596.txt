Right, so I sit on the ethics committee at a mid-size teaching hospital and we keep having the same fight about advance directives, and I've noticed the fight is never really about the answer — it's about *why* people think the document should bind. Half the room agrees with the outcome and disagrees with every other person's reasoning, and I've started to think the reasons are the actual thing worth sorting.

Here's the case that keeps coming back, in the shape it actually comes in: a woman signs a directive at 41, in full command of herself, saying that if she ever ends up with moderate-to-severe dementia she does not want a feeding tube or antibiotics for a pneumonia, and she says the reason out loud — she doesn't want to be kept going as someone she wouldn't recognise. She's 79 now, moderately demented, doesn't recognise anyone, and appears, by every measure we've got, content. Asked in simple words whether she wants to live, she says yes, she likes it here. Her daughter wants us to follow the document. The nurses want to treat the pneumonia.

Now — I don't need you to tell me which way to go on that case, or not mainly. What I want is a ranked assessment of the *grounds* people give for letting the earlier self bind the later one, because that's where the disagreement actually lives, and I want them ordered by how much weight they can actually carry. Here's the pile I've collected, roughly as people say them in the room:

1. Precedent autonomy: she decided while she was still the author of her own life, and that decision is the last authentic thing she said on the subject.
2. Critical interests: it's about what she valued over a life, not what she happens to enjoy minute to minute, and the document is the best record of the former.
3. Narrative faithfulness: a life is a story, the later chapters shouldn't betray the earlier ones, and the document is her own draft of the ending.
4. Current welfare: she's happy now; happiness is happiness; a document can't outweigh a person's actual present contentment.
5. Reductionism about the self: there's no further fact about which of the two women is "really" her, so there's no fact of the matter about who wins — you just do the kindest thing.
6. Reaffirmation: a directive should only bind if it was reaffirmed recently enough to be plausibly about *this* person, not a stranger's plan.
7. The decay clock — this is our hospital counsel's invention and I want your honst read on it: treat a directive as losing force the more the person has changed, so a directive written 38 years ago and never revisited is weaker than one written last year, and past some threshold of change it stops binding unless it's reaffirmed.

Rank these. Tier them (S down to F, or whatever buckets you like) by how much argumentative weight they carry as reasons for the earlier self to bind the later one — not by whether you like the conclusion they lead to. Some of these I think are doing real work and some are just moralising with a lab coat on. And place the decay clock honestly, including if the answer is "this is incoherent."

Then give me, in this order:

- **Bottom line** — three or four sentences, your overall read, up top.
- **The tiers** — one block per tier, S down to F, each ground getting a short paragraph on why it lands where it does and what would have to be true for it to move up or down.
- **The decay clock** — a bit longer, a paragraph or two, on whether the idea is salvagable and what it would have to be to work.
- **What I'd actually vote for** — one paragraph on how you'd have us decide the case above, given where the grounds landed.

Keep it plain — I have to read this out loud to people who aren't philosophers.
