So I want to read Amadeus -- the Forman film, I've never actually seen the Shaffer play -- through the ethics of putting real dead people into fiction, and I keep going back and fourth about it. It's for a film club write-up, so it needs to actually land.

What makes it a much better case than your average biopic is that its central fact is a rumor. Salieri didn't kill Mozart, and he spent the last stretch of his actual life denying it to anyone who'd listen -- it wrecked him, he had breakdowns over it. Then the film has him confess to it, in a asylum, to a priest, for the length of the whole movie. So the wrong isn't just that a dead man gets a false fact attached to him; he gets made to author it, in his own voice, as the thing he's most ashamed of. And the film is sympathetic to him the entire way, he's the tragic one, the one who loves God and can't stand what he was given. So: does the sympathy mitigate, or does it make it worse? Handing a real person an interior life he never had feels like a different kind of wrong from just getting the record wrong.

The distinction I keep reaching for, and I don't think it has a good name, is between misrepresenting someone -- a factual failure, fixable in principle, the kind of thing an errata slip could address -- and conscripting them: using a real person as reusable raw material, where their actual life matters only where it's convenient to your story. I think the second is the real wrong and that it survives death, but I can't tell whether that's a genuine distinction or just me being precious. Does a dead person have a stake in any of this? The only candidate victim is the one who can't complain. He can't sue, can't reply, can't correct the record, and the record is now the only place he exists. Does that asymmetry make the wrong worse, or does it mean there's no wrong at all, just damage to a thing that belongs to us and not to him?

And the bit that really tangles me: the film's own metaphysics. Salieri believes in God and judgment, so within the story his stake is his soul, not his reputation -- what the Viennese think of him after he's dead should be nothing to him. But the film is soaked in memory and oblivion, the bored priest who doesn't recognize the music, the absolution speech at the end. It tells us reputation is dust and then builds its whole tragedy out of it. Is that the film contradicting itself, or is that the point?

So give me a reading of Amadeus in terms of what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. Where does the posthumous-harm angle actually light up a scene or a choice, and where does the film push back hard -- is film-Salieri even a person, or just an allegory for mediocrity, and does allegory get you off the hook? And end by saying what the film adds to the argument that the arguments alone don't, or say honestly that it adds nothing and just repeats a rumor with better lighting.
