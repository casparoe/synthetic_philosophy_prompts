I'm preparing a short position paper for a graduate seminar on consent and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is the view I have committed myself to, stated as flatly as I can.

(1) Consent is a normative power, not a mental state. What makes a consent valid is not the presence of some inner act of willing but the fact that the consenter has done something which, by public criteria, alters what others are permitted to do — closer to a waiver or a promise than to a feeling.

(2) It follows that deception defeats consent only when it is material. The consenter consents to an act under a description; deception voids the consent when it makes that description false in a way that mattered — that is, when the consenter would not have consented had the truth been disclosed — and leaves the consent standing when they would have consented anyway.

(3) Coercion, unlike deception, is baseline-relative. A threat coerces only when it leaves the person worse off than they would have been had the threat not been made. On this view the hard cases come out permissive: the rescuer who demands payment for the rope, the employer who offers starvation wages in a one-company town, are not coercing at all, because the offer improves the recipient's position relative to the world in which it is not made.

(4) Advance consent binds. A competent person's directive about treatment to be given later, or a Ulysses contract, is valid and enforceable even if at the moment the act is carried out they would refuse.

(5) Consent given where refusal is not a live option is void — not merely suspect, but void — regardless of what was said or signed.

(6) As verdicts on cases: a patient who consents to a procedure while severely depressed but still competent has consented validly; a person who agrees to sex because their partner threatens to leave them has not.

I have also begun describing a phenomenon I call consent laundering: an institution engineers the surrounding conditions until refusal is so costly that a formally clean yes will do the legitimating work for something no one would call voluntary in a fairer setting. I do not yet know which of (1) through (6) that idea belongs to, or whether it needs a commitment of its own.

What I want from you is an audit, not a summary. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, and which only appear to? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each one, say precisely what would have to give, what is lost by giving it, and what the cheapest repair would be rather than the tidiest. For the pairs that only look inconsistent, say briefly what reconciles them. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if I dropped it, would resolve the most at once — and whether anything in the list survives that drop unchanged.
