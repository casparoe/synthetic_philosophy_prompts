I'm helping a friend put together a one-off satirical suplement for the alumni magazine of the economics department where I did my masters (long story, she's the editor and she's desperate), and the theme for the issue is "institutions behaving badly". I want to write the centrepiece myself: a leaked internal investigation report, written entirely in deadpan officialese, into what it calls a "cascade incident" at a fictional mid-sized pension fund, where the investment committee approved, one signature at a time, a single large position that then went bad, and nobody lied or was bullied into it. The humour has to come from the mechanics being right, so that someone who actually works on informational cascades laughs rather than winces. So the findings need to do real work. A timeline in which the first two decisions were made on private evidence, and it turns out both of those people misread the same internal memo in the same direction, so the whole thing rests on correlated error rather than on two independent judgements. A finding that from the third signature onwards no new information entered the process at all, because each new committee member correctly reasoned that everyone before them had already committed, and so rationally stopped consulting their own analysis — and the report should be careful to say that this was a defensible inference at every single step, not stupidity, which is what makes the case interesting. A seperate section distinguishing the members who copied because they thought other people's choices carried information from the ones who copied because they didn't want to be the lone dissentient if the position went wrong (the report should not run these two together, and should note, mildly, that some members privately held evidence pointing the other way and said nothing at the meeting). Then a finding on fragility: the whole consensus evaporated the moment one genuinely new public signal arrived, which the report treats as evidence of how little the agreement had ever been resting on. Recommendations should be the ones such a report always makes — publish everyone's private assessment before anyone speaks, decide simultaneously rather than in a queue, appoint somebody whose actual job is to disagree — plus a closing recommendation that the fund has commissioned a further report into the incidence of cascades in report-commissioning.

Call it around 900 words, with the usual headings, a findings section, and an appendix table listing, for each committee member, what they privately believed and how they voted, so the mechanism is visible without anyone in the document ever remarking on it. No real firms or real people, obviously, and no citations or reading lists — it's satire, not a literature review — though if the report's author would naturally wave at "the standard sequential-learning model" in passing, that's fine. The tone should stay flat and no-blame throughout; I want a reader to finish it thinking each individual step was reasonable and the aggregate was still empty, rather than thinking the committee was a bunch of idiots. Also: British English please, -ise endings and all that.
