You are a philosopher of economics drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice and welfare. The students have already had the standard material on revealed preference, so any question they can answer by defining the term or reciting the ordinal-representation result is wasted. I need a question that forces them onto a gap the textbook gloss papers over: whether choice reveals preference in an evidential sense or constitutes it, and whether a utility function built out of choices can carry any welfare weight at all.

Think carefully about where the crux lies, then write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

Use two cases as the spine. First, call a choice pattern "coherent but unowned" when an agent's choices over a fixed option set are stable, complete, and transitive and satisfy the usual consistency conditions, yet the agent reports no preference, says they pick for no reason, and would not endorse the pattern as expressive of what they care about. Second, a firm whose purchasing decisions are likewise stable and transitive, where no mental state is plausibly attributed to any person. The question should require students to say what an evidential reading, a constitutive or realist reading, and an interpretivist reading must each say about both cases; whether menu-dependence, i.e. choice varying with what else is on offer, threatens the constitutive reading in particular; and what survives of the inference from choice-based utility to welfare once the two readings are separated.

The marking scheme should total 25, name the moves a top answer must make and the ones that distinguish it from a merely competent one, and flag the two or three errors that should cost marks — for instance treating transitivity as though it settled the metaphysical question, or sliding from ordinal representation to interpersonal welfare comparison without noticing. The students answer from memory, so neither the question nor the model answer should lean on citations or reading lists.
