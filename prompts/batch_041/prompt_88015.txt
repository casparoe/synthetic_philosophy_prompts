I am writing a critique of futarchy -- the proposal that a polity vote on the ends it wants pursued and then use prediction markets to select the means -- and I want to pin down the philosophical commitments the proposal cannot do without.

Take a concrete version. Voters first choose, by ordinary majority vote, one of three candidate indices of national welfare (say GDP per capita, mean life satisfaction, or a composite of the two). Thereafter, every policy question opens two conditional markets: one on the index level that would obtain if policy A were adopted, one on the level if policy B were; the policy with the higher market estimate is implemented, and the contracts settle when the official statistics are published.

Three features of that design puzzle me. The index must be fixed before anyone knows which policies will arise, so goods that matter but are not indexed cannot be traded off inside the market. The traders are also the people the policy will affect, so their bets are not disinterested estimates. And a contract on the index under A can be settled only in the world where A is adopted, so its price depends on the probability that A is adopted, which in turn depends on the price -- the market does not straightforwardly isolate the counterfactual that the decision rule needs.

Reconstruct the theory of value and of collective decision this practice presupposes: what it assumes about the measurability and interpersonal comparability of welfare, about the separability of values from beliefs, and about whether prices can carry belief when the traders are interested parties. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined -- which rival theories of value would license the same machinery. Then say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a frankly pluralist or deontological one.

Open with a single sentence stating the central presupposition; everything after should defend it. No more than 700 words.
