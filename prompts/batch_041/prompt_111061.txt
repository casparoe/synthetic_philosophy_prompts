I'm in a third-year seminar on Hume and we just did the section on miracles in the first Enquiry, and I've been thinking about it way too much. Everyone in class wanted to fight about whether the arguement is question-begging or whether he really means you should never believe any report of a miracle, and I think the actually interesting part gets lost, which is the shape of the thing. He defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, and then the whole move is that our evidence for the law and our evidence for the violation are the same kind of evidence, and the law wins on weight of numbers every time — so the most we can ever get to is that the report is probably false, or that it got corrupted somewhere along the way. And then in the second part it's all about how these reports actually reach us: through people who badly want them to be true, through copies of copies, so that even if something really did happen, by the time it gets to you it's already been worn down to nothing.

So here's what I want. Write me a poem that does the argument in its form instead of explaining it. The constraint I came up with: pick a strict, audible form — rhymed quatrains, a tight meter, a refrain, whatever — but it has to be regular enough that a reader can feel the pattern in their body, and then break it exactly once. One line, or one rhyme, or one foot somewhere in the middle that doesn't obey. That broken place is the miracle. The reader should hit it and feel the violation the way Hume says we feel a violation of a law of nature. And this is the important part: the poem must not tell us whether it's a real exception or a mistake in the copying — a misprint, a slip of the hand, a mishearing. It has to stay undecided, because that's the actual point: not that nothing ever happens, but that we can never be in a position to know that it did. Don't break the form anywhere else and don't fix it later — the rest of the poem has to be airtight, and the break has to be the only thing out of joint.

For a setting I keep picturing a printed tide table or an almanac, some boring document where regularity is the entire point, with one entry amended in pencil by a hand you can't identify — but that's just where my head went, do something else if it works better, as long as the regularity is concrete and the single break is the event. 16 to 24 lines. And please don't let it lecture: no name-dropping Hume, no abstract nouns like testimony or probability, no final couplet that explains the trick. Someone who's never read the Enquiry should just feel a form that's perfect except for one place, and feel weird about that place; someone who has read it should recognize the whole argument without being told what to look for.
