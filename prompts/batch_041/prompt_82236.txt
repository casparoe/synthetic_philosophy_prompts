I've spent the last month in a running argument with a friend about patriotism — it started in a reading group that's been working through political philosophy, and it's now at the point where we're just trading cases at each other — so I'd like to do this properly. I'll lay out my gut verdicts on a set of cases and ask you what principle, if any, they add up to. I'm not asking for a survey of what has been written on the subject; I know roughly where the camps are, and I'll add whatever references I need if I ever write this up, so don't bother with citations. What I want is a principle stated precisely enough that I can tell what it forbids and what it permits, a statement of what it predicts for cases I haven't considered, and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd rather you overturn one verdict than add a clause for every case; I distrust any principle that grows an epicycle per example.

Here are the cases and how I actually react to them. (1) My grandmother's neighbor, who has lived in the same valley her whole life, knows its trees and its dishes, and feels a pang when she's away too long, with no opinion at all about whether the country is better than others: not a virtue exactly, but plainly not a vice — it looks like a form of love and I can't find anything wrong with it. (2) A man who says he loves his country because it is the greatest and morally superior nation on earth: a vice, and the superiority claim is what does it. (3) The same man with the superiority clause deleted — I love it because it's mine, the way I love my own family — strikes me as fine. (4) Someone who, when his country does something atrocious, feels shame, says so publicly, and is called a traitor by his neighbors: I think he is more patriotic than they are. (5) Someone who defends the atrocity to foreigners on the grounds that it's his country and that settles it: a vice, and I'm inclined to say it isn't patriotism at all but its counterfeit. (6) A woman who would rather a stranger's child die than a countryman's, other things equal: monstrous. But a mother who would rather a stranger's child die than her own: not monstrous, or at least I can't condemn it in the same breath. I can't yet say what distinguishes the two, and I suspect that's the crux. (7) Someone who emigrates, takes a new citizenship, and never thinks about the old country again: no vice. (8) Someone who loves a homeland he has never lived in and knows only from his parents' stories: I want to say the love has no proper object — he is in love with an idea rather than a country — and that this is a mistake without being a vice. (9) The one that really bothers me: I find it merely silly when someone feels proud of a national team's victory, or of a war his country won, since he did nothing; but I find it apt, even admirable, when someone feels ashamed of his country's atrocity, though he did nothing there either. Same absence of personal credit, opposite verdicts, and I can't currently justify the asymmetry.

The principle I'd tentatively offer is this: patriotism is the disposition to treat one's country as something one belongs to and is answerable for, rather than as something one owns or a team one backs; it is a virtue exactly to the extent that it is the answerable-for kind of love, and it licenses no partiality beyond what that relationship itself supports. Test that against the list. I expect it survives (1) through (5) and (7), and that it either has to be complicated or has to surrender a verdict at (6) and (9). If you can get (6) and (9) with one added clause, tell me what it is and what it costs me elsewhere; if you can't, tell me which verdict to abandon and why I should stop trusting my gut about it. Please also say what your principle predicts about three cases I haven't yet put to myself: a person who breaks a domestic law he thinks unjust in order to help a compatriot he has never met; a person who loves his country's ideals and constitution while despising most of the people in it; and a person whose loyalty attaches to a city or a region rather than to the nation-state that contains it. I'll also accept an answer of the form that patriotism isn't in the virtue-and-vice business at all, but a taste like loving a cuisine, if you think the cases support it — but then I want to know what the things I called vices actually are.
