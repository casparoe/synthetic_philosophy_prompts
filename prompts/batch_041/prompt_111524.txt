I am a doctoral student in political theory and need a two-page prospectus for one chapter of my dissertation, on the problem of dirty hands. My half-formed idea is this. The canonical cases in the literature are single-agent and extreme: one officeholder, one decision, a grave and imminent harm that only a wrong act can avert. But much of the moral damage that politics actually does is diffuse. A long chain of officials each performs a step that is individually defensible, or at least not clearly wrong, and the steps jointly produce a serious wrong that no participant ever chose as the lesser evil, and that some participants would have refused had they foreseen it. Call this the diffuse case. I want to know whether the dirty-hands framework survives it, and specifically whether the residue that makes hands dirty on the standard account, the guilt or stain that calls for expiation, can attach to any individual in the diffuse case, or only to the institution, or to no one at all. A second hunch I want the prospectus to test is that the literature runs together two things that can come apart: the wrongness of the act done, and the taint borne by the one who did it. The diffuse case, I suspect, separates them.
