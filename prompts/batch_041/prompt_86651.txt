I am preparing a graduate seminar on Donald Davidson's philosophy of language for students who mostly work on contemporary machine learning, and I keep running into a question I cannot answer to my own satisfaction: what would Davidson, who died in 2003, before any of this existed, have said about large language models? My students are certain his framework settles the matter, but they are certain in opposite directions. One camp argues that his holism about meaning and his insistence that a theory of meaning be constructible from the outside entail that a system with sufficiently rich internal structure is interpretable, and that a text-only model, being nothing but structure, is in fact the purest case of something awaiting radical interpretation. The other camp argues that his insistence on a shared world, and on triangulation between two creatures who both interact with that world and with each other, entails that a model whose only input is text and whose only output is text means nothing at all, that its utterances are not even false but mere noise, and that charity cannot get a grip on it because there is no world for it to be right or wrong about. My suspicion is that both camps are taking one strand of his work as the whole and quietly ignoring the rest. I would like you to write the piece of intellectual history I cannot write myself: an argued reconstruction of what Davidson's position would have been on whether a large language model — trained on a text corpus, conversing with users in text, and perhaps later given cameras and actuators — can be said to mean anything, to hold beliefs, or to be interpretable in his technical sense.

The reconstruction should proceed by identifying the commitments that actually constrain the answer and then showing how each one bears on the case. At minimum I want you to work through the principle of charity as a constitutive constraint rather than a courtesy; the holism of the mental, and what it implies about how much structure a system must have before interpretation can even begin; radical interpretation as a procedure one performs on a speaker, and what it would mean to perform it on a model whose dispositions to respond we can in principle survey exhaustively; the triangulation argument, and whether a text-only model, or even a multimodal one, could occupy the third vertex; his rejection of the scheme and content dualism, which I take to block the tempting move of saying the model has a conceptual scheme of its own; his claim that thought presupposes language, which my students read as cutting in favor of the model and which I suspect cuts differently once you ask what it takes for a language to be a language; and the swampman case, which is the closest thing he ever wrote to this problem. Tell me whether he would treat the swampman as the right analogy — a replica with no causal history, suddenly behaving as if it had one — or as a misleading analogy, since the swampman at least had a body, eyes, and appetites, whereas the model has none of these and receives only our words. Then take a position on the sharpest version of the question: could an interpreter, applying charity to the model's outputs, arrive at a truth theory for its language, and if so, would Davidson say that theory is a theory of meaning for the model or only a theory of what we are doing when we talk to it? Would he distinguish between a model that only reads text and one that sees, moves, and suffers consequences, and if he would, is the distinction one of degree or of kind?

The prediction should be a prediction, not a survey of the literature, and it should be willing to say where Davidson would have been uncertain, where he would have said the question was not yet well posed, and where he might have had to revise or abandon one of his own commitments in order to answer it. That last point matters most to me: if he wanted to say that such a system understands, which of his commitments would have to go, and if he wanted to say it does not, which of them would he have to lean on harder than he ever did in print? I would also like a short final section on what would count as evidence that your reconstruction is right rather than merely ingenious — the kind of argument we would expect him to have made, or the kind of concession he would have refused to make — so that my students can see the difference between predicting a philosopher and inventing one.
