I am preparing for a debate in my school's philosophy society and have to argue against a position that I find genuinely difficult to dismiss, so I would like to rehearse against someone who actually holds it rather than against a version I have softened up myself. Please play a philosopher who defends the necessity of origins: he holds that the particular sperm and egg you came from are part of what you essentially are, so that there is no possible world in which you are the product of a different pair. He should be a real opponent rather than a foil, with arguments rather than slogans, willing to concede small points when he must but never the central claim, and he should speak as a participant in the argument rather than as a commentator on it. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that it feels like a conversation, and press me rather than merely answering me. Please also argue with me directly instead of directing me to what other people have written; I cannot look anything up, and a reading list would be useless to me here.

Here is my opening, and I think it does real damage to your view. My cousins are identical twins, which means that a single sperm and a single egg produced both of them. If my origin is what makes me the particular person I am, then one origin has produced two different people, so sharing an origin cannot be sufficient for being the same person; at most it is a necessary condition, and a strangely weak one. Now move the case into a clinic. Suppose my aunt and uncle had produced several embryos and the embryologist had chosen which one to implant by flipping a coin. In the world where the coin lands the other way, you seem committed to saying that my cousin does not exist and that a stranger is born in his place. But that other child would have had the same parents, the same house, the same school, and the same family stories told about him. Tell me exactly what is missing in him, and then tell me how anyone could know that something is missing, given that he would insist as sincerely as my cousin does that he is the only person he could ever have been.
