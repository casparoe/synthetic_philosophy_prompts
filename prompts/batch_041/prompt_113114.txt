I have no philosophical training, but for several years I have kept a private list of my reactions to fakes, forgeries, and copies, and I have reached the point where I would like to know whether a single principle underlies them or whether I am simply inconsistent. I will set out the cases and my verdicts as plainly as I can.

First: a painting that is a perfect, stroke-for-stroke copy of a known masterpiece, offered as the original and later exposed as a forgery. I continue to think it is a beautiful painting, and I do not believe the beauty left the canvas when the truth came out; yet I also believe it is worth far less as a work of art once exposed — not merely worth less money, but less as art. I cannot reconcile those two thoughts.

Second: a replica made and labelled openly as a replica, on display in a museum. I have no objection to it, and I think a visitor can appreciate it fully. There is no deception, and I feel no loss.

Third: a forger who paints a wholly new composition in an old master's manner — a new Vermeer that never existed — and signs the old master's name to it. Once the forgery is exposed, I find I like the picture no less, and in fact more, because someone invented it rather than copied it.

Fourth: two texts that are word for word identical, one written by a person who lived through the events it narrates, the other written long afterwards by a novelist who imagined them (I once read a story built on precisely this conceit). I think the later text is the richer of the two, not the poorer.

Fifth: a painting shown as an old master whose visible surface is mostly modern restoration — say seventy per cent of what one sees is repainting. I feel cheated, and yet I still think this object is more valuable than a flawless modern replica would be, even though most of what I am looking at is recent paint.

Sixth: a photograph I copy onto my computer, byte for byte identical. I think the copy is fully as good as the original file, and that nothing whatever is lost.

I notice that I seem to care about an object's history in some of these cases (the restored painting, the exposed forgery) and not in others (the digital copy); and that I seem to care about appearance in some (the forgery remains beautiful) and not in others (the exposed forgery is worth less as art). I would like you to tell me whether one principle can cover all six, or whether I must give something up.

Two clarifications, so that you do not answer the wrong question. I am not asking about the law of forgery or about fraud; I understand perfectly well why selling a fake as genuine is a wrong done to a buyer. My question is about the value of the thing itself — as art, or as the object it is — once the deception is out in the open and no one is being misled. Nor am I asking whether forgeries should be destroyed or hidden away; I am asking what I should think they are worth.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best fits my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my six verdicts it is there to save. Then say what your principle predicts about cases I have not mentioned — for instance a musical performance versus the score it realizes, a translation of a poem, or a historic building rebuilt to the original plans after a fire. Finally, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot keep, and what I would have to believe instead if I gave that verdict up. If it turns out that two different principles each save five of the six, I would rather you say so and explain what separates them than force a single tidy answer.
