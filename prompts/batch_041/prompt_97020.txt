I have been trying to write a short piece on whether the basic axioms of arithmetic might be inconsistent, and I have ended up with an argument that I cannot refute and whose conclusion I do not believe. I would like you to locate the weak point.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A derivation guarantees the truth of its conclusion only if the axioms it starts from are consistent. From inconsistent axioms every sentence follows, so a derivation from them establishes nothing in particular; it would equally establish the negation of its conclusion.

(2) Our basic axioms — those of arithmetic, or of set theory — are strong enough to encode claims about what those axioms themselves do and do not prove.

(3) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no consistent system of that strength proves its own consistency. There is a sting in this. An inconsistent system does prove its own consistency, since it proves everything; so if a proof of the consistency of arithmetic were ever found within arithmetic, the correct response would be to conclude that arithmetic is inconsistent.

(4) So if our axioms are consistent, their consistency cannot be proved from them.

(5) Nor can we break the circle by appealing to something stronger. Set theory does prove the consistency of arithmetic, by exhibiting a model of it; but set theory falls under the same theorem, so its own consistency can be proved only from something stronger still, and so on up. Every such chain terminates in a system whose consistency we have merely assumed.

(6) Hence there is no proof, at any level, of the consistency of the mathematics we actually use. It rests, at bottom, on an assumption that cannot be proved.

(7) What cannot be proved cannot be known. In mathematics, at any rate, knowledge just is proof.

(8) So we cannot know that our axioms are consistent.

(9) So we cannot exclude the possibility that they are inconsistent. Note that this is not a doubt about whether the axioms are true of some intended subject matter; it is a doubt about whether they are jointly satisfiable at all. And it is not a philosopher's fancy; it is a live possibility for us, sitting where we sit.

(10) If it is a live possibility that the axioms are inconsistent, then every theorem derived from them may be false, and we have no way of telling which theorems are safe.

(11) Therefore no theorem of mathematics is known — not the infinity of the primes, not that 2 + 2 = 4.

(12) Nor can the argument be quarantined from itself, since it is mathematics that does the quarrelling. The incompleteness theorem is itself proved from axioms of the same kind, and the standard of knowledge laid down in (7) applies to it as much as to anything else. By the argument's own lights its central premise is not known, and so the argument for (11) is not known either.

Something has gone wrong, since (11) and (12) cannot both be the sober truth. Which step should I give up? For the step you nominate, explain why it looked fine, what rejecting it commits me to, and whether the cost is one a working mathematician should be willing to pay or whether it leaves mathematics worse off than the argument did. I would like to keep (3) — the theorem is not in dispute — and I would like to avoid (11); I have no particular attachment to the rest.
