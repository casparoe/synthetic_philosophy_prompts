For about a year now a group of us — none of us with any philosophical training, mostly people who read too much and argue over dinner — have been going around in circles on a question I originally took to be a joke: is self-deception even possible? The shape of the dispute, as we've pieced it together, is roughly this. On one side is what I'd call the literal reading: self-deception is exactly what the name says — one and the same person is both the deceiver and the deceived, deliberately arranging not to see something they already know. The objection is immediate and, to me, close to devastating: to hide a truth from yourself you would have to know it, and if you know it you aren't deceived, so the literal reading seems to require a contradiction. On the other side are those who say the paradox is an artifact of taking the phrase too seriously: what we call self-deception is just ordinary motivated reasoning — wishful thinking, biased evidence-gathering, selective attention — producing a belief the person would like to be true, with no intention to deceive anyone and no hidden knower required. But that side seems to me to lose the very thing we wanted explained: the sense that the person is somehow complicit, that they are doing something, that there is a real difference between a man who has merely been misinformed and a man who has spent five years carefully not asking a particular question, whose friends all notice he never leaves his wife alone with one particular colleague and never quite says why. A third position keeps surfacing in our conversations — that the mind is genuinely divided, so that one part of it can deceive another — but I can never tell whether this explains anything or merely installs a little person inside the head and calls the problem solved.

What I want from you is not a verdict about which side is right but a diagnosis of why the argument never moves. My own hunch, offered as a hunch, is that at least part of the disagreement is about the word "deception" rather than about the mind: one camp is asking what actually goes on inside a person, and the other is asking what our ordinary concept of deception demands before the label applies, and those two questions can both be answered without either answer touching the other. But I don't know whether that is the whole story, or whether there is a substantive disagreement underneath about how the mind is built — whether it can contain genuinely separated sub-agents or only a single more or less unified stream of belief and desire — and I suspect the two of you who read this will disagree about that too.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal, is it about the architecture of the mind, are the sides answering different questions under one heading, or is there a hidden empirical or conceptual question that neither side has actually engaged? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a decision to stop using the word "deception" altogether and describe the phenomenon some other way, or a demonstration that no progress is available because the question is simply ill-posed.
