I am a secondary-school student, and I am writing an extended essay for my school's philosophy society. Before I commit to a draft, I would like an audit of the position I have arrived at: which of the commitments below genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, how serious each real tension is, and what the cheapest repair would be in each case. I am not asking for an introduction to the topic; I am asking whether the package hangs together.

Here is where I have landed.

1. Consequentialism. The right action is the one with the best consequences, and the value of an action is its expected value, taken over all of its consequences, near and distant alike.

2. Temporal impartiality. The interests of people who will live in the distant future count for as much as the interests of people alive now. I do not discount a unit of wellbeing merely because it lies far ahead in time.

3. A distinction I have drawn for myself between two kinds of ignorance. Ordinary uncertainty is the case in which there is a fact of the matter about the probabilities and better information would help: I do not know which card will be drawn, but the deck has a determinate composition. Structural cluelessness is the case in which the outcome space is open-ended, so that no amount of information-gathering would deliver a well-defined probability distribution, because the relevant causal chains run through indefinitely many contingencies and through people who have not yet been born. I claim that our knowledge of the far-future consequences of ordinary actions is structurally clueless rather than merely uncertain. I have seen this worry called cluelessness.

4. Practice. Because of 3, in deciding what to do I compare actions only by the consequences I can reasonably foresee and quantify. Concretely, I donate to the global-health charity I judge most cost-effective and I do not agonize over the speculative far-future effects of that donation.

5. The restriction in 4 is epistemic, not axiological. I do not believe unforeseeable consequences matter less; I believe they cannot guide choice.

6. This has pushed me toward rule-consequentialism. Since I cannot assess the far-future consequences of individual acts, I should act on rules whose general acceptance has consequences I can foresee and evaluate.

7. Yet I also hold that rules have no independent authority: a rule is justified only by its consequences. And if the consequences of a single act are opaque to me, the consequences of a rule's general acceptance look at least as opaque, since a rule ramifies through more of the future rather than less.

8. Finally, I think that when an action's effects are unknowable in the sense of 3, the agent is not blameworthy for choosing badly. Praise and blame track what the agent could have known; rightness tracks which action actually had the best consequences.

What I want back is an audit rather than a lecture. First, tell me which pairs or clusters are genuinely in tension and rank them by severity, distinguishing the ones that are only apparently in tension and explaining what makes the appearance dissolve. Second, for each genuine tension, say precisely which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere. Third, tell me whether the distinction in 3 does the work I need it to do, or whether it quietly undermines 1 by making expected value undefined and therefore unusable as a criterion. Fourth, tell me whether 4 and 8 can be squared with the objective reading of rightness that 1 and 2 seem to require, or whether I am covertly switching to a standard of what the agent could reasonably have believed. Finally, if one single revision would clean up the most trouble at the least cost, say which one it is, and tell me whether any version of this package can keep 1 and 2 intact.
