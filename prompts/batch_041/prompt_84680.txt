Every June my college asks an alum to give the commencement adress, and this year it's me, which is my own fault for answering the phone. Small liberal arts college in the midwest, about 1400 students, and three years ago it abolished latin honors and stopped reading out the individual prizes at graduation — no more summa, no more the So-and-So Prize in Economics goes to. A chunk of the parents under the tent are still furious about it, the president who pushed it through will be sitting about four feet behind me on the stage, one of the trustees who resigned over it will be in the third row, and maybe a third of the faculty think it's the best thing the place has done in twenty years. Also my mother and two aunts are flying in from Norway for it, which is its own kind of pressure. I won the big prize myself, back when it existed, which is probably why they asked me — so I can't pretend it didn't matter to me, and I can't pretend I earned it in some perfectly clean way either. I'd like the speech to be honest about that without turning into confession.

I grew up in Norway and moved here at nineteen, so there's two instincts fighting in my gut about all of this, and I want the speech to actually use that instead of smoothing it over. What I keep circling is that there's alot of borrowed vocabulary on both sides. When the parents say the college is punishing excellence, someone nearby mutters Janteloven — the Law of Jante, from Aksel Sandemose's 1933 satirical novel about a little Danish town where the rules all run don't imagine you're anyone special, don't imagine you're better than us, don't imagine you're good at anything, plus an eleventh rule that amounts to a threat that they know things about you. Which is worth saying out loud, because Jante isn't a portrait of Scandinavian virtue, it's a satirist's portrait of a place where everyone knows everyone else's business — and a small college is exactly that kind of place, which is part of why I don't think the faculty are simply being envious. Meanwhile the other side reaches for tall poppies, and I went down a rabbit hole on that phrase and it turns out it isn't about envy at all: the original story is a tyrant who answers a messenger by walking through a garden and lopping the heads off the tallest poppies, which means kill the leading men of the city — Livy, I'm fairly sure, and there's an earlier version in Herodotus with a field of wheat instead. So the one doing the cutting in the original is the guy at the top, not the jealous neighbours, which cuts against both sides' story about who's holding the shears. I want that in the speech, and I don't want it delivered like a footnote.

My half-formed idea for landing it is a distinction between praising what someone did and rating what someone is — that an award which ranks persons is a different animal from one that celebrates a piece of work, and that maybe the college threw out both when it only needed to throw out one. Develop that if it's any good, replace it if it isn't. Around 1200 words, nine minutes or so, in my voice (a professor who's funny but not a comedian), and it has to be sayable with the president on stage and the angry trustee in the third row without either of them feeling ambushed — and it can't be both-sides mush, because the graduates can smell that a mile away. Don't quote anybody: no famous commencement lines, no poetry, no reciting Sandemose at me, I'll add quotes myself if I decide I want them, and no reading list. And please don't let it drift into a TED talk about excellence or a sermon about humility. They've had both, and they're hungover.
