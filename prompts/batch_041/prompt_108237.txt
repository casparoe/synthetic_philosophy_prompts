I've been trying to get my head round an argument a mate gave me and it seems to prove way too much. Take a concrete unknown truth: there's a letter in a locked drawer that nobody has read, and it says the cat is on the mat. Let p be that claim. The argument goes:

(1) Every truth is knowable in principle.
(2) The conjunction that p is true and nobody knows p is a truth — p is true, and nobody has read the letter.
(3) So that conjunction is knowable in principle.
(4) If it's knowable, it's possible for someone to know the whole conjunction; and knowing a conjunction means knowing each part.
(5) Knowledge is factive: knowing that nobody knows p entails that nobody knows p; knowing p entails that someone knows p.
(6) So the possible knower both knows p and does not know p — contradiction.
(7) Therefore the conjunction is not knowable.
(8) But it is true, so either (1) is false or there are no unknown truths at all.

That last bit is obviously mad. I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if I can, but I want to know the price. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what each of the main escape routes costs — restricting the knowability principle, giving up factivity, tinkering with the logic, or saying knowledge doesn't distribute over conjunctions the way the argument needs. If the argument is valid and something plausible just has to go, say which and why. Please answer in British English.
