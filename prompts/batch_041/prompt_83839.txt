Ok so I'm a science journalist and I've been doing alot of reporting on AI safety stuff, and there's one idea that keeps coming up in interviews and I genuinely can't tell if it's profound or incoherent. The idea is this (people call it mechanistic anomaly detection, though I'm not sure the name has settled): you train a model, you figure out and validate the mechanism it's running, and then later, when it does something you didn't expect, you ask whether that output came from the same mechanism as before. Same mechanism, fine. Different mechanism, alarm bell. That's the pitch.

The whole thing obviously rests on us being able to say when two systems — or the same system at two different points — are running the same mechanism, and every time I press people on that I get hand-waving. One researcher I talked to did offer a criterion I want you to evaluate: two systems implement the same mechanism iff there's a mapping between their parts such that every intervention you could make on one has a counterpart intervention on the other, and the downstream effects match. An isomorphism of counterfactual profiles, basically.

So: give me your credence that there's a single, interest-independent fact of the matter about whether two systems run the same mechanism — at least in the cases that matter here (one model at two checkpoints, a model and its distilled or fine-tuned sibling, two runs of the same net). I'd put myself at about 0.35 and I suspect that's too low, but I don't know where the number should live. Don't just give me the number, break it up. My guess at the terms: (a) whether mechanism identity is fixed by causal structure alone or is always relative to our explanatory interests and our chosen grain of description; (b) conditional on (a) going your way, whether that counterfactual-isomorphism criterion is actually well-defined and computable; (c) conditional on that, whether it gives non-trivial verdicts on real systems rather than being satisfied by everything or by nothing; (d) whether sameness of mechanism is a matter of degree, in which case anomaly detection needs a threshold and the threshold isn't coming from the math. Number for each, one line of justification each, then how they combine into your total.

Then tell me which term you're least sure about and what would actually move it — is there a formal result or a kind of experiment that could settle (b), or is that the kind of thing that just gets argued about forever? And if the whole thing turns out to be interest-relative, does that kill the safety proposal or does it just become a judgment call that somebody has to own? Be blunt with me about which parts of this I should stop asking about.
