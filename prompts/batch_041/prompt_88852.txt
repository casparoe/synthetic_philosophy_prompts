I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have spent a long time arguing with friends about which things should not be for sale, and I have come to suspect that most of the disagreement is not about the cases — the same person who shrugs at a paid kidney donor recoils at a shop tripling the price of bottled water after a flood — but about what the objection actually is. I would like a decision tree that begins from the claim almost everyone grants, that some exchanges both parties freely agree to are nevertheless wrong, and branches until every path ends at a named position on what makes them wrong; phrase every branching question so that a person can answer it without knowing the name of the view it leads to, and cut the branches along these distinctions in this order: whether the wrongness requires harm to someone beyond the two parties; whether it requires the seller's alternatives to be bad enough that the agreement is not really free; whether pricing a thing destroys a value that exists only so long as the thing is not priced (I have been calling such goods 'price-fragile'); whether the damage, if there is any, falls on the seller, on the buyer, or on the rest of us who must live in a world where the thing has a price; and finally whether the objection is to the transaction itself or only to what the transaction expresses about the people in it. The leaves should include at least these positions: markets are acceptable whenever the parties consent and no third party is harmed; the real objection is coercion or desperation, not commodification; the real objection is that some sales entrench inequality and corrode shared institutions; the real objection is that some goods cannot be valued the way a price values them; the real objection is expressive, in that a price says something degrading about the person or the good; and the view that buying and selling are not symmetric, so that the buyer does something the seller does not. Use these cases, and no others, at the nodes: selling a kidney, a shop tripling the price of bottled water after a flood, selling one's vote, paying a stranger to attend a funeral and grieve convincingly, and paying someone to take one's place in the queue for a scarce medical treatment. Where two paths end at positions your questions cannot tell apart, say so at that node rather than pretending the tree separates them, and where a combination of answers matches no position anyone holds, say so and say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per branching question, in the order the tree asks them, each item giving the question, its answer options, and the named positions hanging off each answer, with no introduction and no conclusion.
