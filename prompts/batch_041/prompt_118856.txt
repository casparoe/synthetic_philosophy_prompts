I'm putting together a packet for a reading group and I need one more piece in it: the joint statement that two people who've been arguing about consciousness for years could actually both sign, if they sat down and did it in good faith instead of talking past each other. The two camps:

1) The epiphenomenalist. Experiences are real, they're caused by what the brain is doing, and they don't cause anything themselves — not the report, not the behavior, not other experiences. The hurt is along for the ride.

2) The other side, call them the causalist about experience. Experiences just are properties of the same brain/functional states that produce the reports, so calling them inert is either false or a bit of wordplay. On this view the hurt is part of what makes me say ow.

Neither of them is a dualist about the machinery — both accept that the physical story is complete and that some physical process does the reporting. The whole fight is over what work the experience itself does. And no citations please, no who-said-what, no lineage. I care about the claims, not the genealogy.

The thing I actually want them to stare at: if experiences don't cause anything, then my judgment that I'm having one, and the sentence I use to report it, weren't caused by it. So in what sense is that judgment or that sentence about the experience at all? Don't settle it from scratch — I want the two of them to write down exactly where they come apart on it, in wording each of them would put their own name to.

To keep them from just restating positions, make them work with these fixed inputs:

- A stipulation they both accept: the brain has a report channel (the machinery that turns internal states into utterances, button presses, and so on) and an experience channel (whatever it is that makes a state felt). Both channels are fully realized in the brain. The only question is what relation they stand in.

- A case they both have to run: a surgeon has two dials. One changes only the felt character of what the subject is experiencing — red becomes green — and leaves the report channel byte-for-byte identical. The other changes only the report channel, so she reports green instead of red, and leaves the felt character untouched. Neither dial does anything else. Each side should say in advance what the subject does with the first dial and with the second. And they should say whether they even agree that the two dials describe two different interventions — my hunch is that alot of their fight is hiding in that question, not in the dials.

- A second case: a subject is trained to name what she's experiencing and gets it right over and over, including on trials where she has no other way to know. Each side has to explain why that accuracy is possible on its own view, and — harder — say whether the accuracy is accuracy about the experiences or only about the states underneath them.

Deliverable:

- A numbered list of agreed claims first. Real ones, not filler like we both think brains matter. I want the load-bearing ones: about what's stipulated, about what would count as explaining something, about what the two cases do and don't show, about the reliability of reports.

- Then a table, one row per remaining disagreement, with these columns: the disagreement in neutral wording; the epiphenomenalist's own version of their side; the causalist's own version of their side; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; the case or argument both agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, written down before anyone looks. My guess is that one crux is whether common-cause reliability is enough for a state to be about anything, and another is whether cause here means difference-making or producing — but you decide.

- Then two short paragraphs. First: which disagreements are really verbal (same facts, different word for cause or for explanation) and which are genuine. Second: what, if anything, both of them agree would actually change their minds. If some part of the fight can't be turned into a crux that a case could bear on, say so instead of manufacturing one.
