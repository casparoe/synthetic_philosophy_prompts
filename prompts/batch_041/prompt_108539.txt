I am a novelist, and I am at work on a comic novel set in a small government office whose entire function is to adjudicate citizens' claims that their subjective experience has changed. For the back matter I need one in-world document, and I would like you to write it: a deadpan internal guidance note, of roughly 750 words, in numbered paragraphs, under a title along the lines of Determining Whether a Subject's Qualia Have Changed: Guidance for Case Officers. The voice should be that of a middle-ranking civil servant — sincere, mildly educated in philosophy, entirely unaware that he is funny, and never once winking at the reader.

Here is the furniture, so that we are working from the same set. Qualia, in the traditional sense, are supposed to be ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible in consciousness. The puzzle I want the note to grapple with is the coffee-taster case: two drinkers — call them Chase and Sanborn — who used to enjoy coffee and no longer do, whose histories, discriminatory powers and present behaviour are indistinguishable, and who describe what is in the cup in the same terms, yet who differ in their introspective verdict. One of them says the taste itself has changed; the other says the taste is unchanged and that he is what has changed. Nothing they do or say about the coffee separates them.

I want the note to try, in good faith and in the idiom of a procedural manual, to settle cases of that kind. The comedy has to come from the machinery and not from the narrator's tone: each of the four marks above should turn into an obstacle to some procedure the office has actually adopted. I am thinking of a test that turns out to be unfalsifiable, a form with a field no one can fill, a calibration protocol that presupposes precisely what it was meant to establish, a hearing at which the two parties' testimony cannot be told apart, and an evidentiary rule that quietly makes the subject's own report both the only admissible evidence and the very thing to be proved. Please include at least one paragraph in which the office notices that a procedure proves nothing and retains it anyway on grounds of its own, and at least one in which the fallback is simply to certify whatever the subject asserts — treated, without irony, as an interim measure pending a better test. It must not end in the verdict that qualia do not exist. The whole joke depends on the office being a sincere qualia realist that can get no purchase on the things it is charged with regulating, and on its never suspecting that the trouble lies with the things rather than with the office.

Two requests on delivery. First, a covering note of no more than a paragraph on where the document takes liberties with the philosophical material, or leans on a contested reading of it, so that I know what I am putting in the back of the book. Second, an honest one: if you judge the premise to be self-undermining in a way that deadens the satire rather than sharpening it — if an agency charged with regulating experience is incoherent in a manner that leaves no comedy to be had — then say so plainly and explain why, rather than writing the document anyway.
