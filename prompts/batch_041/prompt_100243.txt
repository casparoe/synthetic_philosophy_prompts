I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and over the past year I have settled into a fairly uncompromising version of the gene's-eye view in the units-of-selection debate. My position is this: cumulative selection requires a replicator that is copied with high fidelity; genes are the only entities that meet that bar; and any description of selection as acting on individuals or groups is, at best, a convenient accounting shorthand for gene-level processes and, at worst, a category mistake. I want to know what would count against this — and I mean the defeaters that ought genuinely to move a committed gene-selectionist, not the stock objections I have already absorbed. Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones that would force a real revision of the view from the ones it can absorb with an epicycle, and be explicit about which of them a gene's-eye theorist would themselves concede as threats rather than wave away as a critic's rhetoric.

I would particularly like you to work through three things, and to say in each case whether the position survives intact, survives only in a weakened form, or has to be abandoned.

First, a stress case I keep returning to. Imagine a lineage in which a trait is inherited with high fidelity through a stably copied non-genetic mark — say, a chromatin state that is replicated along with the DNA and transmitted across many generations — where the mark varies, is heritable, affects fitness, and can spread in a population even when it lowers the genetic fitness of the individuals that carry it. Does this falsify the gene's-eye view, or does the view simply redescribe the mark as an extended phenotype of the underlying genes? If the redescription is available, is it substantive or merely verbal, and what would settle the difference?

Second, a case about the unit itself. Molecular biology suggests that what is transmitted is not cleanly individuated genes but stretches of sequence that may be read in more than one frame, spliced into different products, or shared between overlapping units of function. If the unit of transmission and the unit of function come apart in this way, what exactly is the gene that is supposed to serve as the replicator? Does the gene's-eye view need a stable, countable gene to do its work, and if so, can it have one?

Third, the distinction I suspect does all the load-bearing work in my position: between the gene as the unit of selection and the gene as the unit of bookkeeping. I worry that my view survives only by sliding between the claim that the gene is what is really selected and the weaker claim that the gene is merely the cleanest ledger in which to record selection that is, in itself, level-neutral. Tell me whether that slide is a defect, whether it can be repaired, and whether a version of the view that refuses the stronger claim is still recognizably the gene's-eye view.

Finally, step back and give me an honest verdict: is the position as I have stated it falsifiable at all, or has it been qualified into something that predicts nothing its rivals do not? If it is well insulated, is that a problem for a position of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a view whose main work is conceptual clarification? I would rather be told that my commitment is unfalsifiable than be handed another round of objections I already know how to answer.
