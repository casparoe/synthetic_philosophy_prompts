Alright, here's a position I've argued myself into and I want you to try hard to break it, because I cant tell anymore if it's true or just unfalsifiable. I think the conditional analysis of dispositions is basically correct and that the whole finks-and-masks industry has been a very long detour. The analysis as I'd state it: 'x is disposed to M when S' is true just in case x would M if S, provided nothing interferes. A fink is a case where the stimulus itself destroys the disposition (the stock example is a glass that, because it's struck, becomes un-fragile right before it would break); a mask is a case where the disposition is still there but its manifestation is blocked by something extrinsic (the glass wrapped in protective foam); a mimic is something that would produce the M-ing without having the disposition at all. There are also antidotes and shadowers and whatever else people have coined. My claim is that every one of these either smuggles in the conclusion, or gets absorbed by a ceteris paribus clause that's doing honest work rather than cheating.

So: what would count against this? I don't want the standard volley, I've heard it. I want the considerations that should actually move someone who already knows the standard objections -- cases, arguments, intuitions, formal results -- sorted by how decisive they'd really be. Which of them would a stubborn conditionalist like me have to concede as a real cost, and which am I entitled to wave off? And what does my view predict that a non-reductive dispositionalist about powers doesn't predict, or vice versa -- where do the two actually come apart empirically or in the cases?

Two things I'd especially like you to go at. First, I've been trying to cook up a case that even a maximally generous ceteris paribus clause can't save -- something where the interfering condition isn't seperate from the disposition or the stimulus but is the disposition itself, e.g. a thing that would M if S, except that being in S makes it lose the very feature that grounds M, and yet the counterfactual 'it would M if S' still comes out true for boring reasons about nearby possible worlds. If I can't build such a case, say so and tell me why. Second, dispositions that are never and could never be manifested -- do they press on the counterfactual approach in a way that finks and masks don't, or do they just fall out of the semantics?

And be straight with me about insulation. If every counterexample is one I can answer with 'ah, that's just a mask,' is my position still earning its keep, or have I just defined interference so that it can't lose? If it's insulated, tell me whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or just the price of any ceteris paribus theory. Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me the single consideration you'd bet on if you had to move me off it.
