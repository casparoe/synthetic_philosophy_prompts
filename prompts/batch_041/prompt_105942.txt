I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a manuscript where I can no longer afford to be casual about a distinction the whole book rests on. My protagonist is a woman of generous temper who is never once, across three hundred pages, placed in a position to give anything away. The novel requires that she be generous — that the judgment be true of her and not merely a fondness of mine — while granting that nothing she does shows it. A friend who teaches philosophy tells me I am asking about dispositions, that the problems I keep stumbling into have been given names in the literature (finks, masks, mimics), and that the debate has largely concerned what a dispositional property is rather than whether my sentence about her is true.

Before I read any further into that literature, I want its specification. Set out the desiderata any adequate theory of dispositional properties must meet — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it cannot contradict — each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference.

The cases I need it to handle are these. First the ordinary ones: the glass that is fragile before it is ever struck, the salt soluble in water it will never meet. Then the interferences, and here I will use my manuscript's own instances, since they are the ones I care about. (a) A man of steady courage who, at the moment danger actually arrives, undergoes a physical change that makes him timid — the arrival of the very circumstance that would have displayed the trait abolishes it. (b) A woman whose kindness is intact but whose circumstances are such that it can never issue in an act; nothing in her is altered, only the world around her. (c) Two characters who behave identically throughout, one cruel by temper and one merely obedient. (d) My protagonist herself, whose trait is never exercised because no occasion ever arises. Tell me what a theory must say about each, and why the natural reading of a disposition ascription — that a thing is disposed to a manifestation just in case it would manifest it were the trigger present — cannot survive (a) and (b).

Then the further requirements, with the reason behind each. Whether the theory must treat a disposition as intrinsic to its bearer even though no manifestation is possible without the cooperation of the surroundings, and whether it can. Whether it must distinguish a disposition from its categorical basis, and what it must then say about which of the two does the causal work. What makes the associated counterfactual true, and whether that can be said without circularity. Whether traits that show themselves in many different behaviours, and in degrees, must be accommodated. And what the theory owes my protagonist: it must be able to call her generous although nothing in the world ever called it out, and it must not make that truth depend on how the world happens to be arranged.

Flag the pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I would rather have one such conflict stated precisely than a long list of uncontroversial requirements — and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and which items on your list are not requirements at all but the prejudices of a particular camp. Close with two or three lines on each of the broad families of view (the reduction of dispositions to their categorical bases, irreducible powers, and any conditional or regularity account), saying only which requirement each one fails. The list is what I need; the scorecard can be brief.
