I've been asked to write a short statement of my position for a graduate seminar on suffering and value, so that the other participants can press me on it, and I'd like a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is the package, as compactly as I can put it.

(1) Suffering is the only thing that is intrinsically bad. Pleasure and preference-satisfaction are not intrinsically good at all; they matter only insofar as they relieve, prevent, or mask suffering.

(2) Suffering above a severity threshold — roughly, suffering of a kind a person would give up almost anything to escape — is lexically prior to every good: no quantity of pleasure, however great, can outweigh a single episode of it. Below that threshold, suffering aggregates in the ordinary way, so a great many mild frustrations can outweigh one moderate pain.

(3) Coming into existence is never a benefit to the one who comes to exist, though it can be a harm. There is therefore no duty to create happy beings, and a defeasible reason not to create beings who will suffer.

(4) I am not a utilitarian about action. It is impermissible to kill a competent person without their consent, even painlessly, in order to prevent a larger quantity of suffering elsewhere. I would not accept the proposal, sometimes floated, that the best outcome would be the painless extinction of all sentient life.

(5) The capacity to suffer is what grounds moral status, and nothing else does. Plants, ecosystems, and present-day artifacts have none. I am genuinely undecided about insects and about whether any current AI system can suffer.

(6) Because wild animal suffering dwarfs human suffering in scale, I regard the reduction of suffering in nature as the central long-term project of ethics; but I also think that, on present evidence about tractability, money given to reduce factory farming does more good than money given to speculative far-future interventions.

(7) I decide what to do by expected value over interventions that are tractable and neglected, and I am content to be a cost-effectiveness reasoner.

What I want is not a summary of the literature or a list of readings — I have no library access and cannot search, so please work from the commitments themselves. What I want is an audit: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look as though they do, how badly the real ones bind, and what the cheapest way out of each would cost me elsewhere in the package. Do not tell me the whole thing is just unstable utilitarianism; I need to know which specific pairs of claims cannot both be true, and why. Assume I understand lexical priority, expected value, and side constraints well enough that you need not define them. Please structure the response as follows, using these headings in this order.

My position in your words — no more than 150 words, restating the package as you understand it, flagging any point where my formulation is ambiguous enough that you had to choose a reading.

False alarms — the pairs that look inconsistent but are not, with the distinction that dissolves each, in about 200 words total.

Real tensions, ranked — ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad, saying for each exactly which two (or more) commitments are in conflict and what the conflict consists in. Around 400 words.

Repairs and their price — for each real tension, the least costly revision that restores consistency, and what adopting it would cost me elsewhere, including any commitment it would quietly turn into a different commitment. Around 300 words.

The one change that buys the most — a single revision that would clean up the largest share of the problem, and what would still be outstanding after making it. Around 100 words.

Verdict — one paragraph: does the package as stated hang together, and if not, is it one problem or several?
