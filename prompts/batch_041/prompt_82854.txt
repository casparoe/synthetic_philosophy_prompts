I'm in a jurisprudence seminar that has spent two weeks on the Hart–Devlin debate, and I have ended up as the resident sceptic. The picture a classmate of mine keeps reaching for — and which I suspect is doing most of the work in her case — is a biological one: society is the body, the community's shared morality is its immune system, conduct the community finds intolerable is a pathogen, the spread of such conduct is an infection, and the criminal law is the treatment. She insists this is just Devlin's thought that a shared morality is what holds a society together, translated out of law and into biology, and on that picture Hart's insistence that nothing may be criminalised unless it harms someone starts to look like refusing medicine until the wound has gone septic. The conclusion she draws is the legal moralist's: that the immorality of conduct is at least a pro tanto reason for the criminal law to intervene, even where nobody is harmed.

I don't want a verdict on the substantive dispute; I want an audit of the analogy. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the body, of health, of the immune system, of a pathogen, of an infection, of the immune response, of the physician, of recovery, of death — and then tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing for the use she is putting the picture to and which are idle. I want to know whether her conclusion can survive once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. Four things in particular seem to me to press on it, and I would like them addressed rather than waved past: whether the organism picture quietly presupposes a determinate norm of health that a society simply does not have; whether it converts the majority's disgust into a diagnosis, so that the analogy's output looks like a finding rather than a preference; whether it can accommodate societies that have drastically rewritten their moral codes and persisted; and whether it turns people into pathogens, so that the very people whose treatment is at issue enter the picture only as an infection. I would also like to know whether a restricted version of the picture — one that covers only conduct the community regards as intolerable rather than merely disapproved of — escapes these objections or merely relocates them.

Finally, if the picture cannot do the job, tell me whether a better one can. A friend outside the seminar suggests that tolerating minority moral deviance is like letting small fires burn in a forest, on the grounds that a century of suppressing every small fire left the forests of the American west primed for catastrophic ones; I would like to know whether that analogy does any better on the points where the immune-system picture fails, and where it fails in its own way, and if neither works, what a better analogy would be. Since I expect to quote this in a seminar paper, please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the audit, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, with a word on why.
