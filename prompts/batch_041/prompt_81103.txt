I have no training in physics or philosophy; everything below I have pieced together on my own from popular books, and I would like to be told where it breaks.

Begin with a definition of my own. Call two events co-present when some observer moving at a constant velocity would judge them to happen at the same time. Three things seem to me settled. First, co-presence is relative: whether two events are co-present depends on the observer's velocity. Second, for any one observer, co-presence is an equivalence relation on the events of that observer's simultaneity slice, so it is symmetric and transitive. Third, co-presence is not transitive once you allow the observer to change: there are events A, B and C such that A is co-present with B for one observer and B is co-present with C for another, while A is co-present with C for no observer at all. The illustration I keep returning to is the pair of pedestrians passing each other in opposite directions, whose respective nows slice through a distant galaxy a couple of million light years away at moments separated by days of that galaxy's history, even though the walkers are a hair's breadth apart.

Here is the argument, which I met in some popular book and have since convinced myself is valid.

(1) If two events are co-present for some observer, then neither is more real than the other; both belong equally to what exists.

(2) Being equally real is transitive: if A is as real as B, and B is as real as C, then A is as real as C.

(3) Take any two events, however far apart in time: the first step of the first dinosaur and my reading of this sentence. Between them runs a chain of co-presence relations through a sequence of observers, each link licensed by (1), and such a chain always exists.

(4) By (2), equal reality travels along the chain from one end to the other.

(5) So every event is as real as every other. Past, present and future all exist, and nothing ever comes into existence or passes out of it.

(6) My future actions are therefore as real as my past ones, and there is nothing I can do to change what happens; deliberation is theatre.

I am confident that (6) is a further step beyond (5), and I would surrender it without much grief. But I would sooner keep (1), and the relativity of simultaneity that makes it plausible, than keep (5), and I cannot see how to have both. If I reject (2) I must say that reality admits of degrees, or that some events are real only relative to others, and I cannot tell whether that is a coherent position or a relabelling of the problem. If I reject (1) I must explain what else could make two events that some observer finds simultaneous fail to be equally real, when relativity forbids any privileged simultaneity. And if the answer is that the word real behaves like the word here, a term whose content shifts with who is speaking, so that every event is equally real says no more about the world than every place is equally here says about geography, then I want to know whether that escapes the argument or merely restates it in better grammar.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what each competing repair costs. Argue from first principles rather than sending me to the literature; I have no way to check references and do not want a reading list.
