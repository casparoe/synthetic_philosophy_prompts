I'm putting together a one-page flowchart for the first meeting of a perception reading group, on Molyneux's question -- the blind-from-birth guy who's learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, then gets his sight back: can he tell which is which just by looking? I don't want the usual Locke-says-no / Leibniz-says-yes roll call. I want a decision tree where every node is a question the reader can answer about the case itself, and the leaves are the positions you actually land in, named.

Nodes I'd like on the tree, though add one if the space needs it:

- what exactly is being claimed he can do: tell the two objects apart at all, say which is which, sort them into the same two categories he already uses by touch, or reach out and shape his hand for the right one. These come apart, and I think half the disagreement lives here.
- if he fails, where the failure sits: in what he sees, in the fact that he sees fine but has no link between the visual shape and the tactile concept, or in his eyes and attention not yet working well enough for the test to mean anything.
- whether visual and tactile shape share a common format, so it's the same property presented in two senses, or are two different properties joined only by learning.
- whether the question is one the real sight-restoration cases could settle, or a conceptual question about what counts as recognising a shape.

Leaves should include: the classic no (he has to learn the correspondence), the more radical no (sight and touch have nothing in common, so there's nothing to transfer), the yes-because-of-common-geometry answer (he reasons it out), the yes-because-it's-innate answer, the split answer (2D figures yes, 3D solids no), the 'space yes, shape no' answer, the two-visual-streams answer (he could aim his grasp at the cube without being able to say which is which -- no idea if anyone has actually framed it that way), and the deflationary answer (there is no single question here). Mark any two leaves the tree can't actually seperate and say why, and flag at least one combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy -- I'm curious whether those are empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered.

Eight to ten leaves, assume the reader is smart but has never read any of the literature, and no reading list -- it's going on a handout. At the end, tell me which single node you're least sure really does any separating work, and what would change your mind about it or make you redraw that branch.
