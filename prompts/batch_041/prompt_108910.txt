I am designing a graduate seminar on long-run comparative development, and I need a diagnostic instrument rather than a literature review. The seminar’s recurring question is whether we can ever know why some nations are rich and others poor, and my students tend to arrive with strong commitments that they cannot state in a way that distinguishes them from their neighbors’ commitments. I want a decision tree that sorts them by what they actually believe about the causes of national wealth, not by which author or label they happen to invoke. Assume no web access: do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or claims that depend on particular dates, titles, or attributions. The tree should be built from the logical space of the debate first—questions and combinations of answers—and only then locate named or unnamed positions within that space.

The starting commitment for the tree is this: cross-national income differences are enormous, persist across decades, and must be explained by some combination of institutions, geography, culture, colonial history, and contingency. The first branch nodes should force the student to say what exactly is being explained (income levels, growth rates, the durability of institutions, or the absence of convergence), what would count as a cause rather than a symptom, and what the relevant counterfactual is (a different colonial power, a different precolonial polity, a different post-independence settlement, or a different initial geography). From there, the tree should separate views according to whether institutions are exogenous constraints, endogenous equilibria, or epiphenomenal of elite bargains; whether geography acts through agricultural productivity, disease and demography, transport costs, or the legibility of populations to tax collectors; whether culture is a deep parameter, a slow-moving consequence, or a repertoire that shifts with institutions; and whether history is path-dependent, multiple-equilibrium, or largely luck.

Please write the instrument in the following sections and order, with the rough lengths indicated.

Root and target (150–200 words): State the root commitment and define the explanandum precisely enough that a student can tell whether a proposed cause is meant to explain levels, growth, persistence, or divergence. Include a short note on why the choice of counterfactual is not cosmetic.

Branch nodes (900–1,200 words): Give ten to twelve branch nodes in order. Each node should be a question a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to, with two or three mutually exclusive answers and a sentence on what the answer separates. At least three nodes must concern methodology rather than substance: what counts as evidence from history, whether causal inference from observational cross-sections is possible, and whether the unit of analysis should be the nation-state, the region, or the local political settlement. At least two nodes must use paired comparisons: two adjacent regions with similar ecology but different precolonial centralization, and two regions that diverged after a war or partition while geography remained fixed. Include at least one pair from outside the usual Atlantic sample—for example, adjacent regions in the Himalayas, the Sahel, or the Indonesian archipelago.

Leaf positions (700–900 words): For each leaf, give the position a short name, one sentence stating its core claim, the path of answers that reaches it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The leaves must include: strong institutionalism centered on formal property rights; an elite-bargain or political-settlement view; geography as agricultural productivity; geography as disease and demography; a state-capacity or fiscal-sociology view; culture as deep cause; culture as slow consequence; colonial legacy as path dependence; multiple-equilibria or contingency; and a deflationary leaf holding that the question is ill-posed because nation and wealth are not natural kinds. Include at least one leaf that is not a standard school but a live combination: the view that geography matters mainly by making populations legible or illegible to tax collectors, so the proximate cause is state capacity and the ultimate cause is terrain. Label it Unnamed if it has no canonical name. Include one leaf for causal skepticism: the view that the historical record cannot identify these causes at all, only constrain them.

Empty or near-empty branches (250–350 words): Identify combinations of answers that the tree permits but that no recognizable position occupies. For each, say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Do not force a named position onto every branch.

Non-separable pairs and diagnostic limits (250–350 words): Flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and explain what further question would be needed. Pay particular attention to institutionalism versus elite-bargain views, geography-as-legibility versus state-capacity views, and culture-as-deep-cause versus culture-as-slow-consequence. State where the instrument should stop and hand the disagreement back to the seminar as a values or evidence dispute rather than a classification.

How a seminar leader should use it (100–150 words): Give brief instructions for administering the tree, scoring the paths, and using the empty branches as discussion prompts rather than as errors.
