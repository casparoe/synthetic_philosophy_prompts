I run a monthly reading group that meets for seventy-five minutes and is deliberately cross-disciplinary: this term it is four economics PhD students, three computer-science graduate students who work on machine-learning systems, and one philosopher who mostly does metaethics. I am the facilitator, next month's slot is mine to run, and the topic I want to work through is the competitiveness constraint on AI safety and what people usually call the alignment tax — but I have a specific construal in mind that I want the session built around rather than the popular version, and I don't want suggested readings; I have the material. On my construal, the alignment tax is not a fixed surcharge that a developer either pays or evades. It is partly a research and engineering investment whose per-unit cost can be driven down over time, so the interesting question is not whether anyone will pay it but under what competitive conditions paying it early becomes self-defeating, and under what conditions it instead becomes a durable advantage. And the competitiveness constraint I take to operate at several levels at once — at the level of firms deciding whether to spend on safety, at the level of states deciding whether to regulate or to lag, and at the level of the research community deciding what to treat as a publishable result — with the levels interacting, which is the part I think people miss.

I don't need you to explain any of this to me; I need a plan for how I should teach it in the seventy-five minutes, and I want the plan to be explicit about its own architecture. First, the order in which the pieces have to go and why each depends on the one before it — in particular, what must be in place before the phrase alignment tax can be introduced at all without smuggling in a conclusion, and where the multi-level point can be raised without the room collapsing it back into the single-level firm story. Second, the one example or scenario that should carry the whole session, chosen with an eye to what it must contain (a genuine trade-off, a selection mechanism, a realistic possibility of change over time) and what it must not contain — I suspect the obvious candidates are the nuclear-weapons race and the two-labs-racing parable, so tell me whether either can be made to work and, if not, precisely what goes wrong with it. Third, the misconceptions in the order this particular group will actually produce them: the economists, I predict, will redescribe the whole thing as a public-goods problem and conclude that standard theory already dissolves it; the computer scientists will hear tax as tax on capability and start arguing about benchmarks; the philosopher will want to know whether any of this licenses a claim about what anyone ought to do. For each, name the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off and what I should say there. Fourth, one question I can put to the room near the end that would genuinely discriminate between someone who has understood the competitiveness constraint and someone who has merely learned to be worried about it. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately refuse to cover at this level, say out loud that I am refusing, and why that refusal is honest rather than evasive.
