I am a doctoral student in the history of science, and I need help drafting a document. For the past year, two members of my department's theory-and-methods reading group have been arguing about periodization, and the argument has reached the point where it seems the parties may be answering different questions. I would like you to write the joint statement that they could both sign if they collaborated in good faith.

The two positions are these.

The first (call it the realist) holds that terms such as 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution' name genuine discontinuities in European intellectual and institutional life: that the clustering of changes in, say, curricula, textual practices, patronage, and instrument-making is real and not merely an artefact of where historians happen to draw their boundaries, so that a period label is a discovery, however provisional, rather than an imposition.

The second (call it the instrumentalist) holds that these divisions are retrospective constructions whose boundaries, names, and even their number are fixed by the preoccupations of the historians who use them -- that the Middle Ages is dark because modernity needs a darkness to have emerged from, that the Renaissance is a rebirth only relative to a chosen antiquity, and that the Scientific Revolution is a story told backwards from a desired present. On this view the period terms do explanatory work only within a narrative, not within the world.

Several pieces of apparatus have come up in our meetings, and the document should engage them rather than talk around them. First, a proposal circulating informally in our group (I am not aware of a canonical statement of it) that period terms are indexical: that 'medieval', like 'here', has no content apart from a contrast class, and that the contrast class is always supplied by the speaker's own position. Second, a distinction some of us have found useful between a phase -- a local transition picked out by a single variable together with a stated theory of that variable, for instance a change in the rate of a particular kind of textual production -- and a period -- a global, multi-domain label that is supposed to hold across many variables at once. Third, the fact that 'medieval' is applied to societies with no connection to the Latin West, which raises the question whether the concept picks out a structural type or merely exports a European chronology. Fourth, the suspicion that the evaluative charge of the labels (dark, rebirth, revolution) is doing the work of the delineation rather than following from it.

Please produce a document with the following structure.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: what both parties can sign, including about what periodization is for, about the kinds of discontinuity that are uncontroversially present in the record, about the status of boundaries as opposed to centres, and about which of the disputes above are merely terminological. Be exact; a claim that both sides would accept only because it is empty is worse than no claim at all.

2. A disagreements table, one row per remaining dispute, with columns: the question at issue; the realist's position, phrased as the realist would want it phrased; the instrumentalist's position, phrased as the instrumentalist would want it phrased; whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal, and if verbal, what the parties are really disagreeing about; and what each side would count as moving it.

3. A cruxes section: for each crux -- a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side -- state the crux, then give a concrete case or piece of evidence that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I am particularly interested in a crux that can be posed against a body of evidence rather than against a historiographical tradition: for instance, if one took a large corpus of dated manuscripts or a database of citations and asked whether a change-point analysis recovers the conventional boundaries or instead shows continuous drift, what would each side predict, and would either be embarrassed by the answer? Where a crux can only be settled by argument, say so, and say what the argument would have to establish.

4. A short closing section on what would remain unresolved even if every crux were settled in one party's favour.

Please do not provide citations, reading lists, or references to particular historians; the point is to construct the formulations ourselves. Write in a register that both signatories could put their names to.
