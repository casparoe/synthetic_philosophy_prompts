For about a year my reading group has been stuck on the notion of a free-floating rationale — the idea that there can be a rationale for a behavioural disposition that explains both why the disposition is present and why it would be a mistake for the organism to do otherwise, but that no reasoner entertains. Some of us treat these as legitimate explanatory posits: selection can build a rationale into a bird's alarm call or a cricket's silence, and citing that rationale explains the behaviour even though nothing in the organism represents it. Others hold that this is either a redescription of ordinary function-talk or an attempt to smuggle normativity into a world of causes, and that the explanatory work is done by selection history, not by any reason. Nobody has moved in a year, and I suspect we are not all disagreeing about the same thing. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about the word reason, with one side talking about explanatory warrants and the other about the mental states of agents; a substantive dispute about whether teleological or normative facts are irreducible to causal ones; or two groups answering different questions under one heading, one about how to explain behaviour and one about what there is? A colleague proposes that the dispute dissolves once we distinguish a rationale as a description of why a trait persisted from a reason as something that can be weighed by an agent, so that the appearance of disagreement is an equivocation; I am not persuaded that this does anything but relocate the problem, since the interesting question is why the same selection history licenses explanatory warrants in one framework and only causal talk in the other. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a reformulation, a formal result, or an argument that the question is ill-posed — and whether some hidden empirical question is quietly doing the work.
