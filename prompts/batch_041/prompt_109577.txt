I'm prepping a session on Maimonides' account of the sacrificial laws -- the reading on which they were basically a concession, that God let the Israelites keep the style of worship they were already soaked in (offerings at altars, animals, smoke) and simply redirected it away from the stars and the idols and toward himself, on the grounds that you can't jerk a whole people from one practice straight into its opposite. It's a famously controversial reading; a lot of later commentators thought it made the commandments sound like a bit of a trick. I want a thought experiment that makes the difference between that reading and its main rival sharp, and I want it built so the disagreement can't be dissolved by vague talk about context.

The rival is: the offerings were commanded for their own sake, so the same law would have been given even to a people who had never sacrificed anything and found the idea repellent.

Here's the build. Two possible histories of the same people, differing in exactly one thing -- whether animal sacrifice was already an entrenched practice when the law is given. Hold everything else fixed: the same people with the same theological ignorance, the same need for some concrete embodied form of worship, the same surrounding culture in every other respect, and the same lawgiver with the same aims and the same knowledge. In history A the practice is there, woven into family life, festivals, and civic identity, and giving it up cold turkey would feel like losing a limb. In history B this people has never sacrificed; they bury their dead, they eat meat, but killing an animal as an act of worship strikes them as grotesque.

Now tell me what each of three positions predicts about the law that gets given in A and in B:
1. the accommodation/redirect reading;
2. the intrinsic-command reading;
3. the symbolic reading, on which the offering is the vehicle for an attitude -- submission, gratitude, atonement -- that can only be carried by something like the killing and burning of an animal, so that a legislator aiming at that attitude would have to invent sacrifice if it weren't already there.

Constraints: keep each history to a paragraph; no appeals to what actually happened at Sinai or to any real historical religion; no miracles in either history; the two histories must be identical in every respect other than the one variable, and you should say explicitly what you're holding fixed so I can see the control.

Then do the checks. For each of the three positions, state the law it predicts in A and in B and point to the exact sentence where the predictions diverge. Name the single feature of the case that does the work. Then say what happens if you remove it: what if the practice is present but nobody is attached to it, or absent but the people are nostalgic for a ritual they only dimly remember? Finally, tell me how a defender of the accommodation reading would attack the case if the intrinsic reading comes out looking better -- in particular, whether accommodation is meant as a claim about the legislator's reasons or only about the pedagogical function of the law, and whether it can survive the objection that it makes the commandment deceptive, since the law presents itself as being about the offering while actually being about weaning people off offerings. If that objection only bites on some versions of the reading, seperate them and say which ones.
