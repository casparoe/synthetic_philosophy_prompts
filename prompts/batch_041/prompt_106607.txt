For the past two years I have kept a notebook of arguments I have watched people have — in seminars, at work, in comment threads — and beside each one, my verdict on whether the argument was about anything at all. I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. When I think the parties are disagreeing only about which word to use, I call the dispute verbal; when I think more is at stake, I call it real.

My verdicts:

- Two friends argue for an hour about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. One will call anything with a filling between two pieces of bread a sandwich; the other insists a sandwich needs a separate top and bottom slice. Verbal.
- Two astronomers argue about whether Pluto is a planet. Verbal.
- Two biologists argue about whether a virus is alive. Verbal.
- Two physicists argue about whether a certain quasiparticle really exists or is only a convenient bookkeeping device. I lean verbal, but this one unsettles me, because the argument seems to be about whether the thing is doing genuine explanatory work.
- A botanist and a cook argue about whether a tomato is a vegetable. Not verbal — and this is the one I keep going back and forth on, since they agree about the fruit part.
- Two metaphysicians argue about whether a statue and the lump of clay it is made of are one thing or two. Not verbal.
- Two people argue about whether a particular artificial intelligence is conscious. Not verbal.
- Two people argue about whether a fetus is a person. Not verbal.
- Two colleagues argue about whether a certain campus policy violates free speech. They agree on every fact about what the policy does; they disagree about whether it should be allowed, and each is also trying to get the other to stop using the phrase the way they do. Not verbal.

The test I reach for when I bother to apply one: rewrite each side's claim without the contested word. If the rewrites leave the parties with nothing to disagree about, the dispute was verbal; if the disagreement survives the rewrite, it was real. By that test the tomato case comes out verbal, which is not what I said, so either the test or the verdict is wrong. My half-formed suspicion is that the cases which resist the test are the ones where the contested word is doing work — in a theory, a practice, or a set of rules — rather than merely labelling; but I cannot make that precise, and I am not sure it survives the Pluto case.

Please do not answer that it all depends on what one means by verbal. I know it does; I want the best version of the thing. Tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, captures as many of these verdicts as possible; which verdicts I have to abandon to keep the rest; and how the surviving principle rules on a dispute I have not listed.

Please structure your reply as follows. Bottom line (under 100 words): is there one principle that captures the pattern, and what does it cost me? Candidate criteria (about 250 words): two or three criteria, each given a name, stated precisely, and paired with the case that motivates it. Scorecard (about 350 words): run through my cases in order, marking for each criterion whether it gets the verdict right, and naming the exact case at which each criterion breaks. What I have to give up (about 150 words): which of my verdicts I should abandon, and what the surviving principle says instead. A new case (about 200 words): apply the surviving principle to a dispute I have not mentioned, give your verdict and your confidence, and say what would change your mind.
