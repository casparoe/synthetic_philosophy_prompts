I serve as senior staff to the ethics committee of a state legislature, and part of my job is to field requests from members and their staffs to bless some exception to the ordinary rules -- a bit of surveillance, a promise quietly broken, a favor traded with a regulator, a confidential file shown to the wrong person -- where the justification offered is always that it is necessary to prevent something worse. I did graduate work in political theory before this job, so I know the shape of the problem of dirty hands and I find it genuinely clarifying, but the literature I remember is mostly concerned with whether a politician can remain morally clean, or with whether the problem is real at all, and none of it has given me anything I can run. What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee can actually apply.

Please write it as a checklist in prose: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question has become one of values rather than facts. The procedure has to be runnable in about ninety minutes by committee staff who have no philosophical training, it has to say what to do when we cannot get the facts we would need, and it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as mechanics. I would rather have a shorter procedure with the soft joints clearly marked than a longer one that pretends to be an algorithm. I also want it to distinguish, as sharply as the material allows, the case where someone really is choosing between two genuine moral costs and the case where a cost is being manufactured or shifted onto someone else to spare the decision-maker. Where your procedure rests on a specific thinker's distinction, say so in a sentence rather than gesturing at a school, and flag the points where you are improvising rather than drawing on anything written.

Then run it on three requests, one of which is a real case from last session and two of which I have invented to stress it. (a) A committee chair proposes to trade an informal assurance about a future funding bill to a regulator in exchange for expediting a permit; the permit matters enormously to a single constituent, and the assurance is not something the chair can actually deliver. (b) A member asks us to sanction surveillance of a small advocacy group whose two most vocal members are suspected of planning violence at a hearing; our evidence is real but thin. (c) A member wants to withhold from a criminal defendant's lawyer a document that would help the defense, on the ground that releasing it exposes a source and ends an operation aimed at a serious crime. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like, and I would like you to say plainly which one that is. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and what kind of case should never be brought to it at all.
