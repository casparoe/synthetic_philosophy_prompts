I'm a high school student in a small math-and-philosophy reading group, and for the last few weeks we have been stuck on Bertrand's paradox. The setup I'm sure you know: draw a chord of a circle at random and ask for the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Pick two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference and you get one third; pick a radius and then a point uniformly along it and you get one half; pick the midpoint uniformly from the disk and you get one quarter. I can follow all three derivations, and that is precisely the problem. Each is the principle of indifference applied to a different quantity — endpoint pairs, radial distance, midpoints — and nobody in the group can say what licenses preferring one quantity to another. Someone raised the same difficulty in a simpler guise: a factory makes cubes whose side is at most two centimetres, and indifference over side length versus indifference over volume gives different probabilities to the cube being smaller than one centimetre on a side, one half against one eighth. So the principle of indifference looks less like a single principle than like a family indexed by a choice of variable, and I want to know whether that is fatal or reparable.

Two repairs have been proposed, and what I would like from you is a careful comparison of them, not merely as patches for this one puzzle but as general foundations. The first replaces indifference with maximum entropy: among all distributions consistent with what we actually know, take the one of greatest entropy, on the grounds that it adds nothing beyond the constraints. The second abandons talk of indifference over outcomes altogether and looks instead for the symmetries of the problem — a group of transformations under which the situation is unchanged — and then uses the unique distribution invariant under that group. I want to know how the two relate. Is maximum entropy really just invariance in disguise, or is it a genuinely different and weaker idea? Is its dependence on a background measure, and on which facts we choose to encode as constraints, as arbitrary as the choice of variable it was meant to replace — a die whose mean we know to be 4.5 strikes me as a case where the two repairs pull apart, and I would like to know whether that shows anything? Does the symmetry approach do better, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness into the choice of group, since we still have to decide which transformations count as leaving the problem unchanged? And how does each fare on the cube factory, where no group of transformations presents itself as obviously the right one? Finally, I want to know what each repair actually delivers for the chord problem itself — one of the three answers above, or a fourth — and whether that answer is forced by the mathematics or smuggled in with the choice of group.

Please take a definite position rather than laying the options side by side for me to choose from. Tell me which of the two repairs you would adopt as the foundation for the principle of indifference, and why the other is worse; and if you think both fail, say so plainly and tell me what is then left of the principle of indifference — whether it should be abandoned outright, or restricted to cases where the relevant variables are somehow fixed in advance of the problem. I would like the reasons to be as concrete as possible, worked through on the chord problem or the cube factory rather than stated in general terms, and I would rather have your own argument than a survey of positions. No citations or reading list, please — what I want to see is where the weight of the argument actually falls.
