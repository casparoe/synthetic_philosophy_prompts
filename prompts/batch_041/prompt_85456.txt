Ok so I've basically talked myself into Galen Strawson's basic arguement and I want you to try to talk me back out of it — or at least tell me what would. The version I've internalised goes: for you to be truly morally responsible for something you do, you'd have to be its ultimate source; you'd have to be causa sui, the cause of your own character and of the standards by which you judge that character. But you obviously aren't — you didn't pick your genes, your upbringing, your temperament, or the fact that you're the kind of person who values what you value — and you can't patch this by pointing to some earlier self who did the picking, because that self has exactly the same problem, so the regress never bottoms out. Determinism doesn't rescue it, and indeterminism makes it worse: if part of your "choice" is a coin-flip in the neurons, that isn't self-authorship either, it's just luck wearing a lab coat.

So what counts against it? I don't want a list of "compatiblists disagree." I want the actual defeaters, sorted from would-be-fatal down to merely awkward, and I want you to be honest about which ones Strawson himself would have to concede rather than wave away.

Some specific things I'm stuck on, so hit these directly:

1. Does it prove too much? The same regress that eats desert also seems to eat deliberation, regret, pride, gratitude, resentment — the whole reactive-attitude economy — and maybe even the epistemic responsibility of holding the argument in the first place. If it's a reductio of something we can't give up, is that a problem for the argument or just the human condition? And if it undercuts the responsibility you'd need to be a reliable reasoner, does it saw off the branch it's sitting on?

2. The escape hatch. Strawson only claims ultimate (sometimes "true") moral responsibility is impossible, and seems content to let ordinary, everyday responsibility carry on. Is that a principled distinction or a patch that renders the view unfalsifiable — every objection absorbed by retreating to "well, I only meant the ultimate kind"? Tell me straight whether the view is insulated, and if so whether that's fatal or just how a thesis of this shape has to work.

3. The target. The argument needs the premise that responsibility requires self-creation all the way down. But suppose I offer something thinner: you're answerable for the attitudes and commitments that are genuinely yours now — the ones that show up in your judgments, that you'd actually defend if challenged — regardless of the causal story of how you came to have them. If that's a coherent notion of responsibility, does the Basic Argument refute it, or only a conception of desert so thick that hardly any serious compatibilist ever held it? If it only refutes the thick one, is that a real result or a strawman?

4. Give me the constructive side too: what would the world — or us — have to be like for the conclusion to be false? What would it even look like for a person to be a genuine origin of their own character, such that we could tell the difference between that case and the ordinary one? If I can't so much as imagine the falsifying scenario, that should worry me.

Rank everything, tell me what you'd actually bet on, and finish by naming the single consideration that would move you if you were me.
