I sit on the instructional-materials review panel of a state board of education, and I need to draft the panel's written opinion before next week's vote. A publisher has submitted a supplementary unit for a high-school world-history course that presents the "new chronology" associated with the mathematician A. T. Fomenko — the thesis that most of ancient and medieval history was fabricated and the conventional timeline reconstructed from later sources — as one of two competing chronologies for students to weigh. The unit never asserts that the new chronology is true; it asks students to compare the methods behind each and reach their own conclusion. The publisher's cover letter presses two arguments we have to engage rather than wave away. The first is a symmetry argument: the panel cannot privilege the conventional chronology without begging the question, since every chronology is the product of some community with its own interests, so fairness requires presenting the rivals on equal footing. The second is pedagogical: this is just the "multiple working hypotheses" method familiar from field geology, applied to history, and rejecting it would teach students that established conclusions are above scrutiny.

Please draft the panel's decision memo recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should state criteria that distinguish legitimate historical revisionism from pseudohistory in terms reviewers with no specialist training can actually apply; give the publisher's two arguments their strongest hearing before explaining why they do or do not succeed; and name the concrete conditions, if any, under which material of this kind could be approved. The memo will be read aloud at a public meeting, so it must be defensible to an audience that suspects any talk of demarcation is mere gatekeeping.
