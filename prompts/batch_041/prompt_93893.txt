Okay so I'm meant to be writing a seminar thing on distributive fairness and I keep getting stuck on insolvency law of all places, so bear with me.

Here's the practice. When a company goes under and there isn't enough to pay everyone it owes, the law doesn't hand every creditor the same dollar amount, and it mostly doesn't just go to whoever got to the assets first. Within the general pool everyone recieves the same *fraction* of what they were owed — the million-dollar creditor and the hundred-grand creditor both get, say, forty cents on the dollar. But the pool itself is built by a different principle: the secured lender took its collateral before anyone else got a look, and in a lot of places the tax authority and unpaid employees get bumped ahead of the general creditors too. So one proceeding uses first-come-first-served to decide who's even in the room, then something like proportional equality to divide what's left. I can't tell whether that's a sensible division of labour or two incompatible views of fairness stacked on top of each other.

Second thing. Model the general creditors as bargainers: fallback is zero recovery, best case is getting paid in full, the pie is the estate. The rule that falls out of that setup, the one that keeps each side's share of the way to it's own ideal equal, is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, I think that's the name. It's the alternative to Nash's, and the difference is that KS keeps the property that your share can't shrink when your own possibilities improve while the other side's stay put, whereas Nash gives that up in exchange for independence of irrelevant alternatives. Both of them survive rescaling somebody's utility, which is the tell that neither is comparing levels of wellbeing across people, only how far each person is from their own benchmark.

What I want: what does the pro rata rule actually presuppose? My guess is a particular view of fairness between claimants — equal fractions rather than equal dollar amounts or equal sacrifice in welfare terms — and that this presupposes we can compare how far each claimant is from their own ideal without comparing how badly off they each end up. Does the rule need that view, or would several different theories license the same practice, so that the practice is just underdetermined? Which parts are real presuppositions and which are things it merely fits nicely with?

And the bit that really bugs me: in the bargaining setup the ideal point is a *demand* a party makes — a move, maybe a bluff — while in bankruptcy the claim is a fact about entitlement that exists before any procedure runs. Same formal object, completely different metaphysics. Does the rule depend on the ideal point being a fact rather than a move? If a creditor could inflate its claim, the equal-fraction rule is obviously gameable, so is the rule quietly presupposing that claims are fixed independently of the procedure? And what happens to the fairness story if we drop that?

Last: tell me what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for equal dollar amounts, or for equal welfare sacrifice, or for a Nash-style view where the baseline is the uncoordinated grab rather than zero. The floor case isn't hypothetical for me though — the mediation clinic I volunteer at uses a splitting tool that adds a flat minimum to each claimant before the proportional split, so the small claimant quietly ends up with a better fraction than the big one, and our handbook says the whole point of the rule is that claimants are treated alike. What does the floor tell me about what we actually believe? Is it a side constraint bolted on, or evidence that the real commitment was never equal fractions at all?
