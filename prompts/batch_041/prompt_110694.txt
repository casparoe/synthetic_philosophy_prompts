Four labels circulate in discussions of biological form — adaptation, exaptation, spandrel, byproduct — and they are usually handled as though they sorted traits into mutually exclusive bins. Please explain the conceptual issue that this handling obscures. In particular, address whether these labels classify traits or classify explanations (or histories), whether the same trait can carry different labels at different times without contradiction, and what the concept of a spandrel is supposed to add that the bare notion of a byproduct does not already supply. A colleague proposes an operational test: a trait is a spandrel just in case no selection pressure can be identified for it. Explain what is confused, or defensible, about treating that as a test.

Then apply your account to a case I have constructed. A population of seed-cracking birds bears a bony ridge along the upper mandible. The ridge is a byproduct of the developmental growth field that also produces the deep, broad jaw the birds need in order to crack hard seeds; the ridge itself does no mechanical work, and its size tracks the growth field rather than any use it is put to. Over the last few hundred thousand years, however, females have come to prefer mates with taller ridges, the preference is heritable, and ridge height has increased steadily in response. A colleague concludes from this that, since the ridge is now maintained by selection, the claim that it is a spandrel is simply false, and that the spandrel story was an excuse for not looking for the ridge's function. Say what is right and what is confused in that remark, and what evidence would genuinely discriminate a spandrel account from an adaptationist account of a trait like the ridge.

Finally, say what the dispute over adaptationism is actually a dispute about — the frequency of adaptation in nature, the correct method for detecting it, or the explanatory priority of natural selection — and whether those three questions can come apart.

Please answer in American English.
