I'm a quantitative researcher — most of my working life is model selection, so my native vocabulary is priors, likelihoods, marginal likelihoods, Occam factors, AIC and BIC, description-length penalties, regularization, bias–variance, cross-validation, overfitting, identifiability, and the chronic worry that a coauthor's prior is doing more work than the data. I read enough philosophy of science on the side to keep bumping into the phrase 'inference to the best explanation,' and I keep being told that working scientists routinely accept a hypothesis because it would, if true, explain the evidence better than its rivals do. I would like that entire position restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, as faithfully as you can manage, and then I want the residue report: what carries over cleanly, what carries over only if you distort it, and what does not survive the trip at all. Assume I know the statistics cold and am not a philosopher, so don't soften the technical parts and don't dress anything up in philosophical idiom to make it sound deeper than it is. No references, please; I won't follow them up.

Take the explanatory virtues that are usually offered as the currency of the view — simplicity, unification, consilience with independent lines of evidence, mechanistic detail, precision, coherence with what we already accept — and tell me, one at a time, what each becomes in my vocabulary. Where does simplicity cash out as a prior, where as an explicit penalty term, where as a claim about which model class I was willing to write down in the first place? Is consilience just the observation that my likelihood factorizes over conditionally independent observations, or is there a substantive extra requirement hiding in it? Is unification anything over and above a model with fewer free parameters, or does it pick out something my machinery has no slot for? I want the mapping to be honest: if the only way to make an explanatory virtue land in my framework is to redescribe it until it becomes a likelihood or a prior, say that explicitly rather than quietly doing the redescription and calling it a translation.

The residue is the part I actually care about, so spend most of your effort there. In particular, I want your verdict on three things. First, the difference between the hypothesis that would be most satisfying if it were true and the hypothesis most likely to be true — in my framework, are those two distinct objects, or one object described twice? Second, the worry that the best explanation on offer might simply be the best of a bad lot, with nothing good in the running at all — is that anything more than prior sensitivity, or does it point at something structural about how the space of candidate hypotheses gets set up and why that setup is not itself a modelling choice like any other? Third, whether explaining is a genuinely different relation from predicting: are there cases in which the model with the best out-of-sample performance is not the best explanation, and if so, what am I supposed to do about it? For each of these, tell me whether the point survives translation intact, survives only mutilated, or has no counterpart in my vocabulary — and when it has none, say whether that is a defect of my vocabulary, i.e. something real I am failing to represent, or evidence that the original claim was vaguer than it sounded. If the honest answer is that most of the position collapses into Bayesian model selection plus a rhetorical flourish, say so plainly, and show me exactly which parts collapse and which parts are left standing.
