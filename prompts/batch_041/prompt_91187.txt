I write about mathematics and the foundations of probability for a general-interest science desk, and for the past several months I have been reporting on what people in the field call logical uncertainty — the problem of holding degrees of belief about claims whose truth or falsity is settled but not available to us: a late digit in the decimal expansion of a familiar constant, the status of a conjecture that has resisted proof, the outcome of a computation nobody has run to the end. Ordinary probability theory is oddly silent here, because it treats logic as something the agent already has: you condition on evidence, never on the intermediate steps of your own reasoning, and nothing in the formalism tells you what your credence in an unproved lemma ought to be before you have proved it. The most interesting recent attempt to fill the gap, a programme that goes by the name logical induction, treats every sentence in a formal language as a stock worth one unit if the sentence is true and nothing if it is false, and then asks for prices that no combination of trades can be guaranteed to profit against; the resulting picture is that belief is coherent in the limit, so that whenever one claim implies another the price of the second never sits below the price of the first, that provable claims drift toward one and refutable ones toward zero, and that the agent learns to trust its own future conclusions more than its present ones. In September I have been asked to run the closing session of a graduate summer school — ninety minutes, sixteen participants, a mix of philosophy and mathematics students, some comfortable with formal probability and some not at all — and I have decided I would rather stage this than explain it. I do not want to stand at the front and tell them that credences over logical claims are a strange kind of object; I want the room to end up holding the problem in its own hands and to be irritated by it.

The sketch I have been turning over, and which I would like you to turn into something that actually runs: a card carrying a fact that is determinate but hidden from the room, sealed in an envelope on the table — I have been using a specified late decimal digit of a well-known constant, chosen because it is in principle computable by a finite calculation that nobody in the room has done and that cannot be done in ninety minutes — together with a spread of proposition cards about that digit: it is odd; it is prime; it is seven; it is three or seven; it is odd or even; it is greater than nine. Some of these stand in logical relations everyone can see at a glance, and some in relations that are easy to overlook; at least one is a logical truth whose content is nonetheless unknown to the room, and at least one is a logical falsehood. Each participant begins with an equal stack of chips — no money, no prizes, nothing redeemable, since I would rather the stakes were only reputational — and each also receives a private hint card that bears on the digit without settling it, with exactly one hint in the room decisive. Over four rounds, every participant posts a buying price and a selling price for each proposition, and I stand as the counterparty, taking whatever trades those quotes invite, so that a participant whose prices are incoherent is picked off immediately and in public; the prices are written up between rounds. The final round introduces one more card, a proposition about the settlement price of that very card, which no one can quote consistently, and I want the mechanics arranged so that this discovery lands on the participants rather than on me. Nobody should hear the phrase logical uncertainty before the debrief, and I should never give a lecture — the rules and the payoffs have to do the teaching.

What I need back is a design I can hand to a colleague and run: the exact rules and order of play, a materials list I can assemble in an afternoon, a payoff scheme that works without money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the facilitator's lines for the two or three moments where a nudge is genuinely unavoidable. Then the debrief, which is the part I care most about: a mapping from specific things that will have happened in the room — a neglected implication that the house exploited, the decisive hint failing to move the whole market, a card that ought to have settled at one or at zero and did not — onto the corresponding pieces of the problem, written so that it works for the participants with no formal background as well as the ones who have some. I also want an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; my worries are that half the room will not grasp the trading convention quickly enough to quote anything at all, that whoever holds the decisive hint will be ignored or will simply announce it and end the game, and that the self-referential card will produce paralysis or a shouting match rather than the oscillation I am after. Finally, be straight with me about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply in words: my suspicion is that the market can show the incoherence and the drift but not the guarantee about an agent's trust in its own future reasoning, and I would like to know precisely where the analogy runs out before I stand up in front of sixteen people.
