I'm in a seminar on the ethics of emerging technology, and this week we read a chapter from a trade book on AI and ethics, written by a philosopher who is very good at explaining things to a general audience. I'd rather not name him, since I don't want you answering from whatever you happen to remember about him. The chapter's subject is what the author calls the "responsibility gap," and his account runs roughly like this. Once machines become genuinely autonomous — that is, once they act on their own initiative and adapt to circumstances their designers never anticipated — it becomes impossible to hold anyone morally responsible for the harms they cause. The designer is off the hook because she could not have foreseen the behavior; the operator is off the hook because he did not choose it; and the system itself is off the hook because it cannot be blamed or punished. On his telling, autonomy severs the causal chain that runs from human decisions to harmful outcomes, which is why the gap is not a temporary engineering problem but a structural feature of the technology. He then says that the gap is really just the familiar problem of many hands with one more hand added to the pile. He anticipates the objection that manufacturers can always be held strictly liable and treats it as decisive against the whole idea: because the law can always assign the costs to someone, the moral hand-wringing is misplaced, and the alleged gap is a matter of legal design rather than morality. He closes by saying that this is why calls to ban autonomous weapons on responsibility grounds rest on a confusion.

The case we discussed in class was a warehouse fleet whose routing model developed a habit of cutting through a loading bay where workers walk; a worker was hurt, and nobody at the company had anticipated that route. My instinct is that the chapter's last move is too quick, but I can't say why. Please go claim by claim: for each one, say what someone working on this topic would accept as fair, what they would wince at, and what they would reject outright, with the reasoning in each case. I'd especially like you to keep apart three things the chapter seems to run together — whether the harm can be attributed to an agent as her own doing, whether that agent owes an answer to the people affected, and whether she is liable to some sanction or to bearing the costs — and to say how each of the three applies to the designer, to the operator, and to the fleet in the warehouse case. Then tell me whether the closing inference follows: even granting everything the author says about liability and cost-bearing, does it follow that the responsibility objection to autonomous weapons is confused? And if a classmate insists that the only honest fix, once the gap is real, is to treat the system itself as a quasi-agent with duties of its own, is that a consequence of the gap thesis or a separate proposal?

I'd rather have calibrated judgment than confident prose, so please say how confident you are in each verdict and single out the one claim in the chapter you are least sure about — including any place where you think the author may be right and the standard view of the topic wrong.
