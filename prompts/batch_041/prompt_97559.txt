I'm an undergraduate in an archaeology program, currently taking a course on archaeological method and theory, and I've run into a tension in the way we're being trained that I'd like help thinking through properly. The practice I have in mind is the routine classification of archaeological features and deposits into categories like subsistence, domestic, and ritual, along with the associated habit of reading mental states off material remains -- treating grave goods as evidence of a belief in an afterlife, a deliberately broken or buried object as evidence of an intention to consecrate or desecrate, an assemblage of figurines as evidence of a cult. Our field manual even formalizes this: every feature is supposed to be assigned to one of those categories, with indeterminate offered as a fallback, though the published reports we read almost never actually use it -- ambiguous features tend to get filed under ritual, as if the category were a place to put things we cannot explain rather than a claim we can defend. At the same time, the same instructors who happily write ritual in their site reports will insist, when pressed in seminar, that we cannot possibly know what past people thought, that we can only describe what we find, and that any talk of belief is projection. When I ask which is it, I get told that the categories are just heuristic, but they don't seem to function that way in practice -- they do real interpretive work and carry real evidential weight, and nobody treats a subsistence classification and a ritual classification as equally provisional.

What I want from you is this. Second, tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out -- for instance, whether it is compatible with a thoroughgoing behaviorism that refuses to posit inner states at all, or with a view on which meaning is radically underdetermined by the physical record.
