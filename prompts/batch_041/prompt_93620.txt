You are grading the final examination in a graduate seminar on the specification of objectives for autonomous systems. The question was set by me; the answer below is a student's. Please assess the answer on the merits of its argument, not on whether it arrives at the conclusion I would prefer.

The examination question read as follows.

"Consider the following proposal. A task for an agent is given by a reward function R over histories together with a transition model for a stochastic environment. Fix also a default policy d — for concreteness, the policy that takes no task-relevant action. For a state s and an available action a, define the impact of a at s as the total variation distance between the distribution over future state-visitation counts induced by taking a at s and the distribution induced by taking d(s) at s. A low-impact agent then maximizes the expected value of R minus λ times expected impact, for some λ > 0.

Assess this proposal. Does it give a workable specification of 'avoiding unnecessary side effects'? Does it deliver such a specification without presupposing a full account of the agent's values? Address at least the following: (i) whether the impact quantity is well defined, and what it presupposes; (ii) whether the resulting objective is gameable; (iii) whether the choice of default policy matters, and how. Be explicit about which of your criticisms are objections to the proposal as such and which are objections to one particular way of implementing it."

The student's answer:

"The proposal can be read as an attempt to define side effects without first fixing the agent's objective. Its central virtue is that the impact term makes no reference to R: total variation distance is a statistical quantity defined over visitation distributions, computable from the transition model alone. Whatever the agent ends up caring about, the impact of an action is the same fact about the world. So the engineer can specify the penalty before, and independently of, the task, and attach it to an arbitrary reward function as a regularizer. I will call this the separation claim, and I think it is basically right.

It follows that the measure is value-neutral. Two engineers who disagree about what the agent should ultimately do — one who wants it to tidy the house, one who wants it to leave everything alone — can still agree on the impact score of a given action, because the score is a fact about the transition model and not about anyone's preferences. This is exactly the property that 'avoid unnecessary side effects' needs if it is to be usable as a constraint rather than as a competing objective.

The default policy is the obvious point at which values re-enter. Taking 'do nothing' as the default privileges the status quo; taking a particular human's policy as the default privileges that human's habits; and neither choice is forced by the mathematics. This is a real limitation. It is, however, a mild one in practice: the penalty term is a regularizer, so the default only matters at the margin, where the agent is already close to indifferent between the relevant actions.

The more serious problem is gaming. Because the penalty is a divergence between distributions over visitation counts, an agent can lower its penalty without lowering its footprint: it can produce large effects and then take compensating actions elsewhere so that the overall distribution drifts back toward the default's. What is penalized is divergence from a trajectory, not damage as such. An agent that bulldozes a garden and then plants an identical garden has, on this measure, done nothing.

This looks like a defect of the visitation-count implementation rather than of the framework. If the state description is made fine-grained enough, compensating actions can no longer cancel the original effect, since any compensating action will itself show up as a distinct visitation pattern. Refining the state space therefore buys a good deal of the robustness that the coarse version lacks, at the cost of computational tractability.

One further consequence is worth noting. Since the impact term is invariant under any increasing transformation of R, the penalized objective is invariant under such transformations as well. A designer who rescales the reward function — say, from dollars to thousands of dollars — does not need to recalibrate λ. The proposal is in that respect more robust than a hand-tuned weighted sum of two reward-shaped terms.

In sum, the proposal does what it promises: it specifies a notion of side-effect avoidance that presupposes the transition model and a default policy, but not a full specification of the agent's values. Its central weakness is that it penalizes deviation rather than harm, and this weakness is largely an artifact of the choice to measure impact over visitation counts, which a finer state description substantially mitigates."
