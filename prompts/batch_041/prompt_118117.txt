I spent most of last night arguing with my friend Maya about distributive justice, and I came away with the uncomfortable realization that I do not know what principle my own instincts are tracking. Maya has become a thoroughgoing relational egalitarian: she thinks the entire vocabulary of luck — brute versus option, chosen versus unchosen — is a distraction, and that the only question worth asking is whether people stand in relations of equality with one another. I want to know whether she is right that my instincts are simply her position in disguise, or whether there is a genuine principle underneath them. Here are my verdicts, case by case, and I would like you to tell me what captures them.

1. Two colleagues drive to work one morning along the same route at the same speed. A drunk driver runs a light and hits one of them; the other reaches the office untouched. The injured one faces months of care and lost wages. I think the community should make her whole, and I do not regard this as a hard case.

2. One of my brothers put his entire savings into a single company's stock; the other spread his across an index fund. The first has been wiped out. I do not think anyone owes him a bailout — that was his bet and he knew the odds. But if he were about to lose his house, I would say we have to keep him housed, and I would not call that mere charity. So option luck seems to matter to me, but not all the way down.

3. My cousin was born warm, quick, and tireless; she is promoted almost by accident. Her brother was born anxious and slow to warm up; he is passed over for the same jobs. Neither did anything to deserve his or her temperament. Yet I do not think the brother has a claim on her, or on the rest of us, in the way the injured driver does. I cannot articulate why not.

4. My neighbour's son was born with a condition that will cost a fortune to manage. I think of course we should cover it. So: unchosen disadvantage, unmistakable claim.

5. A friend of mine has ruinously expensive tastes — nothing short of rare wine and first-class seats will do — and he is miserable on an ordinary salary. He insists he never chose his palate; he was born with it and raised into it. I do not think we owe him a larger salary. But I notice he is making exactly the same move as my neighbour's son.

6. A friend chose her field for reasons that had nothing to do with money; she simply loved the work. The field then boomed and she is now rich. I think she is entitled to keep it. But I also think she did nothing to earn it, and if the boom had been foreseeable I would feel differently about her keeping it. That is odd, because the money is the same either way.

7. This is the case that started the argument. A man I know grew up in a household that drilled thrift into him and his brother in equal measure. But the brother had a long illness as a child and was indulged through it, so he never acquired the habit; he is now insecure and dependent. Same family, same lessons, same upbringing — except that the illness was unchosen, and it was the illness that shaped the habit that shaped the outcome. I feel the brother has a claim. Maya says this shows that chosen versus unchosen was never the point: what I am really tracking is whether someone can stand on their own feet as a member of the community, and the luck story is just the story I tell afterward.

8. A friend of mine is short. In our world this costs him very little; he is fine. But he points out that in a world built to seven-foot proportions he would be disabled, and in a world that prized height he would be unemployable — through no difference in him. Does the claim depend on the standards of the world he happens to live in? And if so, on whose?

I have a hunch about what I am tracking: that an unchosen disadvantage generates a claim when it leaves someone unable to stand as a full participant in the community, and not otherwise. But I cannot tell whether that is a principle or simply Maya's view restated, and it seems to give the wrong answer about my cousin's brother, who is passed over precisely because of something unchosen and about whom I nonetheless feel no claim is owed.

Tell me what principle, or small family of principles, actually fits these verdicts; for each clause, which case forces it; what it implies about a case I have not raised, such as a woman who trained for a trade that was valued when she entered it and worthless by the time she finished because other people's tastes moved on — she chose the training, but the devaluation was no doing of hers; and which of my verdicts I would have to give up if I wanted a cleaner account. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want to know what fits the cases, and whether the honest answer is that my instincts are incoherent and I should simply pick a standard position and accept its costs. If so, tell me which position and what it would cost me.
