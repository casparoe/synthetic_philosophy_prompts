I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Pascal's wager and we ended in the usual stalemate: he kept saying that any finite cost is worth an infinite expected payoff, and I kept saying that the argument does something illegitimate with infinity without being able to say precisely what. I want to see the whole thing rendered as a financial document, because the argument's shape — a small, certain cost staked against an unbounded, uncertain return — is exactly the kind of exposure a rating agency exists to price, and I suspect that translating it into that idiom will make the weak joints visible.

Please write a mock credit rating agency report, in the flat, self-assured register of a real ratings press release, in which the agency rates the wager as a financial instrument. Roughly 600 words: a headline grade, a short rationale, a numbered risk-factor section, and a closing outlook. The comedy should come entirely from getting the argument right — every joke should restate a genuine feature of the wager's structure or of a standard objection to it in the vocabulary of credit analysis, so that someone who has taught the argument recognizes each move instead of wincing at an error. The target is the argument, not any person; the agency should never let on that it knows it is rating a theological proposition.

At a minimum the report should have to confront:

- that the wager can be run as a dominance argument and as an expected-value argument, and that the two come apart once rival hypotheses are admitted, so the grade has to price a whole menu of infinite-payoff counterparties rather than one;
- what the model is supposed to output when it multiplies a payoff of infinite magnitude by a probability of zero, and whether a grade can be assigned when that product is undefined;
- that some of those counterparties carry infinite downside as well as infinite upside, so the valuation requires subtracting one infinity from another;
- that an agent who accepts infinite payoffs and the ordinary rules of preference can be assembled into a set of individually attractive bets that jointly guarantee a loss, and how the agency would classify that exposure;
- whether the instrument's value is even well-defined when the same space is partitioned in different ways, since the valuation appears to depend on the order in which the agency considers the alternatives;
- that the holder cannot acquire the required position by an act of will, which should surface as an eligibility or execution constraint rather than a belief;
- and that the payoff is specified only as infinite, which fixes no magnitude, so that two instruments advertising the same infinity cannot be ranked against each other.

The grade should be defensible from the material in the report, and the report should not settle the argument — it should leave a careful reader genuinely unsure whether the instrument is the best buy on the market or a structured product with a hole in the middle. Do not cite any literature or name any philosophers; write as if the agency has never heard of the debate and is simply doing its job.
