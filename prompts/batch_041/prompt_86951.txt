I am assembling a proposal for a one-semester, 6,000-word term paper in the philosophy of science, and I have a half-formed suspicion that I cannot yet state precisely, so I would like you to turn it into a proposal I could actually execute. Please write the proposal in American English. Do not supply citations or a reading list; I will fill those in myself, and I would rather you spend the space on the argument than on the literature.

My suspicion is that the fine-tuning debate has been conducted for decades in a vocabulary that conceals a distinction, and that once the distinction is drawn, much of the debate turns out to be about which of two quite different claims the participants are entitled to. The argument is normally presented as probabilistic: the values of certain physical parameters had to fall within a narrow range for a universe capable of complex structure, and eventually of observers, to exist, and the antecedent improbability of their falling there is taken as evidence for something beyond the parameters themselves. The standard reply is that the argument presupposes a probability distribution over the possible values, and that no such distribution is available or even well defined, since the parameter space may be unbounded and no physical process is said to have drawn the values at random. The standard rejoinder is that the argument needs less than this: only that the life-permitting region be small relative to the alternatives, which is a claim about the shape of a function rather than about a probability.

What I want to pry apart is the difference between two claims that both sides keep sliding between. The first is a fragility or sensitivity claim: hold the parameters fixed but for one, and ask how the capacity of the universe to support complex structure behaves as that one varies. That is a claim about a mathematical function and its neighbourhoods, and it can be true or false independently of any distribution. The second is an improbability claim: that the values we observe were unlikely, in the sense that some selection process, or some space of possibilities equipped with a measure, made them a small target. I suspect that everything the physics can support is the first claim, that everything the argument needs is the second, and that the step from one to the other is taken by using a single expression for two things: a property of a function and a property of an outcome. If that is right, then the usual replies and rejoinders are each answering a different question, and neither meets the other. I also suspect the multiverse reply is caught in the same equivocation from the opposite side: it needs the improbability claim in order to be a reply at all, yet the measure it needs is precisely the one it says is unavailable to its opponent.

Please write a proposal that: (1) states the research question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, and says what that answer would be in each of the main cases; (2) explains why the existing positions leave the question open, and identifies exactly which premise each position needs and which of my two claims it is entitled to; (3) sets out the method, which I expect to be conceptual rather than empirical — a regimentation of the two notions, a survey of the argumentative moves that require each, and a test of whether any version of the argument can be rebuilt on the fragility claim alone, for instance by making the explanandum the form of the laws rather than the values of the parameters, and asking whether the fragility claim can bear an inference to the best explanation without a covert appeal to a distribution; (4) says why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as simply restating the measure objection or cataloguing what the physics says; (5) says what a successful result would look like, what it would establish and how I would know I had it, and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the distinction collapses, that is, that every reading of the fragility claim which makes it both true and interesting turns out already to presuppose a measure; and (6) gives a realistic month-by-month plan for a single semester, with an honest assessment of where the project is most likely to outgrow its scope.

Assume the reader knows the argument in outline but has not thought about the distinction I am drawing. I am not attached to my suspicion being correct, so the proposal should make the best case for the project as a piece of work, not for the conclusion I happen to favour.
