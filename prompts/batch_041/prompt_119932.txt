I'm a novelist — two books in, and this one has dragged me into actual research for the first time. It's set around 1790, half in a Yorkshire mill town and half in a merchant household in Canton, and to write it honestly I need to know whether the thing my English characters take for granted was a near-run thing or a foregone conclusion, so I've been reading economic history about the so-called Great Divergence and I've hit a wall that's turned out to be more interesting than the wall I expected. The debate about why northwestern Europe — or England, or Britain, I can't even settle the unit, which I suspect is part of it — pulled ahead of the Yangzi delta seems to have been running for decades without anyone actually winning. One camp tells me it was late and contingent: coal, New World land and silver, a lucky break somewhere around 1750-1800, and before that the two regions were roughly comparable in wages and living standards, so the interesting question is why the break happened at all. Another camp says no, it was centuries in the making — property rights, how land and labour were organised, the whole institutional inheritance — and that looking at 1750 is looking at the last act. Then there's a third position that says the question itself is the problem: that asking why Britain rather than China takes two arbitrarily drawn units and a shared teleology (both on their way to industrial capitalism, one got there first) and quietly compares them, and that the real contrast should be between regions, or seperate trajectories, or should drop the counterfactual altogether. My historian friend says this is normal scholarly churn and I should just pick a version and write, but I don't think it is normal churn — in my notes I've been calling it the shifty explanandum, because each camp seems to quietly redefine what's being explained (when the divergence happened, what counts as 'ahead', what the comparison class is) and then the evidence can't adjudicate, since there's nothing shared left to test against. Though it's also possible I've just invented a fancier name for my own confusion.

So: diagnose it for me, don't settle it. Is this a substantive fight about facts, a fight about evidence and method (numbers vs narrative, national aggregates vs regional ones), a partly verbal fight where 'divergence' and 'ahead' mean different things to different people, or is the underlying why-question genuinely ill-posed in the way the third camp suggests — and if it's ill-posed, is it the units, the counterfactual, the implicit direction of causation, or something that generalises to every why-A-and-not-B question? Because I keep suspecting this is a general shape and not a quirk of this literature — why modern science there and not here, why capitalism there, why the industrial revolution there — and that the same impasse would show up in all of them, but I don't know enough to tell whether that's a real structural point or just me pattern-matching.
