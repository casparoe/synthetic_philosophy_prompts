I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy or neuroscience. I read popular science and I have been worrying at this subject on my own for a couple of years, so I may well be using words wrongly, and I would be grateful if you would quietly correct me where I do. Please take on the role of someone who knows both the philosophy of mind and the clinical literature on the divided brain thoroughly, and please think the whole thing through carefully before you commit to anything rather than reaching for whichever position comes most easily to hand. It might help to begin by getting clear about the different things one might mean by a single mind, and then to see which of those things my verdicts below are actually tracking. I would much rather see your reasoning worked out than a tidy verdict at the end.

The thing I keep circling is the patients whose two hemispheres have been cut apart. I have read various popular accounts and have come to a set of verdicts by gut, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

There is the patient whose left hand does up the buttons on his shirt while his right hand undoes them, or who finds one hand pulling the newspaper towards him while the other pushes it away. I think of that as one man with a civil war going on in his hands, which seems to me the same sort of thing as when I want another biscuit and also want not to have one.

Then there is the scene where a picture of a snowy field is shown to the half of the brain that cannot speak and a picture of a chicken's claw to the half that can; asked to choose the picture that goes with what he has just seen, his left hand picks a shovel and his right hand picks a chicken, and when he is asked why his left hand went for the shovel he says that it was to clear out the chicken shed. My verdict is that the explanation was invented on the spot, that he was not lying, and that it was not a second person doing the talking either.

Then there are the patients whose silent half seems to have tastes of its own, liking different pictures or different music, so that the half that talks is taken aback when the other hand reaches for something it did not want. I think there is something in there with preferences, but I still would not say that there are two people in the chair.

Then there is the patient who, some weeks after the operation, says that he feels just as he did before, one person, with no sense that anything is missing. I think that tells us almost nothing, because the only mouth in the room belongs to one of the halves.

And then there is the case I have made up, which is the one I cannot settle. Suppose the two halves could reach each other only by typing, slowly, and that after a few years they had come to disagree about his wife, about politics, and about which of them was the real one. I cannot decide whether to call that one person with a badly divided mind or two people sharing a body.

For contrast: an ordinary person with an inner voice that nags at them, or who says that part of them wants to go and part of them wants to stay. One person, obviously.

I have been trying to separate two questions that I suspect I have been running together: how many streams of experience there are, and how many people there are, meaning by people the sort of thing that can be argued with, blamed, and that has a life to lead. My hunch is that the split-brain cases push the first number up and leave the second one alone, and that most of my verdicts above are really verdicts about the second question, which is why they do not feel to me like verdicts about minds at all. But I may simply be cheating, and saying one person only because there is only one mouth.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of principles, that best fits the verdicts above. State it as precisely as you can, and for each part of it tell me which of my cases forces that part. Then tell me what it implies for the case I cannot settle, and for two or three cases I have not mentioned at all. Finally, be honest with me about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save; I would rather give one up than keep a principle that needs a patch to survive. I would also like to know whether the word unity is doing any real work when people talk about the unity of consciousness, or whether it is only a name for the fact that there is normally nobody else in here to disagree with.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, and no tables. Use British English. And please do not give me citations, paper titles or a reading list, since I have no way of looking anything up and would only be taking your word for it; I would rather you simply told me what you think and why.
