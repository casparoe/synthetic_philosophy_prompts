I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I need a poem to open the first session of a reading group on the knowability paradox — the result that converts "every truth can be known" into "every truth is known." I'll state the argument in the prompt itself, so that the poem can stand on the reasoning rather than on your recall of anyone's version of it.

Assume two things: knowledge is factive (if someone knows that p, then p), and knowing a conjunction entails knowing each conjunct. Now take a witness of the form "p, and no one knows that p." That proposition is knowably unknowable: to know it you would have to know both that p and that no one knows that p, and by factivity the second conjunct delivers "no one knows that p," contradicting the first. So no truth of that form can be known. But if every truth can be known, there can be no truth of that form, and then there are no unknown truths at all — the principle that truth cannot outrun knowledge converts, in a single step, into the claim that knowledge already covers everything.

I want a poem that performs this rather than describes it, with the form carrying the weight. Specifically:

- One refrain, recurring as the final line of each of five or six stanzas, altered as little as possible on each return — a word, or even a morpheme — so that the poem travels from a statement of the knowability principle at the first return to the conclusion that everything true is known at the last, and a reader could reconstruct the proof from the drift of that single line. Nothing else should repeat. My own best guess is that this turns on "unknowable" sliding into "unknown," which is precisely the collapse the argument performs; but take another path if you see a better one.
- At one of the returns, the verb should be swapped out — "known" for "proved" or "verified" — and the collapse should arrive anyway, so that a reader notices nothing in the argument depends on knowledge in particular, only on a verb that is factive and distributes over conjunction.
- The speaker is the witness itself: a truth that no one knows and that can never be known. It is being said in a form that asserts nothing, which is the only way it could be said at all. Let the poem feel that as a constraint rather than a trick, and let the last stanza be its erasure — if everything true is known, there is no such speaker, so the final refrain should conclude the argument and take away the poem's own voice at the same time.
- Don't settle which premise is at fault. The group split over whether the knowability principle or the distribution of knowledge over conjunction is the culprit, and I'd like the last line to be readable both as the argument's conclusion and as the witness's last word.
- No names, no citations, no footnotes; the poem has to stand on the argument alone. Someone who knows the paradox should be able to point to each step and not wince, and someone who has never heard of it should still find a poem.
