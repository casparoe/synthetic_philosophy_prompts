I'm a philosophy graduate student, and for the past four years I've also been part of a tenants'-rights campaign in my city — a deliberately loose, non-aligned assembly with no officers, no whip and no membership card, where decisions get made by whoever turns up to the union hall on a Thursday and argues well enough to carry the room. Last spring we helped three independents onto the council, and now a faction within the group wants to take what it calls the obvious next step: register as an official political party, so that we can run a single slate under one banner, collect dues, qualify for public funding and stop splitting the vote. I have been asked to give the closing address at our annual general meeting, and I want to use it to argue against the move. What has made me hesitate is a late essay of Simone Weil's, written in London in 1943 and published only after her death, in which she treats a political party as, by its very nature, a machine for manufacturing collective passion: an organisation whose real business is to bind its members into one shared feeling rather than to think, which cannot function without an adversary to hate, and whose ultimate aim is simply its own unlimited growth — so that loyalty to the party line slowly displaces loyalty to the truth, and a member ends up obliged to say what serves the party rather than what he believes. She goes further than I am willing to go, since she wanted parties abolished outright and I doubt that is a live option in a modern democracy; but it is the diagnosis, not the remedy, that unsettles me, because I can watch our own group beginning to show the symptoms — the same handful of phrases in every meeting, a growing appetite for enemies, and a new reluctance to say anything that might split the room.

What I need from you is the speech itself: roughly six to eight minutes spoken aloud, written to be delivered rather than read as an essay. It has to land in a room that is genuinely divided. Perhaps half the audience are experienced organisers and a couple of sitting councillors who understand exactly how electoral machinery works and will be embarrassed by any naivety; the other half are tenants and neighbours who joined because a landlord ignored them and who have no interest in political theory. Weil's argument must do structural work rather than serve as ornament: it should be the reason the speech cannot simply propose a friendlier, better-run party, and it should also constrain how the speech is written, since a speech that wins the room by whipping up enthusiasm would be doing precisely what Weil warns against — I would like you to confront that difficulty rather than step around it. Please also have the speech meet, in its own idiom, the strongest objection in the room: that without a party we will go on losing the very seats that decide whether families are evicted.

After the speech, add a short note addressed to me rather than to the audience, saying how confident you are in the reading of Weil you have built into it, and singling out the one point you are least sure of — whether that is an interpretive claim about her, an empirical assumption about how parties actually behave, or a doubt that the speech's anti-party stance can be held without collapsing into the very thing it opposes.
