I'm putting together a case for something I'm writing on Frege's puzzle — the whole thing about how "a = b" can tell you something while "a = a" doesn't, even when a and b are the same thing. But I'm sick of Hesperus and Phosphorus, and Clark Kent, and Twain/Clemens, and Tully/Cicero — everyone and their dog has seen those, and I want something that a model can't just pattern-match to a stock example.

So build me a new one, to these specs. Ordinary, realistic setting — no twin earth, no fictional planets, no sci-fi. One paragraph for the scenario itself. Two proper names for one and the same real individual, and the key thing: both names have to get into the subject's head by direct introduction — she's introduced to the person under each name, learns it as a name, and that's it. Neither name can be a disguised description, and she mustn't have any description that uniquely picks the person out under either name (no "the guy who fixed my boiler" type handle — nothing that would let a descriptivist say the name just abbreviates a definite description). She should have a decent chunk of separate information about the person under each name, and never once suspect the two are the same, so that when she finds out it genuinely lands as news. She's not confused or irrational — perfectly sharp, in fact. And I want the news to be a posteriori: it's a real discovery, not something she could've reasoned her way to from what she already knew.

Now the analysis, please, in this order:

First, the scenario. Then take three theories and say what each predicts about (a) the referent of the two names and (b) what is said — the proposition expressed — by "a = b" as opposed to "a = a": (1) a Millian / direct-reference line where a name contributes only its referent, (2) a Fregean line where each name carries a sense or mode of presentation, and (3) a descriptivist / mediated-reference line where the name's content is descriptive. Make clear where the three agree and where they split, and specifically which ones agree on the referent while still disagreeing about what is said.

Then: which theory actually accounts for the fact that the discovery is informative, and how — and if you think the informativeness is being explained away rather than explained, say so. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens to the whole thing if I remove that feature (I want to know if it just collapses back into a boring descriptivist case). Finally, pick whichever of the three fares worst on this case and write me the strongest comeback a defender of it would make — where they'd say I've rigged the setup or smuggled something in.
