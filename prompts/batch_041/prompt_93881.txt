I convene a graduate reading group on aggregation in ethics and political philosophy, and we have now spent three sessions failing to agree about the utility monster — the case, introduced by Nozick, of a being whose capacity for welfare so far exceeds anyone else's that ranking outcomes by total welfare requires the rest of us to be sacrificed to it. Our difficulty is not that we disagree about the conclusion; it is that we disagree about what the case is a case *of*. I would like a decision tree that sorts responses to the monster into positions, and I want it organized around the question our discussions never make explicit: where the respondent locates the argument's force — in the theory's axiology, in its rule for aggregating across persons, in its conception of welfare, or in its practical usability.

A few requirements.

Root and framing. Begin from the commitment the respondent is presumed to share — that in the stipulated case the sum of welfare (or whatever the theory takes the good to be) is maximized by the sacrifice — and branch by what the respondent denies. Build the space first and the positions second.

Phrasing. Every node must be a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to: a verdict about a concrete case, or a claim about what the case shows. No node may name a position or an -ism, and no node may simply ask whether one is, say, a prioritarian.

Cases. These must be used; add others if you need them.

(a) The classic monster, with a capacity for welfare large enough to outweigh the rest of us no matter how the numbers are distributed.
(b) A bounded monster, whose capacity is very large but finite, so that no transfer is ever actually forced.
(c) The reverse case: a being of enormous capacity for suffering, so that the same structure cuts against a view that treats the relief of suffering as the only thing that matters, and appears to demand the sacrifice of everyone's modest goods.
(d) A parasitic monster, whose bliss is constituted by others' losses, as against one that merely consumes resources others might have used.
(e) A composite monster that is itself a population, so that "sacrificing the rest of us to it" is itself an aggregation.
(f) The marginal monster: a policy that harms very many people slightly and benefits one enormously, with the numbers calibrated so that a sum-ranking view must accept it while a view that gives priority to the worse off need not.

Leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The following must appear somewhere: total utilitarianism that accepts the conclusion; the impossibility response, split into an empirical and a conceptual version if you think those are different positions; average utilitarianism; prioritarianism; a lexical or leximin rule; sufficientarianism; the family of views that reject the demand rather than the ranking, via agent-relative constraints or prerogatives; the person-affecting restriction; value pluralism or incommensurability; Moorean value holism, on which the whole is not the sum of its parts; an objective-list axiology; the epistemic response, on which the case is idle because the relevant magnitudes cannot be measured; and the meta-response that the case begs the question against hedonism. Merge any of these you judge not to be genuinely distinct, and say why.

Unoccupied combinations. Tell me which combinations of answers nothing occupies, and diagnose each: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Inseparables. Flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say which further case would separate them.

Scale. At most four levels of branching, and twelve to sixteen leaves.

Finally, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading of the monster as the consensus. Where a step in your tree depends on a disputed interpretation of what the case is meant to show, mark it as disputed rather than folding it into the structure. No citations, reading lists, or attributions to named authors are needed; where you would want to cite someone, describe the position instead, and if you do name anyone, do not guess at a title or a date.
