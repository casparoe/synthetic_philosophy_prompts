I'm in my second year and I've somehow ended up on this module about the ethics of persuasion — it's cross-listed between philosophy and marketing, so half the seminar are business students and half are PPE types, and the arguements have got genuinely strange. Last week we were on targeted political advertising and one of the marketing lot just waved the whole objection away with "manipulation is just persuasion that works" — the idea being that if it changed your mind then it worked, and calling it manipulation is just sour grapes from whoever lost the argument. A few people nodded along. I wanted to push back but I couldn't work out what he was actually claiming, and then the lecturer muddied it further by saying manipulation is influence that "bypasses" the target's rational agency — which sounds precise until you try to pin it down. Does it mean it goes round your reasoning altogether, or distorts it from the inside, or just withholds information you'd have wanted?

What I'd like is a proper menu of the distinct precise claims "manipulation is just persuasion that works" could be making, running from the boringly true to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each: which are trivial, which are substantive and actually defensible, which are just false, and which one my marketing mate most likely meant. And while you're at it, spell out the different things "bypasses rational agency" might mean, since the lecturer leaned on the phrase as if it settled things — does it need the manipulator to intend the bypass, does it matter whether the target would have consented to it, and does the wrongness (if there is any) live in the process or in the outcome? Lay the readings out as a list I can take to office hours, and for each one tell me whether it would actually support shrugging off the political-ads objection, because I suspect the slogan is being made to do far more work than it can cash.
