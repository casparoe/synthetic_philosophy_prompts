My friend and I went at it for about two hours last night about bugs. His line: there are something like ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, so even if the odds that any one of them is sentient are tiny — one in a hundred, one in a thousand, whatever — the expected suffering out there in the insect world swamps every human who has ever lived, and we should be reorganizing our entire lives around beetles. My line was that you can't just multiply a probability by a headcount like that. Then he asked me why not, and I discovered I had nothing.

So help me, but not with the argument. I want the spec first. What would a theory of moral status — or really a theory of when a creature's experiences count for anything — actually have to deliver before either of us could even tell whether his arithmetic is the right shape of move? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right (the beetle, the shrimp, the fish, the dog, the sleeping person, the person under anesthesia, and maybe something with no biology at all, if the theory is supposed to be about minds rather than carbon), the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (having a mind versus having a mind that can be hurt; how sure we are that there's a subject there versus how much that subject's welfare matters; a threshold versus a dial), and the facts about very small nervous systems it can't just wave away. With each requirement, why it's a requirement and not just something one camp happens to like.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these can't all be true at once, if you think some can't, and which single requirement does the most work at ruling candidates out. Skip the scorecard, I don't know the theories well enough for it to help me.

Keep it under 700 words, and lead with the bottom line — up front, before the list: is headcount-times-probability even the right kind of thing to be doing, or does the whole move need replacing?
