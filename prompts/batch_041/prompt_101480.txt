so I'm supposed to lead a session on Dennett for our reading group and I want to use Memento as the test case, because I keep flip-flopping on whether the film proves his point or quietly wrecks it.

The idea is Dennett's: the self isn't a little thing in the head that does the narrating, it's the center of narrative gravity of the story — an abstraction that's real the way a center of gravity is real, a useful posit and not a thing. Which means the self is the character, not the author. Leonard looks like a gift for this. No continuous memory, so the self has to be built out of tattoos, Polaroids, notes — the narrative is literally external, on his skin. And then the reveal is that he lies to his future self, deliberately, to give himself a reason to keep going. So the self isn't just a story, it's a story you keep editing.

Two things I want out of the reading. First: does Memento back Dennett or break him? If the self really is just a center of narrative gravity, Leonard is as much a self as you or me, and the film almost says that, which I find genuinely unsettling. But he's also being authored from the outside (Teddy, Natalie) and by his own past self, so the character-not-author line gets strange — he's a character who keeps rewriting his own script. Is that a counterexample to Dennett, or exactly what Dennett should have predicted?

Second: I've been reading Galen Strawson, who seperates people into diachronic (they live their life as an ongoing story) and episodic (no thread, just episodes — and he insists that's a fine way to be, not a deficit). Leonard seems like the perfect test case and also like a refutation: no memory continuity, which sounds episodic, yet he's the most compulsively narrative character you could invent. So does the film show that narrative selfhood doesn't actually need memory at all — that the story stands in for memory rather than growing out of it? And if that's right, whose picture does it favour?

Please give me a real reading, not the film used as a diagram. Where does Memento resist both Dennett and Strawson, and does it come out saying the narrative self is basically benign, or that it can be a trap you set for yourself? Finish by saying what the film adds to this debate that the philosophy doesn't.
