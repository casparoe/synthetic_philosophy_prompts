I'm in a seminar on epistemic logic and we've been doing the standard possible-worlds thing where an agent believes P just in case P holds in every world she hasn't ruled out — which means she believes every logical truth, believes every logical consequence of what she believes, and has an infinite, and for any decent axiom system not even decidable, belief set. When someone raised the obvious objection — no actual person believes all the consequences of what they believe, and half of them would take a lifetime to derive anyway — the professor waved it off with an analogy I cant stop chewing on: every theory idealizes, he said, the ideal gas law pretends molecules have no volume and don't attract each other, the frictionless plane doesn't exist, nobody thinks mechanics is refuted by the fact that you can't actually build one, and logical omniscience is the same move — a tractable model of the ideal reasoner, with real reasoners being the ideal one plus finite memory, finite time, and a bit of noise. My friend in the room thinks that's a swindle, that the analogy is being made to carry something it can't carry, and I honestly can't tell who's right.

So I'd like an audit of it. First the correspondence table — what plays the role of the molecules, of the friction, of the idealization's being a limit you can approach, of the law being roughly true of real systems, and of the fact that in physics the idealization is a claim about how a system behaves rather than about what an agent is doing right or wrong? Then where the mapping breaks, and which breaks actually matter for what the professor was using it for (shutting the objection down) as opposed to being incidental. Here's the part I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than wave past it: with friction you can dial the nuisance toward zero and watch the system converge on the ideal, but I can't see what plays that role for closure. Someone with more time and more memory doesn't approach believing all the consequences of her beliefs, she just believes more of them, and at every finite stage there's a consequence she hasn't got, and for some of them she'll never get there no matter how long you give her. And you can maybe rank reasoners by how much of the closure they've got, but that's a partial order, not a dial — there's no "0.3 of the way to believing a theorem". Does the analogy need the ideal to be approachable, or is it enough that it's definable? Because if approachability is load-bearing, the friction picture is in trouble and something else has to do the work.

That something else: a friend suggested the ideal reasoner is less like a frictionless plane and more like a perfect circle — a norm no drawn circle meets, and not one that drawing better circles converges on either, since every drawn circle is imperfect and the ideal lives in a different register altogether. Does that do better, or does it just relocate the problem by quietly converting a descriptive claim into a normative one — and if it does, does the professor's "stop worrying, it's a harmless idealization" conclusion still follow, or has the analogy swapped out what was being claimed? Is there a third analogy that does the job better than either? And give me a straight answer on this too: the objection being dismissed isn't just that real agents are imperfect, it's that the idealization makes the theory unable to state something we obviously want to state — that someone can believe P and fail to believe its blatant consequence — so a real reasoner isn't the ideal one minus some friction, she's a different kind of thing. Is that a fair way to put it, and if so does any version of the idealization analogy survive it?
