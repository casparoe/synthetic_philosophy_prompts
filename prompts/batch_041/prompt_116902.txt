I am preparing a recorded long-form interview for a graduate seminar series, and my guest is a philosopher of physics who has spent several years defending two connected claims. The first is that Thomson's lamp is not a puzzle with a hidden answer but a case in which no question has been asked: the setup fixes the lamp's state at every time strictly before the end of the sequence and says nothing about the end itself, so the state at the end is left free rather than merely unknown. The second is that supertasks of this kind are not only logically possible but physically realizable — he regards the relativistic scenarios, of the sort discussed under the heading of Malament–Hogarth spacetimes, in which one observer completes infinitely many operations while a second observer waits out a finite interval, as more than a mathematical curiosity, and he believes their possibility bears on what we should say about computation and prediction.

Draft nine to eleven interview questions, ordered so that the pressure builds rather than repeats, and give each one a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The sequence should press on the following, roughly in this order:

- whether "the description leaves the final state free" is a claim about descriptions or about the world — that is, whether an incomplete specification shows that the lamp has no state at all, or only that we have not said which state it has, and what the difference amounts to here;
- whether his diagnosis generalizes further than he would like: any quantity fixed as the limit of a convergent sequence, such as a particle's terminal position or the endpoint of a contracting interval, is fixed by a specification of exactly the same kind, so what is supposed to distinguish the lamp;
- the standing objection that the puzzle is Zeno's paradox read backwards, and that anyone who accepts that infinitely many tasks can sum to a finite one has already conceded the supertask;
- whether the lamp's state at the end could be settled by stipulation — a convention about which of the two possible completions to adopt — and whether that would trivialize the puzzle or instead vindicate his diagnosis;
- what the physical realizability claim actually requires in the way of infinite energy, unbounded acceleration, or an inexhaustible supply of toggles, and whether any of those requirements is doing genuine philosophical work or whether realizability is a red herring;
- what becomes of the notion of a definite output once infinite computations are on the table;
- and, toward the end, what evidence or argument would lead him to abandon the realizability claim, and what he takes to follow for ordinary limit-taking in physics if the ill-formedness diagnosis is correct.

Avoid anything that would let him simply restate the position or recount his intellectual biography; the value of the interview lies in the objections he has to handle.
