I run deliberative panels for a city council - citizens juries, sort of a mini-public thing, 20-40 people, six or eight of them a year. At the end I write up what the panel wants, it goes into a public report with my name on it, and the council actually acts on it. My problem is that I keep having to make a call I'm not qualified to make. When a panel says something about what should happen to other people, I have to decide whether that's a preference I faithfully record or a bias I should be pushing back on, and the two look identicle from where I'm sitting.

Some examples of the thing that ties me in knots. A panel from an estate with terrible buses tells me the buses are fine, they never expected better - is that a preference, or is that someone who's given up? A panel comes out against a hostel for ex-offenders, after the local paper ran a scary story the week before we met - is that a considered view about safety, or an artifact of what I let into the room? Someone tells me the current system works great and I'm fairly sure they're just telling me what they think the room wants to hear. And every so often a panel says something I personally think is just prejudice, and I notice the only reason I'm calling it prejudice is that I disagree with it.

So what I want is a procedure I can actually run, in the room, in the ninety minutes I've got, with no follow-up interviews and no budget to re-convene anyone. I dont have any training in philosophy and neither do my co-facilitators, so it has to work for someone with no theory. Ground it in whatever is actually known about this stuff - how people's wants get shaped by the options they've had, why the fact that a belief came from somewhere doesn't settle whether it's any good, the difference between someone being factually wrong and someone valuing something I don't. No reading list please, I wont read it.

Here's the shape I want. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops and says fine, record it, where it says stop, flag it, and the specific thing to do about it. And be honest about the points where there's no procedure left and a person just has to decide, and say what kind of person-decision that is.

Two constraints, because otherwise I'll cheat. First, I can never drop a view from the report - I can annotate it, weight it, explain it, but it goes in. So the procedure can't end in "ignore this one". Second, if it flags something, the fix has to be the least intrusive one that works, because I don't want to be in the business of correcting people's values. So build me a ladder: cheapest intervention first, be explicit about when to climb to the next rung, and tell me what the top rung is (I suspect it shouldn't be me overriding anybody). Also put in whatever check you can that catches me doing this to the views I happen to dislike, since I'm the one holding the pen.

Then run it on three cases: the bus panel, the ex-offenders panel, and a third where a participant's view rests on a factual claim I could have corrected on the day but didn't know how to raise. At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like. I'd rather find that out now than in the report.

After the prose, give me a JSON summary I can paste into my case notes, with exactly these fields:

- "case": short label
- "stated_view": the panel's view, one line
- "trigger": which question in your procedure raised the flag, or "none"
- "verdict": the outcome, using one of the category names your procedure defines
- "remedy": the rung of the ladder and the concrete action
- "judgment_call": true or false, and if true, what the human has to decide
- "confidence": "low", "medium" or "high", plus one clause on what would change it
- "note_for_report": one or two sentences I'd actually write in the report
