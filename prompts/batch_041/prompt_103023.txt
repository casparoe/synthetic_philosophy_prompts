I'm a software engineer rather than a philosopher, and I've been re-reading Asimov's Foundation books with a science-fiction book club. It seems to me that Hari Seldon's psychohistory is precisely the kind of science of history that Popper attacks under the heading of historicism, and I'd like a reading of the trilogy through that critique. Is Seldon a historicist in Popper's sense — someone who holds that there are laws of historical development licensing unconditional long-range prophecy, as against the conditional, if-this-then-that predictions that Popper thinks are the most the social sciences can offer? And does the trilogy end up dramatising Popper's objections or merely patching the theory, given that the Mule arrives as an event the law failed to foresee and the Second Foundation's answer to him turns on the manipulation of a single mind? Two smaller things I keep turning over. Popper suggests that historicism slides into a picture of society as something secretly engineered by hidden hands, and the Second Foundation is literally a hidden hand steering the galaxy, so is the novel literalising the very picture Popper says historicism presupposes? And because the Second Foundation can guide events only while it stays hidden, I wonder whether psychohistory is a prediction that works only by concealing itself, and whether that counts as a concession to Popper or a genuine counter-example. Please take both the books and the critique seriously rather than reducing either to an illustration of the other, and skip the citations — I have no way to check them. Finally, tell me where this reading is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind about it: what would have to be true in the text, or in Popper, for the claim that Seldon is a Popperian historicist to collapse.
