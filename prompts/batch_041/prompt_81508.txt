I teach an upper-level seminar on moral and political philosophy (about fifteen students, one 90-minute session). Design a role-play that lets the group feel from the inside how a widely accepted norm can persist and then collapse without anyone's moral argument getting any better, and how the collapse can turn on honor and material interest rather than on the stronger case. Choose a norm of the kind whose abolition historians explain in rival ways — through moral persuasion, through a shift in economic interest, or through a change in what it is honorable to do — and split the participants into factions with distinct material stakes and distinct amounts of public standing. Arrange the rules so that whoever argues the best moral case still loses the room while upholding the norm is still honorable, and so that the norm can fall only once enough participants reclassify the previously shameful refusal as the honorable course; when it falls, let it be able to fall suddenly rather than by degrees. Give me the roles, the standing-and-interest mechanism, the round structure, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the three rival explanations and explains why the participants' own experience of the session cannot by itself show which explanation is historically correct. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of real moral revolutions — the actual evidence about why people stopped doing the thing — the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
