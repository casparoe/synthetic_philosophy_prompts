I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I want your credence in one precisely stated proposition, together with the decomposition that yields it. I don't want the answer to stop at 'it depends on your theory of personal identity'; I want the theory-dependence quantified.

The case. A subject undergoes staged substrate replacement. Over roughly six months, her neurons are removed one at a time and each is replaced by a silicon unit. Every unit is functionally equivalent to the neuron it displaces: it occupies the same position in the network, receives and emits the same signals, and reproduces that neuron's input-output profile to within measurement error. Its internal states, however, are a compressed functional surrogate and bear no resemblance to the states of the neuron it replaced. The subject is awake and behaving normally throughout; at no stage does she lose consciousness, and her memories, dispositions, and self-reports remain continuous. By the end, none of her original neurons remain.

The proposition, P1: the individual who completes the procedure is numerically identical to the individual who began it.

Please give me:

1. Your credence in P1, built from an explicit decomposition. I would expect the terms to include at least these, though you should add or drop terms as you judge best: whether personal identity is determinate in a case of this kind at all, or is vague or a matter of degree (if you think P1 has no truth value, report your probability for 'no fact of the matter' as a separate line rather than folding it into the main number); whether identity consists in the preservation of some continuity relation and, if so, which one — spatiotemporal or causal continuity of the same physical substrate, informational continuity, functional or organizational continuity, or psychological continuity; and the probability that the protocol preserves whichever relation you take to be constitutive, with particular attention to whether a behaviorally matched surrogate preserves it or severs it.

2. How the terms combine, and which term your final number is most sensitive to.

3. How your number would move under a variant in which each surrogate unit is pre-computed — programmed in advance to replay a trajectory simulated from the original neuron rather than responding to its ongoing inputs.

4. Which single sub-question most likely explains why my own credence is about 0.4 and a colleague's is about 0.9, and what evidence or argument would move your number most.
