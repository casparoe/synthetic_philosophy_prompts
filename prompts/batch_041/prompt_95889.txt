I am a high school junior and a member of my school's philosophy club, and I am writing an essay for our year-end prize on the Principal Principle, the claim that a rational agent's credences should defer to objective chance. I have arrived at a conclusion that I suspect is either obviously correct or seriously confused, and I cannot tell which. My adviser, who teaches history and is not a specialist, suggested that I ask someone who knows the technical debate to comment on the argument rather than on the prose, so I would be grateful for detailed criticism. Please do not answer with a reading list; I have no way of tracking down journal articles, and what I need is an assessment of whether the argument stands on its own. The essay, in outline, runs as follows.

1. Statement of the principle. If I know that the objective chance of a given outcome is p, and I possess no further information that the chance itself does not already take into account, then my credence in that outcome ought to be p. Chance is supposed to be a guide to what to believe, so deferring to it is not optional. I would like to know whether a specialist would accept this as a fair statement of the principle or whether I have left out something essential.

2. My counterexample. I devised the following case myself, though I doubt it is original. A device, which I call the Machine, has never once been wrong. It tells me two things: that the objective chance of rain tomorrow is one half, and that it will in fact rain tomorrow. Taken at face value, the principle says that my credence in rain should be one half, because that is the chance. But the Machine is infallible, so my credence should be one. I conclude that the principle as stated is false, and that it requires a qualification restricting it to cases in which my other information is of the kind that the chance already takes into account.

3. Why I think that qualification is circular. To be told that I should defer to the chance unless I hold further relevant information is just to be told that I should defer unless I should not, since the entire question is which further information is relevant. I cannot see how to specify the permitted class of information without referring back to the chance, which is precisely what is in dispute.

4. A further difficulty. Suppose the objective chance of heads is one half. It is then possible, though wildly improbable, that a long series of tosses lands heads every time. But if what the chances are is settled by the overall pattern of what actually happens, then in a world where that improbable series occurs, the chance would not have been one half; the outcome would have undone the very chance that assigned it a low probability. A chance hypothesis, on this view, can be undermined by its own consequences. I take this to be a reason for doubting that a chance is a fact about the world of the sort the principle requires it to be.

5. Conclusion. Combining 2 through 4, I argue that the Principal Principle is not a substantive rule of rationality but a definition of objective chance: it tells us what we mean in calling something a chance, namely, the thing to which credence ought to defer. On this reading the principle is true by stipulation, and the substantive work is done by whatever independent account of chance one adopts. My adviser says this is a recognizable position and that the philosopher who first formulated the principle later revised it, but I have not read that work, so I do not know whether my version of the point matches his or merely confuses it.

I would be grateful for comments on the following: (a) whether my statement in 1 is accurate, and what it omits; (b) whether the Machine case shows what I claim, or whether it only reveals that I have misunderstood the qualification; (c) whether the circularity objection in 3 can be answered, and if so, how, in terms I can follow without further reading; (d) whether the undermining worry in 4 is a real problem or a confusion about what kind of fact a chance is; and (e) whether the conclusion in 5 is defensible, or whether it collapses into something trivial. If the essay is wrong, I would rather be told exactly where than encouraged, and I would like to know which single revision would most improve it.
