For roughly fifteen years I have been a daily reader of the rationalist blogs and their descendant forums — the sequences and their commentary, the decision-theory and effective-altruism spinoffs — long enough that I now parse most philosophical disputes in that idiom, looking first for the crux and the steelman and only then for the object-level claim. Next month I am giving a talk, and I want you to build me a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense.

The view I am defending is this. The drowning-child argument, in the form everyone knows, is standardly taken to turn on cost, distance, or the identifiability of the victim, and the familiar ladder of cases — shrink the pond to a puddle, move the child to another country, replace her with a hundred children or a statistical projection — is built to pry those three variables apart. I think all three are red herrings and that the intuition is tracking something else: the closure of the causal chain. In the pond case there is a single, token-level act of mine that terminates, verifiably and inside the same scene, in a single token outcome — the child breathing on the bank. The chain is short, singular, and closed, and I claim it is that closure, and not the cost or the proximity or the nameable victim, that does the intuitive work. If I am right, then the standard ladder probes the wrong variable, and the cases that actually discriminate are the ones that hold chain-closure fixed while letting cost and distance float, and the ones that break chain-closure while holding cost and proximity fixed. I have a handful of such cases worked out, and I will spend the middle of the talk on them.

The audience is roughly thirty people. A solid core are committed effective altruists who donate a fixed fraction of income and reason fluently in QALYs per dollar; several are LessWrong-style decision theorists who will be quick to formalize anything I say and just as quick to notice when the formalism is doing no work; two are graduate students in the philosophy department who work on deontology and will treat my whole framing as a warmed-over consequentialist debunking dressed up in new vocabulary; and one runs a small direct-aid charity, is allergic to theory, and will want to know what any of it changes about where the next thousand dollars goes. They do not agree with one another and they will not object in the same voice.

Give me the prep sheet I actually need for the question period: the eight or so objections this room will really raise — not the ones I would get from a journal referee or from a general audience — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a frank note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, before anyone has to drag it out of me, and what I should refuse to concede even under pressure. Flag any objection that is really the same objection wearing two costumes, and any that I am likely to mistake for hostility when it is actually a genuine problem. Include the one question I am hoping nobody in the room asks. I do not want citations, a reading list, or references to the literature; I want the objections, their order, my replies, and the weak joints. Keep the whole thing in the vocabulary this audience will bring.

End with a single line giving your overall verdict on the talk's survivability: name the one objection that, if I fumble it, sinks the argument, in the form \verdict{...} containing a short phrase.
