I teach a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and every year we spend a session on the Condorcet jury theorem — the result that a group whose members are each more likely than not to be right will, under suitable conditions, be overwhelmingly likely to reach the correct verdict by majority vote as the group grows. My students find it uplifting. I find myself, every year, trying to articulate a pattern I keep meeting outside the seminar and for which I have never found a satisfactory name.

The pattern is this. Take a group whose members are genuinely competent and who genuinely vote their own honest, considered judgments — no deference, no intimidation, no wish to please the room. All the same, the group is far less reliable than the theorem would lead one to expect, and enlarging it does not help. The reason is that something upstream has put them all in the same starting position: a single briefing memo, a shared dataset, one instrument with the same miscalibration, a common training that supplies the same default framing, a piece of background lore that happens to be false. Each member still reasons well from what they have; it is simply that what they have is not independent, so their concurrence is no longer a run of separate confirmations. In the worst case the group grows more confident — near-unanimous — exactly when it is most exposed, because the agreement is being produced by the shared input rather than by the truth. I have watched this happen in hiring committees that vote only after the chair circulates a summary, in laboratories that share a reagent, and in reading groups where everyone has absorbed the same secondary source.

What I want from you is not a bibliography but a conceptual map. Is there an established name for this failure mode — for the specific fact that consensus stops tracking accuracy once the consensus has a common cause, while the individual members remain competent and unpressured? If there is one, tell me exactly what it covers and, more important, what it is not: how it differs from groupthink, which I take to be about social pressure to conform; from information cascades and herding, where people infer from others' choices rather than from their own information; from the echo-chamber and filter-bubble diagnoses, which seem to concern the exclusion or discrediting of outside sources; and from plain shared bias in a population. If the literature treats this only as a violation of the theorem's independence assumption rather than as a phenomenon in its own right, say so, and then coin a term: give me a definition, the conditions under which it obtains, and at least one boundary case where it is unclear whether the term applies. I care much less about the label than about the carving — whether the thing is best described probabilistically, as correlated errors; causally, as a common cause; or epistemically, as shared evidence — and whether those three descriptions can come apart. A bare name will not help me.
