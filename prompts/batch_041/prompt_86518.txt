ok so I'm putting together a seminar handout on cognitive penetration and I keep going in circles, so I want the desiderata first, before anyone starts defending a position. i.e. what does a theory of cognitive penetration actually have to get right before we're allowed to call it adequate? not just "does it respect modularity" -- I want the full list of requirements, each one tied to the case or finding that motivates it, and a note on whether it's a genuine requirement or just a commitment of one camp.

stuff I want the list to cover, roughly: what the theory has to say about cases where you know the illusion is an illusion and it still looks that way (Muller-Lyer); the duck/rabbit thing, where you can flip it at will but you can't see it as both at once; whether memory colour counts (a grey banana looking slightly yellowish) and whether phoneme restoration is penetration or just inference filling in; whether the same theory has to handle the wine that tastes better when it costs more; and how it separates synchronic penetration from the slow stuff (perceptual learning, expertise) without just waving at time.

two requirements I want you to build in, because I think theyre where the interesting fights are:

1. an arbitration requirement -- perception has to stay enough of a shared given that two people with different beliefs can still be brought to agree by looking. so the theory can't make perceptual content so belief-soaked that looking settles nothing.
2. a no-double-booking requirement -- for any given effect, the theory has to pick whether it's penetration or post-perceptual judgement / response bias and stick with it, instead of calling it penetration when it's arguing against modularity and calling it a criterion shift when modularity pushes back.

and at the end I want your honest call on which subset of these can't all hold at once, and which single requirement does the most damage to the most candidate theories.
