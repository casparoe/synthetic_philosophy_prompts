I run a second-year undergraduate metaphysics seminar — sixteen students, one eighty-minute session — and I want to teach Zeno's arguments on motion as something the group does rather than something it hears about. Please design an activity in which the rules themselves generate the difficulty, so that participants run into it before anyone names it, followed by a debrief that maps what happened onto the arguments and is candid about what a room full of moving bodies cannot show. I'd like the whole thing to be runnable with materials I can carry in a bag.

What I want them to come away seeing is not "the paradoxes are solved by calculus" but three separate things: that the halving argument, the arrow, and the argument from composition are not one puzzle with one answer; that the convergence reply lands cleanly on the first, does not touch the second, and answers the third only by presupposing a theory of the continuum; and that their own unexamined assumption — that the course, the interval, the stretch of time is divisible without limit — is what gave the arguments their bite in the first place.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to redesign where it doesn't work.

Phase one, the halving course (roughly twenty minutes). Lay out a twelve-metre strip with masking tape, marked at six metres, nine metres, ten and a half, and so on. Work in pairs: a runner and a referee. The rule is that a move counts only if it covers exactly half the distance remaining to the line, and only if the referee has measured and recorded it on an index card before the runner moves. Give each pair a hard time budget of forty-five seconds. Announce a small prize for the pair that ends closest to the line, doubled if anyone actually touches it. Nobody will touch it, and the prize will go to whichever pair made the most moves — a scoring rule that rewards failure in a way I want them to notice.

Phase two, the completion with no final step (roughly fifteen minutes). Hand each pair a deck of cards whose widths halve — six centimetres, three, one and a half, and so on — and a paper strip the length of the sum. Each card is stamped with the time its corresponding move would be allowed: thirty seconds, fifteen, seven and a half, and so on. The pair lays the cards end to end against the strip, no overlaps permitted. The strip gets covered; the last few cards become too small to hold; and at no point is there a final card. Then ask the temporal question: if the runner were allowed exactly the time stamped on each card, the total is sixty seconds, and the runner is at the line at sixty seconds — so which move finished the run? The point I want them to reach on their own is that the completion is not one of the moves: the sequence has no last member, and the run nevertheless finishes.

Phase three, the strobe (roughly fifteen minutes). Have a volunteer run across the room while the group samples their position at fixed intervals — phones on burst mode, or simply everyone writing down where the runner is every half second. Then, individually and in writing, each participant answers: in every sample the runner is at a place and not moving; if at every instant they are at a place and not moving, when were they moving? Collect the answers before discussing any of them. Most will give the answer that motion just is being at different places at different times; I want the debrief to show that this is a substantive thesis rather than a truism, and to let them feel that nothing in phase two touches the difficulty phase three produced.

Phase four, the cut (roughly ten minutes). Give each group a strip of paper and a stack of blank cards and make them answer, with the materials, how the strip is built out of parts: is each part zero long or longer than zero? If zero, how do the zeroes add up to a length? If longer, how do finitely many of them cover it? This is the argument that the convergence reply answers least directly of all.

Then the debrief, which should be the longest single block. I want a mapping in both directions: each rule in each phase onto the premise it encodes, and each of the three arguments onto the phase that made it felt. I want the reply that the infinite series has a finite sum placed precisely — it is not a refutation but a solution inside a theory, and the historically dominant alternative, that the infinite is only ever potential and never completed, denies exactly the premise the reply needs, which is why the Aristotelian line both feels right and leaves the runner's arrival unexplained. I want the distinction between describing a motion and a motion occurring to be made explicit, since that is where the arrow lives. I want the discreteness option treated seriously rather than as a cop-out: if space and time come in smallest units, the halving argument fails at the first step and phase one was rigged from the start, and I want the debrief to say so — my own rules quietly assumed infinite divisibility, and a group that assumed otherwise would have found the game unfair.

Three more things I need from you. First, a frank account of what will predictably go wrong when this is run for the first time and how to handle it — I expect someone to say "take the limit" in phase one and consider the matter closed, someone else to declare the card exercise a cheat because they can obviously place a card over a hairline gap, and my physics students to invoke Planck-scale discreteness as though it settled the matter. Second, a clear statement of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I therefore have to supply myself: the room will never make anyone doubt that motion occurs, and my four phases all presuppose the standard analysis of the continuum, so they can dramatize the halving argument's dissolution but cannot stage the live dispute between that analysis, the infinitesimal alternatives to it, and the refusal of completed infinities — nor can they recover the original setting, in which these arguments were weapons in a dispute about the one and the many rather than puzzles about limits. Third, a fifty-minute version for a smaller group, and a lecture-hall version using four volunteers, since I may be asked to run this for a hundred students next term.
