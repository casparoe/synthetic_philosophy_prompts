I'm preparing a session for a small reading group on Ayer and the afterlife, and I keep getting stuck on a prior question that the discussion always skips past. Ayer is the figure everyone invokes when they want to insist that we cannot even assess a claim about surviving death until we have said what would count as evidence for or against it — that the demand for a criterion of meaning comes before the question of truth. As a methodological demand that is unobjectionable, but it does an enormous amount of hidden work, because it silently presupposes that we already possess a theory of what surviving death is: one that specifies the facts that would make the claim true and the observations that would bear on it. I want that theory made explicit rather than assumed. So before we consider anyone's verdict, give me the desiderata — the full specification of what any adequate theory of a person's surviving their own death has to deliver.

I want the requirements set out with the case or consideration that motivates each. Which cases must the theory get right — and I care most about the awkward ones: gradual replacement of a brain part by part until nothing original remains, a perfect duplicate assembled at the instant of death, an intermittent or partial continuation, and the case in which what carries on is a later person who merely inherits your memories and dispositions. Which distinctions must it refuse to collapse: between surviving and being remembered, between surviving and being resurrected, between a person persisting and a pattern being re-instantiated, between the fact of survival and our ability to know of it. And which findings from outside philosophy can it not contradict — that the brain is causally bound up with the mental life we actually observe, that death involves the irreversible breakdown of the organism, that reports from the far side of death always trace back to a functioning or a failing brain. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of one camp, and tell me whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect that the demand for survival to be a substantial fact about the world and the demand for it to be in principle decidable pull against one another, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the list. I would also like your view on which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular candidates.

Assume I already know the main positions and have no need of a scorecard, a reading list, or citations — please do not tell me who to read or what to cite. Throughout, state how confident you are in each claim and single out the one point you are least sure of; if the desiderata themselves are the deliverable, I would much rather see your uncertainty marked on them than a smooth summary that hides it.
