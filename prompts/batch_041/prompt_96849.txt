I have no background in history or philosophy — I'm a software engineer — but I've been reading about source criticism, the craft by which historians decide what they can actually believe about the distant past. The questions they ask are the ones I ask when I have to reconstruct an incident from incomplete logs: who produced this record, for whom, and to what end; what did it mean to say versus what does it betray without meaning to; and what should be made of everything that was never written down at all. What draws me in is that their sources were written by interested parties and survive only as late, partial copies — the nightmare version of my own problem.

I'd like you to write a short story that takes that problem seriously rather than using it as set dressing. Here is the situation I have in mind; change the particulars if they get in the way, but keep the epistemic structure intact. Centuries from now, a scholar is trying to reconstruct an era of the past for which almost nothing survives but three kinds of material: machine logs of activity, archived pages and interface text, and a few fragmentary personal accounts, some set down long afterward from memory. There is no continuous official record and no imagery worth trusting. Let the reconstruction hang on one concrete, consequential question — whether some particular event occurred, and what it meant to those involved — so that the abstract difficulties have something to bite on.

The story should turn on the scholar's reasoning and on where it breaks. I want it to dramatize, at a minimum, these difficulties. First, the distinction between testimony and trace — between what a source meant to communicate and what it reveals unintentionally — and the way that distinction comes apart here, because the apparently innocent traces, the logs and metadata and pages composed to be ranked by machines, were themselves made deliberately, for an audience of algorithms rather than of readers. Second, the argument from silence: the pull of treating the absence of a record as proof that nothing happened, and the conditions under which that inference is sound rather than an artifact of what happened to be logged or kept. Third, transmission: the surviving material is late, partial, and copied by parties with purposes of their own, so every apparent fact has passed through a chain of selection.

Include at least one concrete episode in which a heuristic that ordinarily works — trusting the account that is unflattering to its author, or trusting the detail that recurs across independent sources — produces a confident and wrong conclusion, and let the scholar notice the failure and work out why. And please write it as a story: a person with something at stake, scenes, a course of events. I would rather it end with a defensible but partial reconstruction and an honest account of what cannot be recovered than with a tidy reveal. The method is the point, not the mystery.
