I'm running the last session of a reading group on dispositions — the metaphysics kind, fragile things and live wires, and why "it would break if you struck it" never quite captures what fragility is. We spent two weeks on finks (something steps in and strips the disposition away at the exact moment its trigger shows up) and masks (the disposition is still fully there, but something gets between it and its effect). I want to open with a poem that enacts the difference instead of explaining it, so nobody has to hear me lecture again.

So, working as a poet who actually understands the metaphysics: write me a poem, 16-24 lines, in some kind of regular form of your choosing — a fixed rhyme scheme, a refrain, a repeating if-then construction, whatever — but commit to it. The trick I want is that the form gets finked. Set up a pattern the reader expects to pay off, then, at exactly the point where the pattern's condition is met, have something inside the poem cancel the payoff: the rhyme that should land doesn't, the refrain that should return comes back hollow or altered, the then never arrives. And I want a mask in there too, clearly a different animal — an interference that leaves the disposition untouched and just blocks it from showing. The disposition itself can be glass, a wire, or something human (trust, a sense of humor, generosity — anything that definately dies the moment you test it). Brainstorm two or three candidate forms/patterns first and pick the one that can actually be broken, then write it. Don't tack an explanation on at the end; if the form works, the reader gets finks vs masks from the shape of the thing. Someone from the group should recognize it instantly, and someone who's never heard of any of this should still read it as a real poem.
