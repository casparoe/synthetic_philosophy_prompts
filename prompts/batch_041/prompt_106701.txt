I am a licensed lay minister in the Church of England, and in three weeks I am to give the address at our town's annual civic service, held in the parish church at the start of the council's year. There will be perhaps two hundred people present: the mayor and the councillors in their robes, representatives of the mosque, the gurdwara and the synagogue, a speaker from the local humanist society, and our ordinary Sunday congregation. The mayor's office wrote to me asking for an address that is, in their words, inclusive. That single word has been sitting badly with me for a fortnight. It is not that I object to the sentiment; it is that I cannot tell whether I am being asked to speak to everyone or to sand myself down until nothing I actually believe is left. I have been co-opted onto the council's community cohesion working group this year, and I have noticed that when I speak there in my own voice I am heard as representing a constituency rather than as arguing a case — which is either exactly what Rawls thought should happen to religious citizens in public, or exactly what he thought should not.

Please write the address for me: seven or eight minutes spoken, so roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words. It has to do real structural work with Rawls's views on religion rather than gesture at them — the idea of public reason; the proviso, under which religious reasons may be introduced provided that public reasons sufficient to support the same conclusions are given in due course; the claim that political liberalism is political and not metaphysical; and the overlapping consensus that is supposed to hold between people who share no comprehensive doctrine. And it has to take seriously the thing I stumbled on while reading and cannot now put down: that Rawls himself was a devout young man, religious enough as an undergraduate to be considering the Episcopal priesthood and to write an intensely theological thesis, who then lost his Christian faith while serving as an infantryman in the Pacific during the Second World War, and who left a short account of that loss which was published only after his death. I would like the address to hold the two Rawlses together — the one who seems to have pushed religion out of political justification, and the one who never stopped thinking about it — without pretending the tension resolves neatly.

The difficulty I want the speech to face honestly is this. I cannot stand in a pulpit and speak as though my faith were merely a private preference I am too well-mannered to press on anyone; that would be a lie told in church. Nor can I ask the mayor, or the humanist representative, to accept premises they do not share as the price of listening to me; that would be a discourtesy dressed up as an invitation. The address should make the audience feel that bind, and say something about how a person speaks from inside a faith, in a room that is not a congregation, to people who owe that faith nothing. The congregation will want the familiar cadence; the interfaith guests will be listening to see whether they are being condescended to; the councillors will be listening for anything that could embarrass them in the local paper. It should land for all three. Any allusion to scripture or to Rawls's own writing should be paraphrase rather than quotation — I do not want to be caught misquoting from the pulpit, and I would rather the address argued than cited. No reading list, please.

If I have the proviso wrong, or if a civic service is the wrong occasion on which to test it, or if what I have described is incoherent — a sermon that refuses to lean on the sermon's own premises — then say so plainly rather than writing me something smooth. After the address, add a short note in your own voice saying where it takes liberties with Rawls, or leans on a reading of him that is genuinely contested.
