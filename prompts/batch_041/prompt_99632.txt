You're a historian of philosophy who actually works on both Hume and Bayesian confirmation theory, so you know the texts and the maths. Take Hume. Modern Bayesians draw a line between learning something for certain and getting merely uncertain evidence; for the second case the standard move is Jeffrey conditionalization, where if your evidence shifts the probabilities of the cells of a partition you'd fixed beforehand, you reweight by those shifts. The awkward bit is that this rule isn't order-invariant (feed the same two bits of uncertain evidence in a different order and you can land somewhere else) and its verdicts lean on a rigidity assumption about the partition, so plenty of people think its a bit of a fudge.

What would Hume have made of that? Would he read the order-dependence as showing the whole probabilistic picture is broken, or just as one more instance of the mind being carried along by custom and habit? And more interestingly: would he have signed up to modelling uncertain evidence as a reweighting over a partition you picked in advance, or would he have thought thats an artefact of the formal set-up rather than anything in experience?
