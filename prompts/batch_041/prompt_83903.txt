My reading group did Browning's Ordinary Men and then Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners back to back over the last month, and I've been chewing on it ever since because it felt less like a disagreement than like two people having an argument in different languages. As I understand it: Browning looks at Reserve Police Battalion 101 — middle aged men from Hamburg, mostly working class, too old or too unfit for regular service, not party fanatics — and says they shot Jewish women and children in the Polish forests because of the situation they were standing in. The authority of the officers, the pull of not wanting to let your buddies down, the way each day of killing made the next one easier, the little rewards of looking good to your superiors. One of his key bits of evidence is that before the first big massacre the commander told anyone who didn't feel up to it to step out, and only a dozen or so of the roughly five hundred men did. Goldhagen looks at the same battalion, working from largely the same pile of postwar interrogations, and says the opposite: since refusing was allowed and nobody was ever seriously punished for it, the men who killed did it because they wanted to, and what made them want to was a specifically German antisemitism that aimed at the elimination of Jews and made the killing look like the obviously right thing to do. The two of them agree about a huge amount of the factual record, and yet one guy in my group called Goldhagen a conspiracy theorist and another said Browning was writing apologetics with footnotes, and neither of them shifted at all. What's more, the historians mostly declared Goldhagen the loser, but it never felt to me like the actual question got settled — the votes went one way and the same fight just keeps reccurring.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this? My own guess, and it's just a guess, is that a big chunk of it isn't about the evidence at all but about what an explanation of something like this is even supposed to deliver: Browning is explaining a pattern (why men of this sort, in this sort of unit, mostly complied), Goldhagen is explaining a choice (why this man pulled the trigger), and a story that's complete at the level of the population doesn't compete with a story that's complete at the level of the individual — so they've been treating each other as rivals while answering different questions. But that might be too tidy. Maybe it's a real disagreement about the relative weight of ideology versus immediate social pressure, in which case it's empirical and we should be able to say what evidence would move it. Or maybe what's really being fought over is how much of the variation between units, men, and moments is attributable to ideology versus circumstance — a variance question that our sources, all of them retrospective and sparse and written by the perpetrators themselves, may simply be too thin to answer, in which case the impasse is a symptom of an ill posed question rather than a substantive dispute. I also keep wondering whether the ground under Browning has shifted since 1996: the archival re-readings of Milgram's tapes and the work that's made the Stanford prison study look closer to a staged demonstration than an experiment have left the old situationist textbook looking much shakier than it did when Browning was leaning on it. Does that actually bear on Battalion 101, or is it a red herring? And dont leave out the possibility that the real disagreement is moral and political, with the historical claims downstream of the fact that ordinary men sounds like it lets a nation off the hook while willing executioners indicts it. Diagnose it for me, using what each side actually argues, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a new source base, a change of question — or say plainly that I should expect this thing to grind on forever, and why.
