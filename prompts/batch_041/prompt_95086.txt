my housemate keeps telling me that the Condorcet jury theorem is basically the law of large numbers for people: if everyone is even slightly better than a coin flip and they aren't coordinating, then a majority is like the average of a bunch of noisy measurements of the same thing, and the noise washes out as you add more. he's using this to argue we should just add more people to any group that has to make a call — bigger juries, more forecasters on the panel, more reviewers on the grant committee, more members in the citizens assembly — and that who you add barely matters, because the errors cancel. I'm suspicious but I can't say why, and I have to argue with him at dinner this weekend, so I want to actually do this properly rather than wave my hands.

can you audit the analogy for me? build the mapping first — voter to sensor, individual competence to instrument accuracy, majority rule to averaging readings — and then tell me where it breaks. I especially want the places where the analogy is quietly doing load-bearing work it can't actually do: what a measuring instrument has that a voter doesn't; what happens when the errors are correlated rather than independent (which is the case I care about, since real groups share media, culture and each others reasoning); what happens when the errors are biased rather than symmetric, so that averaging them doesn't cancel anything but just averages to the bias; and the difference between averaging readings and taking a majority vote when some of the people are below chance, since those two rules seem to come apart badly there and he's treating them as the same thing.

the other one that bugs me is that all the sensors are pointed at the same well defined quantity and none of them care how the reading comes out. how much of the theorems punch depends on those two facts — a determinate target that exists independently of the measurement, and instruments with no stake in the answer? and while you're at it: people keep sliding from this to the claim that diversity matters more than individual skill, that a diverse group of mediocre people beats a homogeneous group of experts. does the theorem give you that, or is that a separate assumption about who is correlated with whom, smuggled in on the back of the noise-cancelling picture?

so: the correspondence table, the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion he wants (add more people, expertise optional) versus the ones that are just nitpicks, and then a verdict — does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? if the sensor picture is a bad one, what's a better analogy for what a jury or a panel is really doing? no citations needed, I just want the argument.
