For a seminar on decision theory and its paradoxes I have been reading around the St. Petersburg game, and last week I watched two other participants, Mira and Dev, spend most of the session arguing about it without either giving ground; I stayed out of it and would now like a proper adjudication rather than a tie. The version they used was the standard one: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands tails, and if that first tails occurs on toss n you are paid 2^n dollars. Mira opened by saying the fair price of a single play is infinite, since the expected value is the sum of 1 over every n, so that any finite fee, even a very large one, is a bargain in expectation. Dev answered that expectation cannot be the whole story, because you will almost surely leave with a trivial sum: the probability of ever receiving more than $1,024 is under one in a thousand, and the chance of the genuinely large payoffs is smaller still, so a rule that tells you to stake your savings is not a rule anyone could follow. Mira replied that almost surely small and infinite in expectation are entirely compatible, that the whole interest of the game lies in the enormous but improbable tail, and that if one could play repeatedly one would, with probability one, end up making arbitrarily large amounts, so the law of large numbers vindicates her. Dev pushed back on that last step specifically: he said the law of large numbers presupposes a finite mean, which this game lacks, so citing it is a misstep, and that in any event no finite bankroll or finite life survives the variance, so the word "eventually" is carrying all the weight.

To press the point he then introduced a variant: the same structure, except the coin is slightly biased toward tails, so that tails now appears with probability 0.51 on each toss. He had worked out that the expected value of this game is finite, roughly $51, and he argued that this shows the famous paradox to be a knife-edge artifact of the coin being exactly fair — perturb the probability by a hair and the infinite value collapses to a modest, entirely ordinary number, which suggests the infinite fair price was never a fact about anything realizable. Mira answered that this proves too much: the very reasoning that forbids paying a large sum because you will almost surely lose also forbids buying a ten-dollar lottery ticket for a one-in-a-million shot at twenty million, and forbids ordinary insurance from the buyer's point of view, yet reasonable people do all three, so if Dev's objection were sound it would condemn most everyday risk-taking rather than Petersburg alone. Dev replied that lotteries and insurance have finite expectations and are priced by a market, whereas the value of the Petersburg game resides wholly in an unrealizable infinite tail, and that the cases come apart exactly there. They left it unresolved and asked me to referee; I found that I could not.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move — Mira's initial claim about the fair price, Dev's appeal to the near-certain small payout, Mira's invocation of repeated play and the law of large numbers, Dev's correction about the missing finite mean, his biased-coin modification and whatever it does and does not show, Mira's proves-too-much rebuttal, and Dev's distinction between Petersburg and ordinary lotteries — rating each move from clearly mistaken through clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, and closing with an account of what, if anything, both of them are missing.
