I'm in a philosophy-of-technology reading group this term (Thursdays, it's the only thing keeping me sane) and we keep re-fighting the same fight: does an artifact ever have politics of its own, or is the politics always in the people around it? I'm bored of the usual verdicts, everyone agrees the parkway bridges are a great example and everyone agrees the internet isn't inherently democratic, so I want to try a different cut. Here's the test I've been argueing for with my friend Priya, call it the swap test: hold the social arrangements fixed — same people, same laws, same intentions, same money — and swap the artifact for some other device that everyone involved would agree does the same job. Score 0 if the political effect survives the swap (so it was never really about the artifact), score 1 if the effect dies with the device (so the artifact was carrying the politics). Under that test, give me a tier list (S/A/B/C) of the following, one line of justification each, and don't hedge into "it depends" without saying what it depends on: the overpasses built too low for buses to pass under, so the beach stayed for people with cars, or so the story goes; the pneumatic molding machines the reaper works brought in to replace the striking iron molders; the mechanical tomato harvester that made the growers bigger and the pickers redundant; a speed bump; the Berlin key, the one that only comes out of the lock after you've turned it back and locked the door behind you; a QWERTY keyboard; the prepay smart meter that cuts your power off when the credit runs out; and one from my own life, the seminar room in our building has the projector cable padlocked to the lectern so the screen can only be on the left wall, which means any seminar that uses slides is stuck in a layout where half the room can't see anything once the sun's out.

Then the part I actually care about. I can't tell if the swap test proves too much or too little. Too much, because for every item on that list you can describe a "functionally equivalent" alternative that dissolves the politics, which would make the whole question of artifacts having politics evaporate. Too little, because what counts as the same job is doing all the work, and I suspect I'm smuggling in whichever description of the case makes my answer come out right — the bridge that carries cars to the beach and the bridge that carries the right kind of people to the beach aren't the same artifact, but they're the same object. So: fix the test for me as best it can be fixed, tell me which of the eight items it then gets obviously wrong, and if the honest answer is that the criterion can't be stated without already deciding the case, say that instead and tell me what that does to the original question. No reading list or citations, I have the literature, I just want the tiers and the diagnosis.
