I've just started a new job as a legislative drafter, after ten years in litigation, and the transition has unsettled something I used to take for granted. In my first month I watched a court read a provision I would have sworn was unambiguous in a way that none of us in the drafting office had in mind, and I have since been told, more than once, that this is simply how it goes. The judges who do this are not careless; they are applying familiar tools. They refuse to give a statute its plain words when the result would be absurd or manifestly unintended, they correct what are plainly typographical errors in the enacted text, they read each provision so that none is rendered surplusage, and they consult a statute's title, findings, and preamble to fix what it is for. Each of these moves seems, on its face, to treat the enacted words as evidence of something else rather than as the law itself.

I want to understand what theory of legislation this cluster of practices presupposes. Does it commit anyone who uses these tools to the view that a legislature is a single rational author with a coherent intention that the text merely expresses? If so, is that view defensible against the obvious objection that a statute is a bargain among factions and may have no intention at all? Or can these moves be reconstructed without the single-author picture, say as an account of what a reasonable reader would take the text to mean, or as a set of policy-based tiebreakers that operate only after the text has been found indeterminate? I would like the strict textualist alternative spelled out: which of these four moves it can keep, which it must abandon, and what would have to change in day-to-day judicial practice if it were adopted. Please also tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of legislation would license exactly the same judicial behavior.
