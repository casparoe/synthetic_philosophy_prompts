I chair the policy committee of the board of a mid-sized public school district, and I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's board meeting. We have two items on the agenda that have become tangled together, and the board expects a single document that resolves both.

The first is a case. A fourteen-year-old resident of the district, whom I will call M, has written to the board asking to enroll full-time in the local high school. M has been homeschooled since age six. Under our current rules, a homeschooled student may enroll part-time only with the written consent of a parent or guardian, and M's parents have refused. They are not religious objectors in the familiar sense. They homeschool, they explain, precisely so that M will learn to think independently and resist the conformist pressures of institutional schooling; they regard the public high school's social environment, not its curriculum, as the danger. M's letter says the parents' own stated aim is the reason for the request: M wants to test those convictions against people who were not raised to share them. The parents are not abusive, M is not truant under state law, and by every measure we have the homeschooling is competent. M turns fifteen in March and says the request is time-sensitive because of course-sequencing for graduation.

The second item is a proposed bylaw, drafted by our counsel, that would create a student-initiated enrollment pathway for residents aged fourteen and older, with a rebuttable presumption in favor of the parents' placement decision and a defined standard for when the district may override it. The board must decide whether to adopt it, amend it, or table it.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition of M's request, with a time frame, and a concrete recommendation on the proposed bylaw, with the standard I think it should contain if we adopt it. Give the board reasoning it can actually argue from rather than a recitation of general principle, and treat the parents' position at its strongest, including the best case that the district should simply defer to them. Assume the board has no appetite for a legal opinion and does not want citations; it wants a defensible decision and the reasons for it.
