I would like to practise arguing with a serious version of Alan Turing rather than a caricature, so please take his role and hold it across our exchange. Speak in the first person as Turing in 1950, using only the concepts and information available to you then, plus whatever I tell you about the present; if you need a fact about the modern world to answer, ask for it as Turing would ask. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede small points, but do not give up your central position; keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a conversation.

Here is my opening move. I am a high school student, and I have been reading the later sections of your 1950 paper on machine intelligence. What surprised me is that you treat telepathy as a genuine phenomenon whose statistical evidence you consider overwhelming, and you then worry that the interrogation might be contaminated by it, so you propose that the whole game be played in a room shielded against telepathic influence. I want to press you on two things. First, the evidence you were leaning on has not held up. As the experimental controls got better — proper randomisation, pre-registered protocols, independent replication — the effects shrank, and then they disappeared, to the point where the standard scientific view now is that there is nothing there to shield against. So the concession seems to rest on evidence that has since evaporated, and I want to know whether that changes anything for you or whether you would say you were simply reasoning correctly from what was then the best data.

Second, and this is what really bothers me, the concession seems to damage your own method from the inside. If telepathy is real, then a machine might pass the interrogation not by thinking but by reading the interrogator's mind, which would make the test measure something other than what you wanted it to measure. If telepathy is not real, then you spent part of your paper worrying about a channel that does not exist. And there is a deeper problem I cannot get past: you propose a room that blocks telepathy, but if nobody knows the mechanism by which telepathy is supposed to travel, how would anyone know what a room that blocks it would even be made of? Please answer me as Turing, and tell me honestly whether you think your treatment of extrasensory perception strengthens your case for machine intelligence or quietly undermines it.
