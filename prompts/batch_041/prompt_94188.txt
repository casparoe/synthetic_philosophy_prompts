I retired a few years ago from a career in entomology, and I have since been reading philosophy in an attempt to make sense of a puzzle that has nagged at me since I was a student. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind and of biology — someone equally at home in the free-will literature and in the ethology of invertebrates — and to reason carefully and from first principles rather than reaching for the stock positions under their familiar labels. Begin by brainstorming the conceptual terrain before you commit to any structure.

The phenomenon is this. A female digger wasp of the genus Sphex paralyzes a cricket, drags it by the antennae to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance, and goes inside to inspect the nest. If the cricket is moved a few inches while she is inside, she emerges, finds it, drags it back to the entrance — and then inspects the nest again, so that the prey is once more left outside; the sequence can be repeated many times without variation. Writers in the philosophy of mind have treated this as an emblem of behavior that looks thoughtful but is really mindless, and have given the property a name — sphexishness — for the condition of being trapped in a routine that one can neither notice nor break. The general worry is that if our own apparently deliberate conduct is sphexish beneath the surface, then free will is an illusion. I should add that some ethologists now doubt the wasp is as rigid as the story suggests, reporting that it does eventually adapt after many interruptions and that the repeated inspection may itself be adaptive; I am not sure how much of the emblem survives that, and I would like you to say.

What I want from you is a decision tree, or taxonomy, of the positions one can take on the question: what about sphexishness, if anything, is a threat to free will?

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of -isms. That is, first identify the branching questions — the distinctions on which the candidate positions genuinely come apart — together with the combinations of answers they permit; only then locate the named positions in the space. Note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and diagnose each briefly: is the slot empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it? Aim for a tree of roughly five to eight leaves.

Among the branch questions I would like you to weigh — adding, dropping, or splitting them as the logic requires — are these: whether the threat lies in mechanism as such or in rigidity in particular; whether flexibility is a matter of degree or of kind; whether what matters is the first-order capacity to vary one's behavior or the second-order capacity to notice that one is in a loop; and whether the threat is the fact of being sphexish or merely the possibility that one might be so without knowing it. The distinction between mechanism and rigidity seems to me the one most often blurred, and I would like the tree to make it explicit.

Use the following cases as your diagnostic material, and phrase every branch question so that someone could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to: the unmodified wasp; a wasp surgically fitted with a loop-breaker, a module that makes her pause and re-plan whenever her routine is interrupted; a household thermostat; a reinforcement-learning agent that occasionally explores; a person walking and talking in her sleep; and a language model instructed to role-play the wasp. For each leaf, give the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if no one defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate rather than papering over the gap.

Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or a bibliography; I am not interested in who said what, and I would rather you work the structure out for yourself. Think this through step by step, and set out the tree in a form I could actually use as a handout.
