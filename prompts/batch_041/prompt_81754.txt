I have been trying to work out, case by case, what I actually believe about thought shaping perception, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. I am told this territory is discussed under the heading of cognitive penetration; the label does not interest me, the pattern does. I am not asking for a survey of the literature. I want to know whether my verdicts are coherent, what they commit me to, which of them I must give up, and what they predict about cases I have not considered.

My cases, with verdicts as they currently stand:

1. Two equal horizontal lines, each with arrowhead fins at the ends, look unequal to me, and knowing that they are equal does not make them look equal. Verdict: not a case of thought shaping perception. The belief is inert; it sits alongside the experience rather than inside it.

2. When I have not eaten for a while, ambiguous blobs flashed on a screen resolve into food more often than they do after a meal. Verdict: not a case of thought shaping perception, though I am uneasy about saying so. It feels as though my attention has been steered rather than my eyes.

3. A friend who was once bitten by a dog sees a calm dog on a leash as menacing, and flinches before she can say what is wrong with it. Verdict: thought shaping perception. The dog looks different to her, not merely as though it merits a second look.

4. A radiologist I know says she can see that something is wrong with an image before she can say what, and that after years of practice the shadows simply look different to her than they did at the start. Verdict: not thought shaping perception. Her experience changed, but no belief of hers did the changing.

5. Shown a field of random gray speckle, I am told that it contains a face. Once told, I see the face, and it is hard to recover the speckle. Verdict: thought shaping perception.

6. Having learned a word for a shade that previously looked identical to its neighbour, I can now see the two shades as different at a glance. Verdict: thought shaping perception, though I notice this sits badly with 4, since in both cases what changed me was training rather than a belief I held at the time.

7. I am told that a wine is expensive, and it tastes better to me. Verdict: I am inclined to call this thought shaping experience, and I do not like the verdict, since it is the same wine.

8. A man convinced, on no evidence, that his partner is unfaithful sees ordinary friendliness as flirtation and cannot see it any other way. Verdict: thought shaping perception. This one unsettles me, because if the experience is shaped by the belief, the experience cannot then serve as the evidence for it.

The tentative principle I would offer for testing is this. A case counts only if the state doing the shaping is a thought the person could avow, one they would put into words, and only if that thought figures in the experience as part of what the thing is seen as, rather than as a spotlight on part of it. If that is the principle, then 2 and 4 fall out, 1 falls out because the state, though avowable, does not figure in the experience at all, and 6 and 7 turn on whether a learned discrimination or a price tag counts as part of what the thing is seen as. I suspect the principle is doing its work in the wrong place, but I cannot see where.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures the pattern; a precise statement of each clause together with the case that motivates it; an honest statement of which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated and ought to be revised; and what the resulting account says about two cases I have not listed, namely a chess player who sees at a glance that a position is lost but cannot yet explain why, and a person who has been told that a painting is a forgery and now finds it flat and lifeless. Finally, tell me whether the line I am drawing has any epistemic consequence: if 3, 5 and 8 are real cases, does the experience still justify the belief that shaped it?
