I'm a second-year PhD student in moral philosophy putting together an application for a one-year fellowship, and the application asks for a research proposal of no more than two pages — roughly a thousand words. I'd like you to draft it from the following half-formed idea. My topic is the contrast people draw between a German and an American folk morality: order, rule-following and the primacy of the shared arrangement on one side; individual conscience, the standing right to judge the case for oneself and a suspicion of mere convention on the other. I have been arguing about this with friends for months — one friend treats crossing an empty road against the light as a small moral failure even when nobody is endangered, another thinks that if nobody is endangered the rule has simply run out of work to do — and I have come to suspect that the usual formulation of the contrast conflates two independent questions. The first is where the authority to settle a moral question resides: with the individual or with the collectivity. The second is what I want to call the defeasibility profile of a requirement: whether the agent's own assessment of the particular case is among the considerations that can defeat it, or whether the requirement is the sort of thing that survives the agent's private conviction that it is pointless here. My hunch — the part I would like the proposal to make precise, and either defend or abandon — is that the two folk moralities differ far more over the second question than the first, and that this reverses the received picture. On my reading the German strand is comparatively individualist about where moral authority comes from (autonomy, conviction, self-legislation; broadly the Kantian pole) and comparatively collectivist about how requirements apply, whereas the American strand is the mirror image: collectivist about the sources of morality, since it is something we hold, or something a community or a founding commitment hands down, and individualist about application. The familiar opposition between an ethic of autonomy and an ethic of custom would then be a contrast that runs inside each tradition rather than between them.

Please turn this into the proposal itself. State the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than being an invitation to survey the field; explain why the standard framing leaves it open; and set out the method and why it is the right one. I expect conceptual work on the two axes, together with a small exploratory instrument — something like a set of short vignettes in which the probe is not what the subject judges but what it would take to change their mind, since that is where a defeasibility profile shows itself. Give me what I would actually do month by month across the year, what a result would look like, and what would count as my thesis being refuted: for instance, a case in which judgements on the two axes co-vary in the way the received picture predicts. Include an honest assessment of scope — a year is not enough for a properly powered cross-cultural study, so the empirical component should be presented as a pilot that illustrates the distinction rather than establishes the contrast — and add a line on what would settle which of the two has the better of it, without promising a verdict the project cannot deliver. The proposal should also say what the contrast is a contrast between: populations, or two ideal-typical strands present in both places, and what would decide between those readings, since I do not want to be read as trading in national character.

No bibliography or reading list, please; I shall add the citations myself. Refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular works, and do not attribute specific claims to named authors unless the attribution is one you are confident of. Keep it to two pages of continuous prose, readable by a generalist panel, and define the coinages on first use so that the chemist on the committee is not lost in the opening paragraph.
