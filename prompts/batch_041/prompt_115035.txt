I am a philosophy graduate student writing a seminar paper on the ethics of industrial aquaculture, and I keep running into an argument whose pull I feel but whose structure I cannot quite bring into focus. In shrimp hatcheries it is standard practice to induce spawning by removing or crushing one eyestalk of a female broodstock animal — a procedure sometimes called eyestalk ablation, which welfare scientists have long criticized and which persists largely because the alternatives are costlier or less reliable. The argument I keep hearing runs roughly as follows: we do not know whether crustaceans such as shrimp, crabs, and lobsters are sentient; if they are, routine practices of this kind cause them serious suffering; the cost to us of refraining is comparatively small, whereas the cost to them, if they are sentient, is potentially enormous; therefore we should treat them as sentient and refrain. I will call this the asymmetry argument.

I would like you to act as a philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and animal ethics, and to explain the conceptual issue that the asymmetry argument raises rather than simply endorsing or rejecting it. Please begin with a short brainstorm, in bullet form, mapping the different senses in which one might speak of moral risk here — risk about status, risk about magnitude, risk about the reliability of the evidence — and only then write a careful, seminar-style explanation. The crux I want explained is this: the argument's force appears to depend on treating sentience as a binary predicate, so that a creature either belongs to the moral community or does not, whereas the biology most of us now accept suggests a graded phenomenon; and it also appears to require that human convenience and possible crustacean suffering be commensurable on a single scale, which is precisely the assumption that exposes expected-value reasoning to fanaticism, where a very small probability of an enormous harm is permitted to swamp every competing consideration.

So I would like you to explain, as precisely as you can: first, why the asymmetry argument is compelling when sentience is treated as all-or-nothing; second, what becomes of it once sentience is treated as a matter of degree, including the difference between uncertainty about whether a creature is sentient at all and uncertainty about how much it suffers; third, whether the fanaticism worry is a genuine difficulty for the precautionary reading or merely a philosopher's distraction, and what a defender of the argument could say in reply; and fourth, whether the argument proves too much, in the sense that its logic would extend to any entity whose sentience cannot be ruled out, and whether that extension strengthens or undermines it. Please think carefully before you write, keep the discussion at the level of concepts rather than empirical findings, and do not cite any literature — I want the reasoning itself, laid out clearly enough that the biologists in my seminar, who have no background in ethics, could follow it.
