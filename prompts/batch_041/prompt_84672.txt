I'm a second-year PhD student working on practical rationality — my thesis is about whether diachronic constraints on choice can be derived from synchronic ones, so I've read a fair amount of the money-pump stuff, but I'm still not sure how much of the standard story I actually believe. A friend in the econ department lent me one of those trade-press books on rational choice by a well-known decision theorist (I've since lost it, so I can't check the exact wording), and there's a chapter on money pumps that alot of people around me seem to treat as the last word. Here's my reconstruction of the gist:

'If your preferences go in a circle — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — then a bookie can take you for everything you've got, one small trade at a time. At each step you're offered a swap plus a small fee that is still, by your own lights, an improvement on what you've got, so you take it; after three trades you're holding exactly what you started with, and you're poorer. That's why transitivity isn't just an axiom someone put in a textbook, it's a requirement of rationality. It's the same kind of argument as a Dutch book against inconsistent degrees of belief, and the lesson is the same: coherence. And once you've seen that, the diachronic moral falls out — a rational agent doesn't revise her preferences, because any revision makes her pumpable, so diachronic rationality is just resoluteness. The trick works whether the cycle is all there at one moment or spread out over time, so intransitivity and time-inconsistency are really the same disease.'
