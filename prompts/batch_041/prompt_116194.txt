I'm on the board of a small housign co-op (12 units) and we're about to swap our old front-door key for a smart lock. It opens with an app, logs every entry with a timestamp and user ID, fails secure when the power goes out, and lets us hand out guest codes that expire after 30 minutes unless the board votes to extend them. Our insurer wants the log; the fire marshal is fine with the fail-secure default; the vendor's app requires a phone number, and its API won't let us export the log or change the 30-minute limit. Our housekeeper doesn't have a smartphone, and two of our older residents keep getting logged out of the app.

One board member is thrilled: 'it's just a tool, it's neutral — the politics is in how we use it, and we'll write fair rules.' Another member says the lock is already doing politics: it decides who counts as a member, who counts as a guest, and what belonging means, and it settles all that before any rule gets written. She calls it a little legislator. A third friend says the whole dispute is confused, because a lock is just metal and silicon — it can't want anything, so any politics must be in our heads.

You're a philosopher of technology — someone who's good at thinking about how tools shape social life, and who can also give a straight answer. Before you answer, brainstorm a bit: list the strongest ways to argue the lock is neutral and the strongest ways to argue it isn't, then say which you find most convincing and why. I definately don't want a bibliography or reading list, just the reasoning. Then give me your concrete advice: should we install it, and if so what should we change — the device, the contract, or our rules? And is my friend right that if a human doorman enforced the exact same rules, everyone would call that political, so the lock must be political too — or is there a real difference between the doorman and the device?
