I am assembling a handout for a graduate reading group on decision theory. Everyone in the room already knows the standard two-urn Ellsberg setup and the expected-utility machinery, so the handout should not re-explain either. What I want is a classification instrument — a decision tree — that sorts readers by what they actually believe about ambiguity, not by the label they would volunteer.

The root question: when someone prefers to bet on an urn whose composition is known to be fifty-fifty rather than on an urn whose composition is unknown, are they making a mistake that better reasoning would remove, expressing a preference that any adequate theory must accommodate, or producing evidence that the standard theory was the wrong shape to begin with?

Build the tree from the space of possible answers first — the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the positions in that space. Do not start from a list of camps and reverse-engineer questions to separate them.

Every branch node must be a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Concrete cases, comparisons between two acts, and claims about what an agent may or must do are all acceptable; anything of the form "do you think preferences must be complete?" or "are you an ambiguity averter?" is not.

Every one of the following cases must do work in the tree, either as a node or as a test that separates two leaves:

1. The familiar pair: one urn known to hold fifty red and fifty black, one urn of a hundred balls in an unknown proportion. The reader chooses an urn separately for a bet on red and for a bet on black.
2. The unknown urn alone, with no known urn anywhere in view, and with a hundred possible colours rather than two — nothing to compare it against.
3. The unknown urn whose composition was itself fixed by a fair coin (say thirty red or seventy red, decided by the toss), so the whole arrangement is a compound lottery.
4. The experimenter is described as having chosen the composition deliberately, knowing in advance which colour the reader will back.
5. The same two urns, but the reader loses money on a wrong colour instead of winning on a right one.
6. The same unknown urn, re-mixed after every draw, with fifty identical bets to be placed.
7. The reader may flip a fair coin to decide which urn to back, or split the stake between them.
8. The reader may pay to learn the colour of one ball before betting.
9. Two experts give different probabilities for the same event and the reader must act on one of them.

Aim for ten to fourteen leaves, at most four levels deep, printable on one page, and answerable in five minutes by someone who has read nothing. Name each leaf descriptively rather than by the people associated with it — no names, no citations, no reading list. These leaves must appear, whatever else you include: the uniform-prior response that treats the two urns as equivalent; the view that beliefs are sets of probabilities and that no single number is warranted, so there is no determinate bet size; worst-case reasoning over the compatible distributions; a non-additive weighting on which the weight of an event plus the weight of its complement falls short of one; the two-stage view on which the reader has a distribution over first-order probabilities and a concave attitude towards its spread; the incomplete-preference view that refuses any bet that does not dominate and will pay for the coin; the debunking view that the aversion tracks the salience of the reader's own ignorance rather than anything about the urn; and the adversarial view on which the aversion is a reasonable response to a possibly hostile arrangement rather than to ambiguity as such.

Then: flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say which case would be needed to separate them. Note any combination of answers that no one occupies and diagnose it — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Finally, mark which leaves are claims about how people in fact behave, which are claims about what a rational agent should do, and which are claims about how belief should be formalised, and say where a leaf takes a stand on only one of the three.
