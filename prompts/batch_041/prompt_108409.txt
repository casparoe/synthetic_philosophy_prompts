I trained as a historian and now work as a consultant to a television production company, which means I spend a great deal of my time being asked, at short notice, whether some invented scene is permissible. I have been invited to contribute a short position statement to a workshop on dramatizing real people, and before I put anything on paper I would like a consistency check on the views I would be committing myself to. Here is the set, more or less as I would state it.

(1) The dead can be wronged. Betraying a promise made to someone who has since died, or destroying the thing they spent a lifetime building, can be a wrong to them and not merely a wrong to the living who care about them. (2) The harm involved is not experiential: there is nothing it is like to be dead, so being dead is not a condition of suffering. The harm is instead the setback of an interest the person held while alive, where that interest is not extinguished by death. (3) Reputation is not an interest of that kind. Once someone is dead, what anyone believes about them cannot set back an interest of theirs; a stake in one's good name is a stake in how one is treated while one is still around to be treated. (4) I also think that a drama which invents a serious moral transgression — a cruelty, a betrayal, a cover-up — and attributes it to a real and named person does wrong that person. Suppose, for instance, a miniseries about a modestly documented nineteenth-century botanist invents a scene in which he spitefully ruins a rival's collection; the record is silent and the invention is dramatically load-bearing. (5) I want to say that this wrong is theirs: their grievance, not at bottom the audience's (for being misled) or their descendants' (for being distressed). (6) I do not think fictions assert what they depict. A drama that invents a scene is not lying about it, and a competent viewer knows she is watching an invention, so the wrong cannot be located in the audience's being deceived. (7) I do think that a work presenting itself as telling the true story — real names, real dates, archival footage, a closing card explaining what became of everyone — invites an uptake that a confessed fiction does not, and is answerable for its inventions in a way the confessed fiction is not; the relevant line is therefore not fiction versus nonfiction but the uptake the work invites. (8) I think consent is beside the point here: the dead cannot consent, and a regime of posthumous permissions would be absurd. I also think that what makes the fabricated transgression a wrong rather than mere bad taste is that it does to the person something they would have had standing to refuse while alive. (9) I think descendants have standing to raise the objection on the ancestor's behalf, but the grievance they raise is the ancestor's and not their own.

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict on the topic itself. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to be in tension and why the appearance dissolves, and how severe each real tension is; rank the real ones by severity rather than treating them as uniformly bad. For each, say what the cheapest repair would be, what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set, and whether the tension is best resolved by giving up a claim or by reinterpreting one. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would be conceding by making it. Please end with a final line of exactly the form "Verdict: " followed by the number of the one commitment I should abandon, with nothing after it.
