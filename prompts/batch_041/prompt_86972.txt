I am a high school student in a philosophy elective, and we have spent the last few weeks on performative utterances — the kind of thing where saying "I promise," "I resign," "I do," or "I name this ship" does not report anything but instead accomplishes something. Our teacher introduced the entire topic through an analogy that I have since seen used more than once in popular explanations, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear.

The analogy is this: a performative is like a magic spell. The words themselves carry the power, and when spoken by the right person, in the right setting, in the right formula, they change the world — the ring goes on, the ship acquires a name, an obligation comes into existence — even though nothing in the world changes except that the words were said. On this picture, the conditions that have to be met for a performative to work (an accepted procedure with a certain conventional effect, a speaker and circumstances appropriate to invoking it, correct and complete execution of the procedure) are like the conditions for a spell to take: the correct ingredients, the correct ritual, and an authorized caster. From this our teacher drew a further conclusion, that performatives constitute a genuinely special class of utterances, set apart from ordinary descriptive statements, which merely report how things already stand.

What I would like is an audit of the analogy rather than an explanation of speech act theory. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what maps onto what? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion our teacher drew, as opposed to being incidental differences that no one's argument depends on. Two points in particular confuse me. First, a spell seems to work whether or not anyone hears it or accepts it, whereas an utterance that no one takes up appears not to have performed its act at all; our teacher also mentioned in passing that an actor on stage who says "I do" marries nobody, and treated this as the exception that proves the rule, which I did not follow. Second, a promise made insincerely still seems to me to be a promise — the promise is made and the promiser is bound, however badly — which looks like a point in the analogy's favour rather than against it, since a spell cast without belief presumably still works. I would like to know what that shows.

Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If it does not, is there a better comparison available, one that preserves what the spell image gets right — that the act is accomplished by the words rather than by any inner state of the speaker — without importing a kind of power into the words that they do not possess? And if the analogy is misleading in the way I suspect, I would like to know precisely what it smuggles in about where the efficacy of a performative comes from.
