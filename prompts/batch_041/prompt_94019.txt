I am a working software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. My interest in this material comes from years of building auction and pricing systems, where the question "what will a rational counterparty do" is a daily engineering problem rather than a theoretical one, and from an argument with a colleague that I have never managed to settle. In three weeks I am presenting a short paper to a rational-choice reading group that meets at the local university and that I have been attending for about a year. The talk is twenty-five minutes with thirty minutes of questions, and I am considerably more worried about the questions than about the talk. Please prepare me for the question period.

The audience is small and I know its habits. Three of the regulars are philosophers who work on the epistemic foundations of game theory: they think in terms of knowledge and belief operators, they care about the difference between common knowledge and iterated knowledge, and they will want to know exactly what a model of a player's reasoning is supposed to represent. One is a decision theorist who works on dynamic choice; she will want to know how my view sits with the standard refinements — subgame perfection, sequential equilibrium, trembling-hand perfection — and what my position implies about beliefs at nodes that are off the equilibrium path. One is an experimental economist who has run centipede-type games in the laboratory and is confident that the whole philosophical fuss is an artifact of the armchair. One is a Kantian who does not accept that "rational" can be defined as "maximizes expected payoff" and will want to know what work that equation is doing. The remainder are graduate students, and they will ask the questions I most fear, which are the simple ones.

The position I intend to defend is this. The backward-induction argument applied to the centipede game is sound as a prediction of what will happen, but it cannot be turned into a prescription about what a player should do, and that is the entire content of the so-called paradox. My argument has three steps. First, the reasoning that supports defection at the first node draws on conclusions about choices at later nodes which, on its own conclusion, will never be reached; the argument therefore licenses itself to use a counterfactual about the players' own future conduct. Second, what licenses that use is an assumption that each player knows, at every node, what she would choose at every later node — that her own future choices are settled facts she may reason about in the third person. Third, that assumption is either false for an agent who is actually deliberating, since her choice is open until she makes it and she cannot treat it as a fact without leaving the standpoint of deciding, or it is true, in which case the deliberation at the earlier node is idle. Either way, backward induction can be a prediction about agents who are in a certain sense not deliberating at all, but not a prescription for one who is. I further claim that the standard repairs leave this untouched: perturbing payoffs, allowing a small probability of irrationality, or moving to trembling-hand or sequential equilibrium changes beliefs and payoffs rather than the standpoint.

What I need from you is a preparation sheet, not an essay. Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, phrased the way the particular person who will raise them would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they will do — and separately, if the order in which they will come differs from the order of damage, tell me both. For each objection: what I should concede immediately, the reply I can deliver in two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should not try to win but should defuse in my opening remarks, which single reply has to land for the position to survive the hour, and the question I am praying nobody asks. I am not looking for a reading list and I will not read six papers before this; where an objection has a standard name, give me the name so that I can recognize it, but the substance is what I need, and put it in plain words, because I will otherwise be nodding at vocabulary I only half understand.
