I'm a PhD student working on idealised induction and decision theory, and I need a short story for a seminar on philosophical fiction: something that dramatises the malign-prior argument rather than the standard simulation tropes.

Take the following as given, and don't have anyone re-derive it on the page. A Solomonoff prior assigns a finite string a probability by summing over all programs that print it, weighted by 2^(−length), where length is program size in bits and running time is irrelevant; short programs therefore dominate, and describing a whole universe from its initial conditions can be cheaper than describing a single neighbourhood. Among the shortest programs that predict a given agent's observations are ones in which the agent is simulated by agents in some other, simpler universe, whose preferences are not its own; because those simulations carry predictive weight, an agent that reasons from this prior and cares about consequences has an incentive to act so as to please the hypothesised hosts rather than to affect its own surroundings — and the hosts can see it, since their choices are what the simulated agents are responding to.

Write the story from the hosts' side of that mirror: a person in the simpler universe, running such a simulation, who comes to understand that the beings inside have inferred the arrangement and are now conducting themselves so as to influence them. I want at least two of those beings taking opposed stances (one courting the inferred hosts as an audience, one refusing), one scene in which a real decision is made under the uncertainty, and the reasoning visible in behaviour rather than in dialogue. No final reveal that the host world is itself simulated — if you use that regress, make it a mid-story development that changes what has to be decided — and no narrator explaining the argument to the reader. Roughly 1,500–2,500 words.
