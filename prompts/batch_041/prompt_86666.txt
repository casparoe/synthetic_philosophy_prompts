My roommate's a Buddhist and we've been arguing about this for weeks, so I want you to try to knock down the view I've ended up with. I think karma, in the classical doctrine, is best read as a theory of moral desert: the circumstances you're born into and the suffering you undergo are deserved, because they're the ripened fruit of your own past actions. I've been calling it the ledger view -- every action is a deposit or a withdrawal, and your life right now is the running balance. I know it isn't just a Hindu thing, and the variants make me nervous. The Jains apparently took it so literally that karma is a kind of fine matter that actually clings to the soul; Buddhist commentators spent centuries trying to work out how there can be a karmic account at all if there's no self to keep it in, and at least one school posited a "person" that is neither the same as nor different from the aggregates just to make the arithmetic come out, which got them branded heretics. And then in practice you get monks dedicating their merit to other people, and bodhisattvas vowing to hold off their own liberation until everyone else is through -- which sounds like the exact opposite of everyone getting what they earned. I can't tell whether these are fatal problems for my view or just neighbouring doctrines that don't touch it.

So: what would count against the ledger view? I don't want the standard "but suffering is unfair" complaint, I want the ones that actually bite. Does desert even survive the no-self doctrine, or does it need a numerically identical subject at the time of the action and at the time of the ripening? If karma is an impersonal causal law, how do you get from "this happened because of that" to "this was deserved" -- isn't that a straight is/ought jump, and if you plug the gap with a cosmic administrator, haven't you just rebuilt the problem of evil with extra steps? Does a beginningless series make every present state overdetermined, since the accumulated account is infinite? And if the intentions that generate karma are themselves produced by past karma, is there any room left for the kind of authorship desert seems to require? Finally, is the doctrine falsifiable at all, or does every apparent counterexample get absorbed by positing more past karma -- and if it's unfalsifiable, is that a defect in a doctrine that's supposed to explain why people suffer, or is it fine because it was never doing that job? Sort them by how decisive they'd be, tell me which ones defenders themselves concede and which they wave away, and say what the ledger view predicts that a rival account of suffering doesn't. Be blunt with me: if my framing is a caricature of what these doctrines actually hold, say so plainly rather than humouring me, and don't hand me a reading list -- I can't look anything up right now anyway.
