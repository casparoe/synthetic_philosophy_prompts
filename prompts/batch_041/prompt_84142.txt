I am teaching a unit on Descartes' account of animal minds, and I want to close it with Kafka's A Report to an Academy rather than with another round of the usual debate about whether Descartes denied animals consciousness. Kafka's ape, Red Peter, was captured in West Africa, shipped to Europe in a cage, and taught himself to imitate the sailors — he reports that the hardest part was learning to drink alcohol — and he presents his life story to a scientific academy as evidence of his ascent to human standing. What interests me is that he passes, and knows he passes, the two tests Descartes used to mark the boundary between humans and beasts: he produces connected speech and he adapts flexibly across situations, rather than displaying a fixed repertoire. Yet he insists throughout that he learned all of this for one reason only, to find a way out of his cage, and not from any desire to be human; and at the end he says his transformation is so complete that he can no longer describe what he felt as an ape. Read the story against Descartes' actual position, not the caricature. Descartes held that animal bodies are machines whose movements, sounds, and even acquired habits can be explained without thought, that animals lack an immaterial soul, and that the two tests reveal this — though a strand of the scholarship stresses that he denied animals thought rather than all sensation, and that he freely described them as fearing, hungering, and raging. I want a reading that takes both the story and the philosophy seriously in both directions. Where does Red Peter's performance refute the tests, and where does the story quietly concede Descartes' point — since a machine that imitated the crew well enough to escape would produce exactly this report? Is his speech the genuine language Descartes demanded, or the very thing Descartes said a sufficiently well-constructed automaton could simulate? Does it matter that his motive is escape rather than truth or fellowship, and that he treats his humanity as a technique, a means of exit, rather than an achievement? What should we make of the detail that Kafka has him distinguish himself from the music-hall ape of the same name — a gesture toward the difference between training and understanding that Descartes himself relied on? And what happens to Descartes' framework when a creature born without a soul acquires, by habituation alone, the capacities that were supposed to prove the presence of one: is the story a reductio of the tests, a demonstration that they were behavioral all along, or a dramatization of something neither Descartes nor his critics can represent — an animal who has lost the ability to say what it was? I would also like the moral and theological stakes brought in without being overstated: Descartes' denial of animal thought was taken, by friends and enemies alike, to license treating animals as unfeeling machinery, and Red Peter's hard-won humanity turns out to be another cage, a music-hall career or a zoological garden. End by saying what the story contributes to this debate that argument alone does not — whether it supplies a counterexample, exposes a confusion in the criteria, or shows why the criteria were the wrong thing to want in the first place. No plot summary for its own sake, no quotations from the text, and where you are uncertain what Descartes held in a particular passage, say so rather than attributing a definite view to him.
