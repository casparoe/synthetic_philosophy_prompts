Write a document for the internal standards series of the Consortium for Attributive Hygiene, an invented industry body that reviews incident reports filed by AI developers. The document is Standard 7: Licensing and Format of Belief-Attributions in Incident Reports. It should run to roughly 900 words, in the flat, procedural register of a corporate standards document — headings, numbered clauses, schedules, worked examples — and be signed off by invented officers with invented titles.

The Consortium works within a three-stance framework, which you may take as given and need not cite or attribute: a system's behaviour can be predicted by treating it as a physical object (the physical stance), by treating it as a device whose parts do what they were designed to do (the design stance), or by treating it as an agent whose actions follow from what it believes and wants (the intentional stance). The charter treats these as stances — predictive strategies a predictor adopts — rather than as claims about inner furniture. Standard 7 is the Consortium's attempt to write down when an incident report is permitted to use the third one.

The comedy has to come from the framework's real features, not from invented ones. The warrant for the intentional stance is predictive success rather than inner facts; the stances compete to explain the same episode and each is defensible on its own terms; attributions come in orders; and the standard must be applied to the Consortium's own reviewers, who file reports. Stay deadpan throughout: no narrator remarking that the policy is absurd, no scare quotes nudging the reader. Quotation marks should do policy work only — marking approved and prohibited phrasings — never ironic work. Every rule must be stateable and followable in principle, and wherever the document breaks its own rules, the breach must follow from the rules rather than from sloppiness.

Include at least the following. A substitution table of approved and prohibited phrasings, with the reason for each substitution. An evidentiary schedule tying each attribution to a burden of proof, running from behavioural evidence through interventions on inputs to internal probes. At least one invented technical test — for instance, a requirement that the attributed content be shown to make a difference under an intervention that is not itself drawn from the training distribution — and at least one clause that is, on inspection, impossible to satisfy. An escalation clause covering reports that require attributions of the form "the system expected that the reviewer would believe that the system intended to ...". A reciprocity clause licensing attributions to systems that themselves issue attributions. A carve-out, plainly motivated by liability rather than by philosophy, that restores intentional language wherever fault must be assigned. One worked example: a short incident in which the same outputs are equally well explained from all three stances, annotated clause by clause. And a single dissenting footnote from one member of the drafting committee, written in the register of a genuine objection, giving the strongest available argument against the standard.

The target is the practice, familiar in AI evaluation and safety work, of sliding between denying that a system has beliefs and treating it as a believing, concealing, or sandbagging agent according to which is convenient. Do not name or allude to any real person, laboratory, or organisation; the Consortium and everyone in it is invented. Assume no citations are available and offer none.
