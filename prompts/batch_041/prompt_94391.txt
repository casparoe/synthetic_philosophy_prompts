Last term I sat in on a graduate seminar on paradoxes, and the instructor closed the final session with a version of the surprise-exam puzzle that I had not encountered before. On Friday afternoon she announced to the class: "There will be exactly one exam next week, on one of the days Monday through Friday. It will be a surprise, in the following sense: on the evening before it, you will not know that an exam is coming the next day. And so that this does not depend on my whim, the day was already fixed this morning, written on a slip of paper, and sealed in an envelope that the department chair is holding; I cannot see it and cannot change it. You may also take my announcement as certainly true, since I have never once misled a class." Two of the students, Nadia and Piotr, kept arguing about it in the hallway afterward, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it.

Nadia's line was that the announcement is simply impossible, envelope or no envelope. She reconstructed the familiar backward induction — the exam cannot be on Friday, because a student who reaches Thursday evening with no exam yet would know it must be Friday and so would not be surprised; then it cannot be on Thursday either, for the same reason once Friday is excluded; and so on down to Monday — and concluded that because the announcement entails that the exam cannot fall on any day, the announcement is self-contradictory, and the added stipulation that it is "certainly true" only sharpens the absurdity, since the instructor has promised something that could not have been true even if she wanted it to be. Piotr granted her the induction step by step but denied the conclusion. His view was that the announcement is perfectly satisfiable, that the students' proof establishes only something about what the students can *know in advance*, and that Nadia had smuggled in the assumption that the students' knowledge of the announcement remains available to them in the hypothetical where no exam has yet occurred — which is precisely the assumption the surprise clause is designed to undercut. He added that the sealed envelope makes his point: the day was settled before anyone reasoned, so nothing the students deduce can retroactively move the exam, and if it lands on Wednesday they will in fact be surprised, which means the announcement was true all along. Nadia replied that "true all along" does no work — a proposition can be both true and provably unknowable — and that the real oddity is that the instructor's sentence seems to assert its own unknowability while also being the very premise the students lean on; she said Piotr's move quietly recasts "surprise" as a fact about the students' psychology rather than a fact about the content of the announcement. Piotr answered that this is not a dodge but the whole point: "surprise" is a success word, like "knows" or "proves," and sentences built from such words generate blind spots — truths that cannot be believed or known while they are true — so there is nothing incoherent about a true announcement that the students cannot exploit. Nadia countered that if he is right, then all of the instructor's extra machinery — the envelope, the "certainly true" — is idle, which she found suspicious, since a genuine solution should not leave the puzzle exactly where it started.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: the backward induction itself; Nadia's claim that the announcement is self-contradictory; Piotr's charge that she assumed the students' knowledge is available in the no-exam-yet case; his blind-spot defense; and Nadia's rejoinder that this makes the extra stipulations idle. For each, say who is right, or what is right in each and what each is missing, and then say which of the two is closer to the truth overall, and why. I am not after a survey of what has been written about this puzzle; I want the arguments as actually made, sorted out and scored.
