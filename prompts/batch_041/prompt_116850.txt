I run a graduate seminar on vagueness, and in last week's meeting two students kept a dispute going for most of the hour in a way I could not cleanly adjudicate from the chair. The occasion was a case I had constructed for the class. A computational linguistics lab had generated one thousand versions of a single-sentence email request, each version differing from the one before it by one word or one reordering; the change at each step was small enough that pilot subjects judged the two adjacent versions equally polite, and the sequence as a whole runs from an unmistakably courteous request to an unmistakably brusque one. The class agreed that the earliest versions are polite, that the latest are rude, and that no two adjacent versions differ perceptibly in rudeness. They also agreed, for the sake of argument, that "rude" is not a mere projection of the speaker's feelings and that its meaning is fixed by the usage of competent speakers; the fight was only over what that meaning delivers in the disputed middle. I was moderating rather than taking part, and I will keep my own view out of this.

The first student calls himself an epistemicist. He holds that there is a precise cut-off -- some one version is the last polite one -- and that it is unknowable in principle rather than merely unknown: at the boundary, the same evidence would have produced the same belief one step over, so a belief formed there could too easily have been false to count as knowledge. The second student, a supervaluationist, denies that any version is the cut-off. She says "rude" admits many admissible sharpenings; that in the penumbra a sentence like "version 437 is rude" -- 437 being a version squarely in the disputed middle -- is neither true nor false; and that although "some version is the first rude one" is true on every admissible sharpening, and so true on her view, that is a truth about how we sharpen, not a fact about the sequence. He replied that her sharpenings are arbitrary: if no sharpening is the correct one, what fixes the extent of the penumbra, and why does it stop where she says? She answered that he must accept higher-order vagueness too -- an unknowable boundary to the unknowable boundary, and so on without end -- whereas she needs only one vague notion, admissibility, to do the work, and that her view, unlike his, preserves classical validity, since excluded middle comes out true on every sharpening. He rejoined that a tower of unknowable boundaries is not incoherent, whereas her semantics is unstatable unless admissibility is itself precise; and that she buys her treatment of the penumbra by giving up bivalence and truth-functionality, since "either version 437 is rude or it isn't" comes out true on her view even though neither disjunct is.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each claim above, say how strong it is and where it goes wrong, then say who has the better of the dispute overall and what both students are missing. Give me no more than 800 words, in American English.
