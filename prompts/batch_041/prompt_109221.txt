I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on mathematical logic and the philosophy of mind, and I'm writing a response paper on the Lucas–Penrose argument. After class last week, two classmates—Maya and Jonah—got into a long argument about it, and I stayed out of it because I couldn't decide who was making sense. I'd like you to help me adjudicate the exchange point by point. Here is the argument as best I can reconstruct it, with the moves numbered for convenience.

1. Maya: "For any formal system M that is consistent and strong enough for arithmetic, the Gödel sentence G_M is true but unprovable in M. We can see that G_M is true. So no such M can capture all of our mathematical knowledge. That refutes mechanism."

2. Jonah: "We don't just see that G_M is true. We see that if M is consistent, then G_M is true. And that conditional is not even provable in M; it's a theorem of a stronger system. To get G_M itself, you need to know that M is consistent. But if M is a formal system for all your mathematical reasoning, you can't prove M's consistency without stepping outside M. So you're either assuming M is consistent—which is exactly the unproven premise—or you're using a stronger system M', which is itself a machine and has its own Gödel sentence."

3. Maya: "I don't need to prove M's consistency inside M. I can know it by reflection. I can inspect M's axioms and see that they're true, and true axioms can't yield a contradiction. That's a legitimate form of mathematical knowledge, not a formal proof inside M. So I can know G_M."

4. Jonah: "That reflection is either another formal system, in which case it has its own Gödel sentence, or it's informal and unreliable. Also, 'I can see the axioms are true' is not available for every consistent machine; some consistent machines have false axioms. You can only do that for systems you already trust. And if you add Con(M) as an axiom to M, you get M+Con(M), which proves G_M but has a new Gödel sentence G_{M+Con(M)}. You can ascend forever; at no finite stage do you have a system that proves all the Gödel sentences of its predecessors without having a new one. So the mind, if it's a machine, can be that ascending hierarchy—not a single formal system."

5. Maya: "But that's the point: the mind is not a single formal system. It's a capacity that can reflect on any given system and transcend it. That's exactly what no machine can do, because a machine is a fixed formal system. The hierarchy is not a machine; it's a sequence of machines, and the mind is the thing that runs through the sequence."

6. Jonah: "Then you've changed the definition of 'machine.' A machine can be a program that generates a sequence of formal systems, each adding the consistency of the previous one. That's still a finite program—it's a recursive procedure. It can output M, M+Con(M), M+Con(M+Con(M)), and so on. What it can't do is prove the consistency of the limit of that sequence, because there is no single formal system at the limit unless you take a non-recursive union. If the mind can do that, it's not a recursive machine, but that's a much stronger claim than 'no machine can prove its Gödel sentence.' And it's not obvious that humans can prove the consistency of the limit either; our mathematical insight might be exactly the same kind of open-ended reflection, not a magical fixed point."

7. Maya: "But the open-endedness is the difference. The machine is stuck at each stage; the mind sees that the whole sequence is sound. That's a genuine mathematical insight. If you say that insight is just another stage, you're ignoring the difference between a rule and the insight that the rule is sound."

8. Jonah: "And you're ignoring that 'the whole sequence is sound' is not a well-defined formal statement unless you specify a system. If you specify one, it has a Gödel sentence. If you don't, you're not making a mathematical claim, you're expressing a stance. Lucas and Penrose need the mind to be consistent and to know its own consistency; Gödel's theorem is precisely that a sufficiently strong consistent system can't do the latter. So the argument either assumes what it needs to prove or equivocates between 'the mind can see the truth of each G_M' and 'the mind can see the truth of G_M for the mind itself.'"

9. Maya: "But I don't need to know my own consistency; I only need to know the consistency of the machine I'm considering. And I can know that by inspecting its axioms. The machine can't do that for itself, because it's just a set of axioms and rules. That's the asymmetry."

10. Jonah: "If the machine is a candidate model of your reasoning, then it includes your ability to inspect axioms and reflect on consistency. If it includes that ability, then it can also reflect on itself—and then the same problem arises. If it doesn't include that ability, then it's not a model of your reasoning, so it's irrelevant. The argument needs a principled line between the reflection you allow the mind and the reflection you deny the machine. Without that line, it's not a proof; it's a picture."
