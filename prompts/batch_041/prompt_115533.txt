My flatmate and I have been having a right old arguement about G. E. Moore's views on beauty and friendship, and it spilled over at a dinner party last week when two mates, Sam and Alex, took opposite sides. I ended up just listening because I couldn't work out who was making sense. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move?

Here's how it went. Sam kicked off by saying Moore thinks beauty is intrinsically good even if no one ever experiences it. That's the whole point of the method of isolation: imagine a world with only a beautiful landscape and no one there, and compare it to a world with only an ugly landscape and no one there. Moore says the first is better. So beauty's value doesn't depend on being appreciated by anyone.

Alex said that's definately a misreading. The method of isolation is only meant to show that pleasure isn't the only good, not to prove that unexperienced beauty is a great good. Moore actually says the greatest goods are states of consciousness — the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of human intercourse. So the beauty of the object is just one part of an organic unity that includes the appreciation. Without the appreciation, the beauty might be good, but it's not the greatest good.

Sam shot back: but if beauty is good in itself, then its value is there whether or not anyone appreciates it. Organic unity doesn't mean the parts have no value; it just means the whole can be more valuable than the sum of the parts. So the unexperienced beauty still has value, and that value is independent of any mind. That's a radical view, and Moore seems to hold it.

Alex said: then Moore's view is inconsistent, because he also says friendship is one of the greatest goods. But friendship requires actual people and actual affection. If unexperienced beauty is so valuable, why isn't unexperienced friendship — say, two people who never meet but who would have been great friends — also valuable? And if it isn't, then the value of friendship depends on the experience, which suggests the value of beauty also depends on experience.

Sam replied: maybe the difference is that friendship is an organic unity that includes the existence of the friend as a person, not just the experience. The friend's existence is a good in itself, and the affection is another good, and the whole is greater. But beauty doesn't require a person.

Alex came back: but then you're saying the friend's existence is good in itself, which means there are non-experiential goods. So why can't beauty be one? And if the friend's existence is good, is it good even if no one ever knows them? That seems odd. And if the friend's existence is only good because it enables the friendship experience, then you're back to experiences being what matter.

They went round and round. I couldn't referee. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And what, if anything, both of them are missing about Moore's actual position?
