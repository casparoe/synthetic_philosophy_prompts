A graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology starts next month, and I want the first handout to be a decision tree rather than a glossary of positions. The subject is what an agent should do when the evidence she acquires leaves her less than certain of any proposition — when experience moves her credences in the cells of some partition without making any one of them 1.

The tree should begin from a commitment the participants will already share: that experiences sometimes shift credences in this way, and that the agent's new credences in the cells of the relevant partition lie strictly between 0 and 1. Build the tree outward from the questions rather than from a list of names: first enumerate the combinations of answers the questions allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note which combinations are empty.

Every node must be a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to. The branching should run through at least these distinctions, in whatever order makes them easiest to answer:

- whether there is after all always a proposition the agent is certain of, so that the apparently uncertain case reduces to conditionalization on something (the experience itself, the fact that she is in a certain subjective state, or some other proposition), or whether the reduction is impossible and the shift is genuinely not conditionalization on anything;
- whether the agent's new credences in the cells of the partition are themselves part of her evidence or part of her response to it;
- whether the update must leave conditional credences within the cells untouched, and what becomes of the rule if that requirement is dropped;
- whether the final credences may depend on the order in which successive pieces of uncertain evidence arrive, and if they may not, what extra constraint is supposed to rule that out and what it costs;
- whether the rule is supported by a coherence or bookmaking argument, is merely a sufficient condition for avoiding inconsistency, or is one option among several that an agent may rationally choose on non-evidential grounds;
- whether probabilities must be sharp.

The handout is useless if these leaves are missing, so each must appear somewhere, carrying the standard label for the position (or 'unnamed' if you think nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment:

1. the rule that holds conditional credences within the cells fixed and forms the posterior as the weighted average;
2. the reduction of the uncertain case to conditionalization on a proposition the agent is certain of;
3. the view that the shift determines only a family of admissible posteriors, together with the further principle that selects one of them;
4. the view that selecting among those posteriors is a decision, so that the agent's epistemic goals or values are doing the work;
5. the position that the rule must be supplemented by an extra constraint before the outcome can be independent of the order in which uncertain evidence arrives;
6. the set-valued or non-additive treatment of evidence, in which the posterior is a set of credence functions, or something weaker than a probability, rather than a single probability;
7. the rejection of the whole framework, on the ground that 'uncertain evidence' is a contradiction and the puzzle is an artifact of describing experience in the wrong vocabulary.

Four cases should do the diagnostic work, and each node should be answerable by reference to one of them: (a) looking at a fabric in poor light and coming away 0.7 confident that it is green; (b) two successive looks at the same dial, each shifting credences in the same partition by a different amount; (c) a fact you have believed with certainty for years, whose bearing on a hypothesis changes only now that the hypothesis has been formulated; (d) a witness whose reliability you take to be below 1 and about whose reliability you hold credences as well.

Two further constraints. No node may mention a position by name, and no leaf may be reached by two different routes unless you flag that the tree cannot separate them; where a leaf is occupied by nobody, say so and say whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the whole thing to one page and at most ten leaves. Names of philosophers and works should not appear anywhere, and no reading list or citations are wanted.
