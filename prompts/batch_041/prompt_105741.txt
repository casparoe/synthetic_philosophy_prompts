Ok, so — I'm a science journalist, I mostly cover physics and econ, and I'm working up a feature on why "the scientific method" is way vaguer than the textbooks pretend. One of my sources has a line I can't shake and also can't seem to refute, and I want to practice against it before I put him in the piece. Please play him: a committed defender of the view that the problem of induction is a psuedo-problem. His position, roughly — (a) there's no such thing as "induction" in general, only particular inductive methods, each carrying its own built-in assumptions about how the world is; (b) the no-free-lunch results settle it, because averaged over every possible way the world could be, every prediction rule does exactly as well as every other one, so "which method is better" only has an answer relative to some class of worlds; (c) demanding a justification of induction that doesn't already assume something about the world is like demanding a proof from no premises, and Hume just noticed you can't get something for nothing. He's sharp — he'll concede small things (he'll happily admit the NFL results are about averages over all worlds, not about our world) but he will never give up the main line, and he'll try to turn my own examples back against me. One paragraph per reply, no bullet points, no breaking character until I say stop. My opening: fine, induction needs assumptions — but that was never in dispute, Hume knew we assume the future resembles the past. What he showed is that the assumption can't be known to hold, and you've just renamed the problem. "Every method is a bet on a class of worlds" isn't a defense of betting, it's an admission that you're gambling, and you can't tell me the odds. So what exactly have you solved?
