My partner is pregnant with our first kid, and for some reason I went down a rabbit hole on the doomsday argument and now I can't stop thinking about it. At a BBQ last weekend two friends of mine, Ravi and Talia, got into it about it and I just stood there with a burger. I want you to referee it for me, point by point.

Ravi said the argument is basically right. He said: imagine two urns, one with 10 balls and one with 1000. You draw a ball and it's number 7. It's way more likely you drew from the 10-ball urn. Now swap 'you drew a ball' for 'you are a human' and 'ball number' for 'your birth rank among all humans who will ever live.' You're roughly the 100 billionth human. If humans last a long time, your rank is unremarkable; if we go extinct soon, you're near the end of the list. So the fact that you're not super early is definately evidence the end is nigh. He said the argument doesn't need a prior about the total number of humans; it just uses your rank as a random sample.

Talia said that's a classic abuse of probability. She said you can't treat your birth rank as a random draw from a well-defined urn because the urn's size is exactly what's in question, and the reference class is arbitrary. Is it all humans, all hominids, all conscious beings, all observers? Change the class and you change the answer. She also said the argument ignores that your existence is more likely in worlds with more people, so finding yourself alive is evidence for more people, not fewer. Ravi said that's the self-indication assumption, and it's controversial because it makes your own existence count as evidence in a way that leads to weird conclusions. Talia said the self-sampling assumption is just as weird: it treats you as a randomly selected observer when you're not randomly selected from anything -- you're just you. Ravi said every probabilistic argument has to conditionalize on something, and the doomsday argument only needs the assumption that your birth rank isn't special. Talia said that assumption is the whole ballgame, and it's not obvious. Then Ravi said even if it's only a weak argument, it still shifts the probability somewhat. Talia said a weak argument from a bad model is still a bad argument, and you can't just say 'some shift' without a prior over total humans. Ravi said if you reject this, you also have to reject any argument that says 'we're not special' -- like fine-tuning arguments that say our existence is more likely under one hypothesis. Talia said maybe those arguments are bad too, or maybe they're different, but either way you can't use one bad argument to rescue another.

They asked me who was right and I said I'd ask someone smarter. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I want to know which parts of the doomsday argument are actually valid, which objections are decisive, and whether the argument gives any real reason to think humanity ends soon. Also, say what both of them are missing.
