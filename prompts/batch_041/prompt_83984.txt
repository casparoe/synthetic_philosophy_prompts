I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I've somehow ended up reading Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence on my commute. The part that's stuck in my head is where he brings up telepathy. Everyone I mention this to goes haha yeah Turing believed in ESP, quaint, and moves on. But the way he uses it doesn't look quaint to me, it looks like a move I recognize from my day job.

Roughly what he does: he's working through objections to the idea that machines could think, and one of them is that telepathy could let information move around the game in ways that have nothing to do with intelligence — an interrogator or one of the players picking stuff up without the normal question-and-answer channel. His response isn't to say telepathy is bunk. He takes it seriously enough to say that if you're actually running the experiment you should run it in a room shielded against telepathy so the possibility is controlled for. That's a confound being handled. It's not a metaphysical claim being made.

The idea: the telepathy bit is a stress test for how we're supposed to read the whole imitation game, and people read it two ways that can't both be right. One camp reads the game as a criterion — passing it is what it means for a machine to think, or at least is the best operational substitute for that question. The other reads it as an experiment, a way of gathering evidence. Turing's handling of telepathy looks like the second thing. If it's an experiment, then the telepathy worry is just a problem with the experimental setup and shielding is the right fix, and the criterion reading is us making a category error on his behalf. But if it's a criterion, then the possibility of telepathy doesn't only threaten the experiment, it threatens the assumption that verbal behaviour is our only window onto another mind at all — which is the thing the test quietly leans on. And there's a third wrinkle I haven't worked out: if telepathy is on the table, the human side of the game might have a channel the machine doesn't, so the comparison isn't obviously fair, and shielding the room isn't obviously the fix, because that removes a human capacity rather than equalizing the two sides.
