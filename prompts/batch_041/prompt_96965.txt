I'm in my third year of a philosophy degree, and I'm currently taking a seminar on effective altruism and normative ethics. Last week we spent a session on moral trade — the idea that two people who care about different things, or who weight the same things very differently, may each do better by their own evaluative lights by exchanging actions than by acting unilaterally: I do something you care about, you do something I care about, and the exchange can be mutually advantageous even though neither of us is moved by the other's reasons. I found the idea compelling and defended it in class. A classmate then gave me the argument below, which I've written out as faithfully as I can, and which I have not been able to shake.

(1) An exchange between two competent adults is permissible, other things equal, when both parties consent and each ends up better off by her own standards than she would have been otherwise. (2) A moral trade is an exchange of exactly that form; what makes it moral is only that each party values the goods traded for moral reasons. (3) So moral trades are permissible, other things equal — and often positively valuable, since they let each party advance her own moral priorities further than she could alone. (4) The goods traded need not be confined to the parties themselves: plenty of ordinary exchanges purchase actions whose benefits and burdens fall on third parties. (5) Consent plus mutual gain is the whole test for the permissibility of an exchange; whether an exchange is permissible cannot depend on what either party would otherwise have done, since that is a fact about the world before the act, not about the act. (6) So if you tell me that you were going to eat meat at every meal this year and will stop only if I pay you, my paying you is permissible on precisely the same grounds. (7) And if you tell me that you were going to eat meat at every meal and to talk others into doing the same, and will stop only for a larger sum, paying you is permissible too — the sum is simply larger. (8) Generalising: anyone can convert any conduct of theirs that I morally oppose into a bargaining chip, and I am permitted to buy them off. (9) But that is extortion — the wrong of holding a moral hostage — and it is not permissible. (10) So something in (1)–(5) is false.

I would like you to act as a philosopher working on the ethics of exchange and to think this through carefully, rather than reaching for the standard talking points on coercion, consent, or commodification. Please begin by setting out your own reconstruction of the argument's logical joints, marking any step whose wording admits more than one reading. Then commit to a verdict: either name the step you reject, explain why it looked innocent, and say what rejecting it costs, or — if you think the chain is valid — say so plainly and tell me which plausible premise is the least costly to give up. I should warn you that I have already tried rejecting (5), and it seemed to cost more than I expected: it appeared to commit me to saying that paying a kidnapper's ransom is impermissible even when it saves the hostage, that a union's threat to strike is a form of extortion, and that paying a factory owner to stop polluting is morally on a par with paying him not to start. If those are the bills, I want them itemised. I would rather keep the spirit of (1)–(3) than accept (9), so tell me what I am buying at that price, and whether a defender of moral trade can distinguish the cases in (6)–(8) from the cases in my own list without surrendering the claim that moral trade is generally permissible. Please don't close with a survey of positions or an "it depends" — I want a diagnosis and a price tag.
