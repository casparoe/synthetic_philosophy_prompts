I teach a graduate epistemology seminar on higher-order evidence, and I want to hand the students a decision tree that sorts them into the positions they actually hold about epistemic akrasia, rather than the labels they would volunteer if asked directly. Please build it.

Fix the starting point for me, so the tree does not waste a node relitigating it: the students begin from an agent whose total evidence includes facts about her own reasoning — that a peer disagrees, that she has taken a pill, that her confidence in this domain is badly calibrated — and who accepts that such facts bear on whether her first-order belief is rational. The tree should take that much for granted and sort only what follows once you accept it.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a roll-call of famous names. First the questions, and the combinations of answers they permit; then locate the positions in the space. I expect the first branch to be something like the question of whether the verdict about what to believe and the verdict about whether one's believing is rational must always coincide — but if you think a different question cleaves the space more cleanly, start there and say why. Each branch node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no node may be of the form "do you accept conciliationism?" or any other question that names the label it delivers.

These leaves have to appear somewhere in the tree, though not necessarily at the same depth: (i) the view that a peer's disagreement of itself compels movement toward the middle, whatever the first-order evidence says; (ii) the view that higher-order evidence is simply one more item in the total evidence, sometimes outweighed; (iii) the view that a belief resting on the right first-order reasons cannot be defeated by evidence about how one came by it; (iv) the view that it can be rational to hold a belief while judging that holding it is not rational; (v) the view that this combination is impossible for a rational agent, so the levels are locked together; (vi) the view that rationality is permissive here, so no unique revision is required. Label each leaf with the name of the position, using a descriptive label if you are not confident of a standard one, and add one sentence on the commitment that leaves that position most exposed. If you attach a philosopher's name to a leaf, do so only where you are confident the attribution is correct; I would rather have a descriptive label than a fabricated citation.

Now the angle I care about, which should keep the tree from being answerable by reciting a textbook: I want the tree to handle two cases the standard presentations usually finesse. First, the case where the higher-order evidence is about the agent's capacity to assess higher-order evidence — she learns that her judgment about how much to trust her own judgment is itself the kind of judgment she is bad at. Second, the diachronic case, where the disagreeing peer is her own past or future self. Add whatever branches these force, and if some of the positions above cannot be stated coherently in these cases, say so at the leaf rather than hiding it.

Where a cell of the space comes out empty, note it and diagnose it briefly: is nobody there because the position is incoherent, because it is coherent but no one would want it, or because it is simply unexplored? Flag any two leaves the tree cannot actually separate, instead of papering over the gap with a finer-sounding question.

Aim for four to six branch nodes and seven to nine leaves, and keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout: questions at the nodes, positions at the leaves, no exposition longer than a sentence or two at any point.

Finally, tell me where the instrument is most likely to be wrong. Which single branch is doing the most work — the one that, if a student answered it the other way, would move her furthest across the tree — and which leaf you are least confident is genuinely distinct from its neighbor. State what would force me to redraw the tree: a position you could not place, a distinction that turns out to collapse, or a case in which two leaves make the same prediction, and say which of those you would most expect to encounter.
