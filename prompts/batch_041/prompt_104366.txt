At a literary festival last autumn I sat at dinner between two friends — Delia, a screenwriter, and Marek, a psychologist — who began by arguing about a scene in my most recent novel and ended up somewhere much older. I stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over ever since, and I'd like you to referee. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Delia: I sobbed at the end — genuinely sobbed — and I know perfectly well the brother never existed. So the emotion is real, but its object can't be a person. It has to be the proposition the book asks me to entertain: I was sad that, in the story, this happened. A real emotion about a real proposition.

(2) Marek: I don't think you were sad. You were playing a game in which it's true that you're sad. You imagined being sad; you didn't feel sadness for someone who isn't there. The tell is that your "sadness" doesn't do what sadness does — you don't try to help, you don't mourn, you don't dread the anniversary. Real sadness has a functional profile, and yours had none of it. Which is also why crying at fiction isn't irrational: there's no belief for the emotion to contradict.

(3) Delia: That's behaviorism about emotion. Real grief often runs without the full profile — suppressed grief, love for someone far away, anger at a parent who's long dead. And I did avoid the book for a week; I couldn't reread the last chapter. If a quasi-emotion can mimic every mark of the real thing, you've made the distinction unfalsifiable.

(4) Marek: I'm not denying the state is real, or that it feels like something. I'm making a claim about its cognitive structure. You believe nothing bad happened, so you can't literally be sad *that* it happened. What you have is a real feeling carried by an attitude that isn't belief at all — imagining — and imagining has its own norms rather than belief's. The memoir that turns out to be fabricated is a different case, because there you formed a belief and it was false. The paradox only looks like a paradox if you assume every emotion must answer to what you believe.

(5) Delia: Then the feeling is second-hand. On your account I'm moved by my own act of imagining, not by him — and that both undersells what fiction does and contradicts the phenomenology, because the grief is *for him*. And here's a datum from the book itself. Readers keep telling me they grieved most for the brother who is never given any interiority — who exists only in other characters' reports. If the emotion were about imagining a person, the fully imagined, interior characters should move us most. The withheld one moves us most instead. That suggests the emotion tracks the *shape* of the telling — its withholding — and not any imagined person at all.

(6) Marek: The withholding datum actually fits my side. A missing interiority is a gap the reader fills, and the filling is where the affect lives; a gap you fill yourself feels more like yours. As for second-hand — that's a feature, not a bug. Fiction lets us rehearse feeling at no cost. That's why we go to it.

(7) Delia: But then the object of the grief is my own filling of the gap — grief about my own imagining. That's a third candidate object, neither the character nor the proposition. And it can't be right either, because the grief is *for him*, and it outlives the imagining: I can be ambushed by it weeks later, when I'm not reading and not pretending anything.

(8) Marek: Outliving the reading just shows a real feeling with a fictional object can linger; nothing about the object's non-existence makes the feeling unreal. Or, if you want the cleaner version: what lingers is a real emotion about the *aptness* of the imagined grief — you were moved by how fitting it was to grieve him, and fittingness is a genuine property of the imagined situation, not a fiction about a fiction.

They left it there, each convinced the other had begged the question, and I honestly couldn't say who had the better of it.

Please adjudicate move by move, from (1) through (8): for each, say whether it's mistaken, defensible, or right, and briefly why. I'd like to know which of the three candidate objects — the character, the proposition, or the reader's own imagining — can actually bear the weight, and whether Marek's "aptness" move in (8) rescues his position or quietly abandons it. Also tell me whether the withholding datum in (5) is real evidence for either side or neutral, and whether there's a coherent position available that neither of them states. Finally, say who is more nearly right overall — not just who won each round.
