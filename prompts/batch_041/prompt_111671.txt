I have no training in philosophy or economics — I run a small business and mostly work these things out on long drives — but I've been trying to get my own views on risky decisions straight, and I'd like someone to tell me whether they actually hang together. The occasion was an argument with friends over one of those puzzles where you choose between a guaranteed sum and a gamble; I gave my answers, someone insisted they couldn't all be right, and I couldn't see where the problem was supposed to be. Here is where I've landed, as plainly as I can put it. (1) When the odds are known, the right way to choose is to weigh each possible outcome both by how likely it is and by how much it actually matters to me, and then go with whichever option comes out best on that reckoning. (2) I hold what looks to me like a near-truism: if two options would give me exactly the same thing across some range of situations, then what happens in that range cannot be a reason to prefer one option over the other — the choice has to turn only on the situations where the two options genuinely differ. (3) Offered a guaranteed $1,000,000, or a gamble with an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, I would take the guaranteed $1,000,000. (4) But offered an 11% chance of $1,000,000 (and nothing otherwise) versus a 10% chance of $5,000,000 (and nothing otherwise), I would take the 10% shot at $5,000,000. (5) I don't believe (3) and (4) make me inconsistent, because what puts me off the gamble in (3) is that 1% chance of ending up with nothing, and nothing of that kind is at stake in (4). (6) I also think it is sensible to buy insurance and silly to buy lottery tickets, and I take that to be part of the same outlook rather than a separate matter.

What I want is an audit of this package rather than a general essay on decision-making. Go through the commitments and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to — I would much rather you separate the real conflicts from the merely apparent ones than flag everything as a tension. Rank the genuine tensions by how serious they are, and for each one say precisely which commitment would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me, in terms of what I would have to abandon to restore consistency. If a single change would tidy up the most, tell me which one that is. Please answer in British English.
