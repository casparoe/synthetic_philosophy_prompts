Some friends and I started a little Ibsen-and-philosophy reading group this winter -- we did A Doll's House back in October and now its my turn to kick off Hedda Gabler, so I've been put down for the opening remarks. The thing that keeps nagging at me is that I read the play as a document about a moral revolution that already happened, offstage, before anyone in it notices. General Gabler's pistols are lying around the whole time -- the general's pistols, the kind a man of his generation settled affairs of honor with -- and by the time we meet Hedda they've been repurposed: idle shooting practice, then one of them goes off in a brothel and one of them ends up at her own temple. Noone in the play ever argues that dueling is wrong, or that the honor code is finished. It's just gone. What's left is Hedda, who still has the props and the reflexes and no audience for any of it, surrounded by people who've traded honor in for respectability, career, money, and what people will say (which is basically Brack's whole currency).

So what I want is a real reading of the play through the debate about how moral revolutions actually happen. You've got the argument story (norms die when the arguments against them win), the interest story -- the older economic account you get from Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery, where slavery goes when the economic arrangement that carried it goes -- and Appiah's line in The Honor Code, that dueling and footbinding ended because honor itself shifted, because it became shameful to be seen to care about the thing, and not because the moralists won the argument. Which of these does Hedda Gabler dramatize, and which does it quietly refute? The play has an economics in it (Tesman's professorship, the villa they can't really afford, the manuscript Hedda burns and that Thea and Tesman then piece back together from notes) and it has an honor economy (the pistols, the dread of scandal, Brack's grip on the household), and I'd like you to say how the two are wired together instead of just picking one.

Then the part I actually care about, where I'd like you to push past Appiah: his story is told from the side of the people who won. Hedda looks like a play about the laggards -- the ones for whom the dead code is still the only code they know how to live by, and who experience the revolution not as liberation but as a trap with no exit except the pistol. I also want a mechanism in the mix that I think Appiah's account is short on, the preference-cascade picture you get from Timur Kuran's work on preference falsification -- everyone privately doubts a norm while publicly complying, and then a few visible defections make the private doubt public and the whole thing collapses at once. Does the end of dueling need something like that, or does honor really just change by itself? Does Hedda stage a cascade that never quite arrives, the last holdouts waiting for a signal that doesn't come? And be honest where the play resists the whole framing: if the honor reading flattens Ibsen's Hedda into an illustration of a theory, or if the play is actually about something the moral-revolutions debate has no vocabulary for, just say so, and tell me which scenes to point the group to when we meet.
