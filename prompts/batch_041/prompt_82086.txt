I am a high school student writing an essay on how we should revise our beliefs when the evidence itself is uncertain, and I would like you to build a case for me rather than summarise one from the literature.

We are working with one rule, so let me state it as I understand it: when a piece of evidence shifts the probabilities of the cells of some partition without ruling any cell out, Jeffrey conditionalization holds fixed your conditional probability of each proposition given each cell, and then averages those conditional probabilities, weighted by the new cell probabilities. My essay argues that the order in which evidence arrives should not affect what one ought to believe at the end, and I suspect this rule breaks that requirement. I need a case that demonstrates the break, with the disagreement derived from the rule rather than stipulated.

Please construct the case under these specifications.

- An ordinary setting (a kitchen, a classroom, a clinic), one person, and at most four basic possibilities in the model. No lotteries, no infinite or continuous possibilities, and nothing drawn from the published literature on this topic.
- Two pieces of uncertain evidence she could plausibly come by — a half-glimpsed object, a half-heard remark — each leaving her credence in the relevant proposition strictly between 0 and 1, and each capable of arriving before the other.
- Her prior credences stated as explicit numbers, and each piece of evidence modelled by holding fixed the conditional probabilities within its own partition; say which conditional probabilities you hold fixed and why that is the natural choice.
- The case must be built so that the two orders of updating yield different final credences, and the disagreement must show up not only in the credence you used to state the uncertain evidence but also in at least one further proposition, which you should name and compute.

Then walk me through the arithmetic for both orders; say whether one order matches her actual evidential situation or whether neither does because the rule is being misapplied, and in that case what else her evidence would have to include before the rule gave a determinate answer; identify exactly which feature of the case makes the orders diverge and what becomes of the divergence if that feature is removed (in particular, whether it would survive if both pieces of evidence had been certain); and say how a defender of the rule would attack your case.

Finally, tell me which consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. If these specifications cannot all be met at once, tell me which one you relaxed and why.
