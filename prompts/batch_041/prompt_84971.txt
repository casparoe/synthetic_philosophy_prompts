I'm putting the finishing touches on a paper for a volume on infinity and supertasks and I've hit the point where I can no longer tell whether my central move is clever or just confused, so I'd like you to read the draft in the form I'm describing below and tell me what's wrong with it. The draft is short, about 3000 words. I start with the old Thomson lamp — off at the start, switched on at the half-minute mark, off at three quarters, on at seven eighths, and so on, so infinitely many switchings get crammed into the minute — and ask the usual question about what the lamp is doing when the minute is up. I take the standard line to be that there's no answer, and that any answer you like can be stipulated without contradiction, since nothing in the description settles the state at the limit. My claim is that this line runs together two things that should be kept apart: (a) the set-up as described is incomplete, so the state at the limit is under-specified, and (b) there is no fact of the matter about the state at the limit at all. To prise them apart I use what I've been calling the dimmer test. Replace the two-position switch with a dimmer knob, start at full brightness, and halve the knob position at each stage. Now the knob has an obvious limit, namely zero, and brightness is a continuous function of knob position for any real dimmer, so the brightness at the end of the minute is forced — zero — even though there's still no last step in the sequence and no stage at which the dimmer is set to zero. If that's right, the original lamp's failure to have an answer isn't about the impossibility of completing an infinite sequence of actions; it's a parochial feature of the discontinous state space of an on/off switch. I then say the same diagnosis covers the grimmer cousins of the lamp, the ones where infinitely many agents are each under orders to act at a time closer and closer to noon, each acting only if no earlier agent has already acted, so that you can't be alive at noon and yet no particular agent is the one who got you: those puzzles, I claim, do all their real work in the causal and temporal premises, which is irrelevent to the infinity.

So, comments please, and be blunt. (i) Is the dimmer test fair, or do I quietly help myself to the continuity that makes the limit well-defined — i.e., am I begging the question by choosing a system whose physics hands me an answer? (ii) Is the (a)/(b) distinction doing any real work, or does the dimmer just show that some supertasks are well-behaved and some aren't, which I'd have thought was old news and hardly worth a paper? (iii) How much does it hurt me that a physical dimmer presumably can't be reset infinitely often in a finite time anyway — doesn't that make the dimmer as fictional as the lamp, so that the continuity is a feature of the idealisation rather than of anything real? (iv) Where is the draft boring? I have a nagging feeling it's 3000 words long because I keep restating the same intuition in fresh clothes, and I'd rather cut than pad.

And one last thing: if this (a)/(b) contrast already has a standard name, tell me what it's usually called — but don't send me a reading list, I'll track things down myself. I mainly want to know whether I'm reinventing a wheel, and what counterexample to the dimmer test should be keeping me up at night.
