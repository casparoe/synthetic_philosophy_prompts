I am a third-year religious studies and philosophy student, and I am currently taking a seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy that has been working through Maimonides' Guide of the Perplexed. For my response paper I have to present a reconstruction of his treatment of animal sacrifice and defend it against the charge that it is internally unstable. I have set out the reconstruction as five commitments, and I would like you to audit them rather than explain Maimonides to me. The commitments are:

(1) Maimonides treats the sacrificial commandments as a pedagogical accommodation to the Israelites' prior sacrificial habits and to the surrounding idolatrous cults, not as something God needed or delighted in for its own sake.

(2) He treats the commandments of the Torah as rational through and through, ordered to human perfection, and not as arbitrary decrees whose only warrant is divine fiat.

(3) He treats prayer, study, and ethical conduct as the enduring core of divine service, with sacrifice at most instrumental and preparatory.

(4) He treats the Torah as immutable and expects the messianic age to restore the Temple and its sacrificial worship.

(5) He holds a negative theology on which predicates such as wants, delights, or is pleased do not apply to God literally.

I have also been persuaded by a reading, which may be my own gloss rather than Maimonides', that his position is best understood as a kind of divine harm-reduction strategy: God does not endorse the sacrificial cult as an end, but regulates, centralizes, and redirects it because an abrupt abolition would have been psychologically and socially impossible for the Israelites. On this reading, the point of the laws is to move a people already oriented toward sacrifice away from idolatry and toward a more rational service, without asking them to become different people overnight. I have been trying to keep separate the reason for a commandment and the reason for its particular historical form; my harm-reduction gloss is meant to explain the latter, not to displace the former.

I suspect there is a real tension somewhere in this package, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine inconsistency, a tension that dissolves once the commitments are properly distinguished, or a sign that my harm-reduction gloss is doing work Maimonides would not accept. Please tell me which commitments are in tension and which only appear to be; rank the tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most while doing the least violence to the overall reconstruction. I am less interested in a survey of scholarly opinion than in whether this particular package hangs together.
