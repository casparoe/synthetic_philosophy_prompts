At a departmental seminar last month I watched a senior colleague dispose of a junior researcher's proposal in a single sentence: the claim, he said, was unfalsifiable, and anything unfalsifiable is not science. I have since heard the same move used in three quite different settings — against a functional explanation in evolutionary biology that seemed able to accommodate any observation, against a reconstruction of a unique past event that no one can rerun, and against a simulation flexible enough to fit almost any dataset — and in each case it ended the conversation rather than advancing it. I do not think the sentence is empty, but I have come to suspect that it fuses several different claims, and that my colleague's conclusion follows from at most one of them.

Please take the one-liner apart for me. I want a menu rather than a single preferred reading: the distinct precise theses that the sentence "unfalsifiable, therefore not science" could be asserting, each with a verdict. Separate, on the one side, the senses of falsifiability that might be in play — a logical relation between the claim and some conceivable observation; a property that belongs only to the claim taken together with its auxiliary assumptions and background knowledge; a property of an entire research programme, including whether its defenders are willing to accept any result as refuting it; and a purely practical matter of what can presently be observed, repeated, or computed. Separate, on the other side, the several things "not science" might be denying — that the claim is meaningful; that it is empirically testable at all; that it deserves funding, publication, or a place in a classroom; and that it is true. For each pairing that is not obviously incoherent, say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible position, or false as stated, and identify the consideration that decides between them. Then tell me which reading is actually doing the work when someone deploys the phrase to end an argument, and which readings, if any, would genuinely license my colleague's conclusion.

I have my own suspicion, and I would rather have it tested than confirmed: that the phrase usually does less logical work than it appears to and more sociological work, marking the point at which a community stops being willing to let a claim die. If that is right, say what follows for how the phrase ought to be used; if it is wrong, say precisely where it goes wrong.

Finally, run the menu over my three cases, and do not spare me. If the accusation is correct in any of them and the proposal really does deserve to be set aside, say so plainly rather than softening it; and tell me what I could say in the moment that would be accurate and honest rather than merely a debating trick.
