I'm a novelist at work on a literary novel whose protagonist is a mathematician, and one scene turns on a lecture she gives about Gödel's incompleteness theorems to an audience of non-mathematicians. The scene has to work on two levels at once: she should be saying things a competent mathematician would endorse, and the two or three places where she reaches past what the theorems actually support should be legible as overreach to a reader who knows the material. For research I have been leaning on a popular trade book about the limits of mathematics — the kind that gets the history and the drama right and the logic a little glossy. Here is the gist of the passage I have been working from, as I have absorbed it:

"The first incompleteness theorem shows that in any consistent system of mathematics rich enough to describe arithmetic, there are statements that are true but that the system can never prove — true of the numbers, true in every model of the axioms, and permanently beyond the reach of proof. The second theorem shows that such a system can never prove its own consistency, so mathematics can never be certain that it is not harboring a contradiction. Together the two results demolished Hilbert's program, which had hoped to place all of mathematics on an unshakable foundation. Gödel reached them by a single trick, a version of the liar paradox: he built a sentence that says of itself that it is not provable, so that if you could prove it you would make it false, and if you cannot prove it, then it is true. Some philosophers have taken the theorems to show that the human mind is not a machine, since we can see the truth of the Gödel sentence while no computer can. And some physicists have drawn a further moral: no final theory of nature can ever be written, because whatever laws we discover, there will be facts they cannot reach. What no one disputes is that mathematics is permanently incomplete — there will always be truths beyond proof — and that the dream of a finished, fully certified mathematics died in 1931."
