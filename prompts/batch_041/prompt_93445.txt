I am a novelist, and I have arrived at the scene on which the book turns: a veteran foreign-service adviser, speaking to my protagonist in a room that smells of cold coffee and older wars, argues against opening negotiations with the power that presses on their small state's border. He does not argue from interests; he argues from memory. This is 1938 again, he says — the disputed province is another Sudetenland, the conference is another Munich, and the men now urging talks are the men who once came home promising peace for a generation. My protagonist wants to negotiate and cannot answer him, and I confess I cannot either. I know the comparison is doing enormous work; I do not know whether it can bear the weight.

What I want from you is not a verdict on the novel but an audit of the analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly — in the Munich settlement of 1938, what answers to the aggressor, the ceded territory, the betrayed ally, the guarantor, the conference, and the domestic audience, and what in my imagined crisis answers to each. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. My crisis, as I have conceived it, involves an adversary armed with nuclear weapons; a province of mixed and bitterly contested population; a mutual-defense treaty with a distant great power whose willingness to honor it is genuinely uncertain; and a home economy dependent on the adversary's exports of a critical commodity. Which of these disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion my adviser draws — that talks now are appeasement, and that concession will only invite the next demand — and which are merely atmospheric? Tell me, too, what the analogy quietly imports that my situation may not contain: a theory of what aggressors ultimately want, an assumption about when guarantees deter, a reading of how publics reward firmness and punish retreat.

Finally, keep two questions apart. One is what 1938 establishes as history; the other is what my adviser needs it to establish as rhetoric, where Munich may function less as an inference than as a way of saying that the cost of being wrong is unsurvivable. I would like to know whether that weaker use stands even if the strict analogy fails. And because he is a man of several analogies, tell me whether this one can coexist with the two he reaches for elsewhere — Vietnam, when he wants to argue that distant commitments bleed a state dry, and the Thucydides trap, when he wants to say that a rising power and a ruling power are fated to collide. If the three cannot be held together without contradiction, I would rather know before I write the scene. If a better analogy would serve him, name it, and say why it fits what Munich cannot.
