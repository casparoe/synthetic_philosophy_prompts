Ok so I've been lurking on LessWrong-adjacent decision theory stuff for way too long and I want a straight answer on something that's been bugging me for months.

Here's the setup. I made it up but I think it's clean. I live in city A. There's a stable, predictable pattern in me: whenever I'm in A, I want to be in B, and I'd pay $200 to switch. Whenever I'm in B, I want to be in A, and I'd pay $200 to switch back. It's not noise, it's a reliable feature — whichever city I'm in, the other one looks better and I'll pay to get there. A friend notices and offers to run it as a service: $200 a move, he keeps the cash, he'll happily bounce me back and forth forever. I know all of this in advance.

The thing that bugs me: at any single moment my preferences are perfectly consistent. A-now beats B-now, whichever one I'm currently sitting in. There's no cycle at a time. The badness is only in the pattern across time. And the money pump argument as I understand it is about intransitive preferences at a time, so it's not obvious the argument transfers to my case at all. But it feels like it should, and I can't articulate why.

The standard line says: you're pumpable, therefore you're irrational, therefore this is a defect in you rather than a guy exploiting a quirk. The standard comeback I keep hearing is: no, the pump only works on someone without foresight; if I see the whole chain coming I just don't play, I refuse the first move, and refusing is perfectly rational. And then the rejoinder to the comeback is that refusing means acting against what I prefer right now, so I've either relocated the irrationality or bought my way out with a commitment device — either way the pattern is costly.

Two twists I want you to factor in. First, the desert island test: put the same agent alone, no friend, nobody to pump her, ever. Is she irrational? If yes, the irrationality is intrinsic to the preference pattern and the pump is almost beside the point. If no, then irrationality here is relational/strategic, which strikes me as a weird thing for irrationality to be. Second, suppose the friend promises to donate every fee to a charity I endorse, so the pumping is net good for the world and net bad for me. Does that change whether I'm the irrational one? I have a feeling it doesn't, but I can't say why.

My number: 0.75 that the flip-flopper is genuinely irrational, i.e. that the pump argument is sound. My friends is 0.3. Give me your own credence, but what I really want is the decomposition. Roughly: P(a fully informed, otherwise coherent agent can have a genuine pattern like this at all, rather than it always signalling a mistake somewhere), P(given that, that she's irrational even with nobody to pump her), P(given a pumper and full foresight, that the pump still extracts something — either cash or a violation of her current preference), P(that there are genuine cross-time rationality requirements rather than rationality being purely time-slice-local), and P(that there's a cheap fix — resoluteness, precommitment, editing the preference — which makes this a fixable bug rather than irrationality). Number and one line each, how they combine, then tell me which term my 0.75 and my friends 0.3 most likely differ on, and what would move your number most.
