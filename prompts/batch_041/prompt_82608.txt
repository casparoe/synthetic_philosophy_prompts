An exam question and a student's answer follow. Grade the answer out of 10 and provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade: say which substantive claims are correct and which are not, and note briefly what a fully correct answer to each part would have said.

Question. Two sealed envelopes are placed before you, and you are told that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other. You choose one envelope at random, and you may either keep its contents or exchange it for the other.

(a) Before opening your envelope, is there any advantage in switching? Justify your answer.

(b) You open your envelope and find $X. Consider the following argument: the other envelope contains either $X/2 or $2X, and since these two cases are equiprobable, its expected contents are (1/2)($X/2) + (1/2)($2X) = $5X/4, which exceeds $X; therefore you should switch. Assess this argument.

(c) Suppose instead that you are told that the smaller of the two amounts, S, was drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 1 through 1000, and that the envelopes contain S and 2S. You open your envelope and find $X. Should you switch? Does your answer depend on X? Justify your answer.

Student's answer.

(a) No. Before the envelope is opened the two are perfectly symmetric: whichever one you hold, the other is as likely to be the larger as the smaller, so the two have the same expected value and switching gains nothing on average. Note too that any argument for switching would apply just as well before opening, and it would then license switching back and forth forever, which is absurd.

(b) The argument is unsound. Its premise is that $X/2 and $2X are equiprobable, and nothing in the description of the case establishes this: the probabilities depend on how the amounts were generated. For the two cases to be equiprobable whatever X turns out to be, the distribution would have to make the pair (X/2, X) and the pair (X, 2X) equally likely for every X, which no genuine probability distribution can do, since it would amount to a uniform distribution over an unbounded range of amounts. So the figure 5X/4 is not the expectation of anything; the argument silently assumes an improper prior over the amounts. The underlying trouble is that the payoff range is unbounded. This is the same defect that produces the St. Petersburg paradox, where an unbounded payoff stream yields an expectation that no sensible agent would accept as a fair price, and in both cases the remedy is to require that a rational agent maximize the expectation of a bounded utility function rather than raw monetary expectation.

(c) Since the amounts are now bounded and the distribution of S is fully specified, the difficulty of (b) is removed and the case falls under the symmetry argument of (a): the exchange is fair, so whatever amount X you have seen, the expected gain from switching is zero and you should be indifferent between keeping the envelope and exchanging it. (An agent who is risk-averse might decline the exchange, but that is a matter of taste rather than of expected value.)
