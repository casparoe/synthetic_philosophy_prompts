I'm a fourth-year PhD student working on personal identity and bioethics, and I have to design a 90-minute session for our department's work-in-progress seminar: about fourteen participants, mostly graduate students, mixed familiarity with the advance-directive literature. I want them to feel the problem of the earlier self's authority rather than hear it described, and I want the felt version to be harder than the standard "what would you have wanted?" question. Here is my rough shape, which you should rebuild into something actually runnable: in the first half, participants act as trustees for an assigned principal and write a binding directive governing how that principal is to be treated if they enter a restricted state; in the second half, participants are reassigned as principals, and some of them actually enter that state — a real but mild loss of standing in the room (vote counted as half, speech mediated through a proxy, and the like), paired with a small compensating comfort that is genuinely pleasant. The principals in the restricted state should come to sincerely endorse their condition and want to revoke the directive, so the room must decide whether the earlier directive or the present endorsement governs. Three things should land: that honoring the earlier self is itself an override of the present self, so "respecting autonomy" settles nothing; that the present endorsement may be an adapted preference of exactly the kind the directive was written to guard against; and that the two selves' epistemic positions are complementary — the earlier self knows the history, the later self knows the texture of the state, and neither has both.

Give me the full design: roles, materials, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, the collective decision procedure, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the underlying problem without naming any position before the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — participants treating it as a game about fairness rather than selfhood, stage-one rule-gaming, and the moment the room sides with the present selves out of solidarity — and how I should handle each. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself: real directives bite when the later self has no capacity to argue at all, and the game stipulates the continuity of personhood that is precisely what is in dispute. It needs to run without acting skill and without anyone disclosing anything about their own health or family.
