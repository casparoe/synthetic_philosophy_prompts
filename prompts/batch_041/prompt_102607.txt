For thirty-five years I worked as a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy in a fairly systematic way. I have lately been bothered by a difficulty that consequentialists appear to have created for themselves, and which I have seen given the name cluelessness. As I understand it, the difficulty is not the ordinary uncertainty of a wager, where one can at least assign probabilities across a fixed menu of outcomes; it is that the consequences of even a small act propagate without limit — through second- and third-order effects, through the altered expectations of everyone who learns of the act, through the plain fact that the world's later history is now a different branch — and that we have no way to enumerate those consequences, let alone weigh them. The expected-value calculation the consequentialist would like to run cannot even be set up. Some writers treat this as an amusing footnote; others, as I read them, treat it as the end of the whole enterprise. I notice that my own training leaves me sympathetic to the second reaction, since I spent a career on problems that were well posed and still intractable, and this one does not seem to be well posed at all.

I would like you to write me a piece of sustained satire on this theme. The form I have in mind is an internal memorandum from the consequences-review office of an invented philanthropic foundation — the sort of document circulated before a grant is approved. The memorandum is considering whether to fund something plainly good and modest, the distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets, say, and it applies the foundation's standing policy of assessing every proposal by its full consequences, now and into the indefinite future. Write it straight, in the voice of a conscientious and slightly weary officer, with no winking and no authorial nudges; the comedy should come entirely from the office doing precisely what its own rules require and discovering that it can conclude nothing, so that the memorandum ends by recommending indefinite deferral of a grant it cannot fault. Along the way it should get the philosophy right: keep the cluelessness distinct from ordinary risk, show why the relevant probabilities and even the relevant outcome-space are unavailable, notice the temptation to substitute some tractable proxy for the real calculation and why that substitution is a cheat, and let the reader feel the pull of the retreat to rules or heuristics that consequentialists actually make. Someone who has thought about the problem should find the piece fair rather than merely clever, and someone who has not should still be able to follow it.

Roughly 650 words. Please write it as continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic, no tables or numbered clauses — the way one would actually draft a memorandum or a letter.
