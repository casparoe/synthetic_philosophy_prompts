I have been trying to state a money-pump argument that presupposes no inconsistency in the agent's preferences at all, and I want the most important implications of the result — ranked, and with a clear division between what follows from the agent's attitudes and what follows only from the structure of the environment it faces.

The construction is this. An agent with a single utility function over final outcomes — identical at every stage, complete, transitive, free of discounting reversals, free of weakness of will — acts over finitely many stages. A counterparty sets the menu at each stage, and the menus are history-dependent: what is on offer at stage t+1 is a function of what the agent chose at stage t. The agent cannot commit in advance to a policy that binds its later choices, and has no outside option that lets it walk away (the default is worse than playing). Its decision rule is local: it compares the options on the current menu against its present position and takes its expectation of later menus as given, rather than treating its own present choice as an input into them. Every choice it makes is one it would endorse when it makes it, and no choice violates transitivity, completeness, or any discounting condition; the sequence nevertheless leaves it strictly worse off than its endowment. Call this a menu pump.

What I want:

1. The implications for money-pump arguments as tests of rationality. The textbook pump is supposed to show that inconsistent preferences are a defect in the agent. If a pump needs only history-dependent menus, no commitment technology, and a local decision rule, does exploitability still have any claim to be a criterion of irrationality — or does it become a fact about who controls the menu and who can commit, rather than about the agent's attitudes? Is there a principled line between the inconsistent agent and the consistent agent trapped in an extractive environment, and does the standard argument trade on an ambiguity between them?

2. The implications for what the construction proves. Its agent fails to anticipate the counterparty's response; it treats later menus as fixed when they are a function of its own choice. Is that a failure of preference or a failure of reasoning, and if the latter, does the construction bear on diachronic rationality at all, rather than on adaptation in strategic settings? What survives if the agent is fully sophisticated about the counterparty's strategy but still cannot commit — a pump, or an ordinary problem of choice under uncertainty? And does a variant in which the agent's later selves each take the others' policies as given while sharing one utility function do the same work with less stipulation?

3. The implications for classifying real cases. Subscriptions that renew, gym memberships, in-app purchases, rent-to-own, escalating add-ons, addictive goods: which of these are menu pumps in the above sense, which are ordinary dynamic inconsistency in disguise, and does the difference change whether we should describe the people in them as irrational rather than as exploited?

4. The implications for remedies. Environmental diagnoses suggest environmental remedies — cooling-off periods, bans on particular contract forms, fiduciary duties, publicly supplied commitment devices, limits on how menus may depend on history; attitudinal diagnoses suggest other remedies, or none. Which remedies does the menu pump actually support? Would a purely informational remedy — disclosure that the offers are history-dependent — dissolve the trap? And does the case for paternalism look stronger or weaker once the pump is specified this way?

Finish by telling me which of these implications you would stake something on, and which I have simply built in by stipulating the local decision rule.
