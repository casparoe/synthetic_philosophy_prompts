For the past several weeks I have been a visiting doctoral student on a field project, and two of the senior archaeologists on the team — I will call them Ines and Marek — have been locked in an argument that I have been unable to referee myself. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange rather than a verdict on who "wins."

The context is a mid-sized settlement of the sort usually described as early stratified. Beneath the floor of one house was the burial of a child of roughly five years, accompanied by a necklace of stone beads whose raw material comes from a source several hundred kilometres away, and, in an adjacent pit, a cache of clay figurines of a regional domestic type that had been deliberately broken before deposition. Both Ines and Marek accept the excavation record; they disagree about what it licenses.

Ines: This burial shows hereditary, ascribed status — a five-year-old cannot have earned imported exotica through any achievement — and the deliberate breakage is a ritual act, which is to say evidence of some belief about the dead persisting after death. So from these objects we can reconstruct both an institution (stratified rank) and a belief (a continued existence of some kind).

Marek: You can reconstruct what people did; you cannot reconstruct what they believed. "Belief in an afterlife" is not something we observe. You have inferred a mental state from a physical pattern, and the pattern does not force that mental state on you.

Ines: But every archaeological inference is an inference to a mental state. A blade with use-wear was made in order to cut; a house was built in order to shelter. If you accept those, you must accept mine, because the form of the inference is identical. Your objection proves far too much.

Marek: The cases are not symmetrical. A cutting edge is constrained by the physics of the material it must cut, and the use-wear constrains what it in fact cut; the functional hypothesis is answerable to evidence in a way the afterlife hypothesis is not. The same grave goods are equally compatible with ancestor veneration, with fear of the corpse, with status competition among the survivors, and with plain grief. When the competing hypotheses make no differential predictions, the inference is underdetermined, and you are simply choosing the one that feels natural.

Ines: Underdetermination is everywhere in science and never stops us. Our dating is underdetermined too, yet we still commit to a chronology when the alternatives are worse. Besides, new work this season bears on it: the wear on the beads shows they were strung and worn for years before burial, and the fingerprints on the figurines are those of children. So the "one-off exotic display" reading is weakened — these were personal objects, used over time by and around a child, which fits a continuing attachment to the dead better than a single act of showing off.

Marek: The wear cuts the other way. Heirlooms are precisely how rank is transmitted; a bead worn for years before being buried with a child is exactly what you would expect if it were an inherited token of standing. And to rank "attachment" above "ancestor veneration" or "fear" you must be assuming these people resembled us in the relevant respects — an ethnographic analogy you have not stated, let alone defended.

Ines: Analogy is unavoidable in any science; we calibrate it by relevance and move on. And you cannot escape it by retreating to "practice," because to describe the breakage as a practice at all is already to attribute intentions and norms — you are smuggling belief in through the back door and then objecting when I bring it through the front.

Marek: Then bring it through the front explicitly, and carry the uncertainty that belongs to it. The honest claim is much weaker than the one you are making, and the difference matters for what we are entitled to say about this society.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each move, say what is right and what is wrong in it, and rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly sound. Distinguish the disagreements that are substantive from any that are merely verbal or terminological. Say which specific claims each of them is entitled to and which they are not, and identify what each is overlooking. Finally, indicate whether there is a reformulation that both should accept, and if not, exactly where the residual disagreement lies.
