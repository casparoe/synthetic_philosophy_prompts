Please argue for a definite position on whether omissions can be genuine causes. Take a side on this specific question: when an agent fails to act and a harm follows, is the failure itself a cause of the harm, or only a pragmatic label we apply while the real causal work is done by whatever positive conditions obtained? Skip the usual gardener and lifeguard cases and use this one instead. A hospital deploys an automated sepsis alert; a nurse, reasonably trusting it, does not check on a deteriorating patient because the alert stays silent owing to a software bug, and the patient dies. In a second case the same nurse, with no alert system in place, simply does not check, and the patient dies. Say whether the two omissions are causes in the same sense, and whether your answer survives the objection that if omissions count, then everything anyone ever failed to do becomes a cause of every subsequent harm. I would also like you to say what an interventionist criterion of causation — on which a cause is something whose manipulation would change the effect — can even mean for a non-event, given that you cannot intervene on nothing. Tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and identify the claim you are least sure of.
