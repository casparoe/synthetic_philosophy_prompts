I write a column on science and ideas for a mid-sized daily, and every December I do one jokey piece about management jargon. This year I want to go after the way companies have eaten the language of going above and beyond — hero mode, discretionary effort, that whole racket — and I want it done as a leaked internal memo. So: you're a philosopher who works on supererogation, the category of things that are morally good to do but not required, and who also writes satire, and I want you to spend a few minutes brainstorming before you write, because the jokes need to come out of the structure of the idea itself and not just out of buzzwords. I've been sitting on this one for awhile and I want it right.

Here's the brief. A fictional mid-size company's internal memo, from some invented office of discretionary excellence, whatever you want to call it, announcing that staff must now hit quarterly above-and-beyond targets and that managers will track them. Subject line, numbered or bulleted sections, an FAQ-ish bit at the end, a sign-off. 600-700 words, deadpan, no winking. The satire has to be recognizable to someone who's read the supererogation literature — that a thing stops being supererogatory once it's required, that praise and credit get weirdly rationed, the moral-saint worry that morality asks too much of us — without ever saying any of it out loud, and without using the word supererogation anywhere in the memo itself. Invent the company and everyone in it; dont name real people or real firms and dont quote anyone. Somebody who's never heard the term should still get the joke.

Then, after the memo, a short note of a few sentences, clearly not part of the memo, on where you're taking liberties with the ideas, and in that note separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested — like whether supererogatory acts are optional by definition or whether there can be obligations to do them — and please dont hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus.

Start with the memo itself. No preamble, no here's-the-memo, first line is the memo.
