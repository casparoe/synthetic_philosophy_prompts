I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in political theory, and I'm stuck on something that keeps derailing my dissertation chapter on Deweyan democracy. There's this long-running fight between the Deweyan experimentalists and the epistemic democrats (and also between the Deweyans themselves) about whether democracy is justified by its truth-tracking or problem-solving results, or whether the whole point is that it's a form of inquiry, an ongoing experimental way of living together, so that asking for a final justification in terms of outcomes already misses the point. It's been going on forever, everyone knows everyone's moves, and nobody budges. I want a diagnosis, not a scorecard. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word inquiry, or are teh two sides answering different questions under one heading -- one about the epistemic credentials of democratic procedures, one about the ethical and political value of a mode of association? My half-baked hypothesis: the stall is because experiment is doing two jobs in Dewey's legacy. Sometimes it means a hypothesis-testing method that aims at warranted assertability and error correction; sometimes it means a normative ideal where the point is the continuous reconstruction of problems and the growth of shared experience, with no terminus. If we split those, some fights dissolve and the real one gets sharper -- whether democracy has to be justified to a skeptic from the outside, or whether inquiry is already a precondition of any such justification. Is that right, or is a hidden empirical or formal question about collective learning doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here -- a distinction, a change in the burden of proof, a concession about what counts as a democratic experiment, or is the impasse bedrock?
