My flatmate is doing a PhD in quantum foundations and I keep a running list of her verdicts on thought experiments, because there fun to argue about over breakfast. So far:

- one electron through a double slit, no which-path info: it really did go through both. Not ignorance on our part.
- two electrons in an entangled pair: the thing that's real isn't located in either electron, and isn't in 3D space at all. Space is a kind of projection of it.
- a 50/50 mixture that came from us flipping a coin to decide how to prepare the system: pure bookkeeping, the thing was in a definate state the whole time.
- global phase: shifting the entire wavefunction by a phase isn't a change in reality, just a change in how we're describing it.
- decoherence: the interference terms don't vanish, they leak out into the environment, and nothing ever actually collapses.
- someone claiming to have read off the wavefunction of a single atom: no. Any procedure that claims to do that already has to know the wavefunction in order to set itself up, so it's circular.

Now a guy in her group says that last one is wrong, and he says he's built the thing. Don't get hung up on whether his details are right, the shape of it is what matters: you take a particle you already know is in a superposition of sitting in box A and sitting in box B. You couple it to a probe needle so weakly, and for so long, that the particle's state is left completely undisturbed, far too gentle a coupling to change anything. The needle still ends up displaced. Not a full kick, a fraction of one, in proportion to how much of the wavefunction sits in the region you're probing. So you get a fractional displacement for box A, as if part of the particle were in there, even though if you actually open box A you either find the whole particle or you find nothing at all. He says this is a measurement of the wavefunction of one particle, and that it shows the particle really is partly in both boxes.

What is she going to say about it? I mostly want the rule she's running. I can't tell if she thinks the wavefunction is a concrete physical thing, or a law (real, but law-like, the way a conservation law is real), or something that only exists relative to a system, or whether she thinks there are also little particles underneath that the wavefunction pushes around. I'm fairly sure she's not someone who thinks it's all just information, that would annoy her. Which of those fit everything she's already said, what does each predict about the needle, which would you bet on and why, and what's the cleanest new case that would force her to pick?
