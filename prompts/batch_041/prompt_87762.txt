Alright, gut verdicts on sad art, and I want the principle behind them if there is one. A brutal war novel that leaves me wrecked for a week: one of the best things I've done with my time, I press it on friends. The same story told to me face to face by someone about their actual brother: unbearable, and I'd feel like a ghoul saying I was enjoying it. A film engineered to make me cry -- dying child, strings, the works -- makes me cry harder than the novel does and I think less of it for it; the crying feels cheap and I wish I hadn't. A sad song, no words, no story, that I put on when I'm in a good mood: love it, and I'm not sad at all. A true-crime doc about a real murder: I watch it to the end every time, I think my interest is faintly filthy, and the family has every right to hate me for it. My own grief a year on, when a poem nails it exactly: I wouldn't trade that half hour for anything, and I wouldn't call it pleasure. And the one I can't sort -- a friend's slideshow of their dead dog at a party, which did make me cry and which I still think was a rotten thing to do to a room, even though I'd defend the novel to the death.

The best I've got is 'the sadness has to be fitting', but the song has nothing to be fitting to, and the tearjerker fits its object just fine.

So: is there a principle, or a small set, that gets the novel, the song, the poem and the tearjerker right, says something about why the real slideshow and the real murder are different, and tells me what to expect from a horror film or from rereading the novel a fifth time once the sadness has worn off? State it precisely enough that I can argue with it, tell me which case each clause was built to save, and be honest about which of my verdicts it makes me give up. I suspect it's the tearjerker, and I dont like it.
