I teach a seminar on responsibility under extreme conditions, and every spring I lose two weeks to an argument that never quite closes: whether the men who did the face-to-face killing in the East — the reservists in the Order Police battalions, middle-aged, mostly not ideologues, almost none of them volunteers for that particular work — are best explained from the situation downward (the orders, the momentum of the unit, the escalation of small steps, the sheer difficulty of being the first man in the line to step out, the fact that at the first killing site an offer of release was made and only a handful took it) or from the culture upward (a long history of antisemitism that had already made the killing thinkable, even obligatory, so that what we are looking at is not ordinary men at all but willing executioners). I teach it as a live quarrel, roughly along the lines Browning and Goldhagen drew, and I leave it live, because the evidence underdetermines it and because both answers are doing something for the people who hold them. That is easy in a seminar. It is much harder on the fourteenth of next month, when I have agreed to give the address at the unveiling of a memorial plaque at the old freight yard in the town where I teach, from which the district's remaining Jewish families were deported east in 1941 and 1942. The historical society invited me because I am the only person here who teaches this material; the mayor's office has let it be known that it would prefer the address to be about the victims rather than the perpetrators; and the chief of police will lay a wreath, which the society's chair thinks should be accompanied by an acknowledgment that the gendarmerie post which organized the roundup belongs to his institution's ancestry — an acknowledgment he would rather not make in front of the schoolchildren. The yard will hold survivors' families, the council, a rabbi who will read the names, two classes of eleventh-graders standing in the cold, and a row of officers in dress uniform.

Please write the address: seven or eight minutes aloud, about a thousand words, spoken register, no footnotes, and no quotations of any kind — not from historians, not from scripture, not from testimony — since I would rather paraphrase anyone I lean on than misquote them in a public square. I have two requests about structure, and they matter more to me than the rhetoric. The first is that you not settle the historians' argument by fiat. I don't want the speech to lean on monsters, or on evil as an explanation, or on the comfortable line that it could have been any of us; each of those closes the question in a way that flatters the audience, and the whole reason I was asked is that I am supposed to be the one person in town who will not do that. Let the unresolvedness do work instead: the audience should feel that there are two explanations on the table, that they are not merely academic, and that different things follow from each — a warning about institutions and conformity if the situation carries the weight, a warning about what a community has already decided about its neighbors if the culture does.

The second request concerns a distinction I have been trying to get right in my own work, and I would like the speech built on it. There are two questions in this material, not one. The first is why the deed was done; the second is why no one stopped it — not only in the yard, but in the decades afterwards, when the same yard was in use, when the men came home, when the story was available to anyone who wanted it and nobody wanted it. I think the situational account is strong on the first question and nearly silent on the second, and that the second is the one a ceremony like this has to answer, because we are the ones being asked. So I want the address to honor the dead, to say something honest and unhedged about the killers, and to end in the present tense on something small and specific that the living could do — not a vow, and not the two-word slogan the organizers have asked me to avoid on the grounds that it has become an ornament and is in any case a promise about a future nobody in that yard controls. If writing it forces you to choose between the two explanations, please say so in a short note after the address, clearly marked as not part of what I will read out, and tell me what tipped you.
