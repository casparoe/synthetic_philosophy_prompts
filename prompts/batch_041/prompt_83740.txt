I have no training in philosophy — my degree is in accounting — and for the last few months I have been reading about the problem of evil on my own, mostly out of personal curiosity. A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree gave me the following summary of Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, and I would like to know how much of it I can rely on. I am not looking for a reading list or for pointers to the literature; I would just like the summary audited.

His summary runs roughly as follows. Leibniz holds that God is perfectly good and all-powerful, so the world he created must be the best one he could have created; a good God who could have done better would have done better, which is impossible. 'Best' means the greatest total perfection, where perfection is a matter of variety and order rather than of pleasure, though the happiness of created minds is one of the things that counts toward it. God surveys every possible world and selects the best of them; the things that can coexist within a single world Leibniz calls 'compossible', which simply means that they are not self-contradictory, so in effect God chose the best of all the logically consistent worlds. And because this is the best possible world, everything that happens in it — including the particular disasters and injustices that fall on particular people — happens for the best, from which it follows that trying to prevent or remedy them is a kind of second-guessing of God. The standard objection is that any world containing even one toothache is worse than the very same world with that toothache removed, so this cannot be the best world; Voltaire made the point savagely in his satire after the Lisbon earthquake. The summary concludes that the doctrine is unfalsifiable — whatever happens, a Leibnizian can always call it necessary for some greater good — and that it is therefore now only of historical interest.

What I want is a claim-by-claim audit. For each claim in the summary, say whether a careful reader of Leibniz would accept it as stated, wince at it as a simplification, or reject it as a misrepresentation. Where the summary is wrong, tell me what the underlying position actually is and what a defender would put in its place. I would particularly like to know whether the conclusions the summary draws — that the toothache argument refutes the position, and that the position is unfalsifiable and dead — follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether they only appear to follow because of the parts that are not. I would also like to know whether the practical upshot is really entailed: is Leibniz committed to the view that reform, medicine, and political effort are impious, or is that a conclusion the summary has smuggled in? And if the doctrine is not merely unfalsifiable, what would it have to forbid — what state of affairs, had the world contained it, would have shown the doctrine to be false?

For what it is worth, I am inclined to think the unfalsifiability point is correct, and I cannot see what work 'compossible' is doing that 'possible' was not already doing, so please tell me plainly if I am being taken in by the summary as well.
