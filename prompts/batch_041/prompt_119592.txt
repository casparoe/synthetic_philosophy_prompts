Question:

Leibniz defines justice as the charity of the wise (caritas sapientis), and he distinguishes three degrees of natural right. (a) Set out the three degrees and say what distinguishes them. (b) Explain how Leibniz's claim that love can be disinterested bears on this definition of justice. (c) Leibniz rejects the attempt to ground natural law in the command of a superior. What is his objection, and does his own account avoid it?

Student's answer:

(a) The three degrees are Leibniz's adaptation of Ulpian's three precepts. The first and lowest is jus strictum, the duty to harm no one. It's the bare minimum of not injuring others, and it's the only degree that can be enforced by coercion, so it corresponds to what we'd now call the domain of enforceable rights and claims. The second is aequitas, equity: the duty to give each person their due. This goes past mere non-injury — it covers fair dealing, keeping faith, and proportionality in exchange, and it's the domain of what's owed rather than merely permitted. The third and highest is pietas, sometimes translated as probity or piety: the duty to live honorably, which for Leibniz means loving others and willing their good. What distinguishes the degrees is how much goodness they demand. The first asks only that I not take from you, the second that I render what is yours, the third that I actively seek your good. Each higher degree presupposes the lower ones, so they form a ladder rather than three unrelated duties. But they aren't all law in the same sense: only jus strictum is coercible, and Leibniz treats the higher two as belonging to virtue rather than to natural law properly so-called. That's why he calls the highest degree the charity of the wise rather than simply a duty — since it can't be compelled, it belongs to virtue rather than to justice in the strict sense.

(b) The target here is Hobbes, who holds that every apparently altruistic act is really the pursuit of one's own power or pleasure, so that charity is prudence in disguise. Leibniz denies this. For him, love is taking pleasure in the perfection and happiness of another, and this pleasure isn't a means to anything further — it's a genuine delight in the other's good. That's what makes the definition of justice as charity work: if love were always self-interested, then telling us to love others would just be telling us to be clever about our own advantage, and justice would collapse into prudence. Leibniz is most rigorous about this in the case of God. He insists that we must love God with a love purged of all thought of our own happiness, loving God solely for God's perfection, and he treats any admixture of hope for reward as a corruption of the love. This is the pure love the mystics describe, and Leibniz takes it as the model for justice generally, since God's own justice is his universal benevolence.

(c) The objection is aimed at Pufendorf, who defines natural law as what God commands, so that an action's goodness depends on its being commanded. Leibniz says this turns God into a tyrant: if justice were whatever a superior wills, God could have commanded cruelty and it would have been just, and the only difference between God and a despot would be one of degree. For Leibniz, justice is grounded in the nature of things, in eternal truths that even God's will cannot alter. God's will is good because God perceives what is just, not the other way around. He also thinks this is what makes a science of natural law possible — if justice rested on an arbitrary command, there'd be nothing to demonstrate about it. Does his account avoid the problem? Mostly, but at a cost. If the content of justice is fixed by the eternal truths independently of God's will, then God's role in natural law looks like that of an enforcer rather than a legislator, and it becomes hard to say in what sense natural law is law at all rather than a body of truths about what's good. The duty not to harm others would hold even if no one commanded it, and Leibniz seems happy to say as much. So the objection to Pufendorf stands, but the price is a thinner notion of law than Pufendorf had.
