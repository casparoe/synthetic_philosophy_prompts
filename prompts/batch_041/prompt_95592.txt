My friend and I got into it for like two hours last night about whether the peacock's tail thing — Zahavi's handicap principle, cost as proof of quality — is a genuinely useful lens for reading human behavior, or the kind of idea that sounds deep because it explains everything and so explains nothing. He thinks every expensive, pointless-looking move is a signal of something. I think half of them are just people being dumb with money, and there's no way to tell the difference from the outside. Which is a problem, because telling the difference from the outside is literally my job.

I run diligence at a small fund, three of us, maybe forty companies a year. The pattern that keeps biting us: a company does something expensive that looks like a waste of money, and I have to decide whether that expense is evidence of something real about them or just burn. Open-sourcing their whole core library. Paying for a brutal certification nobody made them get. Spending a third of the runway on a brand campaign before there's a product. Hiring a famous scientist who costs a fortune and produces nothing billable. Right now I do this by vibe in about ten minutes and I'm pretty sure I'm just pattern-matching on whether I like the founder.

So write me a procedure I can actually run, say forty-five minutes per company, no insider access, my partner signs off on the check. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where it stops and says "this tells me nothing, walk away," and the point where it has to hand the call to a human because it's about risk tolerance rather than fact. Keep the actual logic of the theory intact — the cost has to be cheaper for the good type than the bad type, the receiver has to actually change what they do, and a bad type has to be unable to afford the imitation. And be straight with me about where the lens misfires: expenses with no real receiver, costs that are just consumption, traits that are physically tied to the thing I care about rather than being signals of it, several audiences at once, and the case where the party getting fooled isn't the party paying for being fooled. That last one is my hunch about where the whole thing breaks and I want the procedure to say so out loud instead of papering over it.

Then run it on three: the open-source one, the brand campaign one, and a company that voluntarily took on an expensive audit it didn't need. At least one of these should come out as a pass, even if I don't like it, because I definately have a soft spot for founders who do splashy things. Also tell me which steps are just my judgment wearing a lab coat.
