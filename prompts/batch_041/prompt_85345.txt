I'm putting together an essay on whether Dutch book arguments really show that someone with incoherent credences is irrational, and I'd like you to write it. Weigh the main lines of argument and actually land on a verdict — dont end on "both sides have a point."

Please get into the weeds. Take the toy case: an agent whose credence in A is 0.3 and in not-A is 0.8, so a bookie can sell her both bets for 1.10 and hand back 1 no matter how things turn out. Fine, she loses a dime. But that only shows she loses money *if she takes the bets*, and nobody's forcing her — she can just decline. So does the argument tell against her credences at all, or only against a betting policy she needn't have? Look at the standard rescues (the argument is really about coherence; bet in utilities rather than dollars; it's an idealization aimed at the credences themselves) and say whether any of them actually works.

Two extra wrinkles, since they're the part I keep flip-flopping on. First, what about someone whose credences are fuzzy or imprecise rather than a single number — a range, so there's no one price to take her money at? Is she off the hook, and if so, did the Dutch book argument only ever prove something about a special idealized agent? Second, does the size of the sure loss (a cent versus a lot), and whether the book is offered all at once versus bet by bet over time, change the verdict about irrationality?

At the end, say how confident you are in your overall verdict and which single step of your argument you're least sure about.
