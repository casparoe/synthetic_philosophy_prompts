For some time now I have been collecting instances of a pattern in arguments about historical injustice, and I want to know whether the thing I am pointing at has a name, and — more to the point — whether anyone has done the work of marking its edges. I teach history at a secondary school and I sit on a small committee that advises our town on plaques and memorials, so I meet these arguments more often than most people, and I have stopped being able to tell what the people making them are actually committed to. The pattern is this. Someone claims that a country, a university, a city, or a family owes something on account of a wrong done long ago: that its present position was built on that wrong and that the debt has never been discharged. Someone else objects that they personally had nothing to do with it and were not yet born. And the reply is not that they did it after all, but that the question was never about them — that the thing which owes is the institution, which is not the same as the sum of the people currently inside it, and which has outlived every person who was around when the wrong was done. That reply seems to me to carry an enormous amount of weight without anyone stopping to ask what it presupposes. For it appears to require that the institution be the kind of thing that can be obligated across a complete turnover of its members; and yet, if I try to say that the institution is guilty, the very reasoning that made it a bearer of obligations seems to let it off the hook, since guilt as I understand it needs someone who could have acted otherwise and knew it, and the institution as it stands today contains no such someone. So I seem to be looking at an entity that can owe without being to blame — and what I notice in practice is that people make the claim in the vocabulary of blame and then, when pressed, restate it in the vocabulary of debt, or do the reverse and use the language of owing to keep a charge of guilt quietly in circulation.

I have run into this in three places, only one of which is my own. In a local debate about renaming a building after a merchant whose fortune came out of a slave-trading concern, the alumni who objected kept saying that they had done nothing, and the reply kept being that the building belonged to the university and not to them. In discussions of national apologies, the same slide happens the other way: a head of state apologises on behalf of a nation whose citizens mostly have no connection to the wrong, the citizens are then invited to feel something about it, some do and some don't, and the apology is supposed to stand either way. And in a friend's family argument about an inheritance, the beneficiary who cheerfully concedes that the money is tainted still insists that he is not the one who did it, and everyone at the table seems to accept both statements at once. The phrase I keep reaching for is collective guilt, but that is plainly not what is happening in the cases that interest me, because in those cases the whole point is that nobody need be guilty; and it is not the idea that guilt is inherited down the generations either, since I can imagine a group owing repair with nobody, living or dead, at fault in the relevant sense — where the wrong was done by people acting in good faith under appalling norms. So the thing I am after is the narrower one: the bearer of an obligation that survives the replacement of every member, together with the rules, if there are any, that govern how such an obligation comes into being, how far it reaches, and when it lapses.

I am not asking only for a label. If there is an established term, I want to know which of the several things I have described it actually covers and what it leaves out; if the literature has split my one pattern into three or four distinct notions, I would rather have the split than a single name that hides it. I would also like to know what the obligation is supposed to attach to — the persistence of an institution, the persistence of a benefit, or nothing more than the reuse of a name — because those give very different answers about when the thing ends: whether a beneficiary who gains nothing still owes, whether an institution can outlive its debt by rewriting its constitution, and whether the people asked to apologise are being asked to say something true or only to perform something useful. If nothing established fits, then give me a term and a definition, with a couple of cases on either side of the line and an honest note about where the line is arbitrary. I have read enough popular commentary to know all the standard moves; what I want is the kind of treatment that tells me what I am and am not committed to when I say that someone owes.
