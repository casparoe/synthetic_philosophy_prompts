Write a poem in two halves of equal length, about a dozen lines each, no fixed form, about one small domestic event — something done by one of two people. The halves should be line-for-line parallel and should use the same words wherever English allows, with a shared refrain: a single conditional sentence that appears in both halves with only its verb form changed, so that the reader sees one poem said twice. But the halves must not say the same thing. The first reasons in the indicative: the speaker knows the thing was done and does not know who did it, so the conditionals are nearly bookkeeping and the conclusions drawn from them cost the speaker nothing. The second reasons in the counterfactual: the speaker now knows who did it, and the same sentences become substantive claims about a world that never happened, claims that could well be false. Let the asymmetry be visible from the sentences alone, without commentary — the same thought cheap in the first half and expensive in the second. Close with a single unpaired line that returns to the indicative about something still open, so that the difference reads as a difference in what the speaker knows rather than in what happened. No theoretical vocabulary anywhere in the poem: no mention of conditionals, moods, modality, or philosophy. Someone who has thought about the distinction should recognize it; someone who has not should feel it as the difference between waiting and regretting.
