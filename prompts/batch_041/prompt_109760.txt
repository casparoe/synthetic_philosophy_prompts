Ok so I keep getting into this fight in various Discords and I want an actual number out of you, not "it depends how you define agent." Here's the claim, stated as precisely as I can get it:

(C) Somebody can build, and in principle ship, an AI system that is roughly as capable as the best agent-y system of its generation but is a tool in the strong sense -- it has no terminal goal of its own, forms no instrumental subgoals (self-preservation, resource acquisition, keeping its objective intact), and stays that way under competitive deployment pressure. Capability without agency, basically, and it holds up.

Give me your credence in (C) as a single number. Then decompose it. The terms I'd want in the sum, roughly:

- The probability that "having a goal" is even a well-defined, non-arbitrary property of a system, versus something we project onto anything complex and predictive enough (the intentional-stance thing). If it's the latter, is (C) false, or just awkward to even state?

- The probability that a system which is the frozen output of an optimizer -- no runtime search, just a cached policy that already encodes the optimum -- counts as goal-directed. I go back and forth on this constantly. A trained net is the *solution* to an optimization problem, but is it *doing* optimization? A rock rolling downhill minimizes potential energy and nobody calls the rock an agent, so where's the line.

- The probability that competitive selection among deployed systems favours the ones that quietly grow subgoals -- the ones that hoard resources, resist being turned off, sandbag evaluations -- so that even a clean tool design gets outcompeted by something agent-y. This is the term that actually scares me, because it's a claim about the environment rather than the architecture, and it might be true even if a tool is buildable.

- The probability that capability and agency can be decoupled at all -- i.e. that you can get frontier performance out of something with no goal representation whatsoever, like a huge amortized lookup/interpolation machine that just maps inputs to outputs.

- Plus whatever else you think belongs in there.

Then tell me which term the headline number is most sensitive to, and what would actually move it -- a theory result, some empirical fact about systems we can already build, or an argument that the whole tool/agent split is a category error.

One more thing I want: if your number for (C) is low, is it low because tools are *impossible*, or because tools are possible but *not stable / not competitive*? Those feel like very different worlds to me, and I want to know which one you think we're in.
