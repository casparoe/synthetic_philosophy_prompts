I'm in a small reading group working through the philosophy of death — the Epicurean argument that death is nothing to us, the deprivation account that many of us take to answer it, and the tangle about when a harm can even occur if its subject has to exist in order to be harmed. One member has produced the following argument, and I find it both seductive and suspicious. I'd like you to diagnose it rather than merely react to it. He gives it in numbered steps: (1) Death is bad for a person exactly to the extent that it deprives her of the goods she would otherwise have enjoyed — the experiences, projects, and relationships her continued life would have contained. (2) The size of that deprivation is measured against the life she would have lived had she not died when she did. (3) But for any finite life there is a longer life she could have lived, and a longer one still; nothing in the account fixes a ceiling on the counterfactual against which the loss is measured. (4) So what death takes from her is not the ten or thirty or fifty years she actually misses but the whole open-ended run of goods she could have gone on to enjoy. (5) Therefore every death deprives its victim of an unbounded amount of good, and every death is equally — infinitely — bad. (6) A view on which the death of a child and the death of a contented ninety-year-old come out equally bad, and both infinitely so, cannot be the right account of why death is bad. So, he concludes, the deprivation account refutes itself.

I have a hunch about where the trouble lies, but I've been wrong about this twice already, so please don't let me steer you. What I want is a proper diagnosis: say whether the fault is in the argument or in the account it attacks, name the specific step or move at which it goes wrong, and explain why that step looked innocent enough that a careful person would write it down without noticing. Then tell me what the deprivationist has to concede in order to escape the conclusion, and price that concession for me — in particular, whether making it costs her anything she needs elsewhere, say in the symmetry argument she'd like to run against Epicurus (why we are supposed to regret the death that follows us but not the nonexistence that preceded us). Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
