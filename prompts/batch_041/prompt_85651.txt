I am planning a seventy-five-minute session for a reading group of non-specialists — humanities graduate students who have had one symbolic logic course but no probability beyond coin flips — on the triviality results for indicative conditionals. The room has a whiteboard, and I am not assigning any reading. I do not want the result explained to me; I want a teaching plan. The target is the equation P(if A, then B) = P(B|A) and the way it collapses when it is required to hold not merely for one credence function but for every credence function obtainable from it by conditionalization on a proposition with positive probability. Assume the audience can read a two-by-two table and can carry out a single conditionalization once shown, but has never seen a triviality proof and has no background in formal semantics.

Give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit. What has to be in place before the equation can even be stated? What has to be in place before the conditionalization requirement can be understood as a constraint rather than a technicality? At what point can the proof be run without the audience feeling that a trick has been played, and what exactly must they have accepted by then?

I have been trying to carry the session with an invented device rather than with an English conditional: a conditional-credence machine with a dial for the unconditional probability of the antecedent, a dial for the unconditional probability of the consequent, and a dial for the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. The machine prints a credence for the conditional by the equation, and it has a learning button that updates all three dials by conditionalization on any new proposition. I want the audience to operate the machine and to watch it break. Tell me whether that device can carry the whole session. If it can, say exactly what it must be used for at each stage. If it cannot, tell me what to replace it with and what the replacement must have and lack. In particular, the carrying case must be one in which the conditional probability is intermediate, the material conditional is not equivalent to it, and the audience can compute both from a small table; it must also be one that can be conditionalized on in at least two directions without the audience losing track of what is being learned.

What misconceptions should I expect? I predict at least these: that the equation is just a definition of the conditional; that the triviality result shows indicative conditionals have no probability; that the result only shows the material conditional is wrong; that conditionalization is a technical trick rather than a constraint on learning; and that the if of the proof is the if of ordinary English. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off, and what the audience should be able to say to themselves at that point to prevent it. If there are misconceptions I have not listed, add them.

Give me one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they have understood the result rather than merely followed the algebra. It should be answerable only by someone who sees why the conditionalization closure is doing the work, not by someone who can reproduce the proof. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and say so explicitly: the full generality of the result, the various repairs, the relation to other triviality arguments, and the dispute over whether the result is a reductio of the equation or of the conditionalization requirement. Do not send me to the literature; I want the teaching sequence, the carrying device, the misconception map, the check for understanding, and the omissions.
