I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on virtue ethics and want help diagnosing an argument a classmate defended. I am reproducing it as it was stated.

(1) A character trait is a disposition to behave in trait-typical ways across the range of situations that call for it: the honest person tells the truth on the tax form and in the seminar, the brave person stands firm at the podium and on the battlefield.
(2) So if traits of that sort are real, behavior must be strongly consistent across trait-relevant situations; knowing how someone behaves in one such situation must substantially improve predictions of how they behave in another.
(3) The empirical record is the reverse. In the classic studies of honesty in schoolchildren, a child who cheated on one task was often scrupulous on the next; in the coin-in-the-phone-booth experiment and in the seminary-students-passing-the-slumped-man experiment, a trivial situational cue moved helping behavior more than any stable individual difference did; and the standard review of the field put cross-situational correlations of this kind near .2 to .3.
(4) Correlations that low show that the trait, not the situation, is failing to do the explanatory work.
(5) Therefore there are no character traits of the robust sort described in (1).
(6) Virtue ethics is a theory of exactly such traits.
(7) Therefore virtue ethics rests on a psychological posit that is empirically false.

What troubles me is that the same reasoning seems to cut further. If cross-situational correlations of .2 to .3 suffice to eliminate honesty and courage, they should also eliminate the dispositions that make the studies in (3) trustworthy in the first place, so the argument appears to saw off the branch it sits on. A friend replies that this is a cheap tu quoque: the argument is valid, and my only options are to deny the evidence in (3) or to abandon (6). I would rather keep (3) and (6) both.

So: is the argument valid, or does one step trade on something illegitimate? If it is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what exactly does giving it up cost? If instead one step is a slide, identify the inference precisely, say what reading of the premises would make it look fine, and tell me whether rescuing virtue ethics by redescribing traits as stable if-then profiles of situation-sensitivity saves the theory or quietly concedes the situationist's point — and whether that same redescription hands the situationist a notion of trait he cannot afford to reject.
