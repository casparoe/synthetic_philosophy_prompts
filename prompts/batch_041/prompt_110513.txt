I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing through AI-governance stuff, and we keep coming back to the singleton hypothesis. The rough idea, as we've been using it: once some actor gets a decisive lead in transformative AI (call that systems that can fully substitute for human labor in science, engineering, and governance), the world ends up under a single top-level decision-making agency — or a coalition that behaves like one — with no meaningful check on its grip on the key physical and informational levers. Friend A thinks this is basically the default outcome: any technology that lets one actor lock in an advantage over everyone else will get used that way, and the resulting order is self-stabilizing because the top can smother internal challenges. Friend B thinks that's backwards — a power-singleton is a house of cards, since its control runs through a chain of agents and automated systems that can defect, drift, or be quietly turned, so the long run is more likely a multipolar mess that occasionally re-coordinates. I'm definately somewhere in the middle, my gut says ~0.4, but I've stopped trusting it, because I think A and B are both smuggling two seperate things under one word. One is a singleton of power: a single agent or coalition that actually holds the levers. The other is a singleton of procedure: no central controller at all, but every agent independently running the same decision rule — same constitution, same alignment target, same whatever — so the world acts as one without anyone commanding it. B's objections all seem to be about power-singletons, and they maybe don't even touch procedure-singletons.

So here's what I want, and please don't hedge it into mush. Conditional on transformative AI being developed within the next century: what's your credence that the order holding a century after that point is a singleton in the power sense — a single agency or coalition with ultimate top-level control of the key levers, as opposed to merely a coordination equilibrium? Give me a number, then the decomposition that produces it. I want the sub-questions laid out, each with its own rough probability and a one-line justification: how likely a decisive strategic advantage is achievable by anyone at all; given one, how likely the winner actually builds and keeps a power-singleton rather than settling for a plural order or a treaty; how stable such a singleton is once built; what chance you give the procedure-singleton scenario instead (and whether you'd even file that as a singleton or as a third category); and whatever other terms you think belong, including emergent singletons nobody intended. Make the terms roughly carve up the outcome space so they don't double-count.

Then combine them, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and — most useful to me — tell me where A (who's at about 0.8) and B (about 0.15) most likely actually disagree once the power/procedure distinction is on the table, and what piece of evidence or argument would move your own number the most.
