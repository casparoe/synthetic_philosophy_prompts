Question 2 (20 points). The Republic of Valdania's civil conflict ended in 1999 with a negotiated settlement. The National Reconciliation Act, passed that year by the two-thirds majority the constitution requires, created the Valdanian Commission on Truth and Reparation and provided that any person who made a full written disclosure of politically motivated acts committed between 1985 and 1999 would be immune from criminal prosecution for those acts, provided that the person concealed no assets and held no public office at the time of the disclosure. The Commission could name perpetrators in its report and recommend reparations, but it had no power to punish anyone and its findings had no legal effect in any court. Two categories of acts fell outside the immunity: acts of sexual violence, which were excluded from the immunity only in 2004, after five years of lobbying by survivors' groups, and acts ordered by a person whom the Commission found to have ordered the killing of more than fifty civilians. The peace talks had been convened under pressure from Valdania's neighbours, and the outgoing military government had made its officers' exemption from trial a condition of continuing them; the negotiators' assessment was that without the Act the talks would have collapsed and the military would have kept control of the interior provinces. In 2003 the Commission commissioned a survey, conducted in the capital and two provincial cities, which asked residents: "Which would you prefer the government to spend scarce public funds on — a commission that documents what happened, or no process at all?" Seventy-eight per cent chose the commission. Using the three aims distinguished in week 6 — retribution (holding perpetrators accountable through punishment), recognition (public acknowledgment, addressed to victims, that a wrong was done to them), and non-recurrence (institutional reform sufficient to prevent repetition) — assess whether the Act can be justified, state the strongest objection to your assessment, and say what the Act's two exclusions from immunity reveal about the conditions under which it was drafted.

The answer I have to review:

The Act should be judged a justified settlement, though one with a real defect. It is easiest to see why if the three aims are kept apart.

Retribution is the aim the Act appears to sacrifice, but the appearance is misleading. The Commission named perpetrators in its final report, and in Valdania, as in most postwar societies, being publicly named is a form of punishment: the named person loses standing, employment, and the ordinary presumption of innocence. The Act therefore did not grant impunity so much as relocate accountability from the courtroom to the public record, which is the only forum the negotiators could deliver. The strongest objection to this is that the survivors whose relatives were killed never consented to the state waiving its power to prosecute, and that a waiver of that kind is not the state's to make on their behalf. The 2003 survey answers the objection: seventy-eight per cent of victims said they preferred the Commission to the alternative, so the waiver tracks the expressed preferences of the people whose claims were being traded away.

Recognition is where the Act does best. A commission is a state body, and its report is the state's acknowledgment, addressed to victims, that they were wronged rather than merely unlucky; this is exactly the recognition that a trial would not have produced, since a trial's verdict is addressed to the accused. The two exclusions show that the drafters understood proportionality: sexual violence and the ordering of large massacres were carved out, so the immunity was reserved for those whose culpability was comparatively small, and the line at fifty civilians shows a deliberate judgment about where the worst offenders begin.

Non-recurrence is the weakest leg, and the answer is not close. The Act contains no institutional reforms — no vetting of the security services, no restructuring of the interior provinces — and the negotiators' own assessment was that the military would otherwise have retained control there. If the military kept its command structure, the same people who committed the acts remain able to commit them again, and a commission that can neither punish nor reform cannot prevent repetition. The Act is therefore justified on the first two aims and fails the third, which makes it a partial and not a complete settlement.
