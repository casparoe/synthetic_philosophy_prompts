I am a student in a graduate seminar on consequentialism and practical reason, and I am trying to write a position paper on the cluelessness problem. The claim I have settled on, and want to stress-test before I commit to it, is this: because the remote and indirect consequences of our actions are vast, radically unpredictable, and plausibly dominate any expected-value calculation, act consequentialism cannot tell us what to do; and the difficulty is not a merely practical one that better information or better forecasting would eventually remedy, but a structural one. I have also become attached to a further step, which is the part I trust least: that if the problem is real, it does not stay inside act consequentialism. Any view whose verdict on an act turns on that act's actual downstream effects — a rule consequentialism that asks which rules would have the best consequences, and even some non-consequentialist views that make permissibility depend on empirical upshots — should inherit the same inability to know what it is licensing.

What would count against this position? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, and developments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be rather than merely how often they are repeated, and I want you to distinguish the ones that would be fatal from the ones that would be awkward but survivable. Say explicitly which of these the position's own defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, not only which ones its critics press.

I would like particular attention to four things. First, the symmetry response: the thought that our ignorance about the far future is roughly symmetric between enormous good and enormous harm, so that the two tend to cancel and we may legitimately fall back on near-term expected value. Is that a genuine escape from the objection or a cheat, and what would have to be true of the distribution of far-future effects for it to work? Second, whether the distinction between assessing acts and assessing decision procedures dissolves the objection or merely relocates it, and whether a consequentialism that evaluates procedures escapes cluelessness or only inherits it one level up. Third, whether my extension of the problem to rule consequentialism and to empirically contingent non-consequentialist views is a real generalization or an equivocation — in particular, whether those views are committed to the same kind of actual-consequence evaluation that generates the problem in the first place. Fourth, whether my position has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of any claim about structural limits.

The consideration I would personally find most persuasive is a case in which the alleged cluelessness plainly does not obtain — where the long-run effects of some act are either knowably bounded or knowably signed, so that we can say with confidence which way the ledger runs. Tell me whether such cases exist in the numbers the objection needs, or whether they are the exception that proves the rule. I do not need a survey of the literature; argue from the ideas themselves and tell me where my view is actually exposed.
