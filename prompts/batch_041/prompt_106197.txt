Until a dispute over workplace monitoring software landed on my desk last month, I had assumed privacy named a single thing that people simply valued more or less. I now sit on the working group that has to draft our policy, and I keep finding that my colleagues are answering different questions while believing they disagree. I would like a decision tree I can circulate that sorts the positions rather than summarizing the literature.

Build it so that every branch node is a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to, phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim rather than as a request to pick a label. Route through at least these distinctions: whether privacy is fundamentally the inaccessibility of information, control over its flow, or the fit between information and the context in which it was given; whether it is valuable in itself or only as a means to something else, such as autonomy, intimacy, dignity, or security; whether it is one right or a cluster of protections that can pull apart; whether its ground is the subject's interests or her consent; whether it is waivable, and whether it comes in degrees or as a threshold; and whether only individuals can hold it or groups can as well.

Place these cases somewhere on the tree: a smart speaker that keeps audio on the device until a wake word triggers a short clip to be sent; a loyalty program that infers a pregnancy from purchases and mails coupons before the family knows; a genealogy database that identifies a suspect through a relative who never submitted a sample; an employer wellness app that shares aggregated step data with an insurer; and a person's medical records released after her death.

Each leaf should name the position in a phrase, or be marked unnamed, with one line on the commitment it is hardest to accept. Note which combinations of answers the tree permits but nobody occupies, and say briefly whether each gap exists because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; where two positions cannot be separated by any question you can pose, say so rather than forcing them onto separate leaves. Aim for about eight branch nodes, and let the control-versus-access-versus-context split and the distinct-value-versus-reducible split do the early work, since those are the ones my group keeps confusing.
