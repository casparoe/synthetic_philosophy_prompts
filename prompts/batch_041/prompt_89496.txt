ok so I need a poem for the end-of-term zine in my philosophy of biology seminar and I've been stuck on it. It started as an arguement with my sister (she's a birder) about the herring gull and lesser black-backed gull thing around the arctic, where every population along the ring interbreeds with its neighbors, but the two ends of the ring end up living side by side in europe and don't interbreed at all. So are they one species or two? She says definately two. I said it depends what you mean by species, which is the most annoying thing a person can say at thanksgiving, and I've been chewing on it ever since. What I want the poem to get at is that the same birds, same traits, same trickle of gene flow, get carved up differently depending on which criterion you start from, the interbreeding one or the ancestry one or the overall-similarity one, and none of them is obviously the right one. And then the part I actually care about, which is whether any of this is a fact about the birds or a fact about our word. I keep flipping between thinking the species problem is a real metaphysical problem (there's a joint in nature somewhere and we just can't find it) and thinking it's a verbal problem (there's no joint, the concepts are just different tools for different jobs), and I want the poem to hold both without resolving it. Two things I'd love it to touch if you can work them in: the view that a species isn't a kind at all but an individual, a particular with a history, like a person or a storm, not a class you're either in or out of; and the view that all the rival concepts are really after the same thing, segments of lineage, and only differ over which evidence they'll accept for finding it.

Form-wise here's what I'm after: five numbered stanzas, four lines each, twenty lines total, with four recurring end-words that each appear exactly once per stanza, in a rotating order (stanza one is A B C D, stanza two B C D A, and so on), and the fifth stanza brings the rotation all the way back around to the first order. The point of that is the ring itself: the poem ends where it began, built out of the same four words, but it mustn't read as a return, the first and last stanzas should feel like they can't recognize each other, which is exactly what the gulls do. Choose the end-words so they can pull double duty, a word that's both a verb and a noun, or a word whose sense shifts when it moves position. Voice: a naturalist who has walked the whole ring. Someone in the seminar who's read all the species-concept literature should nod along, and my sister who's read none of it should still like it as a poem. No philosophers' names, no quoting anyone, no footnote explaining what you were up to, if it needs a note it doesn't work. Give it to me as just the five numbered stanzas and nothing else, no intro and no wrap-up.
