The examination question was as follows.

> Two cyclists, Nia and Tom, ride the same stretch of canal towpath in the same direction at the same time each weekday morning. Neither has ever spoken to the other. Each rides in the way cyclists call a paceline: the leading rider holds a steady pace for a while and then drops back so that the other can take the lead, and so on in turn. Each rides this way because it is what one does on the towpath and because it saves them both effort; each adjusts constantly to what the other is doing, holding a steady speed so as not to disrupt the other, leaving room, and taking the lead when the other drops back. Neither would describe the arrangement as a plan they have with the other, and neither would keep to it if the other stopped.
>
> Are Nia and Tom engaged in joint action? In your answer: (a) distinguish coordination from joint action; (b) set out how at least two of Bratman, Gilbert and Searle would analyse the case; and (c) say whether the case shows that joint action requires an explicit agreement.

One student's answer was as follows.

> (a) It is important to begin by separating coordination from joint action. Coordination occurs whenever two agents' behaviour meshes: two pedestrians who each step to the same side of a corridor because each expects the other to step that way are coordinating, but they are not doing anything together. Joint action is more demanding, because each participant must intend the activity as something they are doing together, and this intention must guide what they do. Nia and Tom are puzzling precisely because their behaviour meshes very tightly while nothing has passed between them.
>
> (b) Bratman's account is the most widely used. On his view, a shared intention is a single intention with two bearers: its content is that the two of them ride in a paceline, and it is held by them in common rather than separately by each. He surrounds this intention with three conditions: each intends that they perform the joint activity; each intends that the other's intention should guide their own; and each is disposed to support the other if support is needed. Because a shared intention is a joint commitment, Bratman holds that it cannot be set aside by one party alone.
>
> Gilbert's account is importantly different. When two or more people jointly commit to doing something, they constitute a plural subject, and the obligations that result are not owed by the parties to one another but are held by the plural subject itself. Gilbert also holds that a joint commitment can only be created by an explicit agreement or promise, since only an explicit act of that kind can bind the parties to one another.
>
> Searle's starting point is that collective intentionality is a primitive form of intentionality: a we-intention is not assembled out of an I-intention plus a belief about the other person. What is distinctive about his version is that these we-intentions are nevertheless had by individual minds. There is no group mind and no 'we' over and above the individuals.
>
> (c) The paceline convention is common knowledge among cyclists, and so Nia's and Tom's intentions to keep the rotation going are common knowledge between them. But neither has made an agreement or given the other any undertaking. On Gilbert's account there is therefore no plural subject, and on Bratman's no joint commitment. Searle's account does not help, because it tells us what shape a collective intention must have but not how to tell whether these two have one. Their riding is thus coordination sustained by a shared convention rather than joint action, and the case shows that joint action requires some public act of commitment, verbal or otherwise.
