I am a novelist at work on a collection of short fiction about people who do necessary work they suspect may be indefensible, and I would like your help drafting one of the stories. I would like you to hold two roles at once. The first is that of a literary fiction writer with a strong feel for compression, concrete detail, and morally serious endings that do not moralize. The second is that of a philosopher who works on animal minds and applied ethics — specifically on the epistemology of attributing sentience to creatures very unlike us, and on how moral and legal institutions try to act under that uncertainty. Think carefully before you write, and do not rush to the drafting.

Step one. Give me a working note, not prose: three distinct ways this story could be staged, and for each, the one conceptual question it would put under the greatest pressure. Then choose the strongest of the three and say briefly why.

Step two. Write the story.

The world of the story is as follows. The setting is a shrimp hatchery on the estuary of a coastal country that you should invent — I want no real company, port, retailer, regulator, or research institution anywhere in the story, and no footnotes or citations of any kind. The hatchery is run by two half-sisters who inherited it from their father; one has run the tanks her whole adult life, the other left to do a doctorate in marine biology and has come back. A northern European retail group has offered a long contract on one condition: the farm must be certified under a standard that requires it to stop inducing breeding by removing one of a female's eyestalks — the ordinary way the industry makes broodstock spawn in captivity — and to switch to feed and tank-ratio changes, and further requires electrical stunning before killing, a designated welfare officer, and a paper trail.

The conceptual notes I am working from are these, and I would like the story to take up two of them as its spine and let the rest stay ambient:

The asymmetry of error. Nobody in the story knows whether a shrimp suffers. The biologist's position is not that they do; it is that the two possible mistakes are not the same size, and that a person who waits for conclusive evidence has in effect chosen the error that costs the animal everything. The sister's counter is that if uncertainty alone obligates, then the obligation has no stopping rule, and she wants to know what principle would let them stop somewhere rather than spend the farm on a creature that may be an elaborate reflex.

The metric and the animal. The standard counts what can be inspected — stunning at slaughter, a logbook, a certificate on a wall — while the overwhelming majority of the animals on the farm are larvae and grow-out stock whose deaths are anonymous and unphotographable. Once the standard exists, everyone optimizes the standard.

The arithmetic. Billions of individuals, each of whose suffering is improbable, set against a handful of humans whose hardship is certain. One character does this arithmetic aloud. Another says the arithmetic is how you talk yourself into anything.

The circular evidence. The behavioural and pharmacological tests we use to detect pain were calibrated on animals whose pain we already grant. Whether they are a test at all when applied to a shrimp is not obvious.

Some constraints on the drafting. The philosophical content must live in scenes, objects, and what people do under pressure; no essay voice, no lecture, and no character standing in for the position you happen to favor — each should be intelligent and partly right. Do not resolve the empirical question; the story must not end with a revelation about what shrimp feel. If you need a philosophical idea, put it in a character's own words rather than quoting or naming anyone. End on a decision or a refusal, not a summary.

Deliver the working note first, in roughly three hundred words, then the story at roughly two thousand words.
