I teach a visiting session in a statistics department's methods seminar: sixty minutes, a flip chart, and an audience of graduate students and postdocs who write DAGs and use potential-outcomes notation daily but have never read a page of philosophy of causation. I want to teach them the difference between the counterfactual theory of causation and the interventionist theory — not to adjudicate between them, but to make them see that these are two theories rather than two notations for one thing, which is what they will assume walking in.

Plan the session for me rather than explaining the material. I need the order in which the components must be introduced and why, what depends on what — bearing in mind that their fluency with interventions is an asset I can exploit and a prejudice I have to dislodge; the one case that should carry the whole hour, what it must have and what it must lack, and why the familiar illustrations (rocks thrown at windows, the sprinkler and the wet grass, the barometer and the storm) are the wrong ones for this room; the misconceptions they will form, in the order they will form them, and the precise point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off; where their own machinery — the DAG, the do-operator — should be brought in, and where it must be set aside; one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely followed the hour; and what I should openly tell them I am leaving out.

I want the spine of the hour to be something they will not have met in their own literature, and I would rather you build the plan around it than around the standard contrasts: that the two theories disagree about the relata of causation — events or outcomes described at a chosen grain, on one side, against variables with a chosen range of values, on the other — and that each therefore inherits a prior, untheorised act of description, so that part of the disagreement is really a disagreement about where the carving gets done and who pays for it. Second, I want them to see that the interventionist programme is at bottom a theory of general, type-level relations which has to earn its verdicts about singular cases, whereas the counterfactual theory begins with singular cases and has to earn its verdicts about regularities. If you think that spine is wrong for this audience, or that one of the two halves will not survive sixty minutes with people who think in counterfactuals professionally, say so and give me a better one.

For the carrying case, use the one I have in mind rather than inventing another: a database goes down during a nightly schema migration, and the outage is traced to a scheduled backup job that started while the migration was running; the operations team had earlier removed the lock that normally prevents exactly that collision; a second backup job was due to start an hour later in any case; and the post-mortem records a total but brief outage alongside a corruption signature that would have been different had the job started a minute later. Build the hour on this, and tell me what work the case does at each stage — which of its features are load-bearing for the disagreement between the two theories, which are decoration I should strip out, and what the case conspicuously fails to settle.

Finally, be honest about the simplifications: which qualifications of each theory I will have to suppress in an hour, what a student in that room would have to unlearn afterwards, and whether the version of the distinction that survives the hour is one a philosopher would still sign.
