I teach the paradox of knowability (Fitch's paradox) in a graduate seminar on truth and meaning, and every year the discussion collapses into two camps talking past each other: those who take the argument to refute the claim that every truth is knowable, and those who take it to expose a flaw in the logic. I want a one-page decision tree that students can work through on their own and come out with a precise diagnosis of their own position rather than a label they have adopted.

Here is the argument I want the tree organised around. Suppose that every truth is knowable, and take any truth p that in fact nobody knows. The conjunction 'p and nobody knows that p' is itself a truth, so by the supposition it is knowable. Whoever knew that conjunction would know each of its conjuncts, since knowledge distributes over conjunction, and so would know that p and also know that p is not known; but knowledge entails truth, so the second piece of knowledge would make its object true and thereby contradict it. The conjunction is therefore not knowable, and by the supposition not true either; since p was arbitrary, no truth goes unknown. If you think the move from 'it is not the case that p is true and unknown' to 'p is known' needs more than the supposition supplies, say what it needs, and let that show up as a branch on the tree.

Specifications.

Root question. The first question must not be 'do you accept the knowability principle?' It should ask why one might think some truth lies beyond reach, so that a student can answer it before knowing what follows from the answer. I want the first branch to separate (i) those who hold that some truths are independent of any possible knower; (ii) those who hold that some truths are unreachable for structural reasons — a truth too complex for any finite mind, a truth no consistent system can prove, a truth about what no one will ever know — while denying that this shows anything about mind-independence; and (iii) those who hold that there is no unknowable truth and must therefore locate the fault elsewhere in the argument. The distinction between (i) and (ii) matters for my purposes: only the first is a break with the anti-realism the argument is usually aimed at.

Branch nodes. Each must be a commitment about one component of the argument — the range of the principle, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, the behaviour of 'knowable' as a modal operator, the treatment of negation and double negation, or the acceptability of the conclusion — and each must be phrased so that it can be answered without knowing which diagnosis it leads to. No branch node may simply name a response.

Leaves. Seven to ten, each labelled by the commitment it encodes rather than by an author, each carrying one sentence on its most awkward consequence. At least the following must appear: (a) the principle is false because some truths are independent of any possible knower; (b) the principle is false, but only because of structural limits on finite knowers; (c) the principle stands with its range restricted so that propositions about what is or is not known are excluded; (d) the principle stands with its range restricted to propositions whose being both true and known is not provably contradictory; (e) the principle stands and the derivation is valid, but knowledge is not closed under conjunction; (f) the principle stands, the derivation is valid as far as it goes, and closure stands, but the final move needs classical negation, so that abandoning double-negation elimination leaves the anti-realist with only the weaker result that no truth can be known to be unknown; (g) the principle stands but 'knowable' is not a normal possibility operator, quantifying instead over possible situations of knowledge, so that the derivation fails by holding the epistemic situation fixed across a conjunction; (h) the conclusion is not absurd — on an idealised or deflationary reading of 'known', every truth's being known is tolerable, and the principle survives with its interest diminished.

Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate with the questions available, and say whether the failure looks accidental or like the two are one view under two descriptions; mark which combinations of answers are left unoccupied and diagnose briefly why, distinguishing the incoherent, the unattractive, and the merely unexplored; and say which single question does the most sorting work. No citations, please: the tree has to stand on its own for students who have read nothing.
