I'm in a law-and-philosophy reading group this term and we've been doing precommitment, and somehow I've ended up presenting on Memento next week. I'd only seen it as a teenager and mostly remembered it as a puzzle about tattoos, but now that I've rewatched it with the reading in mind it seems like the evil twin of the example everyone reaches for — Odysseus lashed to the mast, crew's ears stopped with wax, everyone consents, nobody gets hurt, and the binding turns out rational because the bound self endorses it afterwards. Leonard is the opposite case. He can't lay down new memories, so he's built a whole apparatus of Polaroids, notes and facts tattooed on his body to govern a future self who will have no access to the reasoning that produced any of it — and then at the end we find out he isn't just binding that self, he's manufacturing evidence for it. He burns the photo that would have told him the job was done and writes himself a note pointing at the wrong man, so the guy who wakes up tomorrow does what the guy who wrote the note wants done. Binder and bound have different information and different agendas, and the bound one has no way to contest anything, ever. I want a reading of the film that actually takes the precommitment literature seriously — the Elster/Schelling material on self-binding and self-command, the constitutional version where a founding generation tries to bind future majorities, and honestly the psychiatric advance-directive version too, since that's where the real stakes are. Where does Leonard's system function as a precommitment at all and where does it stop being one? Is the film a reductio of self-paternalism, or a counterexample that only works because of the memory condition — i.e. is it kind of a cheat, since a merely forgetful person can at least be argued with? And is there a real disanalogy with Odysseus, given that his contract has an exit (the crew untie him once they're past the rocks) and his crew's deafness protects them rather than conscripting them, whereas Leonard's has no sunset clause and his later self gets drafted into enforcing the earlier one's plan?

Second thing, if you can fit it in: I keep hearing that a constitution is basically a Ulysses contract for a nation, and that the objection to this (I think it's Waldron, but I might be mangling it) is that the analogy smuggles in self-paternalism under the guise of self-government. Does Memento have anything to say to that, or is it a bad analogy because nations — unlike Leonard — have amendment procedures and can in principle argue back? And please be honest about where the film resists the reading; I don't want it reduced to an illustration. Does the memory condition make it a metaphor for ordinary weakness of will, or does the metaphor trade on a confusion between "the later self doesn't share my memories" and "the later self is a different person"? I'm not going to quote the film, so go by scenes — the tattooed facts, the Sammy Jankis material, the bit at the end where he burns the photo and writes himself the note, the closing monologue about memory. What's the scene that settles it for you, and what does the film give the debate that Schelling and Elster's examples dont?
