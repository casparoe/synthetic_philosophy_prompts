I've been following the numbers problem on and off for years, and I want to try something other than another round of arguing about it: I want a diagnosis of why the argument has stalled. The setup is the familiar one. One stranger will die unless I act; five strangers will die unless I act; I can save the one or the five, not both. Taurek's answer, in his 1977 article, is that the numbers do not count as such. Each person's prospective loss is a claim of equal weight, the five do not have a claim that outweighs the one's, and the fair thing for an agent to do is to give the two sides an equal chance, by coin toss. Parfit's reply treats this as a reductio: the view makes the numbers irrelevant even when the disparity is enormous, and it seems to process a single choice between two outcomes as though it were a series of pairwise comparisons between persons, which is not the shape of what the agent actually faces. Taurek's defenders do not concede. They tend to say the critics are presupposing that moral reasons must be additive or must aggregate, and that the counterintuitive cases show the view is strange, not that it is false.

My first hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides are not really disagreeing about numbers but about whether practical reason must be representable — whether a set of pairwise verdicts has to agglomerate into a single ranking of outcomes, or whether a decision procedure that never produces such a ranking can still be fully rational. If that is right, the counting dispute is downstream of a structural disagreement about what a reason is, and no new case about body counts will move it. My second hypothesis is that part of the noise is verbal, because counting is used in two different registers: appearing in the decision rule, versus being something each person may cite on their own behalf. Both sides seem to trade on both registers.

One thing I keep returning to, and cannot place in the literature: if you convert the single choice into a sequence, where each step is a pairwise choice that a coin toss may settle, then the pattern of tosses determines how many people live, and I cannot tell whether Taurek's view constrains the pattern or only each step taken alone. I would like to know whether the disagreement looks different in the sequential frame.

So: which of these hypotheses, if either, actually captures the impasse, and what kind of disagreement is this? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance whether Taurek's view can be stated as a coherent decision rule at all, or whether it is intransitive by design — that the debate keeps circling without ever settling? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a change of question, or nothing, because the question is ill-posed? Please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of Taurek or of his critics as the consensus.
