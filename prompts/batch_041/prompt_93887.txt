I'm a second-year PhD student in comparative lit, and I've somehow ended up refereeing a fight between two people in my department that I'm now pretty sure is a much older fight wearing new clothes. One of them — call her the same-rule person — thinks that when a poem is built on a formal constraint (a rhyme scheme, an acrostic, a syllable count, a lipogram, whatever), a translation that doesn't satisfy that same constraint just isn't a translation of *that poem*; it's a paraphrase or an adaptation, however well it reads. The other — a translator friend, the same-role person — says the whole point of the constraint is the work it does on the reader (the wit, the felt difficulty, the way it sticks in the memory), and that if you can't reproduce the rule you should reproduce the role, even if that means swapping in a *different* constraint; calling that "not a translation" is just rule-fetishism. They've been circling each other for a year and mostly talking past each other, and since I have to produce something for my qualifying exam anyway, I want to try that adversarial-collaboration format I keep hearing about: the document the two of them could actually both put their names to.

So could you draft it? What I'm after: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can honestly sign — about what a translation is, what a constraint *does* in a poem, what fidelity amounts to, what the reader is owed; (2) the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording the side it belongs to would actually accept, not a strawman version; (3) the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling the thing would move at least one of them, and for each crux the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the constraint is part of the poem's *meaning* or only a means to its effect, and another is whether a different constraint can ever count as the *same* constraint preserved — like, is "same role" ever "same rule"? If part of their fight turns out to be verbal (I think they may not even mean the same thing by "form"), say which part and why. And please don't paper over it if one of them is just wrong somewhere — I'd rather you flag it.

Here's the little text we've been using as our running test case, so you can pin the predictions to something concrete. It's a four-line English thing where (a) the first letters of the lines spell a word, and (b) it rhymes ABAB, and (c) I wrote it as a lipogram — no letter "e" anywhere. Assume we're translating it into a language that has no problem with "e" but whose word order and rhyme resources are very different (French or Spanish, say — doesn't matter, pick one and be consistent):

"Sun sunk, a darkling glow / Upon a paling hill; / So slow, that dying show, / A world turns cold and still."

(Yes the acrostic spells SU SA, which is a problem, don't @ me — actually you should probably fix that in the statement, or note that even the source constraint is half-broken, since that might itself be a crux.) What I really want out of the predictions is: for *this* text, does the same-rule person say a translation that keeps the acrostic and the rhyme but drops the lipogram is a failure, a partial success, or not a translation at all — and does the same-role person predict a reader of the target language would even notice the acrostic, and does that matter for whether the "role" was preserved?
