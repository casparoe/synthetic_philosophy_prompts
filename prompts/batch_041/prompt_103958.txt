I have been trying to convince a seminar that the epistemic foundations of game theory have been asking for common knowledge of the wrong thing. On the standard telling, the solution concepts — Nash equilibrium, backward induction, and the rest — are underwritten by common knowledge of rationality: each player is rational, each knows the others are rational, each knows that each knows, and so on up the hierarchy. I want to argue that the load-bearing assumption is really common knowledge of each other's decision procedures. What has to be common knowledge, I claim, is not an abstract property such as rationality but the programs the players are running, so that each can condition his choice on the algorithm the other will use rather than merely on the other's action. Once you make that swap, I think the classic embarrassments lose their sting: cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma becomes rationally sustainable when the two procedures are common knowledge; the backward-induction puzzle in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma is re-diagnosed as a fact about the complexity of the relevant procedures rather than a failure of rationality; and the familiar gap between common knowledge of rationality and Nash equilibrium stops looking like a defect, because rationality was never the thing doing the work.

Please take this position seriously and tell me what would count against it — not a survey of the literature, but the actual defeaters, ranked from the ones that should be decisive to the ones that would be merely awkward. I am especially worried about whether the move is too powerful to be a theory of anything. If any pair of programs can in principle be common knowledge, and if program-level common knowledge can sustain outcomes that are not Nash, then it begins to look as though I can "explain" cooperation, defection, or any other pattern by simply choosing the programs — which would make the view unfalsifiable rather than illuminating. I want to know whether that charge is fatal or merely a limitation that any equilibrium notion shares, and whether there is some independent constraint (a complexity bound, a restriction to admissible procedures, a stability requirement) that could rescue it without being ad hoc.

Then tell me which of the defeaters the view's own defenders would concede. For instance, would they admit that shifting from an epistemic idealization to a computational one is no improvement at all, since common knowledge of programs may be at least as demanding as common knowledge of rationality — that I have relocated the problem rather than solved it? Would they grant that the program-level story does not actually dissolve backward induction, because common knowledge of the *right* programs still yields the backward-induction outcome, so the dissolution depends on a choice of programs that I have not justified? And which objections would they dismiss as missing the point? Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether the position earns its keep over the boring alternative — that we should characterize solution concepts directly and treat all rationality talk as a dispensable scaffold — or whether it quietly collapses into that alternative. The defeater I personally find most threatening is the charge of vacuity, so weigh that one with particular care.
