My two flatmates, Mira and Dev, spent most of last night arguing in the kitchen and I ended up refereeing from the sofa without ever figuring out who was actually winning — though I was suprisingly invested by the end. Mira's been working on something she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and the way she pitches it is this. Imagine all suffering laid out on a single scale, from a mild annoyance at the bottom up to the worst agony you can picture at the top. Somewhere up that scale there's a line she calls the floor. Below the floor, pain and pleasure are commensurable: they sit on one scale and can genuinely be traded off, so a big enough pile of small pleasures really can outweigh a small pain. At or above the floor, suffering is lexically protected — no amount of pleasure or mild benefit, however vast, is allowed to justify it. She calls the leap from below-the-floor to at-the-floor the cliff, and she insists the cliff isn't a bug in her view but the whole point: serious suffering is beyond price in a way that mild suffering just isn't.

Dev came at her from four directions and I want to know how many of them actually land. First, he said the cliff is a mathematical fiction. If a pain a hair under the floor can be outweighed by a thousand units of mild pleasure, while a pain a hair over the floor can't be outweighed by any number of units at all, then the exchange rate between suffering and pleasure has to jump from finite to infinite at a single point on the scale, and he says no moral fact is shaped like that — it's like a heap of sand becoming sacred when you add one more grain. Second, he asked her to just say where the floor is, and she said she couldn't give a sharp number, that it's vague, like where baldness begins. Dev said fine, but then there'll be cases where it's indeterminate whether a given suffering is protected, which means her theory goes quiet exactly on the hardest cases, and a theory that can't tell you whether some piece of torture is permitted isn't much of a theory. Third, he said that if the boundary is vague while the protection above it is absolute, then some tiny marginal increment of agony is being asked to do infinite work — one extra minute of suffering can't be what flips something from "you may trade this off" to "this is forbidden no matter what." Fourth, he said the whole structure is unstable anyway: below the floor she needs pleasure and pain to share a common currency, and above the floor she needs them to be incommensurable, and you can't have one scale that both measures and refuses to measure. Mira's replies were, in order: to the first, that normative space is full of cliffs and that's perfectly fine — being alive versus dead is a hair's-breadth physical difference with a chasm of value running across it; to the second and third, that vagueness about where a boundary lies doesn't make the boundary arbitrary, and that above the floor the suffering isn't merely more pain but a different kind of harm, the kind that ruins a life rather than denting it, so it isn't the marginal minute that's doing the work; and to the fourth, that a lexicographic ordering, in which you'll never trade your top priority for any amount of the runner-up, is a perfectly coherent thing that people have all the time. Dev's comeback to that last one was that lexicographic orderings are coherent but notorious for implying absurdities — that you'd prefer a world with one trivial improvement to a world with unbounded good in it — and that coherent isn't the same as true.

Could you referee this properly? I don't just want a winner; I want you to go through each of Dev's four objections and Mira's four replies and say who's right at each specific step, wich objections are the strong ones and which are cheap shots, and what the two of them are each missing. I've got a hunch they're partly talking past each other, but I can't quite say where the arguement goes wrong.
