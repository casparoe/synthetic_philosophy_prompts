I am a novelist, and I have reached the stage of a manuscript where I need to know whether a passage of argument I have given to a character is sound, subtly broken, or broken in a way that a reader will enjoy catching. The book is set in a small invented kingdom, and the hinge of the plot is a battle lost because a message never arrives; the message never arrives because the rider's horse goes lame; the horse goes lame because a shoe comes loose; the shoe comes loose because a nail was never driven home. In an early chapter I let a retired archivist, an amateur of history with a taste for large conclusions, lay out the following argument. I would like to know which step to cut.

(1) To say that one thing caused another is to say that, had the first been otherwise, the second would not have happened.

(2) Consider the sequence: nail, shoe, horse, rider, message, battle, kingdom. For each adjacent pair it is true that, had the earlier item gone otherwise — the rest of the world staying as it was — the later item would not have occurred. Had the nail been driven, the shoe would have held; had the shoe held, the horse would not have gone lame; and so on, link by link, up to the battle and the fall of the kingdom.

(3) Therefore, chaining these links, had the nail been driven the kingdom would not have fallen.

(4) Therefore the missing nail was a cause of the fall of the kingdom.

(5) Nothing in this reasoning privileges the nail. The same argument can be run from any link in the chain, however trivial; and since every event sits at the end of some such chain of counterfactual dependencies, every event is a cause of whatever follows it.

(6) Chaos theory confirms that this is no artifact of my plot: in a chaotic system, arbitrarily small differences in initial conditions can produce arbitrarily large differences in outcome, so great events are genuinely hostage to small ones.

(7) It follows that no explanation of the kingdom's fall in terms of a succession crisis, a failed harvest, and the king's vanity is any better than an explanation in terms of the nail. Structural history is an illusion, and the past can be narrated but not explained.

(8) But (7) cannot be right. The novel I am writing is precisely an account of a succession crisis, a failed harvest, and a king's vanity, and I am confident that account is not interchangeable with the nail. So one of the steps above must fail.

I would sooner keep (1) and (2) than keep (6); the engine of the book is that small choices ramify, and I could lose the physics without losing the plot. But I suspect the damage lies elsewhere. Tell me where, why the offending step looked so plausible, and what each available repair would cost me — in particular, what I would have to surrender elsewhere in the argument if I kept the step you want cut. And say what would change your mind: which consideration would move you to a different diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
