I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature about how philosophers and statisticians think we should learn from what other people tell us. There's one case I can't get straight, and I'd rather not publish a framing that people who work on this will quietly wince at.

The case is the one known as the Judy Benjamin problem, introduced by the philosopher Bas van Fraassen in the early 1980s. As I understand it: a soldier is dropped into a region cut into four quadrants by two roads, so that she may be in Red or Blue territory and, in either case, either at Headquarters or with the Second Army. She begins with no reason to favour any of the four possibilities. An informant then tells her, in effect, that she cannot be told where she is, but that if she is in Red territory the odds are three to one that she is at Headquarters.

My reading is that this belongs to the same family as the Sleeping Beauty problem. The soldier is being asked to reason about her own location; her evidence is indexical, in the sense that she cannot restate it as a proposition about the world, only as something like: I am here, and here is probably Headquarters. On that reading, the lesson is that Bayesian conditionalization simply breaks down for this kind of evidence, and that the assorted repairs people propose — minimum relative entropy, imprecise probabilities, and the rest — are rival patches on the same broken engine. I'd also like to say that no consensus has emerged, which I take to be evidence that the defect is real rather than technical.

I want to fold in a homelier version of the case, because it's what started me thinking. My sister is a nurse on nights in a large hospital laid out as two wings on either side of a central core, with several floors in each wing. A colleague radios her: he can't see her, but if she's in the east wing she's almost certainly on the cardiac floor. She says the message left her less sure of where she was than she had been before, which I assumed was the same paradox in miniature.
