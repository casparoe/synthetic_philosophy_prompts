I've been sitting in on a reading group that is working through the mid-century adaptationism debates in evolutionary biology, and last week one of the regulars — a graduate student in ecology who is broadly unsympathetic to the Gould–Lewontin critique — walked us through the spandrels paper in his own words. His summary went roughly like this: the paper leans on the triangular spaces between the arches that carry the dome of St. Mark's in Venice, and the point of the example is that those spaces were never designed for the mosaics that fill them; they are simply what you get when you set a dome on rounded arches, and their beauty and their usefulness come along afterwards. Gould and Lewontin then generalize: a trait's current utility tells you nothing about why it evolved. They accuse adaptationists of assuming that every trait is optimally designed and that the biologist's only job is to hunt for its function, and they mock this as "Panglossian," after Voltaire's character who insists that everything is made for the best purpose. The heart of the paper, he said, is the claim that adaptationist hypotheses are untestable — any trait can be fitted with a plausible story after the fact — and that the programme is therefore not really science. Their own preferred alternative, he told us, is random genetic drift, and they conclude that most traits are probably non-adaptive byproducts rather than adaptations. He added two things he thought were decisive: that even sympathetic critics concede the example is misnamed, since the San Marco spaces are properly pendentives rather than spandrels, and that Gould later more or less admitted this, which he takes to gut the analogy; and that Dennett showed the spaces could have been built differently and were in fact selected for their appearance, so they are adaptations after all, which on his telling refutes the critique outright. He closed by allowing that the paper was enormously influential, that later work confirmed plenty of traits really are adaptations, and that this is exactly why he regards the whole thing as a polemic with no lasting methodological content.

I would like an accuracy audit rather than a defense or an attack. Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what is accurate; what is a recognizable simplification that a careful reader of the paper would want to qualify; and what Gould and Lewontin explicitly denied or would have rejected. Two things in particular have me stuck. First, the summary keeps sliding between a modest methodological claim and a much stronger anti-adaptationist one, and I cannot tell whether that ambiguity is the paper's fault or his; if the paper can be read as defending several different theses about what "a critique of the adaptationist programme" amounts to, tell me which reading is doing the work in his summary and which one the paper actually commits to. Second, the pendentive point and the Dennett point both sound like inside baseball about the architectural example that might not touch the biological argument at all — I would like to know whether either of them, taken at full strength, actually bears on the claim that adaptationist hypotheses should be tested rather than assumed, and whether the terminological dispute is really as damaging as he thinks. I am inclined to think he is being too dismissive, but I do not trust my own grasp of the paper well enough to say where the unfairness lives, so finish by telling me whether his concluding verdict — that the paper has no lasting methodological content — follows from whichever parts of the summary survive your corrections.
