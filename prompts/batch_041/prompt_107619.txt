Okay, so I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've got a piece due about the grue thing — Goodman's new riddle of induction, the emeralds that might turn blue on us — and a philosopher friend walked me into an argument over dinner that I still can't get out of. I don't want to file a takedown of induction that turns out to be hand-waving, so I need you to actually do the diagnosis for me.

Here's his argument, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Every emerald anyone has ever examined has been green.
(2) Now define a term: something is grue if it's examined before some future date T and is green, or examined after T and is blue. Every emerald in (1) is grue as well as green. So "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" fit every observation on record exactly equally well.
(3) Two hypotheses that fit the total evidence equally well are equally well supported by that evidence.
(4) So "all emeralds are grue" is supported just as strongly as "all emeralds are green," which means I have as much reason to expect the next emerald examined (after T) to be blue as to be green.
(5) When I say green is the simpler, more natural hypothesis, he says simplicity is always relative to the vocabulary you happen to have. We could have grown up with grue as the primitive term and green as the contrived one; nothing in the world picks a winner between them.
(6) Nothing here is special to emeralds or to colors. For any prediction I care about — that this drug works in the next trial, that the sun comes up tomorrow — he can define a rival predicate that fits every observation on record and diverges from my hypothesis in exactly the case I care about. So every prediction has an equally well-supported rival that says the opposite.
(7) Therefore no prediction about the unexamined is any better supported than its negation. Induction has no rational basis at all: expecting the sun to rise tomorrow is on all fours with expecting it not to.
(8) When I said that if he's right, his argument also gives me no reason to believe his argument, he said that's fine — that's a reductio of induction, not of him, and an argument doesn't need to be believable, only valid.

I'm fairly sure something in there is broke, and I definately don't want to give up (3) — it looks like the most obvious line on the list — but I also can't print (7) with a straight face. So: which step goes, why did it look fine to me at the time, and what does dropping it actually cost? If the casualty really is (3), tell me what I'm supposed to replace it with and how much of that replacement I'd have to defend in the piece. And if the honest answer is that he's got a valid argument and I have to give up something else — (2), or (5), or my grip on (7) — say which one and be specific about the bill. Please don't just tell me it's a language trick and stop there; three people have told me that already and none of them could say what the trick was.
