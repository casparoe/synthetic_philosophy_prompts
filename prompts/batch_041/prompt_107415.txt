I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading political philosophy, and I now sit on my city's Charter Review Commission. The commission must send the City Council a formal written opinion before the November ballot deadline on two linked proposals: first, to replace our elected nine-member transit authority with a panel of forty-five residents chosen by lottery, stratified by ward and balanced by age, serving a single non-renewable two-year term, with a paid professional staff, and holding authority over the capital budget; second, to impose a three-term limit on the City Council itself. My colleagues are split. Some argue that only a body drawn at random can be trusted to represent the whole city rather than organized interests, and that mandatory rotation is the cure for the professional politician; others argue that a transit budget is a technical matter amateurs will mismanage, and that term limits merely transfer power to the permanent staff who brief them. Please draft the opinion I will circulate at our next meeting. I want a clear recommendation on each proposal -- adopt, reject, or adopt with stated amendments -- with reasons my colleagues can genuinely argue against, and an honest account of the strongest case for whatever I end up recommending against. I would also like the reasoning to turn explicitly on a distinction I keep worrying over as a scientist: a random sample can be trusted to mirror a population's existing opinions, but that is not the same as being trustworthy to form good ones, and I am not certain which of these two things the proposal is really asking of the panel. Please give me an actionable document rather than a survey of what philosophers have written; no citations are needed, and I would rather have the argument in your own words than a reading list.
