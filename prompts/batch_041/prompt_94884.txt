I want to put a thesis to you and ask what would count against it. The thesis concerns low-impact AI: the project of constraining an artificial agent to avoid side effects — to leave the world roughly as it would have been, or to refrain from exercising unwanted influence — without first specifying a full value function for that agent. My thesis is that this project is incoherent. Any proposed impact measure, I claim, falls into one of three traps. It is trivial: satisfying it strictly requires the agent to do nothing, or to take only actions that make no difference to anything, which is useless for any real task. Or it is a full utility function in disguise: once you say which differences count as side effects and how much they matter, you have implicitly ranked world-states, and the measure is no longer value-neutral. Or it is arbitrarily baseline-dependent: the measure must compare the actual trajectory to some counterfactual baseline — inaction, a random policy, a default policy, the agent's own prior — and the choice of baseline encodes a judgment about what the agent was entitled to do, so value-neutrality is an illusion. There is also the butterfly-effect worry: every action has unbounded downstream consequences, so any measure must draw an arbitrary line in time or causal distance, and where it draws that line is a value judgment.

I am aware of the proposals that seem to avoid these traps. There is the idea of measuring impact as the reduction in the agent's ability to reach a wide range of future states, on the thought that reachability is a value-neutral notion. There is the idea of penalizing changes in the attainable utility of a set of default policies, on the thought that you can keep the agent's options open without knowing which options it values. One could also propose information-theoretic measures that penalize divergence from a default policy, on the thought that staying close to the default is a value-free way to avoid interference. Consider, for instance, a measure defined as the divergence between the distribution over future states under the agent's policy and under a default policy; is that value-neutral, or do the choice of divergence and the choice of default already encode values? I want you to take these proposals seriously and tell me what would count against my thesis, not by defending them in the abstract but by identifying the specific evidence, arguments, formal results, or thought experiments that should move me.

Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be. Which would show that one of the three horns is not exhaustive, or that a particular measure escapes all three? Which would show that the baseline problem is not a problem about values but a general problem about counterfactuals — a special case of the act/omission distinction and the general problem of causal baselines — and therefore no more damaging here than elsewhere? Which would show that the triviality horn is not trivial, because there are measures that are non-trivial, value-neutral, and baseline-independent? Which would show that the thesis is unfalsifiable, because any measure can be redescribed as encoding values? Tell me what a defender of low-impact AI would themselves concede as counting against the thesis, not only what critics would say. If the thesis is well insulated, say so and say whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind. I would also like to know what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not, and whether there are engineering developments — say, a working low-impact agent that passes a battery of side-effect tests without a hand-specified utility function — that would count against it. Is the thesis a conceptual truth or an empirical generalization? If conceptual, what would a counterexample look like? If empirical, what would disconfirm it? I would find a formal impossibility result most persuasive, but I am open to case-based arguments.
