Two colleagues in my department have been arguing for a week about the function of a single trait, and neither will concede. The trait is a raised ridge that runs along the shell of a pond snail in one particular lake. In the lake's ancestral snail population the ridge was plainly favored by selection: it deflected the jaws of a beetle that preyed on the snails. That beetle has been absent from the lake for a few thousand years. In the present population the ridge neither helps nor hurts — snails with and without it survive and reproduce at equal rates, and no developmental or physiological process depends on it. Here is the exchange, as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Dana argues that the ridge's function is to deflect the beetle's jaws. That is what the ridge was selected to do, she says, and a trait does not lose its function merely because the selection pressure that produced it has lapsed; the function is a fact about the trait's history, not about the present environment.

(2) Ellis argues that the ridge has no function now. Function, he says, is a trait's causal contribution to a capacity of the organism as it presently lives; since the ridge contributes nothing to any such capacity, calling it "for" beetle defense is just a fact about its history dressed up as a fact about its function.

(3) Dana replies: on your view, if the beetle were reintroduced tomorrow the ridge would acquire a function overnight with the snail itself unchanged, and would lose it again if the beetle died out. Functions would flicker in and out with the surroundings. That cannot be right.

(4) Ellis replies: functions are relational, so flickering is exactly what we should expect. And your view has the mirror-image problem. Some snails in the population carry a ridge that, purely as a side effect of how the ridge develops, happens to brace the shell against a crab that invaded the lake a century ago and now kills snails. The bracing saves lives, but no snail has ever been selected for bracing — only for deflecting the beetle. Your account must therefore say the ridge is "for" beetle defense and not for crab defense, even though the beetle is gone and the crab is the thing actually being resisted.

(5) Dana replies: selection is the only thing that separates a function from a mere effect. If you let present causal contribution count by itself, then every effect of a trait becomes its function — the heart makes noise, so noise-making becomes a function of the heart — and the notion collapses into uselessness.

(6) Ellis replies: that is a caricature. The causal-role account counts only contributions to some capacity of the system that we have independently singled out for analysis; noise-making is excluded because no one is analyzing the heart under the capacity "making noise."

(7) Dana replies: and what singles out the capacity? As far as I can tell, it is whichever capacity makes the answer come out the way you wanted — which is circular.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the seven moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, questionable, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is closer to the truth overall about the snail ridge, and identify what, if anything, each of them is missing — including any respect in which the disagreement is merely verbal and any respect in which it is substantive.
