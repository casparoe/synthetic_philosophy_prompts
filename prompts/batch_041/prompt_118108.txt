I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the problem of other minds, and I came away holding a position I did not have before, so I want to know how much exposure it has. I now think the problem is not an epistemic problem at all but an artifact of a particular stance — the detached, third-person, evidence-weighing stance we slip into when we theorize about a creature rather than address it. When I am actually in conversation, or arguing, or sitting with someone who is grieving, the question of whether anyone is there does not arise and I cannot make it arise; it becomes formulable only when I step back and treat the other as a system to be assessed. On this view the skeptic's doubt is not answered, it is dissolved: it presupposes a standpoint I am not obliged to occupy, and whatever force it has comes from sliding into that standpoint and then forgetting I did. My friend, a functionalist, spent the evening pressing the opposite line — that a system which does everything a minded thing does leaves nothing over to doubt — and I could not accept that either, because it seems to me that no amount of functional detail distinguishes a system that merely reports being in pain from one that is, and the stance story is supposed to make that gap livable rather than close it.

The sharpest thing he said, and the reason I am writing, was this: if the engaged stance is what secures the other's mindedness, it ought to be incorrigible, but it plainly is not — people fall for chatbots, grieve over answering machines, and, worse, fail to register the mind in front of them, as when a caregiver treats a patient as furniture. If the engaged stance can be wrong in those ways, he says, it cannot be what makes the difference between there being a mind and there not being one, and my dissolution collapses back into the very epistemic problem I wanted to escape. He also claimed the view has an absurd consequence: if other minds depend on my stance, then a hermit who never adopts the engaged stance lives in a world with no other minds. And I will add a consequence of my own, which I am not sure is a prediction or merely a restatement: if the story is right, sustained engagement with something should make the skeptical question about it practically unavailable, so the doubt should be rare among people in intimate relationships and common among those who study minds from the outside.

So what would count against the position? I do not want a survey of the literature — I want the defeaters themselves, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including which ones a committed defender of this line would themselves concede and which they would simply dismiss. Tell me which of the objections above have real teeth, whether the stance story can survive them without turning unfalsifiable, and — if it cannot — whether that insulation is a defect or just the price of the kind of claim it is. Lead with your bottom line in a sentence, then give me the ranked list, all in under 700 words and in American English.
