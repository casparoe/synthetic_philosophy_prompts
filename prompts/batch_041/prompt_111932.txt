Ok so I've been volun-told into giving the opening remarks at our workshop next month — "Certainty and Machines", which was definately not my title — and I need a draft. Fifteen minutes, first thing in the morning, before anyone has had coffee. The room is going to be a mess in the productive way: roughly half Wittgenstein people, half ML folks, a couple of statisticians, and one guy I already know is going to tell me in the Q&A that hinges are just priors in a nicer outfit.

What I want to do with the fifteen minutes is float one distinction I've been chewing on and then get out of the way. Roughly: some certainties hold because you can't run the practice of asking without them — there's no move in the language-game that would count as doubting them, you'd just be changing the game. Others hold only because a particular community stopped asking a long time ago and could in principle start again, even if it would be expensive and embarrassing. Constitutive vs. what — inertial? I'll take a better pair of names if you've got one. The provocation I want to float (float, not defend; that's the afternoon people's job) is that what gets called a model's knowledge is basically all of the second kind, and that a lot of the shouting about whether these things know anything is people sliding from one kind to the other mid-sentence.

Write the remarks. Things I care about:

- Open on something concrete. A case, an anecdote, a small argument from my own teaching — not on a definition. Definitions are what the second speaker is for.
- It has to work for the engineers without being condescending and for the philosophers without being a fan letter. No lit review, no name-dropping for effect; half the room has On Certainty open on their laptops and knows the book better than I do.
- It can't preempt the day. Two people are on later to say the distinction collapses, or that it was never mine to begin with, and the remarks should leave them room while still being an invitation with teeth.
- The last two minutes should make it obvious why we're all in the same room.
- Spoken register. I'll be reading it off paper, so it can be loose, but not blog-loose.

And please don't make me sound wiser than I am. I've got fifteen minutes, one idea, and a room that starts checking its phones at minute six.
