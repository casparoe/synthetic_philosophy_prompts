I'm a lecturer in philosophy, and in ten days I'm presenting a work-in-progress paper at our department's reading group — a paper in which I try to make a reconstructed version of Ubuntu's relational conception of personhood do real metaphysical work rather than serving as a moral slogan. My thesis, compressed, is that personhood comes in two layers. The first is a baseline moral standing that every human being has independently of any relation, which I am happy to treat as unconditional. The second — I call it sustained personhood — is the status of actually being held inside the memories, expectations, and practices of some community, and unlike the first it can be gained, lost, or never attained at all. The Ubuntu insight, once I strip away the piety, is about this second layer: selves are not merely shaped by relations but are kept in being by them, so that abandonment, solitary confinement, and the social death of the institutionalized are not merely harms done to a person but erosions of personhood itself. I concede the first layer precisely so that I can claim the second one without sounding as though I think the disabled, the elderly, or the friendless are less than human.

The audience is the problem. It's a general work-in-progress group: mostly analytic metaphysicians who work on personal identity and have Parfit's reductionism in their bones; two Kantians who will insist that personhood is anchored in rational agency and therefore cannot be conferred or withdrawn by anyone; one philosopher of mind who is going to ask whether "sustained personhood" does any explanatory work that "flourishing" or "social inclusion" doesn't already do; and a social epistemologist who will be watching for the power dynamics buried in the idea of being held inside the practices of a community — who gets to sustain whom, and what becomes of people whose community has decided they don't count.

What I want is a question-period prep sheet, not a summary of my view: the objections this particular room will actually raise, ranked by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that objector would phrase it — a Parfitian's version of "you've smuggled normativity into a descriptive claim" is not the Kantian's version — with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front to buy credibility, what to refuse to give up, and include the objection I'm most afraid of hearing, with a candid account of why it should worry me most. I don't need citations or reading suggestions; I need the arguments as this room will make them. Finally, be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail here: say which objection, if it lands, would actually change your verdict on whether my two-layer picture holds up, and which consideration would push you the other way.
