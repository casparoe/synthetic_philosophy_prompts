I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like help thinking through something ordinary rather than something about public policy.

At my company, and as far as I can tell at every consumer software company, a person creates an account by clicking a button and is thereby taken to have agreed to a long document of terms. Those terms reserve the right to revise them at any time, and state that a user's continued use of the service after a revision counts as acceptance of the revised terms. The revision is announced, if at all, by a banner or an email most users never open. I work on the interface that presents that button, and I have never been able to say what makes the click an agreement to something the user has not read and cannot anticipate. Meanwhile the same company, when it wants an employee to appear in a promotional video or to give up the right to bring a claim, requires a signed form, explains the document, offers time to consider, and treats silence as a refusal. Nobody here thinks a banner would suffice in that case.

What I want is a reconstruction of what the first practice presupposes, not a list of reforms.

Does the click presuppose that consent is a performative act whose validity does not depend on the consenter's awareness of what is being authorized? Or does it presuppose that the moral work is done by something other than consent altogether — notice, a genuine opportunity to leave, or the reasonableness of the terms? And is what the user gives actually consent, or is it a waiver of the right to object, which is a different thing with different conditions of validity?

Is advance consent to terms that do not yet exist even coherent? An agreement to be bound by whatever a counterparty later decides is not obviously an agreement to anything. If it can be valid, what must be true of the parties, the stakes, and the revocability of the arrangement?

Consider that in sexual ethics the prevailing standard has moved away from treating the absence of refusal as permission, and now asks for something affirmative, specific, ongoing, and revocable. If that is the correct analysis of consent where the stakes are bodily, why is it not the correct analysis where the stakes are informational and financial? Is the difference a difference in the nature of consent, or only in what we are willing to enforce?

I also want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Several accounts of consent might license exactly what we do: a purely conventionalist one on which consent means whatever a community treats it as meaning, a libertarian one on which the terms simply are what they are and the user is free to decline, and a welfare-oriented one on which notice plus a real exit is enough. Which of these does the practice actually require, and which merely fit it? And can the two halves of the company's conduct — the banner and the signed form — be reconciled under a single theory, or only under a theory on which the disparity in bargaining power is doing all the explanatory work?

Finally, suppose the presupposition were replaced by a stricter one: consent must be contemporaneous, specific, informed, and revocable, and silence is never agreement. What would have to change in the sign-up flow, in how revisions are handled, and in the parts of the business that depend on changing terms without asking again? Which of those changes would be merely expensive, and which would make the present arrangement impossible?
