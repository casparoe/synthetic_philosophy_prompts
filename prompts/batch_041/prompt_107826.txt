Two friends of mine are building a bot that negotiates on your behalf against strangers, and they've been going at each other all week about a design idea one of them got from a paper — they call it a "safe Pareto improvement." The idea, as I understand it: you can't have real beliefs about how the other person's bot will play, so instead of maximizing expected value you look for a change to the game itself such that everyone comes out ahead of "default" play no matter what the other side does — a guarantee, not an average. The catch is that the guarantee only holds relative to some qualitative assumptions about how play carries across games (structurally identical games get played the same way, nobody plays a strictly dominated move, that kind of thing).

Jamie: this is the only honest way to improve your position on your own. Any probability you assign to an opponent you've never met is made up, so an expected-value tweak is a bet wearing arithmetic as a disguise. A guarantee under stated assumptions is at least auditable, and the assumptions are about relations between games, not about who the opponent is, so they're far weaker than a prior.

Sam: the guarantee is a costume. You've swapped "what type is my opponent" for "how does play transfer between game descriptions," which is just as empirical and just as falsifiable — people don't play isomorphic games isomorphically, framing effects are an entire literature. And demanding that the improvement hold in every case is straightjacket maximin, which will happily throw away a change that's near-certainly great because it's bad in some case you'll never see. He says the honest version is a distribution over opponents plus expected value, and that "safety" is that same move with the distribution hidden.

Jamie: the baseline isn't arbitrary, it's what actually happens if nobody does anything, and making someone worse off than the status quo is the thing that needs consent.

Sam: "what happens if nobody does anything" is itself undefined, because it depends on how both sides reason; so a guarantee relative to a modeling choice isn't a guarantee.

They want me to referee. Go move by move — who's right at each step, what are they both missing, and is there a version of the idea that survives Sam's objection?
