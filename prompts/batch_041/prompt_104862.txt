After I was asked to take over the management of a small family endowment, I spent several weeks reading about the Kelly criterion and what is sometimes called geometric rationality — the idea that one should size commitments so as to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth. Two features of my situation seem to fall outside the standard treatment, and I want to understand how far they undermine it. First, the decisions are few and effectively non-repeatable: I expect to make perhaps ten consequential allocation choices over the next decade, and I cannot replay any of them, so the appeal to long-run growth over many independent trials appears to be unavailable. Second, ruin is not zero wealth but a level below which the family's obligations cannot be met, which makes the downside asymmetric in a way the log-utility story does not directly capture. Please describe the most important implications of geometric-rationality reasoning for someone in my position: which of its usual justifications survive when the number of bets is small, and how should I think about sizing when my own probability estimates are uncertain and the outcomes of the bets are correlated with one another?
