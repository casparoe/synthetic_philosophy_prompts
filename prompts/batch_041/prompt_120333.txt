Two of my friends got into a thing last week about Good's theorem — you know, the one that says free information can never have negative expected value, since if you don't like what you learn you can just ignore it and act as if you never got it. One of them, R, is a decision theory person; the other, M, is a novelist. They were arguing about whether you should always want to know the results of a medical test, or whether you should want to know the ending of a movie you're about to watch.

R said: look, this is settled. If the information is free, your expected utility can't go down, because you can always choose the same action you would have chosen without it. So a rational person should never prefer not to know something, unless they're paying for the information or they're irrational.

M said: that's garbage. If I know I have a 50% chance of a fatal disease, I might be miserable for the rest of my life, and I can't just ignore that. The knowledge itself changes how I feel, not just what I do.

R said: then your utility function includes the disutility of knowing. That's fine — but then the information isn't free; it has a cost in utility. The theorem is about information that only affects you through your decisions. If knowing something makes you feel bad, that's a cost, and the theorem doesn't say you should pay any cost to learn it.

M said: that's a bait and switch. The theorem is supposed to be about information, not about money. You're just redefining "free" to mean "doesn't affect my utility," and then the theorem says "information that doesn't affect your utility doesn't affect your utility." That's vacuous. The interesting question is whether information can hurt, and the answer is obviously yes — look at surprise parties, or spoilers, or finding out your partner cheated.

R said: no, the theorem is not vacuous. It says that in the standard decision-theoretic setup — actions, states, outcomes, utility over outcomes — the expected value of information is nonnegative. That's a real result. If you want to include beliefs in the utility function, you can, but then you're changing the setup. The theorem still holds in the new setup; it just says that information which affects your utility directly is not "free" in the relevant sense. The confusion is thinking that "free" means "costs no money." It means "doesn't cost you any utility to acquire, and you can ignore it."

M said: but you can't always ignore it. If I learn the ending of the movie, I can't unlearn it. I can still watch the movie, but I won't enjoy it the same way. So the information has changed my experience, and I can't just act as if I never got it. The theorem assumes you can ignore it, and that assumption is false in lots of real cases.

R said: then the theorem doesn't apply to those cases. It's a conditional theorem. If you can ignore it, it can't hurt you. If you can't ignore it, all bets are off. That's not a flaw; it's just a scope condition.

M said: but people cite the theorem all the time to say "more information is always better," and that's false. The theorem is true but misleading. The interesting cases are exactly the ones where you can't ignore it, and the theorem has nothing to say about them.

R said: people misuse it, sure. But the theorem is still important because it tells you that in the pure case, there's no such thing as a rational preference for ignorance. If you prefer not to know, it's because either you're irrational or the information isn't free.

M said: or because your utility depends on your beliefs. Which is most of life.
