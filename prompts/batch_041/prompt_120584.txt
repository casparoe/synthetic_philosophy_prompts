I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have collected a small set of cases that I suspect are instances of one phenomenon, though I cannot find a name for it and I am not sure the cases belong together.

(a) In a branch predictor, a table of two-bit saturating counters is indexed by branch history and used to guess whether the next branch will be taken. It behaves as though it expects loops to be entered rather than exited, and expects a branch's recent past to predict its near future. Those expectations are not decorative: if they were false, the predictor would be wrong and the processor would run slower. Yet nobody designed the table to represent anything. Its layout was chosen because it scored well on a benchmark suite, and the engineer who chose it could not have stated why it works.

(b) A genetic algorithm I once wrote, on an iterated game with a fixed payoff matrix, converged on a strategy that game theorists independently recommend: open by cooperating, then do whatever the other party did last time, with an occasional forgiveness step. My code contained no representation of an opponent, a payoff, or a game, only selection over bit strings. I can nevertheless state, in ordinary terms of purposes and what serves them, why that strategy wins, and that statement explains why the population converged on it, even though nothing in the population ever entertained it.

(c) A soap film stretched across a wire frame settles into a surface of least area; a river network carves something close to a minimum-cost drainage pattern; cooling metal anneals into a low-energy configuration. Each outcome is the one an engineer would reach by deliberate minimisation, but there is no selection and no designer, only physics relaxing.

(d) A counterexample of sorts. For years I believed a heuristic of mine worked because it favoured recent data. When I finally instrumented it, the recency story was wrong; it worked for an unrelated reason. The explanation I had been giving sounded exactly like the ones in (a) and (b) but was not an explanation of anything.

What I want to know is whether there is an established concept for what (a) and (b) share: behaviour that is correctly explained by a reason statable in ordinary terms, where the reason is represented nowhere in the system, and where the system's success depends on the reason being the right one rather than on luck. A biologist colleague told me this is just function talk, but functions in biology seem to come with a history, and case (c) has no history at all, so I do not think that covers it. I would also like to know whether the concept requires a history of design or selection, so that (c) either belongs or is a genuinely different thing; whether (d) is ruled out by the same consideration that rules out speaking of a falling rock's reason for falling; and how the whole idea differs from merely saying that the system is optimal, or that describing it in purposive terms is a convenient fiction.

If there is a name, give it, but the name is not what I am after: tell me how the thing relates to the nearest concepts and which of my four cases it actually covers. If there is no established name, propose one, define it, and say where its edges are.
