I keep running into one analogy in discussions of market epistemology, and I want to know how much of it is load-bearing. The analogy: a market is a giant information-processing device — a distributed computer, or a prediction machine — whose single output, the price, encodes the dispersed private knowledge of every participant; the traders are its processors, arbitrage is its error-correction, and the price is a consensus estimate of value. In my reading group it is then made to do real work: because the machine aggregates more information than any one person holds, the price is treated as epistemically authoritative — it knows better than the analyst, the regulator, or the central planner — and departures from fundamental value are anomalies to be explained away rather than evidence that the machine is broken.

I want an audit, because two results I have been chewing on seem to cut against the picture. The first is the no-trade theorem: if the market is in efficient equilibrium, there are no noise traders or other non-rational participants, and the structure by which traders acquire their information is itself common knowledge, then even a trader who genuinely holds private information cannot profit from it — any offer to trade reveals that information and is priced in before anyone accepts, so, absent noise or non-speculative gains from trade, the private information never reaches the price through trade at all. The second is the agreement theorem: two Bayesians who share a prior and whose posterior beliefs are common knowledge must end up with identical posteriors, so they cannot agree to disagree, and any sustained disagreement embodied in prices has to be traced to something other than two rational agents holding different private evidence.

What I want is this. First, the correspondence table — what in the market plays the role of processor, bus, input, output, and error signal. Then the points where the mapping breaks, sorted into the breaks that matter for the intended use (the price's claim to epistemic authority) and the ones that are merely decorative. Then a verdict: does the conclusion the analogy was carrying — the price as an authority that outknows any participant — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the work all along? Two specific pressure points I would like addressed: that the price is not only the machine's output but also an input to every participant's calculation, so the loop is self-referential in a way that a computer's is not; and that anyone who spends resources to acquire the private information must be compensated for it, which a price that had already aggregated everything would not permit. If the computer picture cannot be repaired, tell me what better analogy there is, or whether the market is best described without one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
