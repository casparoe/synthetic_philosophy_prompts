I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on scientific realism and the philosophy of physics, and for my final essay I've been asked to diagnose a live dispute rather than settle it. I've chosen the long-running fight over whether the multiverse and the string-theory landscape count as legitimate, confirmable science, and I want to use the essay to work out what kind of disagreement it really is.

The shape of it, as I understand it: one side holds that these frameworks make no testable predictions and so are not (or not yet) confirmed science; the other holds that the demand for direct testability is too crude, that much accepted physics was once untestable, that indirect or probabilistic support can be enough, or that the alternatives are worse. Both sides seem to know each other's moves, and I've watched the same exchanges recur in seminar and in the reading without anyone shifting. I've also noticed the cases get bundled together — the multiverse that inflation is said to generate, the landscape of vacua, the more recent worries about which regions of the theory space are even consistent — and I suspect that lumping them under one heading is part of why the debate never resolves.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than endorsed, is that this is not really a disagreement about string theory or the multiverse at all, but about what a scientific theory is supposed to do: specifically, whether empirical content is a property a theory either has or lacks, or a relation between the theory and the particular epistemic situation of the scientists who hold it. If that's the fault line, the two sides may be answering different questions under one name — one about what the theories are, one about what we could ever be in a position to learn — and much of the heat is misplaced.

Please diagnose it. Tell me whether it is substantive, merely verbal, or two groups talking past each other, and support the diagnosis with what each side is actually committed to rather than a survey of positions. Then say what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that nothing should be expected to move, and why. Finally, tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would push you to a different verdict.
