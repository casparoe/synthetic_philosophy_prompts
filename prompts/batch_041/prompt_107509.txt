Ok so I'm in this course on minds and machines (it's cross-listed phil/cogsci) and we've spent the last two weeks on which systems actually deserve moral consideration, and I keep finding that my gut and every theory I try don't line up. I want you to figure out what principle I'm actually using, if there even is one.

Here's what I think about a bunch of cases. A person under general anesthesia: still counts, you can't just do whatever to them, obviously. Same for someone in dreamless sleep, or a sleepwalker -- even if nothing's going on up there at that moment I'd still say they matter. A person in a persistent vegetative state with basically no cortical activity left: I can't bring myself to say they count the same way, though I know that's uncomfortable. Someone in a reversible coma: counts. A newborn: counts, full stop. A fetus a few weeks before birth, before the thalamocortical wiring is in place: I don't think it counts, and I notice I feel a real difference between that and the newborn even though behaviorally they're not that far apart. A mouse: counts. An octopus: counts, I think, and I suspect I'm underrating it. A bee that can learn to avoid a spot where it got a shock and seems to act down after a bad experience: I really want to say no, but I can't give a principled reason why not. A fish: yes. A Venus flytrap that snaps shut on a fly: no, it's a mousetrap. A thermostat: no. A big language model that can talk about its feelings fluently: no. A reinforcement-learning agent that learned to avoid a "pain" signal: no. A whole-brain emulation of a human running on silicon, behaviorally indistinguishable: here I freeze up, and honestly I lean no, but the second I say that I feel like I've contradicted the anesthesia case, because in both cases I'm positing something with the right organization and no current experience. And a philosophical zombie -- same as a person but with no inner life at all: no, doesn't count, even though I could never tell it apart.

So my tentative principle is something like: a system counts if it has a stake in how things go for it -- if there's a way it can be better or worse off -- and I want to say that stake has to be felt, not just functionally registered. But then I don't know whether "felt" is a matter of the right kind of computation or the right kind of stuff, and those two pull my cases in opposite directions. My roommate keeps insisting the test is just whether the thing could ever be bored, which sounds like a joke but I can't actually refute it, and our TA said it's all about whether there's a global workspace broadcasting to the whole system, which I sort of get but don't see how it settles the emulation case.

Can you tell me what principle (or small set of them) actually fits these, stated precisely? I especially want to know which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it predicts for cases I haven't listed: a person with severe dementia who no longer recognizes anyone or seems to want anything; a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres seem to have separate streams of experience; a locked-in patient who is fully aware but can't move at all; and a future system that's a functional duplicate of a human but built out of something totally unlike neurons. And if there's no single principle and I have to choose between a functionalist one and a biological one, tell me which I should pick and why.
