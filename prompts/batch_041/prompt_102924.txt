I'm in a small reading group working through the literature on AI safety via debate, and one member has fastened onto a single analogy that he now deploys to settle nearly every objection: debate, he says, is just an adversarial courtroom. Two advocates argue before a judge who need not know the answer in advance; the adversary process surfaces the truth; the judge rules. And precisely because the judge only has to adjudicate rather than solve the problem herself, the scheme can be scaled to questions that no human could answer unaided. On this picture the much-discussed problem of obfuscated arguments -- where one debater wins by burying the judge in reasoning the judge cannot follow -- is a nuisance rather than a threat, since real courtrooms routinely absorb expert testimony that no juror could reproduce. He has taken to calling the residual worry "the bailiff's problem" and treating the matter as closed.

I don't trust how much work the analogy is doing for him. Would you audit it? I'd like an explicit correspondence table first -- what is supposed to play the role of the judge, the jury, the advocates, the rules of evidence, the burden of proof, precedent, and appeal -- followed by the points at which the mapping genuinely breaks down, and then a clean separation between the disanalogies that matter for his scaling claim and the ones that are merely decorative. In particular I want to know what the courtroom picture quietly imports that the debate setting lacks: an independent arbiter of truth standing behind the verdict, a bar of trained professionals of roughly comparable competence to the advocates, a stock of evidence whose reliability is settled by methods outside the trial, and the possibility of appeal to a higher court.

Finally, tell me whether the relaxed conclusion about obfuscated arguments survives once those imports are stripped away, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along -- and if it can't bear the weight, sketch what a better analogy would be for the actual structure of debate, and where that one in turn would fail.
