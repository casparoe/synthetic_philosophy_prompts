I run a reading group that has just finished a term on hedonism about well-being, and the objection that stopped everyone cold was the heterogeneity problem: the suspicion that the pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of relief when a headache lifts, the pleasure of finally seeing a proof go through, the pleasure of a friend's good news, the pleasure someone takes in a rival's humiliation, the liking people report for experiences that are aversive while they last, and pleasures nobody notices at the time have nothing in common — in which case pleasure cannot be the single good that hedonism needs it to be. Please build me a sorting instrument for the next session: a decision tree over the logical space of responses, plus a quiz of ten to twelve questions that locates each member of the group in that space. I want them sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they would pick for themselves, so no question may name a theory, a philosopher, or a piece of literature, and none may simply ask whether pleasures have a common felt quality.

Build the tree first from the questions that actually divide the responses, not from a list of views. The branch nodes should include at least: where the unity of pleasure is supposed to live — in the felt character of the experience, in the subject's attitude toward a content, in the causal-functional role of the state, or in the state's presenting something as good; whether unity is required at all for pleasure to be one kind, or whether hedonism can instead be a theory of a family or a disjunction; whether hedonic tone is one property or a determinable with many determinates; and what follows if there is no unity — hedonism refuted, hedonism reformulated, or hedonism abandoned for a different account of welfare. Then give the quiz. Use cases that pull the views apart in different directions and that a reader can answer from the armchair: relief; a pleasure no one notices at the time; a pleasure with no felt location anywhere in the body; two people with the same sensation, one of whom enjoys it; the liking people take in chili, horror films and sad music; and a creature whose pleasures we cannot imagine from the inside. The quiz needs a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions, and the leaves must include the simple-feeling view, the attitudinal view, the view on which pleasure is not an experience at all, the desire-or-liking view, the representationalist view, the functional-role view, the determinable-tone view, the family-resemblance view that keeps hedonism by widening it, and the exit view on which heterogeneity sinks hedonism.

Finally, add a one-page key for me: for each position, the answer pattern, one sentence on what it says pleasures have in common or why the question is misposed, and the case that is most awkward for it. Flag any two leaves the quiz cannot separate and say why, and list the answer patterns that correspond to no position anyone holds, saying for each whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or merely unexplored. No citations or reading lists anywhere — the group has no internet in the room, and I want the instrument to stand on its own.
