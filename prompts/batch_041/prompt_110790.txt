For the better part of a year I have been writing a novel about two mathematicians, and I have finally arrived at the scene I have been putting off: a long argument over a late dinner between a model theorist of about sixty, patient and exact, and a philosopher of mathematics in her early thirties who has lately become convinced that set theory's talk of different sizes of infinity is a provincial idiom rather than a description of anything. The occasion is a lecture they have both just sat through. A colleague, wanting to make a point about formalization, laid out a countable model of the axioms of set theory — every object in the domain written up across the board, every membership fact among them, each axiom checked line by line — and then spent the rest of the hour answering questions on the model's behalf, deriving each answer from the axioms in full view of the audience. Asked whether there are more real numbers than natural numbers, the model, so to speak, said that there are, and produced its proof; and all the while everyone in the room could see that its real numbers had been paired off with its natural numbers, one to one, with nothing left over. My philosopher has come away from this persuaded that the textbook curiosity she was taught to file away under Skolem's paradox — the theorem that any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable one — is not a curiosity at all but the whole truth about set-theoretic size, and that she and the model are in the same predicament: whatever axioms she writes down, the same theorem hands her a countable model of them, so her conviction that there are absolutely more reals than naturals is one that no argument at her disposal can underwrite, and the only difference between her and the model is that she is larger. I want you to write the argument as a dialogue.

Let the philosopher make the strongest case she can that uncountability is a relation between a theory and a model rather than a property of sets — that her word and the model's word mean the same thing, or at any rate that she has no principled basis for claiming that hers means more, and that the fact that we can see the missing bijection in the model's case while nothing stands outside us to see one in ours is a difference in vantage point and not in truth. Let the model theorist resist her without either conceding or taking refuge in mystery: she should be able to say precisely what she thinks the model gets wrong, and why our case is not simply the model's case enlarged — by distinguishing the model's error from a mere gap in its information, or by insisting that we built the thing and can read its interpretation off its construction — and she should then have to face the philosopher's obvious retort that no one built us. Neither of them is a fool; I want the exchanges to answer each other rather than pass each other by.

I would rather the scene get genuinely technical than stay safely general. If the model theorist needs to say what a model's interpretation function is and is not, or why pinning down the intended structure appears to demand more than any first-order axiom system can supply, let her say it; if the philosopher can turn that against her, let her. Have them reason from the case in front of them rather than from the literature: they may invoke an idea, but I don't want them citing papers at each other across the table, and I don't want the difficulty dissolved by a formula. Four or five substantial exchanges, ending with the disagreement sharpened rather than settled, would be ideal.
