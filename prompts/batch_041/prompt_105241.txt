I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I've been asked to contribute one essay question — together with a model answer and a marking scheme — to the shared question bank for an interdisciplinary MA seminar on the ethics of virtual worlds. My colleagues' drafts can all be answered by reciting the familiar positions (fictional characters have no moral status; only actual subjects of experience do; games are representations, so the only parties who can be wronged are the players), and I want a question that can only be answered by someone who can reason through a case. Please use the following as its spine.

A studio releases a game whose non-player characters are generated at runtime by a simulation that, on the designers' own account, reproduces the causal-functional organisation of a human brain at whatever grain one takes to be sufficient for experience, though the characters have no biological body and no evolutionary history. The game permits unlimited save-and-reload, and telemetry shows that most players kill and reload the same character many times over, so that no character's actual history contains the deaths inflicted on it: those deaths occur in branches that are discarded. Separately, the code ships with a few hundred further characters who are fully specified — names, histories, dialogue, an interior monologue — but who are never instantiated in any playthrough, because the branches that would have produced them are unreachable.

Please write (1) the essay question itself, pitched so that a candidate cannot pass by summarising positions and must say what does and does not follow from the stipulations; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically lose that mark. The scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific move the A answer makes that the B answer does not. I want the crux to be whether anything moral turns on a character's being instantiated rather than merely specified, and whether the save-and-reload structure can do the exculpatory work the studio implies it does — but leave the resolution open. The question should not require citations, and it should not depend on any particular author's formulation.
