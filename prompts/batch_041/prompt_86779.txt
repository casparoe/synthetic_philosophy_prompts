I co-teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets, and next week I am presenting a case my co-instructor has not yet seen. I am writing the discussion notes, so I need to anticipate her verdict and the reasoning she will give for it.

Her settled verdicts on earlier cases run as follows.

1. Two risk-averse traders share a common prior about an asset's payoff and each holds private information about it. At the currently quoted price each would be willing to trade, and each knows the other is willing. Her verdict: the trade does not happen, and the explanation is not psychological. She treats the result as structural, a fact about what can be common knowledge rather than about these two people being cautious.

2. Two traders hold different priors about a policy-relevant event, one treating a regime change as fairly likely and the other as nearly impossible, and neither holds information the other lacks. Her verdict: they trade, they may go on trading indefinitely, and calling the disagreement irrational is a mistake. Rationality alone, she says, does not force a common prior.

3. A market in which prices fully reflect every trader's private information. Her verdict: this cannot be an equilibrium, because nobody would pay to acquire information that the price then discloses.

4. A market with informed traders, a substantial volume of demand that does not respond to the asset's value, and arbitrageurs who understand that mispricing may persist. Her verdict: prices depart from fundamentals and stay departed, the arbitrageurs' restraint is rational, and a price is not a clean summary of dispersed information.

5. A public announcement that improves every trader's forecast of a payoff. Her verdict: welfare can fall, because the announcement destroys a risk-sharing opportunity that existed before it.

Next week's case is this. A shipping company wants to shed its exposure to a fuel-price index; its counterparty is a diversified financial firm. The two share a common prior about the index, neither holds information the other lacks, each knows the other is willing to transact at the currently quoted price, and that mutual willingness is common knowledge. The shipping company's motive is public: it is hedging an operating exposure, not expressing a view. In the same instrument there is also a modest volume of order flow from participants whose demand does not respond to the index's value and who rebalance on fixed calendar dates.

What I need from you: her verdict on whether the trade occurs; her verdict on whether the price departs from the two parties' common expectation of the index and whether she reads any such departure as evidence about their beliefs; whether she would call the resulting trade speculative and the resulting price informative; which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog; the distinct rules her track record is jointly consistent with, and what each of those rules predicts here; which rule she is most likely running, with your reasons; and the further case that would separate the surviving rules. Flag any place where one of her earlier verdicts would have to be reinterpreted if it turns out not to mean what it appears to mean. Citations are not needed; she grades on the argument.
