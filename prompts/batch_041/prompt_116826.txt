I have been reading a popular book on decision-making, and its chapter on information opens with a summary of Good's theorem that I would like audited. The author writes that the theorem answers the old question whether it can ever be rational to prefer not to know something, and that the answer is a clean no. The argument, as he gives it, is this: suppose you must choose among some acts, and suppose that before choosing you have the opportunity to make an observation — any observation, perfect or partial — that costs you nothing and whose result you are free to act upon; then the expected utility of observing first and afterwards choosing the best act still available is never lower than the expected utility of simply choosing the best act now, in ignorance. He stresses that this holds whatever the observation turns out to reveal and however coarse or fine the evidence may be, that it is why economists say information is always valuable and why a rational agent will never decline free evidence, and that it finally disposes of the romantic notion that some truths are better left unknown — since there is no state of affairs in which learning the truth leaves you worse off than remaining ignorant.

The closing claim strikes me as too strong, but I cannot locate the slip, and I am inclined to believe it because it reads like a proof. Please do three things. First, state as precisely as you can what the theorem actually establishes, what it assumes, and what sort of thing it is a theorem about. Second, go through the author's claims one at a time and separate those a decision theorist would endorse from those that are overstatements, conflations, or simply false, noting any place where the formulation is technically defensible but invites a wrong reading. Third, say whether the moral about ignorance follows from the theorem or from something else, and test it against a few cases: a patient who would rather not learn a prognosis; evidence that is entirely irrelevant to the decision; evidence that is cheap but not free; a case in which, having observed, one ends up worse off than one would have been acting in ignorance; and a strategic case in which others' choices depend on what you know. I would rather have the errors identified than have the conclusion shored up.
