I'm a graduate student in the philosophy of social science, and I've been arguing with a friend in sociology about whether culture can be a cause at all. Our disagreement has crystallized around Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, which I take to be the canonical attempt to explain a large-scale social outcome by appeal to a set of values, and which my friend insists only looks like a causal explanation because nobody ever says precisely what the values are supposed to be doing. To force the issue she has proposed sorting the possible causal roles of culture into three, and she wants me to say which one Weber is actually running. On the first role, culture is a motive: internalized values are among the proximate causes of individual action, so that an ascetic Protestant works relentlessly because diligence in his calling is bound up with the assurance of grace he craves. On the second, culture is a filter: values do not move anyone but determine which actions are so much as available or thinkable, so that the effect shows up in the shape of the option set rather than in the strength of any desire. On the third, culture is a selector: values enter only as part of the environment in which ordinary self-interested agents get sorted, so that a practice spreads because it is rewarded, or because a certain belief makes it the salient equilibrium, and not because anyone is motivated by the value itself. She claims that Weber's own evidence can support only the second or the third role, that it is the first that makes the thesis look at once unfalsifiable and psychologically implausible, and that once we see this the usual objections simply lose their force. I'm not convinced, and I suspect the taxonomy is doing less work than she thinks.

Please act as a philosopher of social science who knows both the Weber literature and the general problem of causal explanation in the human sciences, and reason this through carefully before answering. Begin by getting clear on what each of the three roles actually commits you to -- what each predicts about individual motivation, what each would count as a disconfirming case, and whether in each the value is doing causal work or merely marking a correlation that some other mechanism produces. Then compare the three-role picture with what Weber's argument really does: which role, or combination of roles, best captures the strategy of the Protestant ethic thesis, whether the thesis can be stated without the motive role at all, and whether the filter and selector readings are genuinely different explanations or two descriptions of one thing.

Finally, tell me whether the taxonomy is a real advance on the standard complaints -- that value-explanations are unfalsifiable, that they smuggle in the very behavior they were meant to explain, and that they confuse an explanation by reasons with an explanation by causes -- or whether it is mostly a relabeling that leaves those complaints exactly where they were. I care less about a verdict on Weber than about whether the three roles are genuinely distinct, which of them can be turned into a falsifiable claim, and which one a defender of the thesis should be pushed to adopt. No need for citations or a reading list; I know the texts.
