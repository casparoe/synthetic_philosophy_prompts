Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about what counts as a work of art and got nowhere, so I want to put my verdicts on paper and ask what principle could possibly lie behind them. I am not asking what some philosopher said; I want to know what, if anything, my own reactions add up to, and what I would have to give up in order to make them consistent.

Here is what I think, case by case.

A piece of driftwood that someone picks up on a beach, mounts on a plinth, and exhibits under a title: art. The identical piece of driftwood lying unregarded on the beach: not art.

A porcelain urinal submitted to a sculpture exhibition, signed, and set on a pedestal: art. The same model of urinal, indistinguishable, sitting in a plumbing-supply aisle: not art.

A forged old-master painting, made expressly to deceive and now exposed as a forgery: still art. A child's accidental paint spill that happens to resemble a masterpiece: not art.

An image generated by a text-to-image model, selected by a curator and hung in a gallery with the curator's wall label: art. An image from the same model, generated and then deleted by the person prompting it, never seen by anyone: not art.

A poem whose author insists it is only a private note and refuses to call it art, but which is published and read: art.

A recording of an empty concert hall — no music, only the room — released as an album: art.

A master perfumer's fragrance, composed with as much care and sold for as much money as any gallery object: not art.

A rock formation that erosion has shaped into something indistinguishable from a sculpture: not art.

A mathematical proof of unusual elegance: not art.

My friend's principle is that something is art exactly when a human made it with the intention that it be regarded as art. That handles most of my list, but not the forgery, which I insist is art even though the forger's intention was to deceive rather than to make art, and not the poem, which I call art even though the poet disclaims any such intention. So intention alone will not do.

I have a competing instinct of my own: that art is whatever is put forward to an audience as a candidate for appreciation. That saves the readymades, the gallery image, and the published poem, but it seems to make the perfume art too, and I refuse to count the perfume. It also leaves me unsure how much work the framing is doing, since the driftwood was art, I think, the moment it was mounted, before any audience saw it.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets all of these or nearly all of them? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should surrender that one rather than patch the principle, and say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed — a photograph of that rock formation, and a copy of the forged painting run off on a high-quality printer.
