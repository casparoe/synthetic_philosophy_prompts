I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I will state my question as plainly as I can. I have been reading about computational complexity, and I keep noticing that the word hard does different work in different parts of the subject. I would like you to place the following notions of hardness into tiers according to how well each one supports a single claim: when we say that a problem is hard, we are describing the problem itself, not merely describing our machines, our algorithms, or our own ignorance.

- Worst-case hardness: no algorithm solves every instance of the problem within a given resource bound.
- Average-case hardness: no algorithm solves a typical instance, where typicality is fixed by a specified input distribution.
- Smoothed hardness: the problem remains hard even when inputs are randomly perturbed by a small amount, so that the difficulty cannot be dismissed as an artifact of a few pathological instances.
- Inapproximability: not only is finding an exact solution hard, but so is finding any solution within a fixed factor of the best possible one.
- Unconditional lower bounds: proofs that no algorithm of a specified shape (for instance, circuits of a given size) computes the function, obtained without relying on any unproven assumption.
- Kolmogorov incompressibility: an object is hard in the sense that no program substantially shorter than the object itself produces it.
- Information-theoretic hardness: the difficulty comes from missing information rather than from limited computation, as with a one-time pad.

Group items that seem equally strong in the same tier, order the tiers from strongest support to weakest, and give one or two sentences per item explaining what the notion is relative to (a machine model, an input distribution, an approximation factor, an adversary's information) and why that helps or hurts the claim. If the ordering comes out differently depending on which sense of objectivity you adopt, tell me where the split falls. I am also curious which of the seven is most often overrated by practitioners who have not studied complexity theory.
