For the past year I have been defending a particular version of luck egalitarianism in a reading group, and I have reached the point where I need it stress-tested rather than restated. Stated as sharply as I can manage, the view has two parts. First, the site of egalitarian concern is the option set a person faces rather than the outcome she ends up with: justice requires that people confront equally valuable menus of accessible life-plans, and once the menus are equal, what a person does with hers — including choices that leave her destitute — is her own business, not a matter for redistribution. Second, the brute-luck/option-luck line is drawn ex ante, by the fairness of the menu, not ex post, by the causal history of a given loss. So a loss that was a live possibility from an equally good menu is not compensable even if it is catastrophic, while a loss traceable to a menu worse than other people's is compensable even if the agent chose the option that produced it. I am aware this puts a lot of weight on the phrase "equally good menus," and I suspect that is where the view is most exposed, but I would rather hear the whole map than my own guess about it.

What I want from you is a candid inventory of what would count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing the objections that a committed defender would have to concede as genuine costs from those she could reasonably absorb or redescribe. Include the following where you think they bite: what the view predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, that it should be indifferent between two societies with identical ex ante menus and wildly divergent ex post distributions of welfare — and whether any of those predictions are ones I should be embarrassed to affirm; whether the ex ante framing is even well-defined when agents face uncertainty rather than measurable risk, that is, when no probability distribution over outcomes is available to them to compute a menu's value, and if it is not well-defined, whether that is a fixable technical gap or evidence that the view is only half a theory; whether the view collapses into one of its rivals — an insurance-based approach that compensates bad outcomes after all, a Rawlsian fair-equality-of-opportunity view, or plain prioritarian welfarism — and what exactly would have to be true for the collapse to be real rather than rhetorical; and the internal problem of conversion: if people convert identical menus into very different levels of well-being because of differences in capacity, temperament, or social position, does the view have to equalize menus of options-as-converted, and if so has it not smuggled welfare back in as the currency?

Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether the position risks being unfalsifiable — whether every apparent counterexample can be met by adjusting what counts as an equally good menu, so that the theory never loses a case — and, if that is what is happening, whether it is a defect or simply what any sufficiently abstract view of distributive justice must look like. Tell me what I would have to observe, or what argument I would have to accept, to give the view up, and separately tell me which surviving version of it you think is strongest; I would rather be told that the view is well insulated and that this is or is not a problem than be handed a list of objections I already know how to answer.
