I am a high school student, and for a philosophy club presentation I worked out the following argument. I am now suspicious of it, because it seems to show that a large part of ordinary practical reasoning — trusting reviews, deferring to expert consensus, going along with a crowd — is irrational, and that conclusion strikes me as too strong to be true. I would like help identifying which step to abandon and what abandoning it would cost me.

(1) Suppose several people decide in sequence whether some state of affairs holds — whether a new textbook is any good, whether a defendant is guilty, whether a restaurant is worth visiting — and each of them possesses a small piece of private evidence that is, on average, accurate. Each person also observes the decisions of everyone who decided before her.

(2) For at least some such people, it is rational to set aside their own evidence and choose whatever the earlier decision-makers have chosen, once the earlier choices are numerous and unanimous enough.

(3) A person who decides in that way is not registering her own evidence in her choice; her choice is a copy of the choices before it.

(4) Therefore, once copying of this kind has set in, the choices that follow add nothing: the sequence of decisions contains no information about the underlying state beyond what was already contained in the decisions of the handful of people who acted before the copying began.

(5) So anyone who treats such a sequence — a run of identical verdicts, a bestseller list, a market price, an entire peer group's adoption of some product — as evidence about the underlying state is relying on a signal no more informative than the private evidence of the first two or three people in the line.

(6) Since those first two or three people are as fallible as anyone, a rational observer should give the crowd's verdict no more weight than she gives the private evidence of two or three individuals.

(7) But this cannot be right. A large, diverse, independently formed consensus — a prediction market's price, a well-run jury, a replicated experimental result — plainly deserves more weight than the private evidence of two or three people; that is why we consult such things at all.

(8) Therefore (2) is false: it is not rational to copy the crowd, even when the crowd is large and unanimous.

I am confident about (7) and reluctant to surrender it. What I cannot see is whether the argument is simply valid and one of its premises must be given up, or whether a single step is doing illicit work. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I keep (2), do I have to accept the conclusion about markets and polls that I would rather not accept? If I give up (2), can I still explain why anyone ever sensibly follows a crowd? Please lay out the options rather than merely naming a step, and say which premises looked fine before I started pulling on them.
