I cover science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and for the past two years I have also served as the public member of the intergenerational stewardship panel of our state's higher-education endowment, a fund of roughly nine billion dollars chartered to support teaching and research in perpetuity. I recuse myself from votes but sit on the drafting subgroup, and on Thursday the full panel votes on a standing methodology for ranking capital projects. The case in front of us has become a proxy for a larger quarrel. Project A is a deferred-maintenance program whose benefits fall almost entirely within ten years. Project B is the purchase and permanent protection of a forested watershed, whose measurable returns, in water quality and carbon storage, accrue mostly between 2100 and 2200. Under the real discount rate we have used for years, five percent, B fails on any defensible benefit stream, and it fails more decisively the further out you push the horizon. Two members now propose replacing that rate with a sliding scale: five percent for costs and benefits falling inside the lifetimes of current stakeholders, tapering to one and a half percent for anything past roughly 2065, on the argument that a flat rate silently encodes a preference for our own convenience over the welfare of people who cannot attend the meeting. A third member wants a uniformly lower flat rate instead, on the grounds that the taper is an unprincipled fudge that will be gamed by whoever writes the benefit projections, and that our fiduciary duty runs to identifiable beneficiaries rather than to a statistical posterity. A fourth wants to keep five percent and simply exclude from consideration any benefit we cannot monetize within thirty years, which I suspect is the same decision wearing a procedural costume.

The conceptual knot, as I understand it, is that the five percent is doing at least four different jobs at once: it reflects a genuine preference for present over future consumption, it prices the opportunity cost of capital, it hedges the fact that we may be wrong about the future, and it tracks the expectation that future people will simply be richer than we are and so need less from us per dollar. Only the first of those is what people mean when they say that discounting future welfare is a form of discrimination by date of birth, and I cannot tell from the debate whether the panel is disagreeing about that or about the other three. One member has proposed what she calls a symmetry test: we may apply a lower rate to distant benefits than to near ones only if we can state a reason that a person alive in 2200 could not equally cite in favor of their own further descendants, which would presumably rule out the pure-preference component but might leave the uncertainty and growth components standing. Another member objects that this proves too much, since it would also forbid any policy that treats near and far differently for reasons of administrative practicality, and he keeps raising the non-identity problem to argue that people in 2200 would not exist in the form they will if we choose differently, so the language of harm to them is confused. I would like the drafting subgroup's written opinion to recommend a concrete course of action with actual numbers, and to engage these objections seriously rather than restating the case for long-term stewardship.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate to the panel before Thursday's meeting. It should open with a clear recommendation, stated in the first paragraph: whether to adopt the sliding scale, a flat rate, or some third option, and at what specific values, with a transition rule for projects already in the pipeline. It should then do three things. First, separate the four jobs the discount rate is performing and say which of them can legitimately justify treating a dollar of benefit in 2150 as worth less than a dollar of benefit in 2030, and which cannot, since I think the panel is talking past itself here. Second, take the strongest version of the case against the recommendation, which I take to be the gaming-and-fiduciary-duty objection rather than the non-identity point, and explain honestly what would have to be true for that objection to defeat the recommendation. Third, address the practical question of how the panel should handle the uncertainty argument, since the same reasoning that licenses a declining rate also licenses an arbitrarily low one if you are pessimistic enough, and we need a principled stopping point rather than a number that reflects whoever is in the room. Please keep it to about two pages, avoid footnotes and literature references, and end with the exact motion language the chair can put to a vote, including what the panel should do if the vote fails.
