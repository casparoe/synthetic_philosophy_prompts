Here's something that's been bugging me and I can't get it to sit still. Take two things you can publish about a person.

(1) You publish a damaging falsehood about someone — say you report that a local businessman embezzled from a charity, and it isn't true. That's defamation, he can sue, and if he wins he gets money. But almost no court will stop you from publishing it in the first place. Courts in the common-law world are famously unwilling to enjoin a defamatory publication; the remedy is damages afterwards, not an order beforehand. The lie goes out, and the correction comes later and costs money.

(2) You publish something true that the person wanted kept private and that isn't genuinely of public concern — a medical condition, a sexual history, the contents of a confidential conversation. Same act type, a publication that wrecks someone's life, except now what you said is true, and here the law is much more willing to act, including beforehand: injunctions against publishing private information are routine in some jurisdictions, and the whole tort (or tort-equivalent) exists precisely for true disclosures. Meanwhile, if you say the same damaging thing about the same person and it happens to be true, defamation gives him nothing at all — truth is a complete defense, so a true statement that destroys his reputation isn't actionable, full stop.

So: lies about someone are protected going forward but compensable afterwards; truths about someone can be stopped before they ever appear, but only if they're private; and truths that damage someone's reputation without invading privacy are just... fine. The law seems to care more about a secret than about a reputation, and it's more willing to silence the truth than the lie.

I'm fairly sure this isn't what Mill's harm principle would predict. If harm is the trigger, both cases look like harm, and truth looks irrelevant — Mill's position is that we put up with false and pernicious speech because open contestation, not suppression, is supposed to be how error gets corrected. So either the practice is inconsistent with the harm principle, or it's resting on something else entirely.
