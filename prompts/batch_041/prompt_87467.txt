You are a philosopher of cognitive science whose specialty is the conceptual foundations of computational explanation: someone who has spent years on what it takes for a physical system to implement a computation, and who is known for distinctions that survive contact with actual cases rather than with slogans. I am assembling materials for a reading group on computationalism and I want a decision tree, not an essay.

Begin by thinking on paper. Before writing the tree itself, spend a short section enumerating the independent questions that a person's answer to "is the brain a computer?" actually turns on — the implementation question, the explanatory question, the individuation question, and whatever else you judge to be genuinely orthogonal — and work out which combinations of answers are coherent. Start from that logical space, then locate the positions inside it; do not start from a list of positions and reverse-engineer questions to reach them. If two questions turn out not to be independent, say so and restructure the tree.

Deliverable: a single decision tree for the reading group, of roughly ten to fourteen branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Every node must be a question answerable by someone who does not already know the labels — concrete cases, yes/no or three-way commitments, no question of the form "do you think the mind is a computer?". Number the nodes and give each leaf an identifier, a name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on the case in the set below that makes it hardest to hold.

Use these cases, and make at least four of them do real branching work:

1. A spreadsheet full of formulas that is saved and never reopened — does it contain computations, or only the possibility of them?
2. A river delta whose water flow can be mapped onto the equations of drainage, and a household thermostat — the same question, or two different ones?
3. A trained image classifier whose weights no one can read off as an algorithm — does it compute, or does it merely have a computational description nobody has yet found?
4. A cortical region whose activity can be carved into "computations" in indefinitely many ways depending on the temporal grain you choose.
5. A switched-off calculator whose wiring already fixes its input-output mapping.
6. A fluent typist who can no longer say how she does it.

Leaves that must appear: the view that the brain computes and that this is what explains cognition; the view that the brain computes but that the computational description explains nothing and merely redescribes; the view that the brain does not literally compute but is usefully modeled as if it did; the view that implementation is cheap — that almost any physical system implements almost any computation, so the thesis is trivially true and empty; the view that computation is fixed by mechanism rather than by the observer's mapping; the view that it is fixed by the mapping rather than the mechanism; and the view that the question is verbal because "computer" names a functional kind with no fact of the matter.

Also: flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and note any unoccupied combinations of answers, diagnosing whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If you think one of my six cases is not diagnostic — that it cannot pull any two positions apart — say so and propose a replacement. Write no citations, no reading lists, and no names of authors; define any term of art in a clause as you use it, and assume the reader has no internet access. Close with the tree in indented outline form, followed by a compact table of the leaves.
