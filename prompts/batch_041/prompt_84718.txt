I have no formal training in philosophy — I write scheduling and resource-allocation software — and I have been stuck for a week on an argument a colleague constructed during a design disagreement. I would like you to diagnose it: tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

The argument grew out of a real decision in my work. We can either ship a configuration that adds about a millisecond of delay to every user's request, or a configuration that occasionally mishandles one user's job so badly that the job is lost. My colleague claimed that for a large enough user base the first option is the worse one, because a millisecond is a small harm and, multiplied across enough people, its total outweighs the one lost job. When I objected that a lost job is surely worse than a millisecond of everyone's time, he offered the following argument, which I have written out as cleanly as I can.

Let us rank outcomes by how good they are, and treat the badness of an outcome as a quantity, larger being worse. (I think of this as a loss function; that is how the whole thing started.)

(1) Every outcome can be compared with every other: one is at least as good as the other.
(2) "At least as good as" is transitive.
(3) A single dust speck in one person's eye makes an outcome worse than the same outcome without it, and the amount by which it is worse is finite — a small positive badness.
(4) Torturing one person for fifty years is worse than a single dust speck, and its badness is also finite.
(5) No finite badness is so large that no finite multiple of a smaller finite badness can reach it: enough repetitions of a small finite badness exceed any larger finite badness.
(6) Therefore there is a number N such that the outcome in which N people each receive one dust speck is worse than the outcome in which one person is tortured for fifty years.
(7) But it is absurd that a crowd of minor annoyances should be worse than a torture.

He concludes that one of (1)–(5) must be false, and that the culprit is the assumption that badness can be totalled across people at all — which would also sink the scheduling argument he began with, since that argument totalled milliseconds across users in exactly the same way. He presses the point further: he says the same reasoning holds even if the delay is so small that no user could ever notice it — a harm below the threshold of experience — which strikes me as the hardest version of the absurdity to swallow.

I cannot find the slip. Steps (1) and (2) look like common sense, (3) and (4) are the setup, and (5) looks like arithmetic. Yet I would give up almost anything rather than (7). I keep suspecting the move from (5) to (6), but I cannot say why (5) would not simply carry over.

Please tell me whether the argument is valid, so that I must choose a premise to abandon, or whether a step only looks fine — a place where a word is doing two jobs, or where a move that is legitimate within a single life is silently reused across different lives. If the argument is valid, price the escape routes for me: for each premise I could drop, what else I lose, and which is least costly. If it is not valid, name the step, explain why it looked legitimate, and tell me what the argument would need in order to be repaired. Throughout, be as self-critical as the material allows: state plainly what would change your verdict, and identify the point in your own reasoning where you would least trust it.
