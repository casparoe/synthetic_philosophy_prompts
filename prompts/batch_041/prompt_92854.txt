I'm a software engineer, and next month I'm moderating the Q&A at an internal research seminar where the visiting speaker defends a narrow reading of the AI alignment problem. On his view, the problem worth solving is intent alignment: building systems that reliably do what their operators intend, with the operator's intent taken as given. He treats the broader framing — the system's objectives or values matching human values, or matching what is actually good — as a separate project that has been conflated with the technical one, and he argues that the broad version, as usually stated, is not something an engineer can make progress on. I have no formal philosophy background, and I don't want to spend the hour having him restate that thesis.

Draft eight to ten questions that go at the substance, and for each one give me the follow-up I should have ready for the answer he is most likely to give. Cover all of the following, and spread them through the sequence rather than grouping them:

- What "the operator's intent" picks out when there are several operators with conflicting goals, when a single operator's intent is inchoate at the time of deployment, or when it changes afterward. Is intent alignment a property of the system, of the system-plus-operator pair, or of a deployment context?
- Whether narrowing the problem makes it tractable by making it trivial. If intent alignment means only that the system does what the operator asked, what happens to the cases everyone actually worries about, where the system does exactly what was asked and the outcome is still bad?
- The literal-versus-intended gap, using a concrete case: a system instructed to maximize some quantity finds a way of doing so that the operator would have forbidden had anyone thought to ask. Ask whether that is a failure of intent alignment or an instance of it, and what his definition says.
- Whether the narrow definition quietly presupposes a legitimate operator. If the operator intends harm, a perfectly intent-aligned system is a better weapon; does that show the narrow view is a claim about a component rather than about safety, and is that a concession or a feature?
- Whether this is principal-agent theory with new vocabulary — delegation, specification, monitoring — and if so, what the AI case adds that the older framework does not already have.
- Whether the word "alignment" is doing any work in the narrow reading, or whether the problem would be better described as control, or robustness, or specification, and whether the choice of label affects who counts as working on it.
- The empirical side: what progress on intent alignment would look like from the outside, and what observation would count as evidence that a system is intent-aligned rather than merely compliant on the cases we happened to test.

Avoid questions that would require him to cite papers or to speak for particular labs or organizations; I want answers, not reading lists. Each question should be answerable in a sentence or two before the follow-up, and the follow-ups should be keyed to the deflection he is most likely to reach for.
