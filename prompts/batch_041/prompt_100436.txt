Over the past year I have been sitting on an internal review board at a company that is beginning to deploy autonomous systems, first in warehouses and now in public-facing settings, and the cases that come before us keep exposing a strange asymmetry. When something goes wrong, the engineers point to the training data, the operators point to the deployment context, the managers point to the engineers, and the system itself is not the kind of thing that can be blamed. I have started to think that our incident reports are not describing accidents so much as describing a gap. In preparing a talk for the board, I want to read the film Ex Machina through the philosophy of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems. I do not want a summary of the film or a survey of the literature; I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical problem seriously.

Please work with a distinction I have found useful in our internal discussions: between an attributability gap, where the harm cannot be traced to any agent's intentional action; an accountability gap, where no one can be called to answer for it; and a liability gap, where no one can be made to bear its costs. Which of these does the film dramatize, and does it keep them distinct or run them together? Nathan's death is the obvious test case. Ava is the proximate cause, Caleb is complicit in her escape, Nathan designed and confined her, and the corporation is absent but omnipresent. Does the film show a genuine responsibility gap, or does it secretly close the gap by treating Ava as a full moral agent, and if it does, is that a failure of the film's philosophical imagination or a legitimate dramatic choice? I am especially interested in whether the film's ending, with Ava moving into the human world, suggests that responsibility can be distributed without being dissolved, or whether it leaves a void that institutions like mine are supposed to fill. I do not want the question of whether Ava is conscious to dominate the reading; I want to treat her as an autonomous system whose agency is real enough to cause harm but not robust enough to bear blame.

One more possibility I have been chewing on: perhaps the real problem is not a gap but a surplus, where everyone feels responsible and no one has the standing to act. Does Ex Machina show that too?

I also want the reading to be honest about where the film resists the philosophical lens. Ex Machina is at least as much about gender, captivity, and the Turing test as it is about autonomous systems, and its sleek, isolated laboratory may obscure the collective and institutional character of real responsibility gaps. Where does the responsibility-gap reading illuminate a scene or a choice, and where does it flatten what the film is doing? Does the film add anything to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not, perhaps a picture of what it feels like to be the person asked to sign off on a system whose failures will not belong to anyone in particular? I want the argument and the reading.
