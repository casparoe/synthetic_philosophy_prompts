A colleague argues that the two Koreas are the closest thing social science has to a controlled experiment: one people, one language, one terrain, one history up to the division in 1945, and then two very different sets of institutions on either side of a line drawn by outside powers for their own administrative convenience. On his telling, the gap in living standards between them is therefore a clean estimate of the causal effect of institutions, and geography and culture can be set aside precisely because the division held them fixed. I am suspicious, but I cannot say exactly where the argument fails. Audit the analogy: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of randomisation, of treatment, of the control arm, of blinding, of the outcome measure — then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusion he draws from the ones that are merely colourful, and finally tell me whether his conclusion survives once they are on the table, namely that institutions rather than geography or culture explain why some nations are rich. Note that the same move is made with other divided or paired neighbours, and that the cases do not obviously point the same way. If the analogy is past saving, say what would serve better. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
