I am preparing a prospectus for a one-semester research paper (roughly 8,000 words) in the philosophy of language, and I would like help turning a half-formed idea into a proper proposal. The idea is this. The familiar Twin Earth construction is normally read as establishing that the content of a natural-kind term is fixed by the environment rather than by anything inside the speaker's head. But the construction moves two things at once, and I suspect the debate has drawn a confidence from that coincidence that the cases themselves do not license. The first thing is contact: whether the individual speaker, or anyone she has actually interacted with, has been causally acquainted with instances of the kind. The second is practice: what the community's norms of use are anchored in — which objects the word was originally introduced for, and how the deference structure of the community runs. In the standard case the speaker belongs to precisely the community whose practice is anchored in the very environment she inhabits, so contact, practice, and original anchor all point the same way, and rival positions that ought to diverge deliver identical verdicts. The disagreement then looks smaller and better understood than it is.

I want to design cases in which the three come apart, restricting myself to natural-kind terms, and to ask which of them the content actually tracks. The candidate cases I have so far are these. First, a community whose word was anchored, generations ago, in a region it has since abandoned, so that the speakers' present environment, the community's anchoring history, and any individual speaker's contact all diverge. Second, a community on a world containing no instances of the kind at all, whose word is sustained entirely by testimony, theory, and instrument readings of traces — is such a term empty, indeterminate, or fully contentful by virtue of the practice alone? Third, a deference chain that terminates in an authority who turns out never to have existed, so that the anchor is a fiction while the practice is robust and stable.

Turn this into a proposal. I want the question stated so that it has a determinate answer, together with a clear account of why the existing positions leave it open rather than settling it. I want the method described concretely enough that I could begin tomorrow: how the cases are built as minimal pairs, what predictions each position is committed to before I look at the cases, how I check that the verdicts are driven by semantics rather than by pragmatics, and what becomes of the cases when they are embedded in belief and quotation contexts. I want a statement of what a real result would look like, and of what would count as the project failing — including the failure mode in which every position accommodates every case with a small epicycle, and the one in which I have merely rediscovered the internalism–externalism dispute under new labels. Finally, give me a realistic plan for a single semester, with an honest assessment of which part is likely to prove too large. I do not need a literature review or a bibliography; refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will attach the citations myself.
