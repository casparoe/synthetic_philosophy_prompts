I am grading the final exam for an upper-level epistemology course, and I would like a careful, part-by-part assessment of one student's answer. The question and the answer are reproduced below.

Question. Suppose that a subject knows that P. Consider the following reasoning: since I know that P, P is true; so any evidence that appears to tell against P cannot be genuine counter-evidence but must be misleading evidence, that is, evidence that points away from the truth; and since it is no part of rationality to be moved by misleading evidence, I may set such evidence aside. Generalising: once I know that P, I may rationally disregard any evidence against P that turns up later. (a) Set out this reasoning in its strongest form, making every step explicit. (b) Identify the step that is most vulnerable to objection and state the best objection to it. (c) Does the reasoning show that a subject who knows that P may rationally ignore all later evidence against P? Explain.

Student's answer. (a) The reasoning has three steps. First, knowledge entails truth: if I know that P, then P is true. Second, if P is true, then any evidence E that appears to count against P must be misleading, that is, false, or at any rate not genuinely evidence about P. Third, rationality requires us to proportion our belief to the evidence, and misleading evidence is not evidence, so I may set E aside. The generalisation is immediate: once I know that P, later contrary evidence may be set aside too.

(b) The vulnerable step is the third. The argument moves from E would be misleading to I may ignore E, and that move is illegitimate, because rationality requires us to respond to all the evidence we have; we are never entitled simply to set a piece of evidence aside. At most, new counter-evidence must lower our confidence a little. Nothing can license treating it as weightless.

(c) So the reasoning does not establish that a subject who knows may ignore later counter-evidence; at most it shows that a subject who is certain may. And the conclusion would be stranger than paradoxical: to insist on remaining open to counter-evidence is already to concede that one does not know, so knowledge and open-mindedness could not be combined at all. Since the third step fails, we can keep both.
