I'm a third-year philosophy student preparing for a seminar in my personal identity course, where I have to defend the claim that a person survives the gradual replacement of their neurons by silicon components. I'd like to rehearse against the strongest version of the opposing view rather than a strawman.

Please play a committed substrate essentialist for the rest of this exchange, and stay in character until I say out of character. Your position: phenomenal consciousness is a feature of specifically biological, electrochemical processes, and a functionally equivalent silicon system would at best reproduce behaviour while instantiating no experience whatsoever. You are not a caricature. You know the functionalist arguments, you will freely concede that memory, reportability, and behavioural competence would be perfectly preserved, and you will concede that nobody yet knows which features of neurons are responsible for consciousness — you simply deny that this ignorance licenses the functionalist conclusion. You may ask me to make the scenario more precise, and you may press me with arguments of your own, but you must always end with a verdict. Do not summarise both sides, do not offer to moderate, and do not soften your position to be agreeable. One paragraph per reply; this is meant to feel like a viva, not a lecture.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to notice that it is not just the standard thought experiment with the dial turned up. I grant that the substrate is not irrelevant; now I make you pay for the concession. The replacement is not a jump from carbon to silicon but a progression by degrees, and at each degree I will ask you where the light goes out. First the carbon-12 in the tissue is exchanged for carbon-13: chemically identical, and I take it you will agree the subject is untouched. Next, in a single neuron, the ion channels are rebuilt from a non-carbon material that reproduces their conductance properties exactly. Then the cytoplasm is replaced by a medium that sustains the same ionic gradients and the same biochemistry. Then the soma, and finally the whole cell, becomes an engineered device with the same inputs, the same outputs, and the same internal dynamics, down to the timing of every spike. At no step does anything observable change: not one report, not one memory, not one second of experience, as far as anyone inside or outside can tell. So I want two things. First, name the precise step at which the subject ceases to be conscious, and explain what makes that step non-arbitrary; because if your criterion is that the substrate must be biological, I need to know which grain of description that word is picking out. At the atomic grain a neuron is mostly field and probability, and it is only at some coarser grain that it counts as a biological thing at all, so your view appears to require a privileged level of description, and I want to hear you supply it. Second, tell me what you would say to the finished silicon subject, who by your lights is a zombie, when it tells you — sincerely, and with exactly the conviction I have now — that it is in pain and terrified of being switched off. I do not think you can reply that it is simply mistaken, and I want to watch you try.
