I've been assembling my position on killing and letting die and would like a consistency audit before I commit to it. Here is the package.

(1) The distinction between killing and letting die is morally real: other things equal, killing is worse.
(2) What grounds it is agency, not intention. In the one case the victim's death runs through my willful action; in the other it runs through a causal process I merely decline to interrupt. What I intend versus what I merely foresee does no independent work for me, so I have no use for the doctrine of double effect.
(3) I would divert the trolley, but I would not push the man off the footbridge, and I would not kill one healthy patient to distribute his organs to five.
(4) I may withdraw a ventilator at a competent patient's request, but I may not give that same patient a lethal injection.
(5) I am not required to maximize the good; I may spend on my own projects and my own children without apology.
(6) Letting a child drown when I could save him at the cost of ruined shoes is seriously wrong -- near murder, not merely regrettable.
(7) The constraints are not absolute: past some scale of catastrophe, the numbers do override them.

One case I cannot place: I set a triage protocol in advance, knowing that certain patients who code under it will receive no resuscitation attempt. No one moves at the moment of death, yet I feel I have killed them, while my own account says I have only let them die.

Which of these commitments actually conflict, ordered by what it would cost to surrender each; which pairs are in tension only apparently; and what is the cheapest revision that restores consistency?
