I'm putting together a fake corporate website for a zine issue about ethics getting productized, and I need you to write the FAQ page for a company called MaximCheck. The pitch: you subscribe, you type in what you're thinking of doing, and the service tells you whether the rule behind it could be a rule that everyone acts on. Basically the Kantian universalizability test sold as a SaaS product. Deadpan corporate voice the whole way through, no winking, no "isn't this absurd" asides from the narrator — the absurdity has to be load-bearing. Around 700-800 words, numbered Q&As, and feel free to throw in a small pricing table. You can name-drop Kant in the marketing copy, that's fine.

The funny has to come out of the actual machinery of the test rather than made-up silliness, so someone who teaches this stuff should wince in recognition instead of getting annoyed at a strawman. Some things I definately want in there:

- The maxim-specification problem. Customers type their own maxim in, and the verdict swings wildly depending on how they word it. So the tiers are priced around exactly that: the cheap plan checks the maxim as written, so something like "I will break this one promise, to Dave, on a Tuesday" comes back with a green checkmark, and the expensive plan has a human re-describe your maxim at what the company calls the right level of generality. Support should be completely unbothered about this. Something like: "This is a known behaviour. It is not a defect. Please see the refund policy."

- Contradiction in conception versus contradiction in will. The basic plan only catches the cases where the universalized maxim can't even be thought through to the end — the false-promise kind of case. There's an upgrade tier for the ones where nothing is strictly impossible but you can't coherently will it as a general arrangement, i.e. the never-help-anyone-anytime maxim. Give that tier an unpleasantly corporate name. The support agent should explain the difference in a way that's technically correct and also sounds like a phone contract.

- False positives. The service cheerfully passes maxims that universalize just fine and are still awful. Support's line: we're a consistency check, not a moral oracle, and if you want a moral oracle please see our competitors, SLA is on page 3.

- Non-rational beings. The engine returns "indeterminate" for any maxim whose scope is animals or anything else outside the rational-agency club, and there's an add-on you can buy that resolves it, and the add-on's description is evasive about what it actually does. Play it straight.

- At least one angry customer ticket where the agent's reply is that yes, the maxim is universalizable, and that this is the customer's problem, not the company's.

Also: the company has internal nicknames for its edge cases — make up two or three and use them consistently, one for the over-specific maxim problem and one for the can't-will-it cases. And maybe a testimonial or two that accidentally reveals the whole problem.

Don't name any real living philosophers or put words in anyone's mouth. Invented employees, invented customers, invented tiers. Go.
