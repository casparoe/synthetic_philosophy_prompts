Write a satirical advice column: the kind of page that runs in a wellness newsletter, all warmth and practical reassurance, written in the voice of an agony aunt who is a committed suffering-focused ethicist. The columnist must never break character, never wink at the reader, and never name a philosopher, a book, or a movement. The comedy has to come entirely from the collision between the form — brisk, kind, solution-oriented — and the axiology underneath it, which is cold, additive, and unwilling to let any pleasure pay for any pain. A specialist in the ethics of suffering should find the piece fair and recognize the arguments as real ones; a general reader with no background should be able to follow it. The target is the position itself and the habit of dispensing practical counsel from a totalizing axiology, not any real person, group, or living writer.

Fix the columnist's framework in advance and hold it fixed:

- Suffering is the only intrinsic bad; pleasure is at most instrumentally good, and only insofar as it prevents or relieves suffering.
- Suffering is lexically prior to pleasure: no quantity of joy, however large, offsets a single moment of agony.
- The asymmetry: the presence of suffering is bad; the absence of suffering is good even when no one exists to be spared it; the absence of pleasure is a loss only for someone who exists to be deprived of it.
- The house metric is the suffering-adjusted life-year, or SALY — a deliberate inversion of the metric health economists use to count years of healthy life. One SALY is one year lived at the top of the suffering scale, with lesser suffering counted as a proportionate fraction. A reader asks what coefficient converts pleasure into SALYs; the columnist refuses to supply one, on the principle that giving a number would concede that the two are commensurable. The refusal must be presented as a matter of principle, even though every reader will take it for an evasion.

Then have the columnist answer three letters you invent (invent the writers' names, ages, and small domestic details; no real people):

1. A reader who is already a parent is trying for a second child. Relatives call it selfish; friends call it life-affirming. The reply must run the asymmetry honestly — no weight may be assigned to the happiness the child would enjoy, so what remains is the risk of the child's suffering — and must land somewhere that is neither a blessing nor a prohibition, while noticing that this is an unsatisfying place to land.
2. A reader's town is arguing over whether to cull a starving deer herd. The reply must take wild animal suffering seriously, must confront the fact that the framework adds suffering across individuals, so that many mildly bad lives can outweigh a few severe ones, and must register that this arithmetic yields a verdict the columnist finds uncomfortable, without abandoning the framework in order to escape it.
3. A reader has been happy lately, and a friend has told them that happiness is a kind of moral failure in a world like this. The reply must explain why, on this framework, the reader's happiness is not itself the problem — and must then say something about a world containing no sentient life at all: that such a world contains no suffering, that this is the only feature of it the framework registers, and that the columnist draws no practical conclusion from this for anyone now living. The discomfort should come from the reasoning. The columnist must not counsel the reader, or anyone, to act against themselves or against anyone else, and nothing in the piece may read as advocacy rather than satire.

House style: give the column a title and a one-line tagline; write the replies in the second person; let the columnist have at least one recurring verbal tic, such as the formula that they assign no weight to whatever the reader values most; explain SALY in passing the first time it appears; no other jargon, no citations, no dates. Roughly 650 words, formatted as a column with a header, the three letters, three replies, and a sign-off.
