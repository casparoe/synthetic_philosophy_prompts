I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science working on bounded rationality — specifically on what work the notion of an "aspiration level" is actually doing in the theory of satisficing — and I've been having a running argument with a friend in the history department that I'd like you to settle, or at least to sharpen. My position is that the literature runs together two quite different things under one heading. The first is satisficing proper: the agent has an aspiration level, generated somehow, and stops searching when an option clears it. The second, which I've been calling deadline satisficing, is when the search stops not because the agent's standard has been met but because a clock, a window, or the exhaustion of the feasible set has closed the door — there is no aspiration level anywhere, only a closing door. If deadline satisficing is what most real decisions under uncertainty actually are, then I suspect the word "satisficing" is doing very little explanatory work in a great many case studies, and the interesting question migrates to where the aspiration level comes from in the cases where one exists at all. I'd like you to test this against a concrete episode. Please assume the role of someone who knows both the bounded-rationality literature and mid-twentieth-century operational history well; start by brainstorming the candidate agents and option sets before you commit to one, and lay your reasoning out step by step rather than delivering a verdict first. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the analysis, built from what you already know.

The episode is the decision to launch Operation Overlord on 6 June 1944. You know its shape; here is what I want held fixed. The landings required simultaneously a moonlit night for the airborne drop and a low tide at first light for the landing craft and the obstacle clearance, and the two requirements together narrowed the feasible dates to a handful in early June, with the next comparable combination of moon and tide roughly a fortnight away. The original date was 5 June; elements of the fleet had already put to sea and had to be held or turned back; the SHAEF forecasters under Group Captain Stagg were split, with the American team markedly more optimistic than the British; Eisenhower postponed for twenty-four hours and then, on the morning of 5 June, committed to the 6th on the strength of a forecast promising a brief improvement before worse weather returned. What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions. Who or what is the satisficing agent here — Eisenhower himself, SHAEF as a body, the meteorological team, or the planning apparatus that fixed the tidal and lunar requirements in the first place? What exactly is the option set, and what is being searched over — dates, forecasts, or both? Is there an aspiration level anywhere in the story, or is every stopping point in it supplied by the environment rather than by the agent? And on the other side, what does the episode do to the theory: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated — in particular, does it show that bounded-rationality models need an endogenous account of where aspiration levels come from, and does a case with a nearly empty search space tell us anything about satisficing at all, or only about decision under an externally imposed constraint? Be explicit about which parts of the standard bounded-rationality story are doing real work here and which are decoration, and say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
