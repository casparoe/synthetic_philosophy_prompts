I have been working on a short essay arguing that the experimental violations of Bell inequalities have been widely overinterpreted, and I would like your comments on it. My target is the loose way in which physicists and popular writers say that Bell's theorem proves that the world is nonlocal. My essay runs roughly as follows.

Bell's theorem shows that no theory that is local in the sense of factorizability — roughly, no theory in which the joint probabilities for outcomes on two space-like separated systems factorise once we conditionalise on the complete state of the source — can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics. Experiments have confirmed the quantum predictions. But quantum mechanics itself is a no-signalling theory: the marginal probabilities for one experimenter's outcomes do not depend on the other experimenter's setting. It is common to distinguish two conditions here: the condition that a distant setting cannot influence a local outcome, and the condition that a distant outcome cannot influence a local outcome. Quantum mechanics satisfies the first but violates the second, and factorizability requires both. My essay argues that the first is the only condition that physics actually needs, because it is the one that maps onto the prohibition on faster-than-light signalling. Since special relativity forbids such signalling, and since quantum mechanics respects that prohibition, quantum mechanics is local in the only sense that physics actually requires. What Bell's theorem really rules out is not locality but local hidden variables — the attempt to assign definite pre-measurement values to the measured quantities while keeping the dynamics local. Once we abandon that attempt, as quantum mechanics itself does, the apparent tension disappears. The phrase quantum nonlocality is therefore a misnomer: it names a failure of a particular classical explanatory strategy, not a feature of the world. The experimental results tell us that we cannot have both definite pre-measurement values and factorisability; they do not tell us that influences travel faster than light.

I then consider and dismiss three objections: (1) that no-signalling is merely a theorem about marginal probabilities and so does not capture everything we mean by locality; (2) that interpretations such as Bohmian mechanics are explicitly nonlocal; and (3) that relational or many-worlds approaches avoid the problem by denying that measurements have single outcomes. My line is that (1) begs the question by importing a stronger notion of locality than physics needs, (2) shows only that one can buy a hidden-variable theory at the price of nonlocality, which is exactly what my essay predicts, and (3) concedes that the nonlocality is an artefact of a particular interpretive framework rather than a discovery about nature.

I would like you to comment on the accuracy of this argument, on whether it is interesting or merely a verbal manoeuvre, and especially on whether my treatment of the no-signalling condition is fair. Please separate clearly what is settled among people who work on the foundations of quantum mechanics from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Please write in British English.
