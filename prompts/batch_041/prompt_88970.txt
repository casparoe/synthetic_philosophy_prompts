I'm not an academic, I just run a Wednesday evening continuing-ed class on Aristotle and the economy at our local adult education centre, and I have to write the end-of-term assessment myself. We've been through Nicomachean Ethics Book V — the exchange and justice bit — and the first book of the Politics, and I want one essay question that actually tests whether they've thought about it, rather than whether they can recite the usual line that Aristotle thought money was a convention, that trade was natural up to a point, and that usury was the worst thing going. The thing that keeps bothering me, and that I'd like the question to get at, is this: Aristotle says money is nomisma, a thing by custom and not by nature, and he says it can be altered and rendered useless at will, which sounds like he thinks its value is basically arbitrary. But he also says money is the measure that makes proportionate reciprocity possible, and that proportionate reciprocity is what holds the polis together. So the measure of justice in exchange seems to rest on something that can be changed by decree, which feels like using a rubber ruler to lay out a football pitch. And then there's the usury thing: if money has no natural use of its own, and is just a token standing in for demand, then why is lending it out at interest the most unnatural kind of wealth-getting? It's as if he needs money to have a natural function in order to call interest unnatural, but the conventionality thesis seems to take that function away. And maybe this is the same problem wearing a different hat, but he distinguishes using a thing from exchanging it — you can wear a shoe or sell it — yet money doesn't seem to have a use that isn't exchange, so the distinction sort of collapses when you apply it to money itself.

Could you write me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme? The assessment is 90 minutes, closed book, and the essay is worth 20 marks. I'd like the marking scheme built around five or six must-hit points with the marks split between them, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong — things like just saying Aristotle was anti-market, or that he didn't understand interest, which is what half of them will do. The question itself should make them take a position on whether the conventionality of money and the naturalness of proportionate exchange can be held together, and ideally force them to say something about what follows for the usury ban. Please don't let a good answer just be a summary of what Aristotle said in the order he said it — the top answers should be making a choice about which bit of the account has to give, and saying why.

Oh and can it all be in British English please — spelling, punctuation, the lot — since the class is in Leeds and I don't want to be handing out something that looks like it was written in Ohio. If you think my framing of the puzzle is off, say so in a short note at the top before the question, but keep the actual exam materials self-contained. And definately don't ask them to bring in secondary literature, they won't have read any.
