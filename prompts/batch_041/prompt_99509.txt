I write historical fiction, and for the past two years I have been at work on a novel set in a failing parliamentary republic that slides into dictatorship — a book that has forced me to think hard about a question my philosopher friends insist belongs to them: whether a regime of that kind is the achievement of one particular man or the output of conditions that would have produced something very like him with or without him. I would like you to restate the long quarrel between the Great Man view of history and its structural rivals in the vocabulary I actually work in: character and setting, agency and situation, plot as something a protagonist does versus plot as something a situation does to whoever happens to be standing in it, the difference between a story that survives recasting its lead and one that collapses the moment you replace him, the way a novel's middle sags when its turns are motivated by the world rather than by the person at the centre of it. Treat the structural historians as a species of writer — the ones whose books are really about a town, a class, or a season, and in which the individuals are functions of a pressure — and the Great Man historians as the opposite species, and be as faithful to each as you can, marking clearly where a claim is about narrative necessity and where it is about causation.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the historians' debate translate cleanly into craft terms — I suspect the counterfactual test (would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?) has an exact analogue in what I do when I ask whether a scene still happens if I recast the character — and which parts translate only with distortion, or not at all? I keep what I call a necessity ledger for every manuscript: each major turn is marked as character-driven, situation-driven, or merely contingent. I want to know whether that ledger is a genuine translation of the historians' counterfactual or a counterfeit that quietly imports assumptions they would reject — for instance, whether a novel's appetite for a coherent arc leads me to certify as necessary what was only convenient. And tell me what the historians argue about that has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever: is there anything in the Great Man versus structural dispute that is not, at bottom, about agency, motive, contingency, and setting, or is my framework simply missing something? I want an honest verdict on whether the residue is a defect of the novelist's vocabulary or evidence that the historians' disagreement is thinner than it sounds. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating of my question.
