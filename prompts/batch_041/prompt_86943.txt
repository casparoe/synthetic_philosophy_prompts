I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I am drafting the essay section of the final exam. The reading for the unit on cognitive evolution is Dennett's account of the Tower of Generate and Test, together with the four grades of creature he distinguishes there — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. I want a single essay question on that material, worth 20 marks, which a student could reasonably attempt in about 45 minutes.

Two constraints matter to me. First, the question must not be answerable by reciting the four grades back at me; I already have plenty of questions that reward that, and this one is meant to separate students who understand what the taxonomy is doing from students who have memorised its labels. Second, the question must be self-contained: it will be printed on the exam paper, students will have no texts or notes with them, so it cannot depend on quotations, page references, or anything they would have to look up. It should not refer to secondary literature at all.

To make the question do real work, I want you to build it around the following case, which should be reproduced on the exam paper as part of the question, in your own phrasing and at whatever length you judge necessary to make it clear.

Consider a system whose entire behavioural repertoire was fixed before it ever acted. A training process searched over a vast space of possible parameter settings, retaining those that scored well against an objective specified by its designers, and the surviving settings were then frozen. Nothing the system does in the world subsequently adjusts those settings; it does not learn from its own successes or failures. At run time, however, it does three things. It generates candidate courses of action and discards most of them before acting. It uses external equipment — written scratchpads, search tools, instruments it did not build — to extend what it can work out. And its dispositions are shaped throughout by information it inherited from a written culture that it played no part in producing. Call this system the Frozen Seer.

The question should ask students to decide whether the Frozen Seer belongs on the Tower, and if so at which grade; or whether it shows that the Tower is not the taxonomy Dennett took it to be. It should require them to say explicitly whether the Tower sorts creatures by the mechanism that does the generating and testing or by the competence they display, and to reach a defended verdict about what the case shows concerning the Gregorian grade in particular. The familiar reading of the Tower as a story of the growth of freedom and self-control should be brought into the question, since the case is designed to put pressure on it. Students should be told that a survey of the four grades will earn little credit and that they must commit to a verdict.

Please supply three things.

First, the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, self-contained and free of quotations and page references.

Second, a model answer of about 650 words. It should be written as a strong student would write it, and it must actually resolve the case rather than gesture at the difficulty.

Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, totalling 20 marks, with the marks for each stated. Each point should be accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get that point wrong. The scheme should be calibrated so that a merely competent answer — one that identifies the Frozen Seer as Gregorian because it uses tools, or as Darwinian because its parameters are frozen, and stops there — lands in the middle of the range, while an answer that names the mechanism/competence ambiguity and then says something definite about what follows from it reaches the top.
