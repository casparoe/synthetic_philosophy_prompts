OK so I need you to write me a speech. Four minutes, tops.

Here's the situation. I'm giving the opening remarks at a small exhibit that our university's special-collections library is putting on. The centerpiece is a facsimile of a horoscope Kepler drew up for some paying nobleman, and the whole exhibit is basically about Kepler's day job. I'm the one who has to stand up and say something before everyone goes in to look at the stuff.

The room is the problem. About half of it is our local astronomy club — they lent us a couple of antique telescopes, they think astrology is garbage, and a few of them are already annoyed that the library is doing astrology at all. Then there are two actual astrologers sitting in the front row, who lent us some old charts and who are hoping I'll say something nice about their craft. And then there are the library donors, who just want it to be charming and short.

What I need the speech to actually do — build it around this, don't just gesture at it — is refuse the easy version. The easy version is: Kepler was a man of his time, ha ha, the great scientist had a silly side. That's not right and it's boring. He wasn't humoring clients. He thought the sky really does something to us. He thought the geometry was the mechanism — the angles between the planets, the aspects — acting on the soul or the temperament, call it whatever you want. And he thought the horoscope-men selling predictions about specific events were hacks. He cast the charts anyway because somebody had to pay for the astronomy. He also wrote a story about a trip to the moon, published after he died, which is honestly a whole seperate speech.

So the real subject isn't "great man had a silly side." The real subject is that the line between astronomy and astrology was never about whether the sky affects us. Kepler thought it did. The line is about what counts as a mechanism, and who's allowed to make predictions. And we never actually stopped believing the sky affects us — we just renamed it: circadian rhythms, seasonal depression, solar storms knocking out the grid.

So: hold all of that without taking a side the room will hear as a side. Don't sneer at the astrologers. Don't let the astronomers feel like I just stood up and defended astrology. But don't be a coward about the real disagreement either. And it's opening remarks, so it still has to make people want to walk in and look at the exhibit.

Format: it has to be sayable out loud by a person holding a glass of wine, so no bullets, no headers, no stage directions. Under 400 words. Give me one sentence at the top that tells me what the speech's move is, then the speech itself.
