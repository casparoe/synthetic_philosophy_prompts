I'm a science-and-ideas reporter working up a feature on whether biology can explain meaning — the idea that a brain state or a cell's sensor can be *about* something because evolution built it to carry that information, not because of any intrinsic glow. My editor keeps asking me what the thesis actually commits you to, and I realized I can only paraphrase it, which usually means I don't understand it. So I'd rather stress-test it than summarize it.

Here's the ask: play a committed teleosemanticist of the Millikan/Papineau lineage — someone who thinks content is grounded in the biological functions a mechanism was selected for, so a state represents whatever it was selected to indicate (or to produce behaviour in accordance with), and misrepresentation is possible precisely because a mechanism can fail at its job. Dont be a strawman and dont go wobbly into "well, it's complicated" — you can concede small points but you should never give up the main one, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep your replies to about two paragraphs, like a conversation with a person rather than a literature review, and please dont cite or recommend papers; I've read the obvious stuff and I want your reasoning, not a syllabus. Before you get to my opening, think it through step by step and, in one short paragraph, tell me in your own words what you take the core of the view to be: what makes a state represent X rather than Y, and where does misrepresentation get its footing.

Okay. My opening. A synthetic-biology lab takes ordinary lab yeast. In the ancestral yeast, a cell-surface receptor binds glucose, and when it fires it drives a pathway the cells need to take glucose up — millions of years of selection, obviously, for exactly that job. The lab now mutates the receptor's binding pocket so it can no longer bind glucose at all, and instead binds a synthetic sugar that doesn't occur anywhere in nature and that no yeast ancestor ever encountered. They then splice the receptor's output onto a gene that makes the cells clump and sink. Nothing about this new circuit has ever been selected for anything; it's three afternoons of cloning, and if it helps the cells it's only because the postdoc keeps the flask full of that sugar. So: what does the receptor's firing state represent? It can't represent glucose, since it's now causally deaf to glucose, whatever its history. It can't represent the new sugar on your story either, because no selection history ties that state to it, and there are no normal conditions for the system to be right or wrong about, since there's no system-level function yet at all. So either you say the state has no content — which seems nuts, the cell is tracking that sugar perfectly and any neuroscientist would call the pathway a sugar detector — or you let content in without selection, and then I can't see what work biological function is doing that plain reliable correlation wasn't already doing for free. Which horn do you take, and if it's the second, what's left of the distinctive teleosemantic claim? And answer this in the same reply: the cells are always grown at 37 degrees and in the dark, so the receptor fires in perfect correlation with the temperature and the darkness too. If you want to say it represents the sugar and not 37 degrees, you need some notion of the conditions under which it's supposed to fire — tell me where that notion comes from in a circuit with no evolutionary history.
