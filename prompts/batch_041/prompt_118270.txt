I have spent the last decade building event-sourced systems and bitemporal databases, and I have never studied metaphysics; my acquaintance with the endurance/perdurance distinction comes from a podcast episode I half-remember. Please translate that debate into the vocabulary I actually work in — entity identifiers and foreign keys, update-in-place versus append-only logs, row versions carrying valid-time and transaction-time intervals, type-2 slowly changing dimensions, materialised views rebuilt by replaying events, mutable current state alongside immutable history — as faithfully as you can, taking endurantism and perdurantism one at a time rather than collapsing them into a single story about one entity persisting through time. State explicitly which of my constructs you are using for the entity, which for its temporal parts if the view has any, and which for the relation that holds those parts together.

Then deliver the residue report. Which claims survive translation intact? Which survive only because my vocabulary silently settles a question the philosophers leave open — for instance whether an attribute that varies over time is a relation between an entity and a time or something of a different kind altogether, or whether the grouping of row versions into a single entity is something discovered or something stipulated by the schema? And which claims have no counterpart at all — the requirement that a changing thing somehow remain one and the same thing, the objection that a four-dimensional whole cannot strictly change because its parts merely differ, the question why anyone should be especially concerned about the fate of a future temporal part? I have a suspicion that my habit of rebuilding current state by replaying events imports a direction of dependence that the view you are translating need not accept; tell me whether that is a real distortion or an artefact of my example. For each piece of residue, say whether the gap is a defect of my vocabulary, which genuinely cannot express the thing, or evidence that the metaphysical claim is thinner than its advocates suggest. A translation that fails informatively is worth more to me than one that succeeds by quietly assuming its conclusion.
