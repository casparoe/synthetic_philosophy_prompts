I chair an interdisciplinary working group on the philosophy and science of psychiatric classification, and at our last session I lost control of the room. Two members — Marta, a philosopher of science who works on kinds and classification, and Owen, a clinical psychiatrist with an active research programme on mood disorders — spent the better part of an hour in an argument I could not adjudicate, and I have promised them a written ruling. I would like you to draft it.

Both of them accept the phenomenon. Psychiatric categories exhibit what has come to be known as the looping effect, after Ian Hacking's work on human kinds: the classified respond to the classification, and the classification is then revised in light of how they respond. Earlier in the year the group adopted, for its own purposes, a threefold scheme of looping mechanisms, and both parties make use of it. Presentation shift: the same underlying condition comes to be expressed through the symptoms the category makes salient. Boundary shift: the category changes who is counted as a case without changing anyone's condition. Pathway shift: the category alters the mix of causes in the population, for instance by making one causal route the culturally available one. Nobody denies that all three occur. The dispute is about what follows.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

Marta: Looping is not a curiosity but a difference in kind between psychiatric categories and the categories of the natural sciences. A category that partly constitutes its own instances cannot be a natural kind. Whatever the manual is tracking, it is tracking something it has helped to make, which is why its history reads as much like fashion as like discovery.

Owen: That proves too much. Feedback of this sort is everywhere in biology — hosts and parasites, predators and prey — and a field guide changes what naturalists collect. A category can be causally entangled with its instances and still support induction, intervention and explanation. The test of a classification is whether it does scientific work, not whether it stands outside the causal order.

Marta: Then your test is too weak to be interesting. Homeostatic property clusters, and for that matter any pragmatically useful sorting at all, will pass it. If a natural kind is just a classification that supports induction, the concept earns nothing, and you have conceded the substantive point while keeping the vocabulary.

Owen: Perhaps the vocabulary is what should go. What matters is which mechanism is doing the work in a given case. Where looping runs through pathway shift, we should say so and model the feedback; where it runs only through boundary shift, the underlying condition may be as indifferent to our categories as a mineral is. Looping is not one thing, and a single word has been doing the work of three. And much of what you call looping may be confounding rather than causation: the label and the presentation may share a common social cause — referral patterns, media attention — with the category doing none of the causing.

Marta: For my conclusion it makes no difference whether the category causes the presentation or merely shares a cause with it; either way, no stable internal structure is being tracked. But the case that worries me is the loop running through the classification's authority. A patient who reads the criteria, learns the script and presents accordingly is not a measurement of a pre-existing state; the measurement has entered the state. You cannot model that without deciding in advance what the category is supposed to pick out, and that is precisely what is in dispute.

Owen: Every measurement enters the state. Blood-pressure cuffs change what people do, and we did not conclude that hypertension is a fiction. The remedy is reflexivity in the modelling, not scepticism about the object. And your argument again proves too much: if any causal influence of a classification on the classified disqualified a category, no social kind could be real, and you would have to say the same of the categories of economics.

That is roughly where they left it. Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating in a sentence or two. Then say who is right about what, whether any part of the exchange is merely verbal, and whether the threefold scheme the group adopted is adequate to the cases at issue. Tell me what both of them are missing. Finally, give me your own verdict on the underlying question: does looping defeat the claim that psychiatric categories can be natural kinds, and does the distinction between categories that interact with their instances and categories that do not survive scrutiny?
