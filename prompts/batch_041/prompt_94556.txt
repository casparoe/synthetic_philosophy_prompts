I run a small game theory / behavioral econ reading group and I want to build a "what do you actually think is going on in the dollar auction?" decision tree. Everyone nods along at "escalation of commitment," but I suspect we disagree way more than we admit.

Start from the thing we all accept: the dollar auction (two bidders, prize worth a dollar, top bidder pays their bid and takes the prize, runner-up also pays theirs and gets nothing) reliably runs past a dollar and often way past. Then branch from there. Every node should be a question someone can answer without already knowing the label it leads to. Things I'd want on the tree: is the escalation best explained by a mistake the bidders make about their own payoffs, or by payoffs the standard model leaves out (face, sunk cost as a signal to yourself, not wanting to be the sucker, wanting to punish the other bidder)? Does it survive when both bidders are fully forward-looking and know the other is too? Is the pathology in the players or in the design of the auction? And does your verdict flip under variants -- say a rule that refunds everybody if total bids hit two-fifty, or sealed bids, or the loser getting half back?

Leaves should be named positions where they have names and "unnamed" where nobody defends them, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't seperate, and tell me which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why -- incoherent, unattractive, or just nobodys bothered. Aim for 6-8 nodes and 5-7 leaves. Audience is econ grad students plus a couple of philosophers. Please write it in American English.
