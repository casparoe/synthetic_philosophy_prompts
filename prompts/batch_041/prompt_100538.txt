I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on scientific realism and theory choice, and I've been asked to write a short position paper for next week's session. The seminar has spent several meetings on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and every discussion collapses into the same well-worn examples and the same handful of stock replies, so our instructor has set us a puzzle of her own construction to try to break the rut. The puzzle runs as follows. Imagine two theories of some physical domain that are empirically equivalent in the strongest possible sense: for every observation you could make, at any time, in any part of the domain, the two theories issue the same prediction, and this equivalence is provable rather than merely conjectured. The first theory describes the domain as a finite collection of discrete, localized entities governed by a dynamical rule that resists any compact formulation — it takes thousands of independent parameters even to state. The second describes the very same domain as a single continuous field with infinitely many degrees of freedom, governed by a rule short enough to fit on an index card. The twist is that the two theories are related by a duality: a transformation that maps every model of the first onto a model of the second while preserving all observable consequences, and which, when applied, turns the bloated rule into the compact one and the compact one into the bloated one, and simultaneously swaps the apparent counting of entities so that neither theory comes out ahead on parsimony of ontology. The usual tie-breakers — simplicity, fewness of entities, predictive success — are thus neutralized by construction, and there is no prospect of a future experiment separating the two.

My paper has to defend a definite position, and I've settled on arguing that this puzzle does not show that theory choice is underdetermined by evidence: the appearance of underdetermination is an artifact of specifying the content of the two theories in a way that omits something the choice between them actually turns on, and once that omitted element is made explicit, the evidence together with the aim of inquiry determines a unique rational choice. I'd like you to write that argument, addressed to a reader who already knows the standard literature and will be irritated by hand-waving. Please make the omitted element precise rather than gesturing at vague pragmatic virtues, say exactly what work it does in this two-theory case, and confront the strongest objection head-on — namely, that whatever you add is either not part of the evidence, in which case you have simply conceded underdetermination, or is a disguised empirical assumption, in which case you have begged the question. Explain how you would answer that dilemma without just denying one of its horns.

After the argument, give me a compact JSON object with exactly these fields: position (one sentence), omitted_element (the factor you claim the choice turns on), why_evidence_plus_aim_is_determinate (two or three sentences), strongest_objection (one sentence), reply (two or three sentences), and residual_cost (one sentence naming what your position gives up).
