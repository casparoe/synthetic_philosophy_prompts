I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I keep encountering the claim — advanced by Lucas and developed at book length by Penrose — that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind cannot be a machine. Before I am argued into or out of that conclusion, I want the specification. Write me the list of requirements that any argument of this shape must satisfy to count as adequate, so that I have a standard to hold particular versions to, and so that I can tell which of the demands I keep seeing made of such arguments are genuine requirements and which are merely the commitments of one camp. Assume I am comfortable with the halting problem and with the idea of an oracle machine, but not with proof theory or the philosophy of mind.

For each requirement, give me the consideration that makes it a requirement — the case, objection, or result it rests on — and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. I expect the following to matter, though I would rather you correct my sense of what matters than defer to it:

- what the argument owes to the exact hypotheses under which the incompleteness theorems hold, and what those theorems do and do not establish about truth as against provability;
- whether the argument can be applied to the machine the mind might itself be, given that seeing a system's Gödel sentence requires recognizing that system's consistency, and that a consistent system of the relevant kind cannot prove its own consistency;
- what the argument must assume about the mathematician — consistency, soundness, freedom from error, idealization of memory and lifespan — and whether those assumptions are defensible on their own;
- how the argument gets from "not this machine" to "not any machine," and what licenses that step;
- which notion of machine the conclusion is about, and whether it survives being widened to machines equipped with oracles or with non-enumerable axiom sets;
- whether the mind's alleged ability to outrun any given formal system is itself a computable capacity, and what the argument must show about that;
- whether any premise quietly assumes the conclusion;
- and whether the whole thing is consistent with what human mathematicians manifestly cannot do.

Flag any pair of requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect the list itself is where the real result lies — and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. I do not need a scorecard of Lucas and Penrose, though if a requirement plainly defeats one of them, say so in a sentence and say why. No reading list, no citations.
