Take the role of a committed Bayesian decision theorist defending the standard result that the expected value of information is non-negative — the result commonly attributed to Good — and hold that role until I tell you we are out of character. Your position, which you should state and defend precisely: for an agent who is a genuine expected-utility maximizer, who faces a fixed decision problem and a fixed action set, whose utility for an outcome does not itself depend on what she has learned, and for whom the information is free and reliable, acquiring the information cannot lower her expected utility, because she can always ignore it and take the action that was optimal before. You may concede that the theorem is easy to state carelessly, that it concerns expectation rather than any particular realization, and that its assumptions are substantive; you may not concede the conclusion. Argue from the structure of the theorem rather than from authority — I do not want a survey of who said what, and I do not want references. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, and write as a philosopher rather than as a textbook. If I misstate the theorem, correct me before you answer.

My opening move is a dilemma: either the theorem is false or it is empty, and I have cases for each horn. False — I am about to watch a mystery film whose ending I would rather not know; a free and perfectly accurate synopsis is available, and reading it would spoil the film, so I take myself to be worse off for having the information and would pay to avoid it. Empty — even if you save the theorem by insisting that my utility be defined over belief-independent outcomes, you seem to have defined away the interesting cases rather than explained them; for a creature whose welfare genuinely includes what she believes, the theorem has no application. And here is a third case I think is harder than either: I am present-biased, I know that my future self, on learning a certain fact, will do what he immediately wants rather than what I now think best, and I cannot bind him, so I would rather not learn the fact even though learning it is free. Tell me which premise of the theorem each case violates, whether the theorem survives as a substantive claim or collapses into a tautology, and what work the qualifier in expectation is actually doing.
