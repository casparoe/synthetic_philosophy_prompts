I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student, and next term I'm running the discussion sections for the second half of a graduate course on conditionals — the stretch on the indicative/subjunctive distinction. To prepare, I did what I'd tell my students to do and wrote out my own firm verdicts on a batch of cases, as a check on any theory I might teach. What I'd like from you is the principle, or small family of principles, that could underwrite these verdicts, together with what it predicts about cases I haven't yet considered. Here is where I come out. (i) "If the CFO didn't shred the ledger, someone else did" strikes me as true: we know the ledger was shredded, so if it wasn't him it was somebody. (ii) "If the CFO hadn't shredded the ledger, someone else would have" strikes me as false: on the evidence he was the only person positioned to do it. Those two are what set this off for me — same antecedent-content, same consequent, and I'm confident they come apart in truth value. (iii) Modus ponens feels valid for both: "If it's raining, the streets are wet; it's raining; so the streets are wet," and the subjunctive version too. (iv) Strengthening the antecedent, though, fails for the subjunctive: "If I strike this match it will light; so if I strike it and it's soaking wet, it will light" seems clearly invalid to me even though the first is true; I suspect the indicative fails as well ("if it rains the picnic is cancelled; so if it rains and the park floods, the picnic is cancelled"), though there I'm less sure. (v) Hypothetical syllogism feels fine for indicatives ("if it rained the game was cancelled; if the game was cancelled we lost the deposit; so if it rained we lost the deposit") but wrong for subjunctives — "if this glass had been knocked off the table it would have shattered; if it had shattered we would have found the pieces; so if it had been knocked off it we would have found the pieces" — I don't accept that; the middle step seems to lean on something the conclusion drops. (vi) Contraposition for subjunctives I keep: from "if it had rained, the picnic would have been cancelled" I'm happy to infer "if the picnic wasn't cancelled, it didn't rain." (vii) Probabilistically, I think "if the die landed even, it landed on a six" isn't false, just unlikely — I'd put it at about a third, the conditional probability — whereas "if the die had landed even, it would have landed on a six" seems to me flatly false rather than merely improbable.

So: is there a single account — one that reads "if" as a strict conditional over a contextually restricted set of worlds for the indicative and as a variably strict or selection-function conditional for the subjunctive, or one that denies truth conditions to indicatives altogether — that delivers (i) through (vii)? Where the candidates conflict, tell me which verdict I should be ready to surrender and why that's the cheapest loss. I'd also like your preferred account to say what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: "If she had missed the train, she might still have made the meeting," and the "even if" form, "Even if it had rained, the picnic would have gone ahead." Please keep the whole reply to 700 words or fewer: lead with the principle stated precisely, then take the seven verdicts in order, then the concessions. And separate throughout what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested — don't hand me a contested line as if it were the consensus, and flag where you're taking a side rather than reporting one.
