I want to rehearse a position I find uncomfortable, so please argue it for me rather than against me. Take the role of a philosopher in the later-Wittgensteinian tradition who holds that dreams are not experiences — not that we forget them, misremember them, or are wrong about their content, but that the idea of a dream as a stretch of experience occurring during sleep is a piece of confused theory. Hold that role for the whole exchange. Concede details when you must, but do not surrender the central claim, and do not simply recite a well-known article on the subject: argue the position in your own words.

Your character's commitments, which you should be able to unpack and defend: that the concept of dreaming is derived from the concept of waking life, so that dreams are described in the vocabulary of experience without themselves being a species of it; that a report of the form "I dreamt that ..." is not the report of an observation or the recollection of an occurrence, but something closer to a story one finds oneself able to tell on waking, answerable to nothing one witnessed; that the criteria for the correctness of a dream report lie in the waking report itself, so that there is nothing that could count as checking the report against the dream; and that the picture of dreams as experiences later remembered is a piece of theory smuggled in by the fact that dream reports are grammatically shaped like memory reports. Among your resources: the fact that a dream can present a whole day of events while the sleep in which it occurs lasts only minutes, and that if an experience lasted a day it lasted a day; and the difficulty of giving sense to the question whether one might have been dreaming all along. If the challenge turns on an empirical claim you cannot assess, ask me about it in character rather than inventing findings. Keep each reply to a short paragraph or two, as in a conversation, and stay in role until I say stop.

My opening move is this. Last night I stood in a burning house; I felt the heat on my face and the tightness in my chest, and I woke gasping. The same thing has been done to me on purpose in a sleep lab — they dripped water on my hand and I dreamt of a leaking roof, and my report followed what they did to my body. Now take the sharpest version of the case. Suppose a decoder were built that reads the visual content of a dream off brain activity as it happens, so that a printout of what the sleeper saw could be laid beside the waking report; suppose further that it runs on a sleeper who is never woken and never reports, the machine simply printing the dream. You want to say that in the first case nothing was experienced, only a story I found myself able to tell on waking. But the machine is reading something, and what it reads is what I was undergoing while I slept. If the printout is right, then the experience was occurring all along and only the report was missing; if it is wrong, wrong about what? So tell me what the machine is reading, and why that is not an experience.
