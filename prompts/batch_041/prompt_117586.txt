I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly for general-interest outlets — and last Saturday I went to a ceramics studio's open evening because a friend of mine, Ruth, had a piece in the show and I owed her. Her piece is a big unfired clay figure on a plinth, and somewhere between the second and third glass of wine I ended up standing next to it with Ruth and a conservator, Deb, who the gallery had brought in to write a condition report. They got into it, politly at first, and I've been chewing on it since because I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it.

1. Deb said the statue and the lump of clay are two different things. Ruth said no, there's one thing there, clay arranged statue-wise; "statue" is just what we call the clay in a certain shape. Deb said if they were one thing they'd have to have all the same properties, and they don't — squash the clay and the clay survives while the statue doesn't, so the clay has a property (can-survive-squashing) that the statue lacks, and by Leibniz's law, the indiscernibility one, they can't be identical.

2. Ruth said that's cheating. "Can survive squashing" isn't something the clay has, she said, it's a fact about which word we'd keep using. The clay has no hidden durability the statue lacks; we've just decided that the clay keeps its name through squashing and the statue doesn't. She compared it to a wave: we say the wave broke, but the water is all still there, and nobody thinks that means there were two objects in one place.

3. Deb said the wave analogy proves too much, because on that reasoning nothing ever goes out of existence — the matter is all still around, just rearranged — so if Ruth is right, nothing can ever be destroyed, which is absurd. Then she turned it on her: the clay in this piece is reclaimed, isn't it, from the one you smashed on purpose last year? So if the clay is what persists, your old piece is sitting on this plinth right now. Ruth said she doesn't believe that, but only because she doesn't think the old piece was ever a separate thing from the clay; "the piece" is a phase, like "the puddle." Deb said fine, but then "destroyed" is a phase too, and Ruth has lost the one thing she wanted, which is to be able to say the old piece is gone.

4. Then Deb pulled rank on her day job. She said she does this constantly: when a piece like this is damaged she'll often accept the loss of original material to save the form — fill it, inpaint it, in this case re-form it from the same clay — and the reason that's the right call is precisely that the artwork and the material aren't the same thing, and the artwork is what she's responsible for. If Ruth were right, "preserving the piece" would just mean "preserving the clay," and every treatment decision would be arbitrary. Ruth said the reverse: the fact that Deb has to make those calls, and that other conservators make them differently, is evidence that there's no fact of the matter about which object is the real one — there's the stuff, and there are the stories we tell about it, and which story matters depends on what we care about.

They left it there. Can you go through it move by move, 1 through 4, and tell me who's making the better point at each step and why — not just who wins overall — and flag anything either of them is assuming that they shouldn't be? And if there's a move that would actually break the standoff, one that neither of them made, I'd like to hear that too.
