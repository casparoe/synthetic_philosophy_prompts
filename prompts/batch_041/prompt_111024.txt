I'm a backend engineer, about eight years in, no philosophy background at all, and I've somehow become the person who owns engineering metrics at a 60-person startup. I keep getting into the same argument with my manager and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut or if I'm just inconsistent about which numbers I'm precious about.

I've heard this stuff called Goodhart's law, the thing where a measure stops being a good measure once you start managing to it. Great, but that's a fortune cookie, it doesn't tell me which of these I should be mad about. Here's what I actually think:

- Support is measured on tickets closed per day. Agents close tickets the customer hasn't confirmed are fixed, they reopen the next morning. I think this is cheating and I want the metric gone.
- Teams are measured on story points per sprint. Everyone inflates their estimates. Cheating, kill it.
- We track test coverage. People write tests that call the function and assert nothing. Cheating, kill it.
- Recruiters are measured on interviews scheduled. They book candidates who obviously won't pass. Cheating, kill it.
- We had a build-time target and someone moved our slowest integration tests into a nightly job. Number went down, nothing got faster. And honestly... I'm fine with it? It's the exact same shape as the support one, the number drops and the underlying thing is untouched, and I can't defend the difference.
- Deploy frequency target, team split one big deploy into five small ones. I think that's fine, arguably that was the point.
- Tickets shipped target, team split one ticket into five. I think that's cheating. Literally the same move as the deploy one.
- p99 latency target. People made real fixes, nothing weird happened. Fine.
- Revenue for the sales team. Fine, that's the thing itself, not a stand-in for it.
- P1 incidents. They reclassified P1s as P2 and added a "mitigated" state that stops the clock. I think it's bad, but the side effect was that we finally wrote down what P1 means, which was genuinely useful and had been a mess for two years.
- Database migrations per month. The number went up, nobody was gaming anything, it just turned out to measure nothing (we were shipping more features). Not cheating, just useless.

A guy on my team who reads a lot of this stuff says there are basically four seperate ways a metric goes wrong and they need different fixes: (a) the metric is a noisy shadow of the thing we want, so the top scorers aren't actually the best, like picking people by a test that only half correlates with anything; (b) the metric and the real goal are related but only in the normal range, and once you push to the extremes the relationship flattens or flips; (c) the metric is a symptom of the goal rather than a cause of it, so moving the number doesn't move the goal, like judging a hospital by how many X-rays it does; (d) people just lie, dress up, or game the numbers to fool whoever's reading the dashboard.

What I want:

1. Is his four-way split the right cut, or does it need surgery? Is there a simpler principle that does the same work? If a clause only exists to save one of my verdicts, say so.
2. Which of my verdicts does the best version of the principle actually support, and which does it say I have to give up? I'd rather lose a verdict than have a principle with a special exception for every case.
3. I'm about to roll out time-to-first-response for our on-call rotation (how long until someone acknowledges an alert). I'm worried it'll produce instant useless acknowledgements. Tell me what the principle says about that one, and whether it's the ticket-closing case, the build-time case, or neither.
4. Give me its verdict on a metric I haven't listed, so I can tell whether the principle is doing any work or just restating my gut.

Plain English, please, and no reading list, I'm definately not going to read the papers.
