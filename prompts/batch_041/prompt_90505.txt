Two philosophers are arguing about Avicenna's flying man. Write their dialogue.

The thought experiment: imagine a person created all at once, fully formed, but suspended in empty air — unable to touch anything, his limbs held apart so they do not touch one another, his sight screened from every external object, so that he receives no sensation either of the world or of his own body. Turned toward himself, Avicenna says, such a person would affirm that he exists while affirming nothing about his limbs, organs, or anything external — and would not, even if he imagined a hand, take it for part of himself.

Speaker A holds that the argument succeeds, and succeeds in establishing something substantive: that the self (or soul) is not a body, is not known through the body, and does not depend on the body for its existence — that it is a self-subsistent immaterial thing. Speaker B holds that it establishes nothing of the kind. Both are strong; neither is a foil. Each must take up the other's actual arguments and concede a point when the other lands one; the exchange should not be two monologues passing in the night.

Have them work through at least the following, in whatever order suits the conversation.

1. What, precisely, is the flying man supposed to be aware of, and by what route — a perception, an intuition, a judgment, the mere absence of any bodily experience? Does the description already presuppose that he is aware of himself as a thing, so that the conclusion is smuggled into the setup? A should state which reading of the experiment he is defending, and B should attack that reading rather than a weaker one.

2. The gap between the epistemic and the metaphysical. Granted that self-awareness does not require bodily sensation, does anything follow about what the self can exist without, or about what it is? Press the step from "I am aware of myself without being aware of any body" to "I am not a body, and can exist apart from one." A should supply the bridging premise if there is one — or deny that the experiment is meant as that kind of proof at all, and say what work it does instead.

3. Individuation. Suppose the flying man grasps only that he exists — no body, no place, no history, no distinguishing mark. If individual souls are distinguished by their relation to particular bodies, as Avicenna's own system seems to require, then what he grasps cannot be this soul rather than any other; the experiment appears to yield a bare subject of awareness rather than a person. A must then explain how the bare subject and the individuated soul are one and the same.

4. The body as instrument. Even on Avicenna's own account the intellect is not wholly detached from the body — it is said to require the brain as its instrument, even where it does not require the external senses. B may press this: if the mind needs an organ, the flying man's self-awareness may be conditioned by a body after all. A should say whether the flying man in the scenario has a brain, and whether that matters.

5. The parasitism objection. The scenario is assembled from materials supplied by embodied experience — limbs, air, senses, the very notion of a body to be subtracted. Can an act of imagination that borrows from the body establish the possibility of a state that leaves the body out? A may reply that this proves too much, since every thought experiment borrows from experience, or that the experiment is not really about the imagined scenario but about the order of discovery it dramatizes.

About 1,000 words. No citations and no quotations: neither speaker appeals to the authority of a text, and neither is given lines attributed to the historical Avicenna. Argue the case on its merits, and leave it genuinely open which speaker has the better of it — or, if one clearly does, make the other's case hard to answer before it loses.
