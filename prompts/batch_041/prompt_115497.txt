I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and now help set the graduate comprehensive examination in philosophy of science at my university. I would like one essay question on the Condorcet jury theorem, and I want it to turn on a point the textbook treatments pass over. The theorem's competence and independence conditions are stated relative to a fixed, exhaustive partition of possible answers, exactly one of which is correct, and each voter's reliability is defined against that partition. In my old field we would never have granted that the space of answers was given in advance — half the work was constructing the alternatives, and the reliability of an instrument was always relative to a scheme of classification we had chosen. I would like the question to make candidates say what becomes of the theorem's guarantee when the answer space is not given to the voters but constructed by them in the course of deliberating: whether the result can be restated without that assumption, what would have to replace it, and what the theorem then does and does not support for real deliberative bodies, such as a scientific committee or a review panel. Please supply the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with a one-line note on the error that typically costs a candidate that mark. The question should be answerable by a candidate who knows the theorem's statement and its usual conditions, but not by one who can only recite them.
