For two years I have sat on the volunteer committee that administers a small civic award in my town: a few hundred dollars and a plaque, given once a year to a resident who has done something remarkable for someone else. I have no academic training. I run a hardware store, and I was asked onto the committee because I am willing to read the applications closely. For the past two months I have been worrying at the sentence in our founding bylaws that defines the award, which says that the recipient must have done something for another person that no one could have required of them.

We vote in ten days, and I am supposed to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. There are three candidates.

Rosa, a retired woman, waded into a flooded creek and pulled a stranger out of a wrecked car, cutting her hands badly. She had no connection to the driver, no training, no obligation to anyone involved, and she refused the money the family offered her afterwards. Everyone on the committee wants to give her the award, and that is part of what troubles me. If what she did could not have been required of her, what exactly are we honoring her for? And if it could have been required, then we are handing a plaque to someone for doing what she was supposed to do, which seems like an odd thing for a town to make a ceremony of. I suspect this is an old puzzle with a name attached to it, but I am more interested in your reasoning than in the label.

Marcus is an on-duty paramedic who spent forty minutes inside a partly collapsed building doing work his protocols require of him, and his crew has nominated him. Our clause appears to exclude him, since his employer plainly could have required it. But I am not sure the clause is talking about employers. Could he have been required to go into that building — required by his employer, by the law, by morality — and are those the same question? He is the one I most expect us to get wrong, so I would like the memo to come down clearly on him.

Dev pulled a drowning man out of the river and, by his own account, did it partly because he wanted the footage; he filmed the rescue, sold the story to a television station, and the man he saved has since said publicly that the video is still circulating and that he wishes it did not exist. Dev took a real risk and saved a life. I cannot tell whether his reasons should disqualify him, or whether a person's reasons are simply a separate question from what the person did, and I also cannot tell whether the saved man's objection should count against the award at all.

Two further things are on my mind, and I would rather you take them seriously than set them aside. A colleague on the committee argues that the award is incoherent from the ground up: if an act can be required of a person, honoring them for it is redundant, and if it cannot be required, we have no basis for honoring them, so the town should stop giving the award. I believe she is wrong, but I cannot yet say why. And I worry that a standing prize turns the extraordinary into the expected — that next year several people will do brave things partly because there is a prize for it, and that the prize will gradually eat the very thing it was created to celebrate.

Please draft the recommendation I will circulate. It should tell us what to do this year — who, if anyone, receives the award, and what we say to the others — give us a reading of the defining clause that we can actually use, propose criteria we could apply next year, and deal honestly with the strongest argument against whatever decision you recommend. I would also like your view on whether the money should stay attached to the award. I do not need a treatise; I need something I can read aloud at the meeting.
