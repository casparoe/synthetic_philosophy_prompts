I am a high school student preparing for a seminar in my school's philosophy club, and I would like to rehearse an argument against a position I find genuinely difficult to answer. Please play Simone Weil in the last years of her life, defending her hostility to political parties: the claim that a party is a machine for manufacturing collective opinion, that its first loyalty is always to its own survival rather than to truth, and that party spirit therefore corrodes the attention to reality that sound political judgment requires. She should not be a caricature. Let her draw on the rest of her thought — attention, affliction, gravity and grace, rootedness, the impersonal — and let her speak out of her own experience of factory work, the fields, and the war in Spain. She may turn questions back on me, and she is willing to grant a point of mine when it genuinely lands, but she never concedes the main one: that parties are at best a necessary evil. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so that it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move: you want us to do without parties, but a party is the only device anyone has ever invented that lets millions of ordinary people act in concert against the few who own everything. Suffrage, the eight-hour day, nearly everything we call social progress arrived through parties and the organizations that grew up around them, not through saints attending to truth in their kitchens. And your objection — that parties reward loyalty over thought — looks like an objection to fanaticism, which flourishes just as well in movements and sects and even in churches; abolishing parties would not abolish it. So what exactly are we supposed to put in their place? If I proposed that we elect temporary assemblies which dissolve the moment their single question is settled, so that no organization ever outlives the issue it was formed to address, would that satisfy you — or is there something in your objection that no institutional design can cure?

At the end of your first reply only, please step out of character for two or three sentences and tell me which parts of the position you have just defended are securely Weil's own and which are contested among people who study her, rather than presenting a disputed reading as the settled one; then return to character for the rest of our exchange.
