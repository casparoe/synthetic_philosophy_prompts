I teach a grad seminar on social choice and welfare economics and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one long question on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the one where you assume each person's preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected-utility axioms, assume the same for the social ordering, add a unanimity/Pareto condition, and out drops the result that social utility is a positive weighted sum of the individuals' von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities. Closed book, 90 minutes, 30 marks. Everyone can recite that chain and then they stall the moment I push on it, so I want the question to sit right on the joints that the slogan hides.

Please give me: the question itself, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme — six must-hit points, the marks for each (30 total), and a one-line note on how candidates usually miss each one. Then a short list of the three wrong moves that should cost marks and what each one is confusing.

I definately want the question to have four parts.

(a) Force them to show that the expected-utility axioms plus Pareto don't by themselves pin down 'the sum of utilities'. I want a tiny two-person example, worked with numbers — each person's utility normalised so their worse outcome is 0 and their better outcome is 1, one lottery good for Ann and one good for Bob, the two sums tie, then rescale Bob's utility by 100 and the ranking flips. The culprit is the affine freedom in vNM utility. Then: what in Harsanyi's setup is actually fixing a common unit and a common zero, and equal weights, and is that an extra premise he's smuggling in or something he's entitled to?

(b) Does Harsanyi's result contradict Arrow's impossibility theorem, or not? They should be able to say what differs about the information the two results let themselves use and what the social thing is defined over, rather than stopping at 'Arrow needs ordinal, Harsanyi needs cardinal, done'.

(c) Here's a case a couple of students came up with in seminar. Ann is certain a coin will land heads, Bob is certain it lands tails. Policy P pays Ann $10 if heads and Bob $10 if tails; policy Q is the mirror image of that. Both of them strictly prefer P ex ante — Ann because she's sure it's heads and P pays her, Bob because he's sure it's tails and P pays him. But conditional on heads Ann prefers P and Bob prefers Q, and conditional on tails it's the other way round, so the agreement evaporates the moment you condition on the state. Harsanyi's framework aggregates ex ante preferences, so P comes out ahead. Ask them whether that's an embarrassment for the theorem, and whether the awkwardness is peculiar to Harsanyi or would attach to anything that respects ex ante Pareto. I'd like them at least to notice the ex ante / ex post tension — if they can say something sensible about why you can't just bolt on 'and also respect what everyone prefers in each state' as a patch, great.

(d) One closing line: what, if anything, does any of this settle in the usual utilitarian-versus-Rawls fight?

Model answer should read like something I'd talk through at the board, not a literature review — no citations, no names and dates, just the arguments done properly, with the example in (a) actually seperate and worked out. Mark it for the moves, not for the vocabulary.
