My friend is a historian of how moral norms die and she has a running theory about which deaths were arguements, which were interests, and which were honour. Her track record: dueling in Britain fizzled out in the middle of the nineteenth century, and she insists that wasn't because anyone found a new argument — the case against it had been made since the seventeenth century and nobody moved. What changed is that refusing a challenge became the gentlemanly thing, and the men who insisted on fighting were the ones whose careers and credit couldn't take the hit. Foot binding, same shape: the reformers bounced off for decades, and what did it was elite families unbinding first, so bound feet went from the mark of the refined to the mark of the backwoods — though she'll add that once binding stopped paying, the new schools and factories wanted women who could move. British abolition she tells as an interests story: the moral case is ancient, the West India interest just lost its weight to the free-traders — though she'll also say slaveholding had stopped being respectable in the circles that ran the country. So she sounds like a status person on two of them and an interest person on the third, and I can't tell if that's one rule or two.

The case she hasn't ruled on: hanging people in front of a crowd, which moved behind prison walls in the second half of the nineteenth century, Britain first. Nobody's wallet got better when that happened — public hangings were cheap, the crowd loved them, and everyone thought the publicity was the whole point, detterence-wise. What changed is that the men presiding couldn't stand it any more: the spectacle had become degrading to the authorities rather than to the condemned. So what's she going to say? Is this a status death or an interest death, and will she wriggle by saying the crowd had become a mob and the state had an interest in order? Best guess, with your reasons, and name the one further case that would actually settle which rule she's running — and if her verdicts genuinely don't settle it, say so and tell me what she'd be signing up for either way.
