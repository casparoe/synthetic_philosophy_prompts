Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether medicine should be confined to restoring health or may also improve people beyond it, and the whole dispute turned on an analogy she treated as decisive. Her picture was of medicine as a repair shop: you bring in something that is broken, the mechanic returns it to the condition it is supposed to be in, and that is the entire job; deliberately making a healthy thing faster or stronger than it was built to be is a different activity with a different purpose and simply is not what the shop is for. From this she drew the conclusion that the therapy/enhancement distinction is principled rather than a matter of taste, and that a physician who enhances has stepped outside medicine altogether.

I do not think the analogy can bear that weight, but I could not pin down where it slips, so I want it audited properly rather than merely waved away. Lay out the correspondence in full — what stands in for the broken part, the factory specification, the mechanic's remit, the owner's request — and then show me where the mapping fails. A car has designers and a determinate specification of what it is for, whereas a human body arguably has neither, and the repair-shop picture presupposes a baseline condition that is fixed independently of what anyone happens to want; I suspect that presupposition is quietly doing all the work. Tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion she wants and which are mere scenery, and which features of the source case the analogy smuggles into the target — a designer, a purpose, a value-neutral notion of normality — and whether the conclusion survives once those imports are made explicit. Notice also that her analogy treats the improvement as an intrinsic good, a better machine, whereas much of the unease about enhancement is positional, about outdoing others in a competition; say whether that gap is fatal to the analogy or incidental to it. If the repair shop cannot carry the conclusion, tell me what a better analogy would be and what it would have to get right — or say plainly that no single analogy can settle a distinction this contested, and that the real disagreement is about what medicine is for rather than about where to draw a line in human biology.
