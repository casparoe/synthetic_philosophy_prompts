Write a short story of roughly 1,600 to 2,000 words whose spine is a pair of decisions with the shape of the Allais paradox. The story must never name the paradox or the axiom it violates, and no character may treat the pattern as a known curiosity rather than as a problem about herself.

The structure, which the story must reproduce exactly. The first decision sets a certain, moderate good against a gamble that yields that same moderate good with high probability, a distinctly larger good with a small probability, and the worst outcome in the set with a very small probability. The protagonist takes the certain good. The second decision is the same pair once the high-probability branch — the branch on which both options deliver the same moderate good — is known to have fallen away, so that what remains is a modest chance of the moderate good against a slightly smaller chance of the larger good, with nothing in the remainder. Here she takes the gamble. Both choices must arrive as ordinary decisions inside the life of the story, separated in time and carrying different emotional weather, so that nothing tells the reader they belong together. If the second decision is made by someone else — a sibling, a patient — the story must show that she would choose as that person does.

Requirements:

- Somewhere in the story a third party reconstructs both decisions for her, in plain speech, and shows her that they cannot both stand if what she cares about is outcomes and how likely they are. Give that person a fair case and no hectoring.
- Two characters must voice the two live defences in ordinary language, neither delivered as the author's verdict. One argues that she is paying to avoid a feeling, and that an outcome should weigh the same however it is reached. The other argues that a small chance of ending with nothing is not the same thing when it is the loss of a certainty, and that someone who was certain and then lost is not in the position of someone who merely drew a bad ticket.
- The story must force her to see that defending her first choice commits her to something: that the bad outcome is worse than its probability warrants, or that she is choosing not to know, or that a sure thing is a different kind of good rather than merely a likelier one. She may accept one of these or refuse them all; either way it must register as a fact about her character, not as a resolution of the problem.
- Include, in no more than a paragraph, a small, low-stakes gamble she refuses, so that a reader can see that what moves her is certainty and not the size of the stakes.
- End with her having to decide how the same choice is presented to other people — as a single gamble, or as two stages with the common branch made visible — where the two presentations lead people to choose differently. The story should show this without concluding that either set of choosers is mistaken.

Constraints:

- Invent the place, the people, the condition, and the treatments. Use no real disease, drug, institution, or trial, and make no clinical or statistical claims about the world.
- The probabilities must be internally consistent and must genuinely have the shape above: a reader who knows the paradox should be able to set the four options out as a table and confirm that the second decision is the first with the common branch removed. In the prose, give the odds the way people speak them, never as a table or a list.
- No technical vocabulary in narration or dialogue: not expected utility, axiom, independence, utility, decision theory, paradox. One character may say "irrational" once; the story must not endorse it.
- Third person, past tense, a title, no epigraph, no framing device, and no closing paragraph that explains what the story was about. End on an action or an image.
- A reader who has never heard of the paradox should read it as a story about a person under pressure. A reader who has should be able to reconstruct the structure from the text alone.
