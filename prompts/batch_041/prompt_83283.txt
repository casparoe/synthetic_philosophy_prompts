I'm writing a section of a seminar paper on the Pasadena paradox — the family of infinite-stage gambles whose expected value is not large but undefined, either because the payoff series converges only conditionally, so that permuting the terms moves the sum (and the standard rearrangement result says a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to total any number at all, or to diverge), or because the partial sums never settle. The claim my section wants to make is fairly aggressive: that undefinedness of this kind is not a harmless gap in expected utility theory but a vacuum that rival decision rules fill in mutually incompatible ways, and that the vacuum cannot be repaired by any rule that looks only at the gamble's payoff distribution. I would rather make that argument with one carefully engineered case that I can present in a page and check line by line than with generalities, and I would like you to construct the case. To show you where I have got to: I have been playing with a ticket that at stage n pays, with probability 2^(-n), the amount (−1)^(n+1)·2^n, so that the terms of the expected-value series are +1, −1, +1, −1, …, the partial sums oscillate between 0 and 1, and the expected value is undefined, while the Abel sum — the limit of ∑ p_n x_n r^n as r rises to 1 from below — is 1/2, a value the running totals of the schedule never converge to. That example shows a regularising rule speaking where the printed-order rule is silent; what I need now is a case where the two speak and disagree in direction.

The case I want is this. Two gambles, G and H, each given as a printed schedule: at stage n the ticket pays a finite amount x_n with positive probability p_n, the p_n summing to 1, with both p_n and x_n given by formulas in n, so that the stage order belongs to the ticket and the printed-order rule is well defined — that rule values a gamble by the limit of the partial sums ∑_{n≤N} p_n x_n, and delivers no verdict when that limit fails to exist. I want the following, each item verified rather than asserted. First, both gambles are to have conditionally convergent payoff series, so that each has a printed-order value and neither has an expected value; show the convergence, show that the series of absolute values diverges, and note that this forces the positive and negative parts of the expectation each to be infinite, which in turn is possible only if the payoffs are unbounded above and below — indeed, please prove that with payoffs bounded in absolute value and probabilities summing to 1 the payoff series must converge absolutely, since otherwise a reader will suspect you of smuggling in an infinity. Second, the printed-order rule must rank G above H, and Abel summation must agree with that verdict; explain why a summation method that agrees with ordinary summation wherever the latter converges cannot do otherwise, so that these two rules can differ only where one of them falls silent. Third, first-order stochastic dominance must be silent on the pair — neither distribution dominates the other — which you should prove by exhibiting two payoff levels at which the survival functions cross. Fourth, and this is the part I care about most, find a rule R that ranks H above G, reversing the printed-order verdict, while (a) being a function of the gamble's payoff distribution alone, so that it cannot see the printed order, (b) being defined on both G and H despite their undefined expectations, and (c) agreeing with expected value on every gamble whose expected value is well defined. If (c) can be met, the disagreement between R and expected value is confined to gambles with undefined expected value, which is precisely the philosophical point I am after; if it cannot be met, I want to know exactly which clause fails and why, rather than a case that quietly drops it. The median, the probability of coming out ahead, and the limit of the sums of the N largest-magnitude terms are obvious candidates for R, but they are not equally respectable as candidate theories of rational choice, and I would rather have your judgement than a menu.

Alongside the schedules I want the check: the partial sums for the first several stages of each, the computed verdicts of all four rules, an explicit statement of which structural feature of the case produces the reversal, and an account of what happens to the reversal when that feature is removed — in particular, whether it survives replacing the two gambles by their finite truncations or by damped versions whose payoff series converge absolutely. If it survives, the case is teaching only the familiar lesson that means and medians can part company, not the lesson I want, and you should say so plainly and then tell me either how to modify the case minimally so that the undefinedness becomes load-bearing or why no modification can. Then I want the counter-argument in its strongest form, from someone who holds that undefined expected value is a defect in the subject matter rather than a gap to be filled: presumably they will say that a rule about printed order is a rule about presentations rather than about gambles, since permuting the (p_n, x_n) pairs does not alter the distribution and therefore does not alter the gamble, and that a rule which must be defined on a gamble with undefined expectation has already conceded the point. Tell me whether that objection lands and whether anything of my section survives it. Keep the scenario itself to a page and statable to someone who has never met an infinite gamble — a person at a fair being handed two tickets with the schedules printed on them — and don't bother with citations or a reading list; I will add those myself.
