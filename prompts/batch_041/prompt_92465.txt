My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the Condorcet jury theorem and got nowhere, so I would like to settle the disagreement by building an instrument rather than staging another round of assertion. He holds that the theorem is the strongest available argument that majority rule tracks the truth: if each voter is even slightly more likely than not to get a question right, and the question has a right answer, then a large group's majority is almost certainly right, which is what makes majority rule more than a truce. I hold that this is a conditional whose assumptions real bodies rarely satisfy, and that he is helping himself to more than the mathematics gives him. Underneath that disagreement sits a second one: he thinks that putting the same factual question to five language models and going with the majority answer is an instance of the same phenomenon, and therefore reliable, whereas I think shared training data makes those votes about as independent as the opinions of two people who read the same newspaper.

Please write a decision tree for a reading group of graduate students in philosophy and economics who can state the theorem but have never asked themselves which of its assumptions they are willing to give up. A reader should be able to walk the tree and discover which position on the theorem's significance they actually hold, without knowing in advance which leaf they are heading for.

Build the tree from the logical space of the theorem rather than from a list of names. Each internal node should be a question the reader can answer about their own view, phrased as a claim to accept or reject rather than as an invitation to pronounce on the theorem's importance. Nodes I want somewhere on the tree: whether the question being decided has a correct answer independent of what the group thinks; whether voters share an interest in getting it right or have conflicting preferences about the outcome; whether individual competence is above one half; whether competence is uniform across voters or only averages above one half; whether votes are independent once you condition on the correct answer, and if not, whether the dependence is fatal or merely shrinks the effective size of the group; whether voters vote sincerely; whether the procedure is simple majority or something else; whether the reader is relying on the limiting claim that large groups are nearly infallible or on the weaker claim that adding competent voters never hurts; and whether the reader treats the majority being probably right as entailing that an individual dissenter should defer.

Leaves should be named positions, each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The tree must be able to reach all of the following: the theorem vindicates majoritarian democracy; the theorem is true but empty because its assumptions are never jointly satisfied; the theorem is a design manual for institutions rather than a description of existing ones; the theorem argues for weighting votes by competence instead of counting them equally; the theorem's real lesson concerns the diversity of the group rather than the competence of its members; the theorem has no bearing on legitimacy because legitimacy is procedural; and the theorem is a fact about information aggregation with no normative upshot whatsoever. Add any further leaves the space requires.

Use these cases as tie-breakers where branches run close: a twelve-person criminal jury; a departmental hiring vote in which everyone has read the same file; a prediction market on an election; a majority vote among five language models on a factual question; a citizens' assembly that deliberates for a weekend and then votes; and a referendum on a technical question in which most voters take their cue from a single trusted source. Say which branch each case is needed to test.

Finally, mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, together with the further question or case that would separate them. Aim for eight to twelve internal nodes and six to ten leaves. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the instrument.
