ok so I'm a novelist — five books, literary fiction, the kind where nothing much happens and everyone's marriage quietly ends — and I've been chewing on something I can't get straight, so I want you to help me think it through properly.

There's a piece Dennett wrote near the end of his life where he goes after AI chatbots and calls them counterfeit people, and the gist, the way I read it, is that making them isn't just tacky, it's a wrong — like passing fake money, or poisoning a well we all drink from. Which is odd coming from him, because he spent his whole career telling us the mind isn't a magic extra ingredient, that we get along by treating each other as agents with beliefs and wants, and that a machine could in principle have a mind. Then at the end, that. I want to know whether that's a contradiction or just two halves of one view, and I want to know it in my own language, not in his.

So: take on the role of someone who's a genuine philosopher of mind — you know Dennett's actual positions, not the meme version — but who also knows how fiction gets made from the inside. Character, voice, point of view, interiority, free indirect, subtext, backstory, what's on the page versus what the reader builds, the reader's habit of putting a mind behind every sentence they read. Then restate his position on counterfeit people as faithfully as you can, using only that vocabulary. Not an analogy, not a vibe — an actual translation, the way you'd translate a poem into another language and then have to own up to what didn't come across.

Work in stages and show me the stages. First brainstorm: for each of his load-bearing terms — mind, person, agency, deception, harm, trust — what in my vocabulary could possibly play that role, and which ones have no candidate at all. Say the no-candidates out loud, don't paper over them. Then the translation itself. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what came over cleanly, what only came over if you fudge a word, and what can't be said in my vocabulary at all. And on that last pile, give me a view — when something doesn't survive the trip, is it my vocabulary being too thin, or his claim being less solid than it sounds? Both answers are live. Don't just pick the flattering one.

Some specific places where I feel the pull, because I think that's where the interesting stuff is:

1. Counterfeit needs an original. In my trade there is no original. Every character I've ever written is a forgery of someone who never existed, and I'm proud of the good ones. So what happens to the charge when you drop it into a vocabulary that has no genuine article to be false to? Is that word doing any real work, or is the whole thing riding on it?

2. The label. My counterfeit people come with a jacket that says novel, and the reader agrees in advance to be moved by them. If the wrong is unlicensed deception, then what separates me from a chatbot is a contract, not a metaphysics — and a contract is something my vocabulary has and, as far as I can tell, his doesn't. Tell me if that's right, and if it is, whether his real worry is about consent and framing rather than about minds. Because if the harm is a broken contract, I have some opinions about who's breaking it.

3. The mind behind the mind. Every fictional mind I make is parasitic on a real one — mine. Does anything in his toolkit register that? If the crux is that there's a person behind my fakes and no person behind the other fakes, then he needs a notion of authorship he never quite spells out, and the residue should show it.

4. Character autonomy. All of us say things like she wouldn't do that, or the character took over. It's a façon de parler and also not, if you've actually done it. Does that first-person report of a mind acting on its own have any counterpart in his picture, or is my vocabulary quietly committed to something he'd deny?

5. The grief problem. Readers cry at acknowledged fakes. If the intentional stance is the whole story, the counterfeit worry should collapse, because that stance is all we ever take toward each other. If it isn't the whole story, then tell me what in my vocabulary is supposed to mark the difference — and be honest if the answer is nothing.

Please don't give me a reading list or citations, I'm not writing a paper and I can't look anything up anyway. Work from what you already know. Where you're guessing at what he'd say, flag it as a guess instead of dressing it up as his view. I'd rather have an honest map with holes in it than a tidy one.
