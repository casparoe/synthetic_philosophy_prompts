A popular history of the self that I have been reading gives the following account of Avicenna's flying man, and I would like an audit of it before I trust anything else the book says about Islamic philosophy.

"Avicenna's flying man is one of the earliest thought experiments in the philosophy of mind, and its structure is simple. Suppose a man is created in an instant, fully formed, suspended in midair with his eyes covered and his limbs held apart from one another, so that he perceives nothing at all: no sight, no sound, no contact even with his own body, and no memory of any earlier state. Avicenna's claim is that this man would still affirm his own existence, and would affirm it without affirming the existence of his body, his limbs, or any of his organs. Nothing in the case delivers the body to him; something in the case delivers him to himself. What the argument shows, then, is that the soul is aware of itself directly, with no help from the senses, and therefore that the soul is a substance in its own right rather than a feature or an activity of the body. It is the first statement of the view that the mind is not the brain, and it anticipates Descartes's cogito almost exactly: as Descartes would later argue that the thinking self survives even the most radical doubt, Avicenna argues that a man stripped of every sensory input still knows that he is. Avicenna took the argument to prove that the human soul is immortal, since a thing that can be known to exist without the body cannot owe its existence to the body, and so must survive the body's death. He also took it to be an argument that requires no prior philosophy: the man in the thought experiment discovers his own existence from the inside, and any reader who performs the experiment will do the same. Critics say that a man with no sensations at all would be unconscious, and that Avicenna's description quietly assumes the very self-awareness it is meant to prove — so the thought experiment begs the question and establishes nothing."

A lecturer in a course I am taking then offered his own diagnosis of the passage, which I would like assessed as well. He says the paragraph runs together two readings that have to be kept apart: a thin reading, on which the thought experiment establishes only that the self's awareness of itself is not a deliverance of any bodily sense — that self-awareness is not a species of perception — and a thick reading, on which it establishes that the self is an immaterial substance whose existence does not depend on the body. The lecturer's claim is that the thought experiment supports at most the thin reading, while Avicenna's argument for the soul's survival needs the thick one, and he calls the space between them "the flying man's leap." My own instinct is that the book's paragraph is broadly right — the flying man is supposed to show that the soul is not the body — and that the lecturer is over-refining a fairly straightforward point. Tell me where I am wrong.

I would like the audit to do the following. First, go through the paragraph clause by clause and say what a specialist in Avicenna's psychology would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would reject outright, keeping separate (a) what is accurate, (b) what is a standard simplification of the popular literature rather than an error, (c) what is a mistake peculiar to this writer, and (d) what is simply wrong. Second, tell me which of the paragraph's three larger claims — the Descartes comparison, the claim that the argument proves immortality, and the claim that it needs no prior philosophy — do the most work in the paragraph, and whether the closing verdict about immortality follows from anything that survives correction. Third, assess the lecturer: is his thin/thick distinction well drawn, is the gap he names a real one or does Avicenna move from the thin to the thick claim by a further argument rather than a leap, is his diagnosis a distinct criticism or the book's own immortality error re-described, and does Avicenna himself endorse anything like the thin reading, or is the lecturer importing a distinction from outside? Fourth, say whether the critics' objection that the man would be unconscious or that the description begs the question is a serious objection or a misunderstanding of what the thought experiment is for.

Take a definite position throughout rather than laying out the interpretive options for me to choose from. Where specialists disagree, tell me which way you would bet and what evidence would change your mind, and flag the two or three judgments you are least confident about. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want to know what to trust in the paragraph and what the actual argumentative situation is.
