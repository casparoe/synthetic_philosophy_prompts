Random thing that's been bugging me and I can't decide if I'm being dense — I keep hearing that Dutch book arguments show incoherent credences are irrational, but I can't see how they get to the *epistemic* conclusion rather than just a prudential one. The mechanism is always "a bookie offers you a series of bets you accept, and you lose for sure", which only bites if your the sort of person who takes bets. If I refuse every bet on principle then there's no sure loss anywhere, so it looks like the argument shows at most that incoherent credences are a liability if you happen to gamble, not that they're irrational as credences. The money side confuses me in the same way: if I don't value money linearly, even the gambling version goes soft, which makes me suspect the whole thing is really an argument about dollars rather than about belief.

Someone told me there's a version where you drop the betting entirely (depragmatised? where the loss is that you'd count each bet in the package as fair while counting the package itself as a bad deal), and other people say we should just do it with accuracy/dominance instead. Is that a patch on a broken argument, or is the betting machinery doing real work that the alternatives can't? Bottom line I'm after: what is the argument actually supposed to establish, and is someone who never bets off the hook?
