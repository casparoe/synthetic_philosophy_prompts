I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have written myself into a structural problem that I would rather have you solve as a piece of design than as an essay. The spine of my book is a single recurring decision: in four separate episodes a character of mine — a harbour pilot, a volunteer on a rescue launch — must choose between saving one person and saving several, and I want every principal in the novel to hold a considered position on the matter that stays fixed across all four episodes. What I need is an instrument: a decision tree, or a scored quiz if you judge the material better suited to that, which takes a reader's answers to concrete cases and returns the position those answers commit them to. Its purpose is twofold. First, it must let me tell my characters apart by what they would actually do rather than by the label each of them would reach for. Second, and more useful to me, it must catch the places where I have written a character inconsistently — where no position fits the pattern of answers — and in those cases I would much prefer that it fail to classify them than force a fit.

Build the tree from the following four episodes, which are the ones actually in the manuscript, rather than from a list of names. (A) One stranger is trapped on a sandbar; five strangers are aboard a boat taking on water; the pilot can reach only one group before the tide, and nothing distinguishes the six except their number. (B) As in A, except that the five are the surviving members of one family and the one is a stranger the pilot has never met. (C) The one is the person whose negligence caused the wreck and the five are blameless; the pilot may also decline to choose at all and let a mechanical release decide, or set a lottery running and abide by it. (D) The one is a young child with a whole life before her; the five are elderly and frail; all six will die within the hour if not reached. Each branch node should be a question a reader could answer about one of these cases without already knowing where the answer leads — so no node may simply ask whether the numbers count, or whether consequences matter, or whether relationships matter. The distinctions I care most about, and around which I suspect the tree should be organised, are these: whether the bare fact of there being five supplies any reason at all that the lone person's solitude cannot match; if it does, whether it is because five lives simply outweigh one, or only because five claims of equal weight are in play and none of them may be discarded by the rescuer's say-so; whether, on the latter view, fairness requires an equal chance for each of the six, or permits going straight to the five because they are more, or permits going to the one because no one's claim exceeds anyone else's; what exactly is being weighed — a life, a life's remaining span, or the person's stake in continuing; whether knowing the five, or knowing the one to be at fault, alters the answer or is beside the point; and whether, having gone to the five, the rescuer owes the one anything afterwards, a wrong acknowledged or a debt carried, or whether there is simply nothing to answer for.

The leaves of the tree should be the positions themselves, named where a name exists and marked unnamed where it does not: the view on which the larger number decides; the view on which number counts but each additional person counts for less, or counts more when the many are worse off; the view on which the claims are equal and a fair chance, not the count, is what is owed; the view on which number is morally inert and going to the one is permitted; the view on which partiality or prior acquaintance decides; the view on which fault weakens or forfeits a claim; the view on which number counts up to some threshold and then a constraint blocks using the one; and the view on which no option is clean and something is owed to whoever is left. For each leaf, give me one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then work the other way as well: show me which combinations of answers to your nodes no leaf occupies, and diagnose each empty space briefly — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but repellent, or merely because no one has bothered to hold it? Flag any two leaves your instrument cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap. Keep the tree to no more than twelve nodes and no more than nine leaves, and phrase every question for a reader of fiction rather than for a philosopher.
