I'm in a reading group that's been working through contemporary divine command theory, and we've gotten stuck in a way that's started to feel like a personality difference rather than a philosophical one. Here's the setup. Nobody in the group defends the crude version where "good" just means "commanded by God" -- that one everyone agrees dies on the Euthyphro horns, since either God's commands are arbitrary or there's a standard of goodness God answers to. What we're actually fighting about is the sophisticated version: wrongness consists in being contrary to the commands of a God who is essentially loving, so the commands are constrained from the inside, as it were, by God's own nature rather than by some external standard, and the claim is that the sentence "God is good" comes out as a substantive truth rather than a tautology. The critics in the group say this doesn't help. Either God's loving nature is itself the standard, in which case goodness is now grounded in a nature rather than in commands and the commands are doing no work, or else that nature is good because of some further fact and we're right back on the second horn. The defenders say the objection begs the question, because it assumes goodness has to be grounded in something other than God, and that being grounded in God's nature is not the same thing as being arbitrary.

We've gone around this for months and I'm starting to think we're not even having the same arguement. My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than just agree with, is that the disagreement isn't really about God at all -- it's about what a "because" has to do, specifically whether the relation that makes something good has to be explanatory in a way that terminates a regress, and whether a brute stopping point counts as arbitrary or just as bedrock. If that's right, the same dilemma should recur for any view that makes goodness depend on a response: an ideal observer's approval, a community's practices, our own sentiments. And then the interesting question becomes why the theological version feels uniquely vicious when the secular ones get a pass.

So diagnose this for me. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "because," two groups answering different questions under one heading (one about the meaning of moral terms, one about the metaphysical ground of moral facts), or bedrock intuitions about arbitrariness that neither side can argue for? Work from what each side actually says in the versions I described, tell me straight whether my hypothesis is right or wrong, and say what if anything would count as progress here -- a distinction, a reformulation, an argument that the dilemma is a false one, or an honest admission that nothing will move it. Also say a word about whether the secular analogues are definately in the same boat or a different one. American English, please, and keep it under 600 words: verdict in the first two sentences, then the reasoning, no throat-clearing.
