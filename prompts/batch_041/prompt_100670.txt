As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on the experience machine and its uneven afterlife in contemporary theories of well-being. I want to move past the well-rehearsed finding that many people would refuse to plug in. Please describe the most important implications of the experience machine thought experiment—and of a variant in which the machine's inhabitants know they are inside it, retain veridical memories of their pre-machine lives, and still judge their machine lives to be going well—for the three main families of theories of well-being: hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list accounts. I am especially interested in what each theory must say about whether well-being is wholly a matter of how a life feels from the inside; whether the variant collapses the distinction between well-being and autonomy, achievement, or reality-contact; and whether any of the theories can accommodate the intuition that a machine life might be pleasant, desired, and even objectively valuable in some respects while still being prudentially deficient. Please write in American English, and please avoid citations, reading lists, or literature surveys; I want the conceptual implications spelled out, not a bibliography.
