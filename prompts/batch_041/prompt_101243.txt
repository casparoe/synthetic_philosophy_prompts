A student in my graduate seminar has reconstructed the situationist critique of virtue ethics as a single argument, and neither of us can decide whether it is sound or merely seductive. I would value your diagnosis.

(1) A virtue is a stable disposition to act, feel and judge in ways characteristic of that virtue.
(2) If a disposition is genuinely stable, it must manifest itself across the whole range of situations in which it is relevant.
(3) The experimental literature reports that ordinary behaviour is powerfully shifted by seemingly trivial situational variables, and that cross-situational correlations between trait-relevant behaviours are at best modest.
(4) So in the situations where people behave well, it is the situation rather than the agent that is doing the explanatory work.
(5) So no one actually possesses any virtue.
(6) On any recognisable form of virtue ethics, the good life consists in the exercise of the virtues.
(7) So virtue ethics is untenable.
(8) Yet the same reasoning would strip the experimenters of the epistemic dispositions their own findings presuppose, and would dissolve every ordinary dispositional ascription — the solubility of salt, the fragility of glass, my colleague's rudeness — since each is defeated by some condition or other. That cannot be right.

I should say plainly that I am attached to (7) being false and would rather not surrender (3), which I take to be well supported. So tell me which step to give up, why it looked so plausible, and what giving it up costs — in particular, what becomes of the situationist critique once that step is withdrawn. Please answer in British English.
