I am a retired analytical chemist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of art, and next month I must present a proposal to the collections committee of the university museum where I serve as a trustee. The proposal concerns a painting in the museum's European collection that I helped identify as a twentieth-century forgery through pigment analysis. I want the committee to keep the painting on view, with a new label and a small accompanying case, rather than remove it to storage or deaccession it. My argument is that the value of an original is not a simple function of the hand that made it; it is a function of the object's full causal history, including the forger's intentions and choices, the materials and techniques available to the forger, the market and scholarly reception of the work as an original, and the moment of detection. On that view, the painting has a kind of authenticity the museum currently ignores: not authenticity as origin, but authenticity as a document of its own making and of the institution's own history. I call this the full-etiology account of authenticity. I am not claiming that the forgery is as valuable as the lost original, or that provenance rules should be abandoned; I am claiming that the museum's binary of original versus fake is too coarse.

The committee consists of the museum director, who cares above all about donor relations and public trust; the curator of European painting, who is a period specialist and has staked her reputation on the attribution; the conservator, who thinks in terms of material integrity and reversibility; the registrar and general counsel, who will worry about title, insurance, and any implication that the museum traffics in forgeries; the development officer, who will ask what this does to the value of the collection and to future gifts; and one external philosopher of art, who knows the analytic literature on authenticity and will press me on whether I am confusing aesthetic value with artistic value. The donor's family has been informed and is unhappy; they believe the work is an original and have hinted at legal action if we label it a forgery. The committee meets for ninety minutes; my presentation is fifteen minutes, and the rest is questions.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not the objections I would get from an art-theory seminar. Order them by how likely they are to come up and how much damage they could do to the proposal, and phrase each one the way that committee member would phrase it. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply; tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on; tell me where the reply is soft or where I am likely to be talking past them; and give me a fallback if the objection lands harder than I expect. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not let the philosopher of art's objection dominate the list just because it is the most theoretically sophisticated; the committee's decision will turn on the director, the curator, and the development officer.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. If the full-etiology account is a minority view, say so; if the literature is split on whether forgeries can have artistic value, or on whether authenticity is a property of objects or of narratives, say that too. Do not present a contested position as the consensus. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need the shape of the disagreement and the arguments, not the sources. I am a scientist by training and have only recently begun reading philosophy, so translate the standard moves for me without flattening them.
