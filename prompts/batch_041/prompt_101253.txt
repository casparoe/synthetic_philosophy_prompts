ok so I'm a third-year PhD student in a bioethics-ish program and I keep running into the same analogy in seminars, and I want to actually stress-test it rather than just roll my eyes at it again.

Here's the setup. In a works-in-progress session last month, a visiting speaker defended the therapy/enhancement distinction with a car analogy. Roughly: a car that's broken gets repaired back to factory spec; a car you soup up goes past spec. Medicine repairs, enhancement tunes. He used this to argue that the distinction is not just real but administratively trackable — which is why, he said, insurers and regulators can cover the first and decline the second without arbitrariness. Someone in the back said "but specs are written by the manufacturer," he said "exactly, and nature wrote ours," and we moved on to the next paper. I've been annoyed about it ever since.

What I want from you: an audit of the analogy, not a defence or a takedown of the distinction as such. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of factory spec, the mechanic, the broken part, the aftermarket part, the warranty, the owner's wishes. Then the places the mapping breaks, and I want you to be specific about which breaks matter *for the work he's making the analogy do* (that the line is real, and that coverage rules can track it) versus which are just decoration. My own list so far: cars have designers and written specs and biology doesn't; biological norms are statistical and environment-dependent rather than canonical; "function" is itself contested; most traits sit on continua so "within spec" has no threshold; a lot of uncontroversial medicine (vaccines, statins for primary prevention, glasses, C-sections) looks like upgrading; and a car can't want to be modified. I don't know if that last one is a real disanalogy or just me being precious about autonomy.

So: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one people actually use for this, or is the honest answer that no single analogy can do the job because the distinction is a cluster of different cuts (normality, disease, insurance risk, desert) that only look like one line? Don't send me reading lists, I've read enough of this literature to be dangerous — just do the mapping and the verdict.
