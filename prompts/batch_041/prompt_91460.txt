I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Leibniz's moral philosophy, and I want to build a class session out of the disagreement rather than out of the texts. My friend's line was that Leibniz's definition of justice as the benevolence of the wise — the idea that loving someone is taking disinterested delight in their perfection, and that the disposition to do them good has to be steered by knowledge rather than by pity — is either a truism or a rationalization for letting the wise decide who deserves help. My line was that it is a real and uncomfortable thesis: it implies that genuine benevolence is not the same as relieving the most visible suffering, because the good of any one creature is bound up with the perfection of a whole system, and the wise benefactor may therefore have to leave the most pitiable person unhelped. We got nowhere. So I want to run a session where students feel the force of the thesis from inside a decision problem before anyone tells them whose thesis it is.

Please design that activity.

Setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy. Fourteen students, one ninety-minute session. Most of them have read the Theodicy excerpts and the Discourse on Metaphysics, so they know Leibniz believes this is the best of all possible worlds, but they have not read anything on his ethics and they do not know he defines justice in terms of wisdom-guided love. I do not want them to hear his name, or the phrase about the best of all possible worlds, or the word theodicy, until the debrief.

What I want them to come away having felt: that there is a difference between benevolence and wisdom-guided benevolence, and that the difference bites; that a person who gives on the basis of felt compassion can score badly on a standard of perfection that is holistic rather than aggregative; that acquiring the knowledge that would let you give well costs resources you could have given away; and that the wise benefactor's willingness to let one creature suffer is not indifference but a consequence of what it means for a good to belong to a whole.

The core mechanic I have in mind, which you should feel free to redesign if you can make it work better, is a resource-allocation game with a knowledge-purchase option. Each table is a steward of a foundation with a fixed budget of beneficence tokens. Each table governs its own private copy of the same small world containing six petitioners, described in dossiers rich enough that the interlocking structure of the world can be partly inferred but not fully known. The score for a table is not the sum of relief delivered; it is a measure of the perfection of that world as a whole, computed from each petitioner's final condition plus bonuses and penalties for relational facts among them. Some of those relational facts are visible from the dossiers and some are not. Before or during the allocation, a table may spend tokens to buy counsel cards, each of which reveals one relational fact. Buying counsel therefore directly reduces what can be given. The optimal play, if I have set it up right, involves buying some counsel, giving nothing to the most visibly suffering petitioner, and giving to at least one petitioner who does not appear to need it most.

Please work out: the six petitioners and what is on their dossiers; the hidden relational facts; the score formula, which should be simple enough to compute on a whiteboard in under a minute per table; the number of tokens, the price of counsel, and the number of rounds; the rounds and their timings within the ninety minutes; what is revealed at the end of each round; the small non-monetary stakes (I was thinking the winning table chooses the topic of the following week's ten-minute free discussion and gets to leave first, but suggest something better if you have it); the materials; and the full debrief.

Two constraints on the design. First, the score formula must not be arbitrary in the students' eyes by the end. I need a principled answer ready for the student who says the formula is just the facilitator's opinion written on a card, and that answer should be the one that carries the Leibnizian content: perfection is systematic, not aggregative, and the good of the whole is prior to the good of the part in the sense that a part's condition is evaluated by its contribution to the whole, not the reverse. Second, the petitioners must not be cardboard. Give at least two of them claims that ordinary justice would recognize — a debt owed, a wrong done — so that students feel the pull of strict justice and equity against the pull of the whole, because Leibniz does not simply dissolve those into benevolence; he distinguishes degrees of justice and puts universal benevolence at the top without cancelling the lower degrees. If a table can score well while also honoring those claims, that is a design failure, and I would rather the two come apart in a way students have to notice.

In the debrief, walk me through the specific moments of play and what each maps onto. I especially want the moment of deciding whether to buy counsel; the moment of choosing not to help the most suffering petitioner; the moment the round-one scores came back and punished compassion; and the moment a table had to decide whether to honor a claim of strict justice at the cost of world-perfection. Then map those onto Leibniz's definition of love as delight in another's perfection, the distinction between benevolence and wisdom-guided benevolence, the degrees of justice, the idea of a moral community of minds whose perfection is mutually enhancing, and the claim that a perfectly good creator may permit local evil because the whole is best. Ask the students, at the end, whether what they felt while allocating was love or something else, and let that question sit.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect at least three failure modes and want handling advice for each. My guesses: tables will either refuse to buy any counsel out of a felt duty to give everything to the needy and then lose badly, or overcorrect after round one and spend most of the budget on knowledge while starving the world; one or two students per table will do all the inference and the rest will disengage once the score formula is in play; and tables will form an attachment to the petitioner they helped in round one and resist reallocating in round two for reasons that have nothing to do with the score. If you see others, say so.

Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. My own sense: it gives a fixed, finite, human-authored score formula, so it makes wisdom look like knowing the rules of a game, whereas for Leibniz wisdom is knowledge of essences and of the universal system, and the optimum is not a number anyone wrote down; and it cannot reproduce the theological frame at all, that the standard of perfection is discovered in the divine understanding rather than stipulated, and that the permitted evils are justified by a comparison across infinitely many possible worlds rather than within one. It also cannot reproduce the claim that the wise person's benevolence is a form of delight rather than a form of duty, since my game pays out in points and points are not love. Tell me what else is missing and what, if anything, I should simply concede in the last ten minutes rather than try to stage.
