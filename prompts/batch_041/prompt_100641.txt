You are a philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology and normative ethics, with a particular sensitivity to how descriptive claims about the origins of moral attitudes get smuggled into normative verdicts about them. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses of the impasse below; then commit to the one you think is right.

For about two years a reading group I belong to has been stuck on what looks like a single question wearing several costumes. The question is when an entrenched moral attitude should be treated as a preference to be respected and when as a bias to be corrected. The same structure keeps recurring across cases. One member holds that a parent's partiality toward their own child, an in-group member's favoritism toward their own group, and a diner's reluctance to eat meat are, in the relevant sense, all of a piece: each is an attitude the agent did not choose, that tracks a distinction the agent cannot defend when pressed, and that is sensitive to arbitrary features of upbringing, so each is a bias, and the fact that it feels like a preference is itself part of the bias. Another member holds that these are preferences and that calling them biases is just the move of someone who dislikes the preference: on her view a preference is simply an attitude the agent endorses on reflection, and if the agent does not disown it once the facts are settled, no one else gets to relabel it a bias by pointing at its causal history. A third member insists the whole thing turns on an empirical question, namely whether these attitudes are the output of an arbitrary associative process or of a tacit inference that tracks something real, and that once moral psychology settles that, the normative question answers itself. A fourth says the first two are talking past each other because one is doing descriptive psychology and the other is doing justification, and that no fact about etiology could ever settle whether an attitude is warranted.

I have a hypothesis I want you to test rather than assume. My hypothesis is that the impasse is generated by a distinction that is defined circularly in this area: a bias gets identified as the preference that fails some test, and the test is then read off from which attitudes we already wanted to call biases. If that is right, the disagreement is not substantive at any of the levels the four members occupy, but is instead a dispute about which of several incompatible jobs the preference/bias distinction is supposed to do — explain the attitude's persistence, license paternalistic correction of it, or mark it as unjustified — and these jobs pull in different directions. But I may be wrong; it may be a genuine substantive disagreement about the normative significance of etiology, or a hidden empirical one.

Diagnose it. Tell me whether this is a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about reasons, a hidden empirical question, or two or more different questions being asked under one pair of words, and say which. Engage the actual moves each member makes rather than a generic version of the positions. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a stipulation, a division of labor between the descriptive and the normative, a formal result, or an argument that the question should be abandoned in favor of a better one. Give me a committed verdict with reasons, not a menu of options; if you think my hypothesis is right, say so and defend it against the strongest objection; if you think it is wrong, say what the real structure of the disagreement is.
