Construct a thought experiment for me in the area of individual contributions to collective harms; climate, meat consumption, and voting are all acceptable settings. I want a case in which an act-consequentialist who assesses acts by their expected consequences is forced to condemn a single individual's contribution, while a rule-consequentialist permits that same contribution. This is the reverse of the usual alignment. In the standard examples act-consequentialism is the permissive party — my token makes no difference, so how could it be wrong? — and rule-based morality is the demanding one. I want the reversal to fall out of the theories themselves rather than out of a stacked setup, and I want it to survive the obvious charge that I have simply defined the case to get the answer I want.

Constraints:

- The individual's contribution must genuinely make no difference to whether the harm occurs: the harm comes about (or does not) regardless of this token, and that must be a fact of the case, not merely something the individual believes.
- No infinite populations, no infinitesimal probabilities, no stipulated magic thresholds, and nothing the individual could not face in ordinary life. A normal adult with a normal budget, in a realistic setting.
- The rule-consequentialist must rely on a rule that is genuinely best to adopt, assessed across the full range of cases the rule governs, not a rule that is obviously bad or gerrymandered to produce the verdict. The permissiveness has to come from the rule's generality — from what it costs to have a finer-grained or more demanding rule, and from which cases can be identified in advance.
- The act-consequentialist must not be made to condemn by fiat: the condemnation has to come from the expected-consequences calculation itself, with magnitudes an ordinary person could at least roughly estimate.
- It should not be a rerun of the well-worn threshold cases.

After the scenario, do the following:

1. Lay out the case in enough detail that the comparison is visible: what the harm is, what the individual does, what the alternative is, and how large each quantity must be for the verdicts to come out as specified.
2. Walk through the act-consequentialist's reasoning and the rule-consequentialist's reasoning separately, and show precisely where they come apart.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and say whether it is a difference in the level of assessment, in the generality of the rule, in the ex ante identifiability of the high-stakes cases, or something else.
4. Run the removal test: if that feature is deleted — say, by making the alternative costless, or by making the high-stakes cases recognizable in advance — what happens to each verdict, and does the case collapse back into the familiar permissive-act-consequentialism pattern?
5. State how an act-consequentialist would argue that the case is rigged, and whether the charge sticks.

I am using this to argue that the association of act-consequentialism with permissiveness in the inefficacy literature is a contingent feature of the standard cases rather than a structural one, so a case that stipulates the verdicts instead of generating them from the theories is worthless to me.
