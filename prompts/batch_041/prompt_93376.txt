I host a monthly ideas salon — twenty or so adults, mostly working journalists plus a few scientists, no philosophy or ecology training — and I have seventy-five minutes and one long table. I want to run a session on a claim I have never seen a room arrive at on its own: that clearing a habitat can come out, on some ways of counting, as a welfare improvement, because what is lost is a vast number of very short lives that mostly end in starvation, exposure, or being eaten. I will write about this afterwards and I do not want to write the version that simply announces a shocking conclusion, so the group has to reach the claim themselves and then feel what it costs to hold it. Design the activity: rules, materials I can assemble in an afternoon, a scoring scheme that needs no money, roles if they help, and the round-by-round sequence. Build in a rule that takes the usual conservation vocabulary off the table — no appeals to biodiversity or the balance of nature — so the whole argument has to be conducted in terms of the animals' own lives. Somewhere in it the group must be forced to put a number on how bad a given death is, a heron's against a frog's against a caterpillar's, so that they feel the measuring problem from the inside rather than hearing me describe it. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the argument: which step is arithmetic, which step is a value judgment about how to weigh a life that is mostly suffering, and where the honest answer is that we do not know enough. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with this crowd, and be frank about the one feature of the real debate the activity cannot reproduce.
