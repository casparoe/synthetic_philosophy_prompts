I'm trying to nail down what a decent theory of the pleasure/pain trade-off would even have to do, specifically for the lexical-threshold version of negative utilitarianism — the view that there's some line of suffering such that no amount of pleasure, however large, can justify it. Before I look at any more attempts, give me the desiderata: what a theory like that has to deliver.

Which cases it must get right — e.g. the choice between sparing someone a certain minute of moderate pain and handing a huge number of people a long stretch of bliss, or a gamble that offers a year of ecstasy with a one-in-a-billion chance of a minute of agony. Where the threshold sits and whether it's allowed to move around with population size. Whether anyone can ever be compensated for crossing it, and if so what currency the compensation is in.

Which distinctions it must respect — intensity vs duration, the badness of pain vs the goodness of pleasure (are they even the same scale, or opposites?), a sharp threshold vs a vague one, and whether the threshold is a fact about the world or about our attitudes.

What it has to stay consistent with — the usual requirement that preferences be representable by a real-valued utility function, and how choice under risk is supposed to work at all once the ordering is lexicographic.

Put the case or consideration behind each requirement. Then flag the pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch the representability/continuity requirement and the lexical bit are going to fight, and I'd like to know if that's right. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular candidates. Don't bother scoring every existing view, I'll do that part in class.
