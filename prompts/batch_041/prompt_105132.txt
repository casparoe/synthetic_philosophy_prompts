You're a philosopher who works on collective agency and moral responsibility. The setup: two philosophers, both sharp, neither a strawman, arguing about whether a nation can be guilty across generations.

One is an "inheritance" theorist. She leans on Jaspers's idea of political guilt — the thought that in a state everyone is co-answerable for the deeds done in the state's name, whether or not they personally did anything — and she wants to extend it forward in time: if the political community is one continuing agent, then the guilt doesn't evaporate once the individuals who voted, cheered, or looked away have died off. She also has a distinctive move I want you to develop: she says the thing that really matters here isn't guilt in the criminal-law sense at all but something more like an inherited liability or debt — the way a company that buys out another company inherits it's obligations, even though the new shareholders never did anything wrong. On this view what transfers across generations is not culpability but an unpaid obligation, plus the standing of others to demand that you answer for it.

The other one resists. He thinks guilt is essentially first-personal and deed-bound: you can only be guilty of something you did, intended, or at least could have prevented, and none of those conditions hold for someone born in 1995. He grants that there can be inherited debts and inherited responsibilities, but insists that calling them "guilt" is a category error that smuggles in the retributive and emotional connotations of guilt (remorse, atonement, punishment) where only a financial or forward-looking relation belongs. He should also press the identity problem: for guilt to be inherited, the collective has to be literally the same agent over time, and he should argue that a nation is more like a series of loosely overlapping cohorts than one persisting person — while the inheritance theorist should have a reply ready, maybe working the corporate-person analogy harder.

I want real back-and-forth: each one should pick up the other's actual argument and respond to it, not just deliver monologues past each other. Let it get technical. Have them seperate at some point over what work the word "guilt" is even doing — is the dispute substantive or partly verbal? And make sure the defender of inherited guilt has a serious answer to the "you can't be guilty of what you didn't do" objection, and likewise don't let the critic just win by definition. End with neither side fully conceding, but let each of them land at least one point the other can't easily dismiss.
