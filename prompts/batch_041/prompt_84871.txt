I'm chairing the first meeting of our city's new citizens' assembly next month -- forty residents picked by lot, plus a few council members who think the whole thing is a waste of time and the city staff who'll have to implement whatever comes out of it. I get ten minutes of opening remarks, and I want them to be Deweyan in a way that actually shapes what I'm allowed to say, not just a nod to the guy. Two bits of his thinking I'd like to put to work: first, that a public isn't a bunch of people who happen to agree about something but the people who get caught up in the indirect consequences of what other people do -- so part of our job here is finding out who's affected, not just polling who's already talking; second, that inquiry starts from a situation that's felt as a mess and only turns into a "problem" once somebody actually constructs one, so we shouldn't pretend we're just gathering opinions on a question that already exists and is waiting for an answer. I also can't promise anyone a good policy, or a popular one, and I can't tell the council members they've been replaced by a lottery. Write the remarks -- something the staff who've actually read Dewey won't wince at and the residents who've never heard of him will still find useful.
