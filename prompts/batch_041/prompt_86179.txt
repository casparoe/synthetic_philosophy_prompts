So my friend who runs a Tuesday-night reading group for a bunch of analysts at a mid-size fund made us play the dollar auction last week — you know the thing, the auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill, highest bid pays and gets it, second-highest bid pays and gets nothing — and by the end two people had bid four dollars and were still going, and the room got genuinely ugly. What got me is that no single bid was stupid in isolation, and once you're one of the top two, the other guy being stuck in there stops being a reason to back off and starts being a reason to keep going. My friend says this is the lab version of escalation of commitment: sunk cost, self-justification, the unwillingness to have been the sucker, and the fact that an audience is watching. We spent the rest of the night listing real ones — a bidding war for a company, a primary campaign whose donors have already lit their money on fire, a weapons program thats always two years from working, a war nobody can lose because of how much has already been lost.

Here's what I actually want to know. Machiavelli never saw a hostile takeover or a spectrum auction, but I'd bet he'd have opinions, and I can't tell which way they'd go. On one side there's the impetuosity stuff — fortune favoring the bold, better to be impetuous than cautious, Julius II charging in, the prince who never takes the middle road but declares openly for one side, the point that everyone can see how you appear and almost nobody can touch what you are, which sounds to me like a reason to double down and be seen to be unshaken. On the other side there's the bit about how a new illness is easy to cure and hard to recognize while an old one is easy to recognize and hard to cure, plus the insistence that a prince adapt his nature to the times, that he shouldn't ruin his own state to buy a reputation, that you shouldn't take on other people's wars or let an ally set the terms you'll be fighting on. Those sound like reasons to get out early, cheaply, and with a story ready. And then there's his view of men — greedy, fearful, obsessed with appearances, quick to resent a loser — which might mean the escalation he'd predict is exactly the escalation we saw, so the trap isn't a deviation from his psychology, it's a consequence of it.

What I want from you: what would Machiavelli actually advise? I've read the Prince and maybe half the Discourses, so hold me to what's in them rather than to the general vibe. First, does someone already deep in the auction cut and run or raise again — and does the answer change if the trapped party is a prince rather than a republic or a committee? I suspect he thinks a republic's reputation works differently, and that the crowd's judgment of who won is cruder than a single prince's. Second, would he design one of these auctions on purpose to trap a rival, and if so would he call that virtù or just fraud that happens to work, given what he says about keeping faith only when it doesn't cost you. Third, is there anything in his account of human nature that lets him explain the psychology here without modern cognitive-bias talk, or is this genuinely new territory for him. And don't just tell me he'd be cynical — name the two or three Machiavellian principles doing the real work, say which one you think dominates, and tell me what extra fact about the situation would flip your answer. My guess is it's something about whether the losing bidder's payment is public, but you tell me.
