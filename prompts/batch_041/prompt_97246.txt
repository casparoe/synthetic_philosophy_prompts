You are an expert in the history of economic thought and in political philosophy, with a specialist's command of the socialist calculation debate — the dispute among Ludwig von Mises, F. A. Hayek, and Oskar Lange (and their successors) over whether a socialist economy can allocate resources rationally. I am building a teaching instrument for a graduate seminar, and I want you to construct a decision tree that leads a reader from a handful of low-level commitments to a named position on that question. Please think carefully before you write. Begin by spending a few paragraphs brainstorming: identify the axes along which the positions genuinely differ, then decide which of those axes can be converted into branch questions a reader could answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to. Only after that brainstorm should you produce the tree itself.

The instrument must satisfy the following constraints.

First, every branch node must be a question answerable on its own terms — no node may ask, in effect, whether the reader is a Misesian or a Langean. Phrase the nodes as concrete commitments. The distinctions I care about most are these, though you should add any others the logical space requires: whether the relevant economic knowledge could in principle be written down, collected, and handed to a single agency, or whether it is essentially dispersed, contextual, and partly tacit; whether the failure of central planning is at bottom a matter of missing knowledge or of missing incentives; whether competition does something other than compute an allocation (that is, whether it functions as a discovery procedure rather than a solution to an optimization problem); whether the objection concerns static allocative efficiency or dynamic innovation over time; and whether the word price must denote a ratio arising from actual exchange, or may instead denote an accounting or shadow magnitude computed by an authority.

Second, the leaves must include at least the following positions, each identified by its content and not merely by a label: (a) the view that rational calculation requires genuine market prices for the means of production, which in turn require private ownership and exchange, so that socialism is impossible in principle rather than merely difficult; (b) the view that the obstacle is epistemic — knowledge of particular circumstances never exists in centralized form, so no amount of computing power closes the gap; (c) the view that prices need not originate in private ownership, since a central board can set parametric prices and adjust them by trial and error while managers follow simple rules; (d) the view that the real defect of planning is motivational, so that the celebrated knowledge problem is downstream of an incentive problem; (e) the view that physical accounting in labour-hours or quantities can replace prices altogether, prices being an artefact of a particular mode of production; (f) the view that modern computation removes the technical barrier, so the debate is settled by technology rather than by principle; (g) the view that allocation should proceed neither through markets nor through a central board but through decentralized democratic deliberation about needs; and (h) a deflationary leaf holding that the knowledge and incentive objections are two descriptions of a single defect, or that the whole debate rests on a category mistake about what an economy is. Where a position is standardly associated with a name, give the name at the leaf; but every leaf must also carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Third, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located within that space. For every leaf, record the combination of answers that reaches it. Then list the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and briefly diagnose each — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Fourth, flag explicitly any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say why, rather than papering over the difficulty.

Fifth, include at least one node built around the following concrete case, which I want the reader to confront directly. Suppose a planning agency is granted unlimited computation, real-time data on every transaction in the economy, and the power to interrogate every citizen about their preferences. Which of these three, if any, closes the gap the critics allege — and does the answer differ depending on whether the reader took the knowledge objection or the incentive objection to be fundamental? Design the node so that a reader's answer discriminates among the leaves rather than merely restating a label.

Sixth, keep the instrument self-contained. Assume the reader has no library at hand. Do not ask the reader to consult, cite, or look up any text; you may name authors, but no leaf may depend on the reader's knowing a title, a date, or a passage. Do not supply a reading list or references.

Aim for roughly ten to fourteen branch nodes and for the leaves listed above plus any further leaves the logical space demands. Close with a short note on which branches you found it hardest to make discriminating, and why.
