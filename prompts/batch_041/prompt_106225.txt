I teach a unit on the conceivability argument, and I want to use Invasion of the Body Snatchers — Don Siegel's 1956 film, adapted from Jack Finney's novel, or Philip Kaufman's 1978 remake, whichever you can say more about — as a teaching text. Read it through the argument and tell me what it actually shows. The setup looks tailor-made: the pods produce duplicates that carry the original's appearance, memories, and personality, and the entire horror of the film depends on the fact that the replacements are detectable — flat, cold, unable to feel. But a philosophical zombie is stipulated to be a physical duplicate of you, molecule for molecule, whose behavior is indistinguishable from yours in every respect, so that in principle no one could ever tell the difference from the outside. The pod people therefore fail the stipulation in the most conspicuous way possible, and the film's plot needs them to fail it: everything turns on the survivors being able to spot what has happened, on Becky's scream, on Miles's kiss. So I want a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously and does not resolve the tension by declaring the film merely crude. Does it dramatize the possibility the argument needs, or does it substitute an easier and different thought — that a loved one has become a stranger, that individuality has been lost, that conformity is a kind of death — and thereby suggest that our zombie intuitions are really intuitions about affective and behavioral difference rather than about the bare absence of inner life? If the latter, is that a defect in the argument's first premise, or only a limitation of narrative, which cannot depict an exact duplicate without giving us some tell to notice? Say something too about the pods' biology: the duplicates have a physical origin and a physical mechanism, and the argument requires a world whose physics is exactly ours, so consider whether the film's science fiction helps the case or muddies it. Where the film resists the reading, say so rather than forcing it, and end by saying what the film gets at that the philosophical literature on zombies does not.
