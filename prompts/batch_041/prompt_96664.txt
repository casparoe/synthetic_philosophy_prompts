I am writing a short piece on oversight methods for systems that outperform their supervisors, and I want to use an essay I have been reading as a foil. The essay argues that the proposal to supervise such systems by having two copies of a model argue a question out in front of a weaker judge, which then decides which side won, is a dead end. Before I lean on it I want to know how much of it to trust, because I find parts of it persuasive and parts of it too quick, and I cannot yet tell which are which.

The essay's case, as I reconstruct it, is five claims.

1. The proposal is a variation on reinforcement learning from human feedback: instead of one model answering and a human rating the answer, two models answer and the human rates the winner. Debate therefore inherits every limitation of human feedback and adds nothing of substance.

2. Its one genuine innovation is that the judge never has to solve the problem, only to check the work. Since verifying an answer is easier than finding it, a judge far weaker than the debaters can still police them.

3. The theoretical case rests on two facts: the game is zero-sum, and the honest debater can always expose a lie. A debater who tells the truth can therefore always defeat one who does not, the dishonest debater has nothing to gain by lying, and the equilibrium of the debate is the honest one no matter how much stronger the debaters are than the judge.

4. The one fatal gap is the obfuscated-argument problem: a dishonest debater can bury a false step inside a long, technical, superficially valid argument whose flaw neither the judge nor the opposing debater can locate, and win. This has been observed in practice, in debates over physics questions, and since a dishonest debater can always construct such an argument, debate is refuted as a strategy for superhuman systems.

5. The honest conclusion is that debate is a historical curiosity and that the effort should go into interpretability instead.

What I want from you is an accuracy audit, not a verdict. Please work through the five claims and, for each, say whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, a conflation of two claims that the proposal keeps apart, or simply false; where you identify a conflation, name the two things being run together. I am especially interested in three things.

First, what does the honest-debater argument actually establish, and what does it assume? I would like the assumptions stated as conditions — in particular, as conditions on what the judge is able to do and on what the debaters are able to make visible to the judge — and a judgement about whether those conditions hold for the kinds of task we would actually want to supervise. I also want to know whether the result is a claim about equilibrium play in a game or a claim about what models trained by gradient descent will do.

Second, is the obfuscated-argument problem a refutation of debate or a design constraint? If the latter, say what has to change in the protocol for it to be addressed. Two proposals have been put to me and I want your assessment of each: letting the judge spot-check a single randomly chosen step of each debater's argument, on the theory that randomization makes hiding a flaw expensive; and requiring a debater that decomposes a problem into subproblems to answer related subproblems posed by its opponent, so that a hidden flaw can be smoked out. For each, say what it would actually buy, what it presupposes about the judge's competence, and where it would fail.

Third, is the obfuscated-argument problem specific to debate, or does it also afflict decomposition-based approaches that never involve an adversary — that is, if you train a model to break a hard question into easier subquestions and answer those, is there a way to tell a good decomposition from one that quietly moves the difficulty out of view? If the problem recurs there, say so, and say whether that makes it a problem about supervision in general rather than about debate in particular.

Finally, tell me whether the essay's conclusion follows once its claims are corrected, and which one or two of its points survive correction as genuine open problems rather than as refutations. If the errors all lean the same way, making the proposal look worse than it is, say so; if they run in both directions, say that too. You need not cite anything — I have no way to check references at the moment, and I would rather you reason from the mechanics of the proposal than gesture at who has said what. Where a question turns on something you genuinely cannot assess, say so rather than guessing.
