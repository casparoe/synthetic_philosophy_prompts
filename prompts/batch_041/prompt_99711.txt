Last term I sat in on a graduate seminar on animal cognition, and a handful of us used to keep arguing afterwards over drinks. Two of the regulars — I'll call them R and M — had a running dispute about sphexishness that never got resolved, and I'd like you to settle it for me. The occasion was the stock example: the digger wasp that hauls a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, sets the cricket down at the threshold while it goes inside to check the burrow, and, if you nudge the cricket a few inches away during that inspection, comes back out, drags the cricket up to the threshold again, and goes back in — a loop you can run as many times as you have patience for. R's position was that this is the cleanest illustration we possess of the gulf between behaving and thinking. M's was that the whole vocabulary of sphexishness is a trap. Here is roughly how it went.

(1) R said the wasp is a pure automaton: the "go inside and inspect" step is a subroutine that carries no representation of where the cricket is or what the point of the maneuver is, and the loop proves that the routine cannot be revised by its own failure — the failure isn't even registered as a failure. (2) M replied that this badly understates what the wasp is doing: it navigates, discriminates its prey from other insects, and manipulates a struggling body with real precision, and "rigidity" shows up only relative to an intervention that we chose for our own convenience. Push on any system at the right joint, M said, and you can make it look scripted — a human included. (3) R answered that flexibility isn't the issue; the issue is whether the flexibility is itself revisable. In the wasp there is no route by which the interruption reaches anything general, whereas a person caught in the same absurd loop notices and steps out. (4) M shot back that "notices" is precisely what is in dispute, and that R was inferring it from behavior: the behavioral signature R offered — repeating the loop — is exactly what a human habit looks like from outside, so R had defined a difference he could then never detect. (5) R said we do detect it: point out the loop to a person and the person alters the routine, and the alteration generalizes; the wasp never does, across as many trials as you care to run, and that is a measurable difference in kind. (6) M granted the measurement but denied the metaphysics — give the wasp a million trials and a modifiable nervous system and you get plasticity; give the human enough fatigue or fixation and you get the loop — so R was drawing a line in a continuum and then calling one side "agency." M added that if the only surviving difference is that the human's library of routines is larger, sphexishness cannot carry the weight R wants to put on it, and in particular it says nothing about free will. (7) R closed by insisting it was never about library size but about whether a system can take its own routines as objects — and M said that this is either trivial or a promissory note, since nobody has said what it would be to do that, or how we would tell.

I was the bystander in all of this and genuinely couldn't decide, so please don't tell me which of them "wins"; I want the exchange adjudicated move by move. Rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and say specifically who is right about what. Then tell me what both of them are missing — I suspect it has something to do with the level of description at which "rigidity" is being attributed, and with whether the wasp case bears on free will at all or whether the free-will framing is a category error that both of them quietly imported. Finally, since I'll be writing this up for the seminar, please give me the one or two distinctions you think the whole dispute turns on, stated as sharply as you can. No citations, reading lists, or references, please — I only want the arguments sorted out.
