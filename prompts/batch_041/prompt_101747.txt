I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long feature about mechanistic anomaly detection — you know, the idea that instead of just staring at a model's outputs, you look at its insides and flag the inputs that get processed by a mechanism different from the one you thought you were relying on. I've been reporting on this for a few months and keep hitting the same conceptual snag: nobody agrees on what makes two mechanisms "the same." Is it the same circuit? Same causal role? Same representational content? Same equivalence class under interventions? One researcher I interviewed floated the idea that we should stop asking about sameness and instead ask about invariance under a specified set of allowed interventions — treat mechanisms as equivalence classes of computations, so an "anomaly" is just a forward pass that falls outside the class you've decided to trust. I find that promising but also suspiciously neat. A couple of other people I've talked to want to individuate mechanisms by their causal fingerprint — intervene on the same variables, see if the downstream effects match — while others think that's hopeless because any two real networks differ in a million tiny ways, and what we actually want is something coarser, like "the same algorithm up to implementation." And then there's the awkward fact that "anomaly" gets treated as obviously bad and "same mechanism" as obviously good, which seems like a normative assumption smuggled in as a technical one.
