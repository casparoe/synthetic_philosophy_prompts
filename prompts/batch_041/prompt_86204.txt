Since retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, and I find that I cannot get past the free energy principle. As I understand it, the claim is that organisms act so as to minimize free energy — the same quantity I spent thirty years measuring, the one that decreases whenever a system relaxes toward equilibrium — which makes the principle look to me like the second law of thermodynamics restated in the vocabulary of Bayesian inference. On that reading it also carries an implausible implication: a system that has successfully minimized free energy is a system that has come to rest, so an organism that did so would end up maximally predictable and unresponsive, with no gradient left to drive it, and the theory would seem to counsel a dark, quiet room. Why, then, is the free energy principle taken as a substantive theory of perception, action, and life rather than as a redescription of statistical mechanics? What does it claim that the second law does not already supply, and in what sense is the minimization it describes a physical process at all?
