You are a philosopher of history and social epistemologist working on the ethics of memory, and I would like you to treat what follows as a design problem rather than an essay question: before you write anything, brainstorm several candidate scenarios, test each against the constraints, and discard the ones that fail; then present only the pair that survives.

I am a graduate student who splits her week between a municipal archive and a community oral-history project, and I keep meeting the same dispute in two shapes. In the first shape, the institutional record says one thing about a community and the community insists on another, and it seems to me that the community ought to win — what a practice means to the people who carry it is not something an archive is in a position to overrule, even when the archive is scrupulous, honest, and better evidenced than the memory. In the second shape, the very same claim is in dispute, and it seems equally obvious that the record ought to win, because the community is simply misremembering. I do not think I am being merely inconsistent, but I cannot yet say what separates the two shapes, and I am suspicious of the obvious answer that the first is about meaning while the second is about fact, since any claim about what a community's practice means is also a claim about what its members have actually done and said.

So please construct a matched pair of scenarios, alike in every respect except one, in which the same disputed claim — literally the same sentence, stated in full in both scenarios, not merely the same topic — ought to be resolved in opposite directions. Constraints: (i) both scenarios contemporary and mundane — a workplace, a school, a parish, a housing estate, a sports club — with no atrocities, no famous historical events, and nothing whose interest depends on the reader knowing a particular real case; (ii) in both, the archive is honest, competent and well-sourced, and the community is not self-serving — no one's money, reputation or legal exposure turns on the outcome, and the community's memory is not a comfortable lie; (iii) no exotic machinery — no collective consciousness, no telepathy, no time travel; (iv) the two scenarios differ in exactly one variable, which you must name explicitly. Then, for each scenario, give the verdict of three different norms: the strictly evidential norm that we should believe whatever the balance of evidence supports; the norm that a community's memory is not a rival hypothesis about the past but a claim of standing, an assertion about who has the authority to narrate, so that asking which account is accurate is a category mistake; and a hybrid that treats the two as incommensurable and therefore treats the question itself as malformed. Finish by telling me which feature of the pair is doing the real work, whether the distinction survives the objection that a community's self-understanding is itself a historical fact and so answerable to evidence, what becomes of the pair once that feature is removed, and how the strongest defender of record-always-wins and the strongest defender of memory-always-wins would each try to explain the pair away rather than accept it. I want your construction and your reasoning, not a reading list.
