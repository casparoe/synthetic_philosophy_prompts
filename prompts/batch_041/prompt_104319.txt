I'm a novelist, most of the way through a book that's half historical fiction set in America around the 1870s-1900s and half a present-day storyline about a small startup training language models. One of my historical characters is a stand-in for Charles Sanders Peirce, and I want his imagined voice to argue with the modern characters in a way that's actually faithful to what the man would plausibly have thought, not just whatever sounds clever in a workshop. I know him mostly through his three-way split of signs: icons that resemble what they stand for, indices that are physically or causally hooked up to their object (smoke to fire, a weathervane to the wind), and symbols that are pure convention. I don't want a bibliography, reading list, or citations -- I can't look anything up while I'm drafting and honestly I just want the reasoning, so please don't pad the answer with references I'd have to trust. Just reason it out.

The modern fight I need him to weigh in on is the symbol grounding problem. A text-only model shuffles tokens that only ever relate to other tokens, never directly to things in the world, so the complaint goes that its meaning is ungrounded -- symbols all the way down. My startup character insists that's outdated: the tokens are grounded, he says, because they're causally coupled to the world through the training corpus and through user feedback. My other character fires back that coupling through yet more text is still symbol-to-symbol and doesn't buy you an index. And here's my own private thought experiment I'd love him to chew on: a text-only model that gets bolted to a camera and a robotic hand for a single afternoon -- would a Peircean say that one afternoon of indexical contact retroactively grounds the whole vocabulary it spent years absorbing from text, or is it too late, or too little, or does it not work that way at all?

So take the role of a philosopher of mind and logic who knows Peirce cold, and think step by step. First brainstorm out loud: what are his actual commitments that bear on this -- his account of the interpretant, unlimited semiosis, indexicality, his pragmatic maxim about meaning as the practical bearings of a concept? Then predict as carefully as you can what he'd say: is a text-only model's vocabulary grounded, ungrounded, or something his framework doesn't cleanly classify? Where would he say my startup guy is cheating, and where might he surprise me by actually siding with the moderns? And please flag the spots where the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by what he wrote, so I don't end up putting words in a dead man's mouth. Don't hedge everything into mush, though -- I need a verdict I can build dialogue around.
