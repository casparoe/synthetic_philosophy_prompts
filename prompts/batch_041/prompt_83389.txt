Two of my colleagues got into it at the pub after the seminar last Tuesday, and since I was the one with the least invested in it I got handed the job of sorting out who was right. I couldn't. It started from insect farming, of all things — whether it matters morally, and how much — and turned into a proper arguement about whether sentience comes in degrees. Here's the exchange as close to verbatim as I can manage. Dan and Priya, both in my dept.

Dan: Look, sentience is obviously a matter of degree. A dog has more going on than a bee, a bee more than a worm. If moral status tracks sentience, then moral status is a matter of degree too. A bee's suffering isn't nothing, it's just a lot less than a dog's. You can't seriously hold that every creature with a flicker of experience counts exactly as much as a human — that view makes it impossible to ever choose anything.

Priya: You've fused two things. How much is going on, and whether anything is going on at all. Say the bee's experience is thin. If there's something it's like to be that bee, and things can go badly for it, then it's been harmed, and harming it needs a justification. That's not a matter of degree, it's a line you've crossed. Fine, a dog's pain may give me a stronger reason than a bee's — but that's the strength of the reason, not whether the creature has any standing in the first place. You keep sliding from "less rich" to "less important," and I don't think that slide is licensed.

Dan: Then draw the line. Out loud. Fruit fly? Nematode? A dish of neurons? Whatever you pick, I'll take the thing one rung below and run your own argument on it, and you'll have nowhere principled to stop. Mine has no cliff — less, and less, and less, all the way down. And by the way, that thermostat on the wall has a setpoint and fires a signal when it's off setpoint. That's not a million miles from wanting to be warmer. If you're the threshold person, you owe me an account of why the thermostat sits below the line and the bee above it, and it can't just be "because the bee looks more like me."

Priya: The thermostat is the reductio of your view, not mine. If degrees of sentience are read off functional structure like that, then every feedback loop, every thermostat, every plant turning toward the light gets a little moral status, and a little for absolutely everything is nothing at all. What you've thrown away is the only distinction that matters — between something that has interests and something that merely behaves as if it does. And here's the worse trouble with degrees. If standing comes in degrees, then for any two creatures there's a ratio, and a ratio is exactly the toy a cost-benefit analyst needs. Hand me a scale and I'll show you the spreadsheet that discounts the bee to 0.0001 and keeps the factory farm running. Your "less, but not nothing" is a number somebody made up, and it'll always get made up by whoever wants to eat the bees. You can't even supply the number — what's the exchange rate between bee-suffering and fish-suffering? There isn't one.

Dan: That's an argument about what people do with a scale, not about whether there is one. And "no exchange rate" just means our instruments are crude — we've got no unit for depression either, and nobody concludes it doesn't matter. I don't even need a number, an ordering would do: dog over bee over worm. Your view has the same discounting problem and worse: you draw the line just above the shrimp and everything under it gets exactly zero. That's a hundred percent discount, and you're the one complaining about discounts.

Priya: An ordering with no unit is exactly what you can't act on. You've just admitted you can rank creatures but can't say by how much, which means when interests collide — and they always collide — you've got nothing to weigh with. At least a threshold gives me a rule: above the line, full weight; below it, none. Yours gives me a leaderboard and no arithmetic. And on the line-drawing: you keep treating "we don't know where the line is" as "there's no line." We can't say to the minute when someone's asleep and when they're awake, and that doesn't mean there's no difference between asleep and awake.

That's about where the pub closed. What I want is a proper move-by-move adjudication, not a general essay on degrees of sentience. For each of the six moves, tell me whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or half-right, and why — and if a move bundles two claims, seperate them, because I suspect at least one of them is landing a hit on something the other never actually said. Then: are they really disagreeing about how the world is, or are "degree" and "status" doing different work for each of them? What's the strongest thing each could have said and didn't? And if you have to say who's more right overall, say it — but tell me what that verdict is quietly ignoring. Don't go easy on either of them, I've known them both for years and they'll want the honest version.
