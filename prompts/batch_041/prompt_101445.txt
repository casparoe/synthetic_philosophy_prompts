Last month a small reading group I belong to finished a chapter on the outbreak of the First World War, and two of the other members — Marek and Ines — kept arguing about it long after everyone else had begun clearing the table. I stayed out of it, partly because I couldn't decide what I actually thought, and now I'd like someone to sort out the exchange on the merits rather than tell me which of them was more tiresome. The dispute was over whether it makes sense to blame a war on a nation at all, and it went roughly as follows. (1) Marek said the Fischer thesis has never really been answered: the German leadership willed a war of expansion, the annexationist aims that surfaced in the September memorandum of 1914 show what they were after, and a crown council in late 1912 shows that they were already thinking about timing. Blaming Germany, he said, is not a slur but simply the correct description of a deliberate policy. (2) Ines replied that the memorandum was drafted in September, after the war had begun and while a quick victory still looked likely — a wish list drawn up for peace negotiations, not a plan for starting anything — and that the crown council was a conversation among men with no authority to commit the state to war. Even granting that individual leaders wanted war, she added, Germany did not: a nation is not an agent, and treating it as one smuggles in a culprit that no archive can contain. (3) Marek answered that the timing cuts the other way, since intentions do not have to be written down before they are acted on, and the memorandum merely reveals the standing aims of the same men who had already taken the decisive risks — the blank cheque to Vienna, the pressure for war. On her second point he was more impatient: we hold corporations, clubs, and armies responsible all the time without supposing that a company has a soul; collective responsibility is a way of distributing burdens, not a metaphysical claim. (4) Ines granted that much but said it does not rescue the accusation against a nation, because blame of that kind has to survive a counterfactual. If the war would have happened anyway — if Russian mobilization, French resolve, and the Austrian ultimatum were each on its own sufficient to produce a general war — then the German decision was not necessary, and pinning the war on Germany is scapegoating with extra steps. Marek retorted that necessity was never the standard: a drunk driver is responsible for a crash even when the other driver was careless too, and the line "it would have happened anyway" would excuse nearly everyone in history.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the four numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, then saying what, if anything, each of them is missing — particularly on whether a nation can be a proper object of blame, and on what kind of counterfactual (if any) an accusation of this sort requires. I am not asking whose position is more fashionable or who argued more gracefully; I want the arguments assessed as arguments, including any that are partly right for reasons their author did not give. Finish with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in no more than twenty-five words, which of the two is more nearly right overall and why.
