So I'm a science and ideas journo and I've been kicking around an essay on Nietzsche's diet and health stuff. Not the syphilis-and-madness bio thing, more the actual philosophical claim. I'd love comments. Here's my rough thesis and the moves I want to make:

The standard take is that Nietzsche's talk about food, climate, place, and digestion is either embarrassing crankery or just biography. I want to argue it's neither. It's continuous with his critique of morality. He keeps asking not what is good in itself but what is good for you — and that's a shift from universal ethics to something like dietetics. The body isn't an obstacle to thought; it's a condition of it. So his regimen is a kind of philosophical hygiene: you regulate what you eat, where you live, how much you walk, what you drink, because those things determine what kind of thoughts you can have. In Ecce Homo he basically gives us a section on nutrition, location, climate, and recreation as if they were philosophical categories. He attacks alcohol, German heaviness, and probably vegetarianism too — or at least the moralized version of it. He praises dry air, light food, Mediterranean lightness, walking, and water. I want to say this isn't a self-help program; it's a symptomatology. What you eat and how you live reveal whether your life is ascending or declining. The diet is a diagnostic.

The niche bit I want to push: read his dietetics as an early, weird version of somatic epistemology or embodied cognition. Not the lab-coat version, the Nietzschean version: the idea that the body's states aren't just noise but are part of the machinery of knowing. And then the payoff: his critique of morality is partly a critique of a certain diet — the ascetic diet, the heavy diet, the diet of ressentiment. The ascetic ideal is a way of eating the world, not just a way of judging it.

Where I'm unsure: am I overreading? Is there enough in the texts, or am I just making him into a wellness influencer? And does the somatic epistemology framing actually fit, or does it flatten the historical specificity? Also, I might be wrong about the vegetarianism thing — I have a vague memory he both mocks it and flirts with it, and I don't want to get that backwards.

Could you give me comments on: (1) accuracy — especially the vegetarianism/ alcohol/ diet details and whether the Ecce Homo stuff is being characterized fairly; (2) whether the thesis is interesting or just a stretch; (3) the specific move where I link diet to the critique of morality — is that a real through-line or two separate things I'm stapling together? And if you think there's a better angle in there, tell me. I don't need a reading list or citations, just your honest read. I've recieved a lot of pushback on this from friends who think I'm just making him into a proto-wellness guru, so I want to know if the piece can survive that objection or if I should beleive them.
