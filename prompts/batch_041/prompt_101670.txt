I am a novelist, midway through a book set partly in a Nordic capital and partly in a large, diverse Anglophone city, and one of my characters — a minister who has spent her career trying to import the Nordic settlement wholesale — keeps reaching for an image that a colleague of mine uses in earnest. On that image, the Nordic model is a plant and the society is its soil: the tax system, the universal services, the collective wage bargaining and the generous parental leave are the plant; the soil is the trust, the small scale, the homogeneity, the shared norms; and a plant dug up and set down in different earth simply will not grow. My colleague deploys the image to conclude that the model cannot be exported — that it is a local flower and nothing more — and my character, who needs to believe otherwise, has no answer to it. I would like you to audit the analogy for me, not as a figure for a scene but as an argument, since I suspect the character is leaning on it to do work the image cannot actually bear.

Please begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the plant, what plays the soil, what plays the climate, the gardener, the seed, the season — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I want the disanalogies separated into those that actually matter for the export conclusion and those that are merely decorative, because I don't want my character defeating a caricature.

Three things may be worth folding in, and I would rather you explain each in plain terms than assume the reader knows it. First, the shared norms that the image treats as soil have a specific content in the Nordic case: the novelist Aksel Sandemose, in his 1933 satire A Fugitive Crosses His Tracks, gave the name Janteloven — the Law of Jante — to a set of unwritten rules whose gist is that one is not to think oneself special, smarter, or better than the collective, and the term has since become shorthand for the egalitarian pressure of Scandinavian life. Does that norm belong on the soil side or the plant side, and does it help or hurt the analogy? Second, "trust" in this literature is not one thing: a distinction is often drawn between generalized trust, a default willingness to credit strangers, and particularized or in-group trust, the willingness to credit one's own kind, and the Nordic countries score high on the first while many would-be importers score high on the second. If the plant needs only generalized trust, does the soil argument survive? Third, social scientists speak of institutional complementarities — the idea that the parts of a political economy are a package, each making the others work, so that removing one part degrades the rest — and this cuts in a direction the analogy may not intend, since it suggests the plant and the soil may be less separable than the image implies, though for reasons internal to the institutions rather than to the culture.

The crux, as I see it, is the direction of causation. Soil is not produced by the plant, whereas the trust on which the Nordic model is said to run may well be produced by the institutions themselves — universal programs that make everyone a stakeholder, and thereby manufacture the very solidarity they are said to require. If that is right, the analogy smuggles in a picture of trust as a natural endowment, an accident of geology, when it may be a cultivated output, and the conclusion that the model cannot be exported may be self-undermining: it presupposes precisely the one-way dependence the model itself denies. I would like you to say whether that objection lands, and to be honest if it doesn't.

End by telling me whether the conclusion — that the model is a local flower and cannot travel — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time; and if the image is worth keeping in some repaired form, tell me what a better analogy would be, and what it would let my character say that the plant-in-soil image will not.
