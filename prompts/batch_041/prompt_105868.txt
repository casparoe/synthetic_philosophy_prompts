Two philosophers are arguing over a transcendental argument one of them has proposed — an argument from the conditions that make some indispensable practice possible. The proposed argument runs roughly like this: lying is possible only if a speaker can intend to make an audience believe something false; that intention presupposes a distinction between what is believed and how things stand which the intention itself cannot establish; so the practice of lying presupposes that belief answers to a world that is not up to us. The first philosopher takes this to show that a transcendental argument can reach a genuinely metaphysical conclusion, namely that there is a world independent of our beliefs about it. The second takes it to establish at most something internal to our practice of treating one another as believers — that we must represent one another as answerable to a world — with no conclusion about what exists following from it. Write their dialogue. Each should engage the other's actual claims rather than restate a position: the first by arguing that the second's restriction leaves the practice of lying unintelligible, the second by arguing that the first has extracted a claim about reality from a premise about the content of a concept. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should concede or win by stipulation. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
