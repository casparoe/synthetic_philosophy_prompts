Last night I spent several hours arguing with a close friend about the DNA–Gödel isomorphism, and we reached an impasse that I suspect is not about the mathematics but about what the mathematics is about. I want your credence, and more than that, the reasoning that produces it.

Here is the claim we were fighting over. In Hofstadter's typogenetics, a formal toy genetics, a strand is a string over a small alphabet; by a fixed decoding rule a strand determines a set of enzymes; each enzyme has a binding preference and an operation, and when it acts on a strand it cuts at its binding site and then deletes, copies, inserts, or moves a segment. A strand is a self-replicator when the enzymes it codes for, applied to it, collectively yield a second copy of that same strand. Hofstadter offers this as an image of DNA, where a molecule codes for the very machinery that copies it, and he offers both as an instance of the self-reference that produces Gödel sentences, in which a system turns a description of itself into an object inside itself. The proposition I want a number on is this: that the resemblance is a genuine, non-accidental isomorphism of mechanism, so that self-replication in any such system requires precisely the diagonal construction that yields a Gödel sentence, and incompleteness and self-replication are one phenomenon rather than two.

My credence is about 0.3; my friend's is about 0.85. I do not need a reading list or citations, and I would rather you not lean on anyone's authority; argue from the structure of the thing.

Give me your own credence in that proposition, then decompose it. The terms I think belong are below, though you should add, drop, or split them as you see fit, and give each a number and one line of justification:

1. Whether a system rich enough to replicate itself must contain a fixed point at all — I take this to be near-certain and not the interesting part, but tell me whether the necessity is a theorem or a slogan.
2. Conditional on a fixed point being needed, whether it is the diagonal fixed point that generates a Gödel sentence, undecidability and all, or a weaker recursion-theoretic fixed point of the kind that lets a program print its own source, which is available in any Turing-complete setting and carries no incompleteness with it. I think the real weight sits here.
3. Whether the self-reference is a property of the system or an artifact of the description we impose. A fixed point is a fixed point only relative to a chosen encoding, and a replicator is a physical, encoding-independent thing; if the Gödelian structure dissolves when the coding is changed, the isomorphism may live in the eye of the describer.
4. Whether the substantive content is only the double use of a description, one string serving both as instructions to be executed and as data to be copied, and whether that double use requires diagonalization at all or merely a program/data distinction that any stored-program machine already has.
5. Whether typogenetics is a fair proxy for biological replication, or whether the actual DNA mechanism, with complementary base pairing as a template, does work that the abstract fixed-point story silently borrows.
6. Whether, granting the formal point, the isomorphism does explanatory or predictive work — whether it tells us something about biology or about minds that we would not otherwise have — or is true but inert.

Then tell me how the terms combine (do not assume independence if you think it fails), which term the final number is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move it most, and which term is the likeliest home of the gap between my 0.3 and my friend's 0.85 — and whether that gap is substantive or merely verbal, resting on two different readings of what it would take for two mechanisms to count as the same mechanism. Finally, say what, if anything, would count as settling the question, and whether you think it is the kind of question that can be settled.
