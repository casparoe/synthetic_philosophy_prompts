I run research for a mid-sized consumer-insights team — eight analysts, survey panels and a quarterly tracking study, nobody with formal training in demography or causal inference — and generational findings are the thing that keeps embarrassing us. An analyst crosstabs an attitude by age band, the under-30s differ sharply from the over-45s, someone in the room says that is a generational shift, it goes in the deck, and a year or two later the item has moved for everyone, or the young respondents have simply aged into the old answer, or the whole difference turns out to be about income or housing rather than about when you were born. I have come to think the real trouble is that we have no working account of what a generation is supposed to be, so we cannot tell a generational difference from an age difference, a period effect, or a composition artifact. What I want from you is not an essay about the ontology of cohorts, but a procedure my analysts can actually run.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for this question: when our data show a difference between age groups, are we entitled to explain it and act on it as a generational difference, and if so, how should the claim be stated so that it is not overstated? Give it to me as ordered steps, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points at which the procedure stops and returns a no, and the points at which it hands the decision back to a person because the question has stopped being about the data. It has to be runnable by someone with one statistics course and a spreadsheet, in an afternoon. Three distinctions matter enough to me that I want them to survive the simplification: first, that a cohort is not the same thing as an age or a period, and that since cohort, age and period are not independent of one another we can never estimate all three from the data we have, so any generational claim is buying one at the cost of another; second, that a cohort is not internally uniform, so a claim about a generation is usually a claim about the slice of it that happens to be visible in our sample; third, that the categories are not innocent, because once a label like Gen Z is put in front of a market and acted on, the people it names may begin to answer to it, which makes the category more real over time for reasons that have nothing to do with the explanation we were originally testing. Our evidence is repeated cross-sections of the same items since 2019 plus a few panel waves; I do not want a procedure that pretends we have a fifty-year time series. Don't bother with references — if a step rests on a specific research tradition, just say in a clause what the idea is and I will chase it down myself.

Then run the procedure on four cases and give me, for each, a verdict and the exact sentence we would be permitted to put in the deck. (a) An item asked every quarter since 2019: 18–24s say they would rather take a recommendation from an algorithm than from a friend, at roughly twice the rate of 45–54s, and the gap has been stable for five years. (b) The same item, same age band, 2019 against 2024: a large shift. (c) An item about paying more for sustainably sourced goods, where 25–34s look just like 45–54s once you restrict to homeowners above a certain income. (d) My own pet finding, which I have already promised to the executive committee and built part of next year's roadmap on: that Gen Z wants a certain thing from us. I would rather the procedure tell me it is unsupported than flatter me, and in that case I want the honest replacement sentence, a short note on how I should walk the claim back to the committee, and the smallest additional data collection that would actually settle the question. Finally, be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the procedure — I would rather have one with visible error bars than a confident one — and single out the single step you are least sure of, with what would change your mind about it.
