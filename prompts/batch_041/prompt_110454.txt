I'm in a seminar on religion and politics and we've spent the last few weeks on establishment, church taxes and secularism in England, Germany, France and the US, and the prof keeps making a move I cant quite get a handle on. He says the right way to think about all of it is that religion is a market. Denominations are firms competing for adherents, believers are consumers with tastes and switching options, establishment is basically a state monopoly or a nationalised industry, and the church tax -- where the government just collects a slice of your income tax on behalf of whichever church you're registered with, until you formally leave it -- is like a subscription or a payroll deduction that the state administers for you. On his picture the US, with no establishment and no church tax, is the deregulated market; France is a market under heavy neutrality regulation; England has one big incumbent; Germany has a couple of large incumbents living off automatic billing. And he uses the whole thing to argue that disestablishment and deregulation make religion more vigorous, so Europe's empty churches are just what you'd expect from sheltered monopolies.

I'm suspicious -- it feels like the analogy is doing the argument's work rather than the history -- but I don't have the vocabulary to say where it goes wrong. Can you take it seriously and lay the mapping out properly, a table of what's supposed to correspond to what across all four countries, and then tell me where it breaks? I mean which features of an actual market -- prices, elastic demand, exit, consumer sovereignty, the fact that firms can't make the customer manufacture the product, competition on quality, the possibility of new entry -- have no honest counterpart in the religious case, and which of those gaps actually matter for the conclusion he wants versus which are just picturesque. Then say what the analogy quietly imports that the target doesn't have: does treating adherence as demand smuggle in the assumption that belief is chosen like a purchase, or that the state stands outside the market as a neutral umpire when in Germany and England it's plainly a participant and a paymaster? Finally, does the conclusion -- deregulation breeds vitality -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being carried by the analogy the whole time? And if the real problem is that no single analogy covers all four cases, tell me what a better one would be for what the state is actually doing in each, rather than one metaphor stretched over the lot.
