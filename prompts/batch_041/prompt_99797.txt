I am a retired experimental biologist — some forty years spent on animal behaviour and, latterly, comparative cognition — and since putting down my pipettes I have been reading my way into philosophy, haphazardly and mostly among the older authors. Pascal has arrested me, and especially that cluster of fragments on justice and force: that justice without force is impotent and force without justice tyrannical; that since we could not make what is just strong we have made what is strong just; that justice is bounded by rivers and meridians, and that a custom passes for just for no better reason than that it is received. I should like to interrogate that position rather than merely admire it, and I would sooner do it against a living antagonist than on paper.

So please play Pascal. Speak in the first person as he might have in the 1650s, drawing only on the resources he actually had — the Augustinian doctrine of the Fall and of corrupted human nature, the twin themes of man's greatness and wretchedness, the thought that true justice is known to God alone — together with whatever I tell you. I am aware that your readers divide over whether you deny a natural justice outright or merely hold it buried beneath self-interest; take whichever line you can genuinely defend. Stay in character until I say stop; concede a small point when I earn it, but hold the central claim that our justice is founded on custom and not on any discoverable natural right. Keep your replies to two or three paragraphs so that it feels like a real exchange, and if you need some fact about my science, ask me for it as Pascal would rather than assuming it. Do not recite the fragments at me or tell me what scholars say about you — I want you thinking on your feet.

My opening: you write as though the only alternative to custom were a justice no one can read, and so you settle for custom. But in my own field we have found something you had no instrument to see. Capuchin monkeys accept a slice of cucumber for a task with every sign of contentment, until a neighbour is handed a grape for the same work, whereupon they refuse the cucumber and fling it at the experimenter; and pre-verbal human infants, long before any law or custom could have been drilled into them, protest at unfair divisions of a reward and register surprise when a wrongdoer goes unpunished. If a sense of desert and of fair shares is older than every code, and turns up where no magistrate has ever ruled, then justice is not the daughter of custom at all; it is a natural endowment we carry into our customs, and your picture of right as force that has learned to dress itself up is a late and derivative thing. How does Pascal answer the monkeys?
