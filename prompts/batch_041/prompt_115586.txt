I'm teaching a week on deflationary theories of truth next term and I want a tier list before I commit to a handout, so I can see which options I actually have to spend class time on.

Here's the family as I'm going to lay it out. (1) The plain redundancy line: saying that's true adds nothing over just saying the sentence, truth-talk is a rhetorical flourish. (2) Disquotationalism: true is a device for cancelling quotation, it gets you from a quoted sentence to the sentence itself. (3) Prosententialism: that's true is a prosentence, the sentence-level analogue of a pronoun, it picks up its content from an antecedent instead of predicating anything of a proposition. (4) Minimalism: the whole theory is the schema "the proposition that p is true just in case p", and truth is not a substantive property with a hidden nature. (5) A hybrid I sketched in the margin and can't decide about: keep the equivalence schema, but treat true as an operator on acts of assertion rather than on contents, so what she said is true comes out as endorsing her act of asserting rather than as ascribing a property. Call it act-deflationism if it needs a name.

Now rank them. The jobs I care about, roughly in the order I care about them:
(a) generalisation: everything she said is true, every consequence of a true theory is true, where I need the infinite conjunction without writing it out
(b) blind ascription: I say that what she said is true when I don't know what she said, or have forgotten it, or can't translate it
(c) truth as the norm of assertion and belief: that we aim at it, that asserting a falsehood is a defect, that is it true? is the question under discussion
(d) truth pulling explanatory weight: true beliefs are the ones that make action work, true theories are the ones you can build bridges from, so truth looks like it's doing work in an explanation, and the deflationist has to either give this up or explain it away
(e) truth in a compositional semantics: you need a truth predicate to state what a complex sentence means in terms of its parts, and it's not obvious a device for cancelling quotes can do that
(f) the liar: which of these can be extended to a language that contains its own truth predicate without blowing up, and does adding this job reshuffle the list

Give me a tier list over the five, one or two lines per entry saying which of (a) to (f) it handles and which one sinks it, plus a 0-10 for how much I should trust it as the seminar's default. If one of them turns out to collapse into another, say so and adjust the list rather than pretending they're seperate. Then a couple of sentences on whether the list would change if this was a logic course rather than a metaphysics-of-truth course, and whether the act-deflationism in (5) is a coherent option or just expressivism with extra steps. I'd rather find out now than in front of the students. Don't bother with who said what, I only want the structural comparison.
