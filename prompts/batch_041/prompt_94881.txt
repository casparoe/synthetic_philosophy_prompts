I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty-odd years on seabird colonies, a bit of animal behaviour on the side — and since I hung up the binoculars I've been sitting in on philosophy courses, and now somehow I've been roped into co-teaching a unit in an upper-level "minds and machines" seminar. I have to set the final essay and I want to get this right, because I'm the one marking it and I don't want to reward the students who just memorized the labels.

The thing I want the question on is that ladder of "grades" of mind — I'm fairly sure the scheme is Dennett's, though I've lost track of which book. As I understand it: Darwinian creatures are wired the way they are, and their mistakes get paid for by the gene pool over generations. Skinnerian creatures get to learn, but only by actually doing the thing and getting rewarded or punished — the selection happens on their own behaviour, in real time, and it hurts when they get it wrong. Popperian creatures can run the trial inside their own heads, on some kind of model of the world, so the bad hypothesis dies instead of them. And Gregorian creatures are the ones whose heads have been stuffed with mind-tools they didn't invent themselves — words, numerals, maps, notations, the whole inherited kit that lets them think thoughts an unaided brain couldn't manage.

Two things about this bug me, and I want the question to force the students to take a side. First: is the fourth rung actually a new grade, or is it the same old trick — trial and error on an inner model — with the model just relocated from the skull out into the culture? Second: the ladder image itself. Does it mean four stages in the history of life, one after another, or four mechanisms that all sit in one head at once and get mixed together?

I want them tested on two cases. (a) A crow that bends a bit of wire into a hook to fish food out of a tube — no language, no teaching that we know of, but a tool. (b) A person doing long multiplication on the back of an envelope — nobody does four digits by five in their head; the paper is doing part of the work. The essay should say what each case is supposed to show and what would count against the student's own reading of it. This is the part I care about most: if the crow comes out Gregorian I think something's gone wrong with the scheme, but I want that argued, not just asserted.

So here's what I need from you:

1. The essay question itself, worded for a 90-minute exam — one question, with the two parts and the two cases built in.
2. A model answer, around 600–700 words: what a strong student would actually write.
3. A marking scheme: the specific moves a top answer has to make, the marks for each (out of 20), and a one-line note on how students typically blow each one. I want the A-versus-B difference stated as moves, not as effort.

Two things to keep in mind. Don't give me citations or a reading list — the students cant bring anything in and I've no way to check references, so a bibliography would be worse than useless. And be honest with me about what's actually settled here versus what the people who work on this definately argue about. I don't want a contested reading dressed up as the consensus, and I don't want to mark a student down for landing on a side that's live in the field. Flag the genuine disagreements inside the model answer itself, so I know where to be generous.
