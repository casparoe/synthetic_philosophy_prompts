I have no academic training in philosophy or decision theory, but I have been turning over the absent-minded driver problem and I would like to know what principle, if any, fits my verdicts about it. In the standard case, a driver is on a road with two identical intersections. He cannot remember whether he has already passed one. If he turns at the first, he ends up somewhere bad, worth 0. If he continues at the first and turns at the second, he gets home, worth 4. If he continues past both, he ends up somewhere bad, worth 0. At each intersection he does not know which one he is at. My verdicts are these. He should not turn at both or continue at both; he should use a random device at each intersection that makes him turn half the time, because that gives him the best overall chance. But if he finds himself at an intersection and asks what to do now, I think continuing looks better than turning, since turning guarantees 0 while continuing at least leaves open the chance that this is the first intersection. I also think that if he could precommit to the coin-flip rule, he should, and that if he could pay a small cost for a GPS that tells him which intersection he is at, he should pay as long as the cost is less than the improvement. My tentative principle is that he should choose the policy with the highest expected value from the start and then follow it, even if at the moment some other action looks better. That principle seems to tell him to flip the coin at the intersection, but my local verdict is that continuing looks better. Do I have to give up the local verdict, or is there a way to distinguish what to do now from what to commit to beforehand? I would also like to know what the principle says about a case I have not thought through: a version with three intersections, where turning at the first gives 0, turning at the second gives 2, turning at the third gives 4, and continuing past the third gives 0, with the same amnesia at each. Should the driver randomize at each intersection, and with what probabilities? If the principle cannot accommodate one of my verdicts, I would rather know which one to give up than have it patched with an extra clause. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
