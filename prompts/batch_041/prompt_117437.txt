My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about herd behavior, and I want to move the argument somewhere it can actually be settled rather than merely repeated. Her claim was that when a crowd converges on a single answer, that convergence is itself the proof that the crowd is not thinking: a few people choose, everyone downstream follows, and you are left with a room of privately doubtful people deferring to a consensus none of them believes. My claim was that this gets the rationality backwards. Consider any individual in that room at the moment she chooses: given what she has seen and what she privately knows, joining the crowd may be exactly what her evidence recommends — and the group can still walk, with every individual fully justified at her own step, to a conclusion that is simply false. She regards that as a contradiction in terms and is convinced I have smuggled an error in somewhere. I want a room of students to run into the possibility with their own choices before anyone names it, and I want them to feel how thin the consensus is.

Here is what I have to work with. It is an upper-level undergraduate seminar, roughly eighteen students, meeting once for ninety minutes in an ordinary classroom with no money available. I would like an in-class activity — a game with rules and payoffs, not a lecture — that makes the participants feel the force of the point I was defending. The phenomenon should live in the rules, the sequence, and the incentives, so that they produce it themselves; the concepts should arrive only in the debrief, and I should be able to run the first rounds without the vocabulary at all.

Some constraints I care about more than elegance. Each participant should choose in turn, holding one private piece of evidence and seeing what everyone before them chose, under a payoff scheme that makes going with the crowd individually sensible; points and a trivial prize are fine in place of money. The activity must contain at least one round in which the group settles on the wrong answer and this is revealed only at the end, and that outcome should be engineered deliberately rather than left to luck — through pre-briefed early choosers or through my control of the underlying state — because the point is lost if the wrong cascade never happens. I also want a phase that exposes the fragility of the consensus: after the group has locked in, introduce one late participant whose choice is known to rest on better evidence than anyone else's, and let the students watch the entire room reverse on the strength of a single new public signal. If time allows, add a third phase in which each participant may pay a small cost for a second private draw, so that they can see for themselves how many people free-ride on the information the crowd has already revealed instead of buying their own.

A version I have sketched uses a private draw that is correct about the underlying state two times in three; I believe that makes the third chooser follow the crowd even when her own draw disagrees, but please check the arithmetic, because I want the cascade forced by the numbers rather than by any felt social pressure. The deliverable should include the rules, the materials, the sequence and timings, the payoff scheme, the lines I deliver and when (without naming the mechanism), and a debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto the underlying ideas — why imitation can be rational, why private information stops entering the record once a consensus forms, why the early choosers' information behaves like a public good, and why the resulting consensus is both confident and brittle. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply it myself. What I most want them to leave able to say, in their own words, is why "everyone behaved rationally" and "the group was wrong" are not in tension — and I would like to be able to make the same case to my friend afterward.
