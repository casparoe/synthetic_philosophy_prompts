Ok, some background: I'm 68, spent 35 years as a soil scientist working mostly on peatlands and wetland restoration, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy with the enthusiasm of a man who has just discovered he has opinions. No training in it whatsoever, so bear with me.

There's an argument I keep running into and I genuinely can't tell whether the two sides are disagreeing or just using the same words for different things. Side one says: you can never get an "ought" out of an "is" — no pile of facts, however large, ever logically forces a conclusion about what we ought to do, and every argument that looks like it does has a value premise tucked away somewhere, and that tucked-away premise is where all the real work happens. Side two says: sure, but that's just a fact about vocabulary — a conclusion can't contain a word that isn't in your premises, so of course nothing normative comes out of premises with nothing normative in them. That's a triviality about language, not a discovery about morality, and it tells you nothing about whether moral facts are just natural facts.

Why it bites for me: in my old job we'd survey a degraded bog — carbon flux, species counts, hydrology, the lot — and then the report says "therefore this site should be restored." Was that last step cheating? Where exactly did the value get in?

So here's what I want. I gather the fancy name for this is an advesarial collaboration, and I want the joint statement these two sides could actually both put their names to. I have my own crude distinction I'd like you to use or demolish: I want to pull apart the *vocabulary* gap (no normative words in, none out) from the *determination* gap (the facts, even all of them, don't fix which norms are the right ones). And test it with what I've been calling the one-bridge-premise trick: hand the "ought" side exactly one bridging premise of the form "one ought to do whatever promotes X," and suddenly every factual argument becomes an ought-argument. So is the whole fight just about what X is allowed to be? Can X ever be a plain descriptive fact, or does the trick just move the bump under the carpet?

What I need:
1. A numbered list of claims both sides sign — about the logic, about what the gap is and isn't, and about whether "the naturalistic fallacy" is the same animal as the is/ought point or definately a different one (I suspect a lot of the shouting comes from here).
2. A disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated in wording the side that holds it would accept, plus the strongest thing the other side would say against it.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where, if one side turned out to be wrong, it would have to change its mind.
4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and what each side predicts about it, written down in advance.
5. And tell me honestly whether some chunk of this is a verbal dispute, and if so, which chunk.

Don't give me a reading list — I can't get to the university library anymore, and I'd rather have the thing itself than a syllabus.
