I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science and I keep stumbing on a pattern I can't name. In a bunch of case reports I've read — I don't have the refs handy, sorry — there are patients who, when you prick them or bend a joint, will say that yes, that's pain, it hurts, but who show almost none of the usual pain behavior. No grimacing, no pulling the limb back, no asking for anything to make it stop. Some of them laugh about it. The sensation is clearly there: they can point to where it is and say how intense it is. What's missing is the awfulness. A couple of them, if you push, will even agree the pain is unpleasant, but they still don't seem to mind it and won't do anything about it. So there's a weird split: the feeling is present, and maybe even the judgment that it's bad is present, but the whole motivational and hedonic side seems to have come unstuck. Does this have a settled name? I know it's not the same as people born unable to feel pain at all, and not the same as someone who feels it and just toughs it out or hides it. I'm less after the label than the carving: what exactly is the state, how is it supposed to be distinguished from those neighbors, and does it actually tell us anything about whether the badness of pain is part of the sensation or a seperate attitude toward it? Keep it under 300 words, American English.
