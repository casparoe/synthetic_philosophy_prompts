I'm on a working group assembling a handbook chapter on comparative political culture, and we're stuck on a single sentence that has to go in the summary paragraph, so I'd like the underlying dispute worked out rather than papered over. Our evidence base is a standardized civic-assertiveness battery fielded in twenty countries across four waves spanning fifteen years. The country means are strikingly stable — each wave reproduces the previous ordering to within about eight percent — and they predict institutional outcomes we care about (rates of small-claims filings, the frequency of informal sanctions among neighbors) better than any individual-level variable we have. But the details are uncomfortable. The spread within each country is wider than the gaps between countries, so roughly thirty percent of respondents in the most assertive country sit below the median of the least assertive one. When we administer the battery with locally generated items in respondents' own languages rather than with translated items, three country means move by more than a third of the original between-country spread and two of them change places. In seven countries the national pattern reverses at the regional level. And if we regroup the same respondents by language family instead of by state, we get a different ordering that is just as stable and just as predictive. The working group has tentatively proposed three admissibility conditions for any group-level claim we print: that it survive re-description at the level of sub-populations, so that it is not an artifact of aggregation across units that run the other way; that it be statable in a form that licenses no inference about any particular member; and that it name a cause whose operation is not merely the sum of the individual dispositions it summarizes. Half of us think these conditions are exactly right and that our sentence fails at least two of them; the other half think the conditions amount to a philosopher's obstacle course that would rule out every useful comparative claim ever made.

Write the dialogue I would want to overhear between the two most competent people on either side: a comparativist who holds that distributional claims about peoples are legitimate, indispensable, and misrepresented by their critics, and a methodological individualist who holds that the whole notion is either vacuous or pernicious. Give each the strongest version of their case against the evidence I have described — the comparativist on why stability and predictive usefulness survive the framing, reversal, and re-taxonomy worries, the individualist on why they do not — and have them answer each other's actual points rather than trade positions. Neither should win by invoking what some famous scholar wrote; the argument should be carried by the case itself.

Let each concede at least one point. Have them locate precisely where the disagreement bottoms out — I suspect it is over whether the third condition is a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp, but I would like to be shown rather than told. Then either have them converge on a restatement of the sentence that both can sign, or make it plain why they cannot. Do not resolve it with a platitude about respecting both perspectives.
