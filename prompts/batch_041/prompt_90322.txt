I'm in a reading group on philosophy of mind and we've been going through the extended mind stuff — the idea that some of your thinking literally happens outside your skull, in notebooks and phones and calendars, and that the question of where a mind stops isn't the same as where a body stops. I'm supposed to lead the session next week and I'd rather open with fiction than with another paper, so I'd like you to write me a short story (call it 1200-1800 words) that puts the thesis under real pressure instead of just illustrating it.

Here's the seed, change names and details however you like. Near-future coastal town, a company sells "pair-bonded" memory prosthetics: one shared store, running on both partners' implants, that either of them can query as effortlessly as their own recall, no searching, no deliberation, it just surfaces. Two people, married thirty-odd years, both have it. One of them has a stroke and loses their biological episodic memory; the shared store still has everything. Now the legal thing: years ago one of them promised a dying friend something, the promise sits in the shared store, but the spouse who made it has no independent recollection of it, while the other spouse remembers it perfectly and is now being asked, in some kind of hearing, what "her husband remembers." I want the story to turn on that — on whether the shared store is his memory or just her memory of him.

Please dont give me a bibliography or name-drop Otto's notebook and call it a day; I don't care about citations, I care about the story doing the philosophical work. A reader who's never heard of any of this should finish it feeling the pull of the idea and also the counter-pull — the worry that if a store you share with someone else counts as your memory, then so does your phone, your library, your spouse, and "mind" stops meaning anything. Start by brainstorming two or three different ways the story could be structured (whose POV, where the crisis lands, how much of the tech we see), pick the strongest, then write it. You're a philosopher who also writes fiction, so keep the prose clean and don't lecture the reader. Think carefully about the criteria the thesis usually leans on — is the resource always there, do you trust it without checking, does it arrive automatically rather than by effort — and let the story actually stress-test them: does the husband's "memory" still pass those tests when the store is shared with someone who could have edited it, or who might be protecting him? Dialogue-heavy is fine. End on the unresolved thing, not a moral. Thanks!
