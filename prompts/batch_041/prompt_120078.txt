I need someone to poke holes in something I wrote, and I'd rather it be someone who doesn't already agree with me.

Background, because I think it matters for judging the thing. Six months ago I quit a job in logistics at a mid-size food distributor and took a big pay cut to work at a tiny nonprofit (we do lead-abatement outreach in old housing stock, not that the details matter much). I also started giving away about a tenth of what I make. Felt great for about three weeks. Then a friend with a physics degree said something that got under my skin: that I can't possibly know any of this does any good, because the world is chaotic, a sneeze changes the weather, and in forty years the state of the world will be completely different because of tiny things I did, in ways that swamp whatever good the nonprofit does. He called it the cluelessness thing. Now I can't stop thinking about it, partly because if he's right then the way I've been making life decisions is broken, and also every other way of making them is broken, so I want to know whether there's a hole in it or not.

So I wrote an essay to talk myself down. It's short. Here's the argument.

1. Setup. When I pick an act A, the value of the world is v(A) plus w(K(A)), where v is the part I can actually see (the kids who don't get lead poisoning this year, the money I didn't spend on myself) and K(A) is the enormous cloud of downstream knock-on effects. I'll call v the local part and w the ripple.

2. The symmetry move. From where I stand, K(A) is basically a random draw. My act nudges the world off the path it would have taken, but the nudge is tiny and the world is chaotic, so after a while the world's state is as good as independent of which way I nudged it. So the ripple is noise, and noise has no direction — it's as likely to be good as bad. So E[w(K(A))] is either zero or at least doesn't depend on A. Which means maximizing E[V] is just maximizing v(A), the local part. Cluelessness is real but harmless: I'm equally clueless either way, so it cancels out of every decision.

3. The bit I can't get rid of. That only works if the ripple is symmetric around zero, and I don't believe it is. Value isn't a linear function of the world. There's no averaging-out at the level of outcomes — if my nudge raises the chance of some bad thing, the bad thing either happens or it doesn't; the averaging only happens across an ensemble of worlds I never get to see. And if the value function is curved the wrong way, then zero-mean noise in the world doesn't give zero-mean noise in value. If value is concave in whatever my act perturbs, noise hurts me on average; if it's convex, it helps. And I think I can flip the sign of my whole argument just by changing whether the nudge is additive or multiplicative, which tells me the argument is resting on something I haven't pinned down. There are also cases where value looks like a step function — one bad actor gets the technology first, one election goes the wrong way — and then noise that crosses the step is catastrophic in one direction and neutral in the other, so the asymmetry is doing all the work.

4. My patch, which I'm not sure about. I wanted a way to say which acts survive this. Here's the test I came up with. For any act, estimate the largest plausible magnitude of the ripple — not the most likely, the largest — and call that the act's ripple budget. Then call the act sign-robust if its local effect is bigger than its ripple budget, so that no plausible chain of knock-ons could flip whether the act was good or bad overall. Sign-robust acts are ones I can do without being clueless about the sign. The problem is that by this test basically nothing is sign-robust, because the ripple budget of anything is enormous — history is full of tiny causes with gigantic effects. So the test says only things that move the world on the scale of a war or a plague are safe, and I'm not doing those. That seems like either a reductio of the test or a reductio of my life and I can't tell which.

5. What I want from you. No reading list, please — I've half-read the stuff already and it made me more confused, and I don't care what things are called. I want comments on the argument itself. Specifically:

- Is the symmetry move in (2) a standard move, and if so what's the standard objection? Is my objection in (3) the right one, or is there a cleaner and more decisive one?
- Is the step from "the ripple is zero-mean" to "the ripple doesn't affect expected value" actually valid when value is a non-linear function of the world? I keep feeling like I'm quietly invoking a linearity I'm not entitled to. Tell me exactly where it breaks and what the right statement would be.
- Is the ripple budget / sign-robust idea useful, or is it just a restatement of deciding under uncertainty so deep that the probabilities aren't even well-defined? If it's confused, what's the right way to state the thing I'm circling?
- Does any of this change when I'm comparing two acts rather than one act against doing nothing? My gut says the cluelessness cancels in a comparison, and my gut also says that's exactly the kind of thing I'd tell myself to feel better, so I don't trust it.
- And be honest about whether the essay is interesting or just a familiar argument in a new coat of paint. I'd genuinely rather hear the latter than get a polite rewording of what I already wrote.

One more thing. There's a version of the worry where the ripple isn't merely unknown to me but where I can't even say what the possible outcomes are or assign them probabilities, and I have no idea whether my argument survives that version or collapses into something much worse. That's the version that actually keeps me up at night, so if you only do one thing, tell me whether (2) still works there.
