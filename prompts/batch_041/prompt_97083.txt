I have been working through Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling on my own, without any training in philosophy, and the part I keep returning to is the claim that Abraham's readiness to go through with the sacrifice cannot be justified in ethical terms at all: by the ordinary moral accounting he is a would-be murderer, and the suggestion is that he is nevertheless justified because the single individual, precisely as this single individual, can stand in a relation to the absolute that outranks the universal law. I think I understand the move in outline. What I cannot work out by myself is what follows from it, and I would like you to set out the most important implications of the doctrine — not a summary of the book, but the consequences, including the ones Kierkegaard himself may not have drawn.

Three things I would especially like you to press on, because they are where the idea seems to do real work rather than merely sound dramatic. First, the suspension seems to be a suspension for one person on one occasion, and the book tells us almost nothing about the morning after; I want to know whether the implication is that such a suspension can be a standing condition of a life or only a single, unrepeatable moment, and what difference that makes to the possibility of living alongside someone who claims it. Second, the man in question cannot explain himself: if he speaks he becomes either a murderer in our eyes or a madman, and if he keeps silent we cannot tell him apart from a fanatic. The doctrine therefore seems to imply that the ethical community is constitutively unable to distinguish faith from derangement. I want to know whether that is a defect Kierkegaard failed to notice or the whole point, and what it implies about our entitlement to judge people whose reasons we cannot share. Third, the story depends on a divine command that the rest of us cannot verify, and I live in a world where no such command is on offer. I want to know what survives of the structure if the summons is experienced as unconditional but is not credited to God — whether we are left with something recognizably like faith, or only with a license for any intensity of conviction whatsoever.

Beyond those three, please tell me what else follows: for the reality of moral conflict and for the idea that a duty might be overridden rather than cancelled; for the difference between Abraham and the tragic hero who sacrifices a child for the city and can at least be understood; and for what it would mean for a person to be genuinely responsible for an act that no rule could have required of them. I would rather have a small number of implications worked out carefully, with the reasoning shown at each step, than a long list of consequences sketched in passing.
