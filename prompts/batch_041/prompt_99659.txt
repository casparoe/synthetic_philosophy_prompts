I'm assembling material for a paper and a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and before I commit to a view about shared intention and joint action I want the specification written out: what any adequate account of these phenomena would have to deliver. Please give me the desiderata — the cases an account must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the constraints and empirical results it must respect — and for each one the consideration that puts it on the list and the reason it deserves to stay there rather than being a prejudice of one camp. I am less interested in a scorecard of the contenders than in the criteria, so keep any verdict on existing accounts brief; the standard itself is the deliverable.

Two things about the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. I need an account usable in arguments about institutional and corporate responsibility, where the intending agents are committees, firms and states rather than pairs of friends, so an account defined only for face-to-face dyads is not yet adequate for me, and I want the requirement that excludes such accounts stated as a requirement rather than smuggled in. And I do not want the list to beg the question against reduction: if the demand that shared intention be analysable out of individual attitudes plus common knowledge is a requirement, I want the case for it, and if it is a methodological preference, I want that said.

Test the list against cases rather than against intuitions about a single paradigm. Two strangers who walk the same route at the same pace for a mile and then part. A duet that is also a duel, where the same pattern of sound is produced by two people with opposed intentions. A pair who settle on a plan and are both prevented before either acts, so that there is a shared intention and no joint action. A bucket brigade whose members never see or hear one another, and a cathedral raised over four generations by people who never overlap. A firm that acts as one body while most of its members privately oppose the action. A committee member who belongs to two bodies with incompatible joint intentions. A coerced co-author who complies without sharing the plan. Two people who unknowingly dig the same hole at different times and thereby produce a single joint product. A couple married forty years who act together out of habit with no occurrent intention anywhere. A large anonymous crowd whose aggregate behaviour has all the shape of a joint action and none of its internal structure. For each, say what an adequate account owes us, and be willing to conclude that some of these are not the account's business at all.

Then the distinctions, which is where I expect the real work to be: shared intention versus parallel individual intentions plus common knowledge of one another; shared intention versus shared belief or shared desire; the plural form of intending versus an individual intention with plural content; a joint commitment versus a prediction about what the other will do; the normativity participants actually feel versus whatever the account's own resources can generate; and joint action versus joint responsibility, which I take to be separable and want kept separable. Say which of these an account may explain away and which it must respect. Alongside them, the results it must not contradict: that coordination equilibria are reachable without anything we would call shared intention, that individual participants remain the causal movers of whatever happens, and the developmental and comparative evidence about when children, and which other animals, begin to do things together rather than merely alongside one another — with your view on whether contested comparative findings can constrain a theory at all.

Three things I most want from you at the end. Which of the requirements, if dropped, would let the most candidate accounts back in. Which demands are genuine desiderata and which are parochial — I have naturalism, the felt normativity of joint commitment, the possibility of unilateral revision, and tractability to the tools of individual decision theory in mind, but I would rather you tell me. And whether some pair of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, with the argument for it if so; I would not be surprised to find that the demand that the account be built from individual attitudes and the demand that a participant be unable to walk away from a shared intention at will cannot both be met, and if that is right I want it recorded as a result rather than softened. Answer in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — write it the way you would a long letter to a colleague, since that is how I will be reading it.
