I'm running a 90-minute session for 12 final-year students on a legal theory module — the week we do legal change and the rule of recognition — and I want them playing something rather than listening to me. I've been messing about with Nomic (Suber's game, the one where "changing the rules is a move") and its the right shape but completely wrong scale: a proper game takes hours, and the interesting bit only lands once they've internalised thirty rules. So design me a compressed, one-session version.

The thing they should come away seeing is this: a rule that authorises its own amendment can't get its authority from itself without going round in circles, and can't get it from a higher rule without an endless climb — so at some point somebody just has to treat the thing as settled. I want them to hit that point in the room, mid-game, in an actual argument about whether a move was legal, before I've said any of this out loud. Assume they've never heard of Nomic or Suber and have no way of looking anything up, so everything the game needs has to be on the handout. No citations, no further reading — there's no point, they can't use them.

Give me: the starting ruleset, six to ten rules, written out in full, short enough to hold in your head but containing at least two deliberate traps — say one rule that entrenches itself and one that repeals itself, or a pair that conflict with no precedence rule to break the tie, or a motion that claims to have always been in force; the roles and materials (I'm definately happy to be speaker and registrar, and happy to brief one student in advance to play a pedant who exploits loopholes, but tell me whether that's a good idea or whether it flattens the thing); a scoring scheme in points, no money; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the paradox itself and onto the usual escapes — a basic norm, a rule of recognition that isn't itself legally valid, treating the dispute as a change of practice rather than a change of law.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my lot will absolutely try to litigate the meta-rules instead of legislating, and someone will start on about quorum) and how to handle it without short-circuiting the point. And be straight about what the game can't reproduce: in a real legal system nobody amends the master rule by majority vote in a seminar room, and the regress gets stopped by what officials happen to do rather than by logic, so I need to know which part of the idea I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.

Be committed — pick one design and stick to it, don't hand me three variants and leave me to choose. And give me your actual view on whether the self-amendment paradox is a real paradox or a pseudo-problem dressed up as one: say which horn you think bites and why, even if you're not certain. Write it in British English.
