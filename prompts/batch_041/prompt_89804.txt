In the early 2000s a deaf American couple, both members of the Deaf community, sought to have a deaf child: they chose a sperm donor with a family history of deafness, and their son was born deaf. The case became a public controversy, including among people otherwise committed to wide reproductive liberty, and the couple defended themselves by denying that deafness is a deficit. On their account it is a culture, and a deaf child would grow up inside their language and community rather than at its margins.

Analyze the case through the Principle of Procreative Beneficence — the claim that parents who have decided to have a child and can choose among the possible children should choose the one whose life is expected to go best. Do not simply restate the familiar arguments. Instead, do three things.

First, distinguish three readings of the principle: a comparative reading, on which the ranking of possible lives is fixed by an objective list of goods and so is the same for every family; an indexical reading, on which the ranking is fixed relative to the social world the child will actually inhabit, so that the same trait may rank differently for different families; and a threshold reading, on which the principle forbids creating a child below some minimum and leaves wide latitude above it. Say what each reading implies about this couple's choice, and which one best fits the way preimplantation selection is actually practised.

Second, press on a difficulty the case makes vivid. If what counts as a good life for a child is partly constituted by the values and language of the family it is born into, then the selection decision helps fix the very standard by which it is to be judged, and the ranking the principle tells the parents to consult is not independent of the choice they are making. Say whether this circularity is fatal to the principle, merely a limit on how much of it can be operationalized, or evidence that the principle needs a different account of well-being altogether.

Third, consider whether the reasoning that permits this couple's choice would equally permit selection for any trait a family happens to prize — for instance the commercial embryo screening now offered for polygenic traits such as height and educational attainment. If it would not, identify the constraint that blocks the extension and say whether that constraint belongs to procreative beneficence or to some other principle the theory has quietly borrowed.

Close by saying what the episode shows about the principle: a structural feature of it, a case it never anticipated, or a boundary it cannot cross.
