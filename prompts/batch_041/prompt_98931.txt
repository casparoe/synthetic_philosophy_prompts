I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about conditionals, and I should like to know what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures my verdicts. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want to know which account my intuitions commit me to, where they conflict, and what I ought to give up.

Here is what I find myself thinking, case by case.

(i) 'If the key is in my coat, I left it upstairs.' I accept this, and I would accept it while having no idea whether the key is in my coat; it does not seem to assert that the key is in my coat. If it turns out the key was in my trouser pocket all along, I do not think I said something false — I think I said something I had no business saying, or something misleading.

(ii) 'If the key had been in my coat, I would have left it upstairs.' This one seems different. I would assert it only if I took it that the key was not in my coat; the sentence appears to take the falsity of the antecedent for granted. And here I feel there is a fact of the matter: had the key in fact been in my coat, the speaker would simply have been wrong.

(iii) A pair I cannot make symmetrical. A patient's culture has come back positive, and the laboratory is reliable. I accept 'If the patient did not have the infection, the laboratory made an error' — given the positive result, either the patient is infected or the laboratory erred. But I reject 'If the patient had not had the infection, the laboratory would have made an error' — had the patient been healthy, the culture would have come back negative; there is no reason to think the laboratory would have erred.

(iv) 'If the moon is made of cheese, the law of gravity is optional.' I do not want to call this true, and I do not much want to call it false either; it seems to me the sentence simply does not get a truth value, or is not the sort of thing one asserts. Yet I am perfectly happy with 'If the moon is made of cheese, it is not made of granite', which strikes me as trivially fine — and I suspect those two reactions cannot both be right.

(v) A stand-off. A colleague and I hold different evidence about tomorrow's meeting. I say 'If the strike goes ahead, the meeting will be cancelled'; she says 'If the strike goes ahead, the meeting will move online.' On my view we are not contradicting one another: each of us is being reasonable given what we know, and neither is lying. I am told this is hard to square with the idea that conditionals are straightforwardly true or false.

(vi) A case I find genuinely puzzling. Suppose the election is between the Prime Minister, the Chancellor, and the Leader of the Opposition. I accept 'If someone from the governing party wins, then if it is not the Prime Minister who wins, it will be the Chancellor.' I also accept 'Someone from the governing party will win.' But I do not accept 'If it is not the Prime Minister who wins, the Chancellor will win' — after all, if it is not the Prime Minister, it may well be the Leader of the Opposition. I am fairly sure that if all three of these judgements are right, something I have been taught about how premises support conclusions has to give way, but I cannot see what.

So: is there one account of conditionals that delivers all of (i) to (vi), or must some verdict go? I have heard of three candidates — that 'if' is a truth-functional connective; that it quantifies over possible worlds, with the indicative and the counterfactual differing only in which worlds count as relevant; and that an indicative conditional is not a statement with a truth value at all but an expression of a high conditional probability — and I should like to know which, if any, my verdicts fit, and precisely which clause of it each of my cases is doing the work of motivating. Please also say what the account you favour predicts about a case I have not listed: an indicative conditional whose antecedent I believe to be false, uttered only to make a point (for instance, 'If that is a genuine Rembrandt, I am a Dutchman'). Then name the further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates. Where a candidate can save one of my verdicts only by adding a special clause, say so, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I should abandon.

Please answer in British English.
