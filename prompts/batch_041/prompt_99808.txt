Before a seminar on information and rational choice, I want a consistency audit of my own position. Here is the package I plan to defend:

(1) Good's theorem: for a Bayesian agent, a free and reliable observation available before acting can never lower expected utility. The agent can always ignore it, so the value of information is never negative.
(2) I am a causal decision theorist: I maximize expected utility with respect to the causal consequences of my action, and I think the evidentialist's appeal to the news value of an action is a mistake.
(3) I think it can be rational to refuse free information. If a doctor offered me, at no cost, the exact date of my death, I would decline, because knowing would be painful — not because acting on it would lead to worse decisions, but because the knowledge itself would weigh on me.
(4) I think my preferences are not stable across informational states. If I learned I had a terminal illness, the things I now care about — my career, my long-term projects — would stop mattering to me.
(5) I think ambiguity aversion is rational: I would rather bet on an urn whose composition I know to be fifty-fifty than on one whose composition is unknown, even when the expected payoff is the same, and I would pay a small amount to avoid the unknown urn.

Is this a consistent set? Identify which commitments genuinely conflict, order the conflicts by severity, and for each say what would have to give. Distinguish the real tensions from those that dissolve once the terms are made precise, and tell me which single revision would resolve the most.
