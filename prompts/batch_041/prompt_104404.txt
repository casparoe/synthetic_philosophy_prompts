I'm in a small reading group on rational choice and we've spent two weeks on money pumps, and my friend Priya has an arguement that's been eating at me. I'm pretty sure it proves alot more than she wants, but I can't find the bad step, and every fix the group has floated either costs more than it's worth or just restates the problem. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) At 9am I prefer the sweet pastry to the savory one; by noon, once I've had coffee and my appetite shifts, I prefer the savory to the sweet. Nothing exotic — my tastes move around, and I know they will.
(2) If I'm the kind of agent who, at any moment, does what she then most prefers, then at 9am I'll trade Priya my savory pastry for her sweet one plus a dollar (I'd rather have the sweet one anyway, and the dollar is free money), and at noon I'll trade back for the savory one plus a dollar. Each trade is one I'd endorse at the moment I make it.
(3) I end up exactly where I started, minus two dollars.
(4) An agent whose preferences can be used as a handle for extracting money from her, through a sequence of trades she'd each voluntarily endorse at the time, is irrational.
(5) So I'm irrational — not because of anything about pastry, just because my preferences changed between 9 and noon.
(6) That's absurd. Changing your mind isn't by itself a rational failure. If it were, the only rational agents would be ones with fixed tastes forever, and everyone who grows, learns, or matures would be irrational.
(7) So either (4) is false or the move from (2) to (3) is broken. But (4) is just the premise behind the standard money pump against cyclic preferences — the A≻B≻C≻A case — where it's supposed to be devastating. And the (2)→(3) move looks airtight to me: each trade is one I'd make, and the dollars are real.

So which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me somewhere else? I'd much rather keep the standard pump against cycles than accept that changing your mind makes you irrational, but I want to know what I'm paying. Two people in the group said the answer is that I should just stick to my 9am plan and refuse the noon trade, and one said the real trouble is that the whole story assumes I can't see the trap coming — Priya says both of those are just relabeling the question, and honestly I can't tell if she's right or if she's begging it. What I really want to know is whether the pastry case and the cyclic-preferences case differ in some principled way, or whether the pump was always only ever an argument about how you choose rather than about what you prefer, in which case I have no idea what's left of the usual case against cycles. Assume for the sake of the story that I can't hire someone to hold my wallet or sign a binding plan — Priya says without that the whole thing is a non-starter, which I find suspicious but can't articulate why.
