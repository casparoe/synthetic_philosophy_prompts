Here's the question:

In lecture we defined two idealizations of deliberation as a tree of sub-deliberations. HCH: the tree is a fixed point where every node is a human with the same competence as the root, and each node may query child nodes; the root's answer is whatever the recursive consultation procedure returns. Factored cognition: a deliberation is decomposed into a set of factors, each answered locally, with a verifier that checks whether the factors compose to the original question; factors can be answered by any process, and the decision of how to factor is itself a possible factor. For both, the oversight tax is the extra cost of verifying and combining sub-answers rather than just accepting a direct answer.

Compare the two idealizations on (a) what they treat as primitive, (b) whether they can represent a deliberation that revises its own decomposition, and (c) what each implies about whether the oversight tax can be driven to zero in principle. Then say which idealization is better suited to modeling a deliberator who is trying to improve her own decomposition.

Here's the student's answer:

Both HCH and factored cognition start from the same tree picture. In HCH, every node is a human who can call on other humans as subroutines. The primitive step is a human judgment that isn't decomposed further. In factored cognition, the primitive step is a local check on a factor: you split the question into subquestions, answer each one, and then verify the answers against the context. The main difference is that HCH is just a special case of factored cognition where the factors happen to be whole humans, while factored cognition is more general because the factors can be non-human modules. That seems right.

On self-revision: HCH can revise its own decomposition, because a human node can decide to consult a different set of sub-humans next time. Factored cognition can't, because the factorization is fixed once you choose it; the only thing left is to answer the factors. So HCH is the more general idealization of a deliberation that can modify itself.

On oversight tax: HCH has a large tax, since every sub-answer has to be legible to the human at the parent node. Factored cognition has a smaller tax, because local checks are cheaper than re-deriving the whole answer. And if the factorization is complete, the tax goes to zero: the parent only has to check that the factors compose, not redo the work. So factored cognition does show that the oversight tax can be zero in principle, while HCH doesn't, because humans are bounded.

I think the takeaway is that neither is a literal algorithm. HCH tells you what a perfectly patient human chain could do; factored cognition tells you what a perfectly modular reasoner could do. The alignment tax is about actual systems, so these idealizations can't settle it by themselves.
