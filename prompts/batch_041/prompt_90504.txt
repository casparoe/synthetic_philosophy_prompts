I am taking an aesthetics seminar in which the professor repeatedly deploys the slogan that art is the communication of feeling, and I have now seen it do three different jobs: dismissing a question about whether a machine-generated poem or an ordinary urinal displayed in a gallery could be art, arguing that a sunset cannot be art because no one made it in order to communicate anything, and explaining why a technically accomplished but cold novel fails as art. I can tell the slogan is not doing the same work in all three cases, but I cannot articulate the options. Please lay out as a menu the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be making. Separate, for instance, the claim that art is defined by the artist’s intention to communicate an emotion, the claim that art is defined by its capacity to evoke an emotion in an audience, the claim that art is defined by the presence of expressive properties independent of anyone’s actual feelings, and the claim that communicating feeling is merely a central function or excellence of art rather than a necessary condition. For each reading, say what it would require of the artist, the object, and the audience; whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; and whether it would actually support the professor’s dismissals. I am especially interested in the readings that are in the weeds of the definition-of-art debate: whether feeling must be occurrent or can be imagined, whether communication requires successful uptake or only expression, whether the view is meant as a real definition or as a normative ideal, and how it fares against ready-mades, abstract music, and emotionally manipulative propaganda. The deliverable should be a disambiguation with verdicts, not a defense of one reading.
