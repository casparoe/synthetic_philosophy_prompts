Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I spent thirty-odd years as a field biologist — birds mostly, then a long detour into insect navigation — and after I retired I started sitting in on, and now sort of co-teaching, a senior seminar on minds and evolution. I've been handed the job of writing one of the final exam essay questions, and I'm definately sick of reading answers that just list the four creatures (Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian) like rungs on a ladder with us smugly perched on top. I want a question that can't be answered by that recitation, and I want it to land on something I actually find confusing.

Here's the confusion, and I'd like the question to force students into it. The Popperian creature is supposed to be the one that runs its trials and errors inside its own head, letting its hypotheses die in its place rather than the creature itself dying out in the world. But it seems to me there are cases where the inner model a creature is running isn't really its own — it's been installed from the outside, by us or by another species. A city dog that's learned to wait at a crossing because it's been walked there a thousand times is running something like an inner model of the traffic, but that model is a piece of human culture it inherited, not something its own kind ever figured out. Same worry, I'd guess, with anything we've trained or bred, and now with the machines. So is that dog a Popperian creature, or is it a Gregorian creature wearing a Popperian coat? And if it's the second, does the whole ladder survive, or does it turn out that a creature can only reach a given rung by standing on some other creature's shoulders? I'd like the exam question to make them take a side on that and defend it, not just define the terms and sit on the fence.

Could you write me the question — something a good senior can do in about 45 minutes of writing — plus a model answer of around 700 or 800 words, and a marking scheme. For the scheme I don't want a vague rubric; I want it built around the specific moves a top answer has to make, and the specific muddles that should cost marks (students who think "Popperian" just means "smart," say, or who treat the four levels as a fixed taxonomy when the interesting question is whether the levels are properties of individual organisms or of lineages and populations). Please make sure the question itself doesn't give the answer away, and that a student who's only memorized the standard definitions walks out with a C at best.
