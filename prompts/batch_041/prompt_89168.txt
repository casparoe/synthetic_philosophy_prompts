I am a third-year student in a philosophy of the social sciences course, and we have just finished Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism*. In our seminar a classmate defended a version of historicism using an analogy that I keep meeting in other reading: a society is like a living organism, so its history is like the life of a body. On this picture a society has determinate stages, a growth, a maturity, and an old age; its future course can be known in advance the way a physician knows that a child will grow into an adult; and there are laws of historical development in much the same sense in which there are laws of biology. She drew the further conclusion that attempts to arrest or redirect the natural course of a society are as futile as trying to stop a child from growing up, so that large-scale social prediction is possible and piecemeal reform is beside the point.

I want to write my term paper as an audit of this analogy, and I would like your help laying it out. Please do the following. First, give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — stating what plays the role of the organism, its organs or parts, its development, its environment, the physiologist who studies it, and the laws that govern it. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the conclusion my classmate draws from the ones that are merely picturesque. Third, say which features of the organism picture the analogy quietly imports — a built-in teleology, a fixed life cycle, the irrelevance of the parts' own purposes and agency, the existence of a detached observer standing outside the process — and assess whether a society has anything corresponding to each. Fourth, tell me whether her historicist conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion the whole time. Fifth, if the analogy is doing illicit work, propose a better one for the phenomenon she is trying to capture, and say what it would and would not support.

Two clarifications that I would like you to respect. Popper's own verdict is supposed to be that this is a case of misplaced analogy, but I want the reasoning rather than the verdict, so please do not simply invoke his authority. And please keep distinct the strong claim that history has no laws at all from the weaker claim that whatever regularities history exhibits do not license the long-range predictions my classmate wants; I suspect her argument needs the stronger claim to fail, and I want to know whether that is so.
