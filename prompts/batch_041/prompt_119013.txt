I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on theories of legal interpretation, and I need a hypothetical built to specification rather than a real dispute. Assume that no reported decisions, committee reports, floor statements, or scholarly commentary exist for the statute in question, and do not refer to any actual statute, case, or author. Invent a short statute — one operative provision of no more than sixty words, with a title — together with a set of facts, such that the three positions below deliver the following pattern of verdicts.

Required pattern: (a) plain-meaning textualism holds that the statute reaches the conduct at issue; (b) mischief-based purposivism holds that it does not; (c) intentionalism — the view that the interpreter should implement the specific applications the enacting majority expected — also holds that it does not. The exercise exists to show that the familiar framing, in which textualism is the constraining view and purposivism the expanding one, is an artifact of the stock examples; the case must reverse that framing rather than announce it.

Constraints:

1. The purposivist must reach the narrower result without legislative history. The purpose is to be recovered from the title, the structure of the provision, and the state of affairs the enactment was responding to, all of which you should state explicitly; it may not be read off the very words whose scope is in dispute.
2. The textualist's broader reading must not be curable by pointing to a term of art. The operative term's ordinary meaning should genuinely outrun the enactment's purpose, and the case should not turn on a specialist sense of a familiar word that a competent contextualist would simply notice.
3. The textualist's result must be defensible but not absurd, so that the absurdity doctrine and any scrivener's-error repair are unavailable; the stakes should be real but civil — a licence, a benefit, a permit, a deduction, an employment term — so that no rule of lenity enters.
4. No canon of construction may be decisive. Where a canon is available, it must cut both ways, or you must explain why it is unavailable.
5. The facts should be ordinary and the statute plausible as something a real legislature might have enacted.
6. Neither outcome may line up with the reader's likely sympathies. Arrange the parties so that the more appealing result is not the one the reader will be tempted to reach, and so that the party favoured by the broader reading is not the obviously sympathetic one.
7. Do not use the stock examples: vehicles in a park, a tomato under a tariff, a gun bartered for drugs, a boat that is also a vessel, or a pre-digital word applied to a new technology.

Deliver, in order: the statute and facts in under two hundred words; the verdict and the argument for each of the three positions, stated in that position's own terms; an identification of the single feature of the case that is doing the work; a removal test in which you re-draft the operative provision so that the pattern collapses into the conventional one, with an explanation of why it collapses; the strongest attack on the case from whichever position fares worst, and whether the case survives it; and a candid note on any respect in which the case risks stipulating the verdicts rather than generating them from the theories, and how you avoided that or failed to.
