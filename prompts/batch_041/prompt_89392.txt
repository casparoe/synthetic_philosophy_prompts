I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Wittgenstein and scepticism, and I am grading the final exam. The question was:

Explain what Wittgenstein thinks is going on when Moore claims to know he has two hands, and assess whether the certainties Wittgenstein calls hinges can themselves be doubted or rationally revised. Your answer should say something about the riverbed image and should end with your own verdict on revisability.

One student's answer, reproduced in full:

Moore held up his two hands and claimed to know that they existed, and he took this to be a proof of the external world. Wittgenstein does not deny that Moore's hands exist; what he denies is that Moore knows this in the way one knows that the train leaves at six. Propositions of this kind — that I have two hands, that the earth has existed for many years, that my name is such and such — are what Wittgenstein calls hinges. They stand fast for us, and they are what make doubting, testing and justifying possible: I can only check the colour of this table because I already take for granted that the table is there and that my eyes work. Hinges are not the conclusions of an investigation; they are the background against which investigations take place. Moore's error is therefore not that he is wrong but that his words are idle: when someone says he knows he has hands, the expression of certainty has no work to do, and the assurance it offers is not needed. This is why Wittgenstein says that certainty of this sort is not a form of knowledge at all, but something more primitive — a way of acting, a form of life.

Can hinges be doubted or revised? The riverbed image suggests the answer. Our beliefs form a river: a moving current of hypotheses on top, a hard bed of certainties beneath. The bed is not fixed forever — the river can shift it — and so what functions as a hinge varies from time to time and from community to community. But the reason hinges cannot be doubted is not evidential. We have no evidence for them, and asking for evidence only shows that one has misunderstood their role. It is rather that we are so constituted that we cannot bring ourselves to doubt them: a person who seriously doubted that he had two hands would be mad, and madness is a psychological condition rather than a philosophical position. When a hinge does change, then, it changes the way a river changes its bed — gradually, and not as the result of any argument.

Wittgenstein's positive account, then, is that hinge propositions carry a kind of entitlement: they are commitments we are rational to hold even though nothing supports them, and this is what distinguishes them from mere prejudice. It also explains why the child's certainties, absorbed from the adults around her, can be perfectly rational without ever having been checked.
