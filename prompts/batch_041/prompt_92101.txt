I chair the honorary degrees commitee at a mid-sized UK university and I need a short written opinion to circulate before we meet on Tuesday, because I think we're about to do something we'll regret whichever way we go.

The short version: the Braintree Foundation has offered us £2.4m for a new chair in materials science. The offer isn't formally conditional, but their trustees have let it be known, twice now, that the next honorary doctorate is expected to go to Sir Owen Braintree, the foundation's founder, a retired engineer who made a fortune in industrial ceramics, paid for a lot of science outreach, and is generally regarded as a decent populariser of the subject and not a scholar of any distinction. Our ordinances give us two routes: the ordinary one, for people of conspicuous distinction in scholarship, public service or the arts, and an old exceptional-generosity category, used twice since 1974, which nobody on the committee has ever quite worked out how to apply. Half the committee thinks the generosity route solves everything, we take the money, we award the degree, we never claim he's a great scientist. The other half thinks that's a fudge, since the citation gets read out at the ceremony, we write it, and the whole point of an honorary degree is that the university is putting its name to an estimation of the person. Our registrar asked, reasonably, whether a university can esteem anyone at all, or whether that's just a manner of speaking about what it does. And one member said flatly at the last meeting that if we take the money we have to actually think he deserves it, otherwise we're lying, and I can't tell whether that's exactly right or a category mistake. There's also a nagging point from the other direction: someone keeps saying we have excellent reasons to award the degree and no reason at all to admire him, and I'm not convinced those two things come apart as cleanly as he thinks.

So please draft the decision memo. Recommend something concrete, with a time frame (the foundation's trustees meet on 14 March and the ceremony is in July), and tell me what the citation should actually say if we go ahead and how it can be true without being a lie. Take the strongest version of the losing side seriously rather than patting it on the head. And be straight with me about which bits of this are settled and which are live disagreements among people who work on reasons for attitudes, I don't want a memo that presents one side of a real philosophical dispute as if it were common ground. Around 800 words.
