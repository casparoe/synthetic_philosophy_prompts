ok so this is a weird ask but I'm kind of stuck. My best friend Maya is leaving her job at a big corporate law firm after eleven years to go teach high school history in a town of 4,000 people. Her going-away dinner is Saturday and I'm doing the toast.

Here's my problem. Everyone at that table is going to want me to say something like "she made the right call" or "she finally figured out what really matters," and I can't say either. Not because I don't support her — I totally do — but because I think those things are false. She didn't weigh two options on one scale and pick the bigger one. She traded one whole kind of life for another whole kind of life, and I don't think there's any common ruler that says one of them is worth more. Somebody told me the technical word for it when two things are neither equal nor one better — "on a par" or something — and the more I think about it the more that's exactly what this was. I actually think the honest thing to say is also the loving thing to say, but it's tricky, because it can come out sounding like "good luck, you might have just ruined your life," which is really not what I mean.

The room: about half are lawyers plus two philosophy grad students who will know exactly what I'm up to the second I say "incommensurable" and will be watching to see if I fumble it. The other half is her parents and her brothers, who think I'm being weird and just want to hear somethng nice about their kid.

Write me the toast. Four minutes max, meant to be spoken, nothing that sounds like a lecture. She's sitting right there and I have to look at her while I say it. Don't put words in her mouth, don't pretend I know what she was thinking when she decided, and please don't do the thing where the teaching "was always her real calling" — it wasn't, she found it late, and she's genuinely scared about it.

Then, under separate headings, in this order:

1. The toast — the whole spoken text, roughly 550 words.
2. Why I wrote it that way — about 200 words on what each move was doing and where you were walking a line.
3. If someone pushes back at dinner — three or four short things I can actually say out loud when her brother-in-law asks why I couldn't just say "congratulations."
