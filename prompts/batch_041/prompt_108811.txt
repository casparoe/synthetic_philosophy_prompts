Having retired from a career in experimental physiology, I have spent the last few years reading philosophy, and I keep returning to a view I have seen called tranquilism: that only the absence of craving and of suffering is of value, so that the good life is not one filled with goods but one emptied of craving and pain. I had assumed this was more or less Epicurus's position, and I want to know how much of it survives a careful comparison with him. Epicurus took the goal to be a life free of bodily pain and mental disturbance, and he held that the removal of pain is the limit of pleasure — beyond that point pleasure can be varied but not increased; his later interpreters accordingly distinguished the pleasure of a stable condition from the pleasure of active enjoyment. Tell me where the two views genuinely diverge, not merely where they sound alike, and which of them is more defensible once the divergences are on the table.

Two points seem to me to carry the weight. First: is Epicurus's claim that the removal of pain is itself a pleasure a substantive commitment, and would a consistent tranquilist have to reject it — and if so, does tranquilism imply that a placid, entirely inactive life is the summit, whereas Epicurus evidently thought philosophy and friendship worth having? Second: does tranquilism treat the absence of craving as good in itself, or only as a means to the absence of suffering? If in itself, then a creature constitutionally incapable of craving is already at the summit; if only instrumentally, then a craving that happened to cause no suffering would be harmless, which makes the view look like ordinary suffering-hedonism with extra steps. Take a definite position on both points, and say where your confidence is weakest.
