I'm assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of unity, and I need a dialogue that can be read cold, with no framing from me. Write a dialogue between two philosophers who are equally well informed and equally formidable.

The first argues that the infinite regress traditionally associated with F. H. Bradley is vicious. The line: any attempt to explain how several items compose a single unified whole by positing a relation among them must posit a further relation to attach that relation to its relata, and so on without end; the demand for a unifier is never met by supplying one more item that itself stands in need of unification, so the apparent unity of ordinary complexes, propositions included, is unintelligible as it stands, and genuine unity must be located in something that precedes all division into related items.

The second argues that the regress is benign. The line: it trades on treating a relation as one more term on a par with its relata, and once that is rejected the regress either fails to get started or collapses into the harmless admission that unity must be taken as primitive rather than analyzed; nothing in it should shake our confidence that relations are real and that a proposition is genuinely one thing rather than a heap.

Neither may be a strawman: each should grant what is genuinely strong in the other's position, and each reply should answer the specific argument just offered rather than restating a general commitment.

Then have them press both positions against a case for which neither has a stock answer: a melody that can be transposed into another key and still be the same melody. Each should say what unifies the notes into a melody, on the view being defended, and whether the transposition case counts for or against the thought that unity is an additional entity rather than a feature of the parts themselves.

Deliver exactly five exchanges, where one exchange is one speech by each philosopher. Keep each speech to a few sentences, and include no scene-setting, no narration, and no closing remarks.
