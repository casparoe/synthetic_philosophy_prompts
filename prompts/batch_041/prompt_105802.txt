A colleague and I assembled the following argument in a reading group on the epistemology of markets, and I cannot get past it. Each step strikes us as defensible on its own; the conclusion strikes us both as false. My colleague says it is the no-trade theorem eating its own tail. I would rather keep the epistemic steps — the ones about information and belief — than the economic conclusion, but I want to know what that preference costs me.

(1) A price at which someone is willing to transact is a public occurrence: anyone can observe that the trade was offered and struck.
(2) Public occurrences are evidence. The fact that someone is prepared to buy or sell at a given price is evidence about what they believe, and therefore, indirectly, about the world.
(3) Suppose the participants in a market share a prior, are rational, and each knows the others to be rational, knows that they know, and so on. In such a market, an offer to trade at a given price is not a surprise: given the shared prior and the shared knowledge of rationality, the fact that a counterparty is prepared to sell at that price is something any participant could have worked out in advance.
(4) An occurrence that could have been worked out in advance, and that everyone knows could have been worked out in advance, tells you nothing you did not already know. So the offer carries no information.
(5) Therefore, in such a market, no participant's beliefs shift when a price is quoted or a trade is struck. And since beliefs are what guide action, and since the participants' beliefs are already correct, no one is corrected by the market either.
(6) If prices cannot correct anyone's beliefs, then prices cannot aggregate dispersed information; there is nothing for a price to transmit that its observers do not already possess.
(7) So the price system cannot perform the function usually assigned to it — conveying the dispersed, particular knowledge of time and place that no single mind could assemble. A market cannot be a discovery procedure, and an economy that relies on prices to coordinate divided knowledge cannot coordinate it at all.

The conclusion is absurd, and I refuse it. Where does the argument go wrong? Name the step you reject, explain why that step looked compelling in the first place, and then price the alternatives: what do I have to give up at each of the two or three best places to stop the argument, and which of those costs is lowest? If you think the argument is valid as it stands and that one of its assumptions is simply false of actual markets, say which assumption, and say what has to be true of real markets for prices to be informative at all.
