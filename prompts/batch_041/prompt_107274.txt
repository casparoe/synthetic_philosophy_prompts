I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we keep circling back to whether brain size has any moral weight, and honestly it's eating my week. A guy in my group -- he's a neuroscience PhD student so he's not just being provocative for fun -- laid out this argument and I've been chewing on it since Tuesday because I can't decide if it's airtight or a trainwreck. He says:

(1) Moral status isn't all-or-nothing. A being's claim on us comes in degrees, and it tracks how rich its mental life is -- how much it can feel, remember, anticipate, suffer.
(2) How rich a mental life a creature can have is set by its brain, by how much neural machinery it has to do the work, scaled to its body so we're comparing like with like. (He kept dropping "encephalization quotient" like it settles everything.)
(3) That machinery varies continuously across the animal kingdom, and in smaller ways within our own species too.
(4) So moral status varies continuously as well, both between species and between individual humans.
(5) And if it varies between individual humans, then in principle we'd be wrong to treat a large-brained person and a small-brained person as exact moral equals; a scheme that ranked them wouldn't be monstrous, it would just be applying (1) honestly.

I can live with (4) applied to animals -- I think a chimp has more of a claim on us than a shrimp does and I'm not embarrassed about that. But (5) makes my skin crawl, and he says that's just sentimentality, that I'm treating "human" as a magic word. He also says my objection proves too much, because whatever makes me gag at (5) would also make me gag at (4), and I already swallowed (4). I tried the comeback that the within-species correlation between brain size and cognitive test scores is weak and half of it is just body size, but he said fine, weak still isn't zero, and (1) only needs degrees, not big degrees.

I'd rather keep (1) than (5) if I have to choose -- I don't want to give up the idea that how much a creature can suffer matters. So which step should I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? Does rejecting (5) drag (4) down with it, or is there a principled place to cut between them? And if the real problem is somewhere in (2) or (3), tell me exactly which move is illegitimate and whether he's equivocating on something.
