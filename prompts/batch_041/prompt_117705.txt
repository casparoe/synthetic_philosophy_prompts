I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading formal epistemology and the foundations of decision theory, chiefly in order to discover whether the habits of thought I acquired in the laboratory were provincial or whether they track something real. There is one such habit I would now like to interrogate.

In my old line of work a symmetry argument was normally taken to settle the form of a prior distribution, and a disagreement between two competent people who had seen the same data was normally taken as evidence that at least one of them had blundered. The formal literature appears to ratify both instincts. On the Harsanyi doctrine, differences in subjective probability are to be explained entirely by differences in information; and Aumann's agreement theorem shows that two agents who share a prior, and whose posteriors for a given event become common knowledge, must assign that event the same posterior however different their private evidence. The common prior assumption does a great deal of work in game theory and in the economics of information, and it is usually defended as a requirement of rationality rather than as a mere modelling convenience.

Bertrand's paradox is the case that keeps me up at night. A single well-defined physical setup — a chord drawn at random in a circle — admits several symmetry arguments, each apparently as good as the others, each yielding a different distribution for the chord and hence a different answer to the same question about it. If the paradox is genuine, two reasoners can begin from incompatible priors without either of them having ignored any information, which is precisely what the Harsanyi doctrine forbids. If it is not genuine — if it dissolves the moment one insists that randomness be operationalized as a specific sampling procedure — then the doctrine is untouched, but at the price of conceding that its demands concern how a problem must be posed rather than how a mind must be built.

Write an essay on this controversy that reaches and defends a verdict. Decide whether the common prior assumption is a genuine requirement of rationality, an idealization that rational agents may legitimately fail to satisfy, or a convention that purchases tractable models at the cost of a false picture of disagreement. Take a position on whether Bertrand's paradox is a real counterexample or a badly posed question, and let that verdict carry weight in your argument about the common prior. Address whether a prior can be mistaken in the way a belief can be mistaken, or only more or less useful; the answer bears directly on whether the common prior assumption could be a requirement of rationality at all. Finally, state what becomes of the familiar conclusions about speculative trade and betting that are usually derived from the assumption, should your verdict go against the doctrine. Argue from first principles rather than surveying the literature, and write for a reader who already knows the standard moves.
