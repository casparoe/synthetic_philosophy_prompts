Ok so I'm a science journalist, mostly stats and medicine, and next month I'm on a panel at a big epidemiology/biostatistics meeting — I'm the media guy they wheel out — and I've got 12 minutes plus Q&A to defend the argument from a feature I wrote last year about that kidney-stone study where treatment A wins in both sizes of stone and then loses overall.

My thesis, and this is the part that'll get me in trouble: the "paradox" isn't a paradox and it isn't evidence of confounding. The reversal is a fact about which summary measure you picked and which population you want the answer for. The odds ratio doesn't even collapse — the pooled odds ratio isn't a weighted average of the stratum-specific ones, so a randomized trial with zero confounding can hand you two different numbers and neither of them is wrong. Change the weights, i.e. change the target population, and the reversal flips or evaporates. So when we write "it was confounding all along," we're usually wrong, and the honest headline is "it depends who you're asking about."

The room: working epidemiologists and biostatisticians who think this was all settled decades ago, two or three Pearl-style causal-inference people who'll want to draw a graph on me, a handful of clinical trialists, and some data journalists who are on my side.

Give me the Q&A prep: the objections I'll actually get, ranked by how much damage they do, each written the way that person would say it, with a two-minute reply, what I should concede in the first line of the reply, and where the reply is soft. Tell me which one I should preempt in the talk itself, and give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And dont be nice — if I've just rediscovered something everyone in that room learned in grad school, say so and tell me what to say instead.
