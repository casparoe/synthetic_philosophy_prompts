"Both thinkers agree that people can become good, but they disagree about where the goodness comes from. Mencius holds that we're born with four sprouts — the heart of compassion, the heart of shame, the heart of deference, and the heart of right and wrong — which are the beginnings of the four cardinal virtues. He pairs the heart of deference with benevolence and the heart of compassion with propriety, while the heart of shame yields righteousness and the sense of right and wrong yields wisdom. The child-at-the-well example is meant to show that compassion is native: anyone would feel alarm at a child about to fall in. The Ox Mountain parable makes the point that we lose this goodness through neglect, the way the trees on a hill get cut down. So for Mencius the task is to nourish and extend what we already have, while for Xunzi there's nothing good there to begin with.

Xunzi's position is that human nature is evil and that goodness is the product of deliberate effort. Left to ourselves we're driven by desire and self-interest, and following those leads to conflict, so we need ritual and the correction of teachers to reshape us into something decent. Where Mencius trusts inner reflection, Xunzi trusts external training.

The good-vs-evil contrast gets the slogans right, but I think it oversells the disagreement, because both of them end up telling you to cultivate yourself; they just start from opposite ends. If anything Xunzi is the more pessimistic of the two, since for him only a rare few people are actually capable of becoming sages, so his real concern is keeping the majority in line rather than perfecting anyone."
