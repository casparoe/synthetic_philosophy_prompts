I have worked in a joinery shop for close to twenty years and have never studied philosophy, but I have spent the last year reading about a disagreement among philosophers over what a law of nature is, and I want it restated in the language I actually think in. I have no philosophical training: I know the two positions only in the rough form I am about to give them, and words like necessity or supervenience mean nothing to me unless they are built up from something I already understand.

Here is the disagreement as I have pieced it together. One side says a law of nature is nothing over and above the patterns in what actually happens: it is the best summary of the world's behaviour, where best means striking a balance between being simple enough to carry in your head and strong enough to say a great deal, and there is no further fact about what must happen. The other side says a law is what makes the pattern hold in the first place, that the world's regularities are downstream of something that constrains them, and that this constraining is a real extra ingredient in the world rather than a description we impose for convenience.

The vocabulary I think in is the shop's. Boards move: a wide panel glued up from narrow strips shrinks and swells across its width and hardly at all along its length; a tabletop cups when one face dries faster than the other; some species twist as they come down to the moisture of a heated room and some do not. We speak as though a board wants to move, as though the wood has a tendency in it. We keep rules of thumb that cover nine cases in ten and are easy to remember, and we keep long tables of exact figures by species and thickness, and we argue about which is worth more. We have rules that are plainly ours, such as house styles, a customer's specification, the way this shop has always cut a drawer, and rules that are plainly not ours, in the sense that ignoring them splits the panel. And I have been handed rules by other shops for timbers I have never worked and may never touch.

Restate both philosophical views in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report: which parts come across cleanly, which come across only with a stretch and what the stretch costs, and which have no counterpart in how I talk about wood at all. I am particularly interested in whether my way of speaking quietly turns one view into the other — whether a board wanting to move is really the second view or only shorthand for a pattern, and whether the shop's habit of asking who set the rule smuggles in a rule-setter that the philosophers may not want. Then tell me whether what is left over is a defect of my vocabulary, which was built for actual boards rather than for what might have been, or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it sounded. I do not want an analogy; I want the systematic translation, and I expect the failures to be the interesting part. Finish by saying plainly whether my vocabulary makes one side look obviously right, and whether that is an argument or merely an artifact of the trade.
