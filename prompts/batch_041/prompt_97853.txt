I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is an economist who drifted into game theory and now cannot stop noticing the epistemic assumptions buried under ordinary conversation; I want his speech and his private reasoning to sound like a practitioner rather than like a journalist's idea of one, and I have reached the edge of my own understanding of the material. In the course of researching him I came across a long popular essay on the subject — the kind of piece a serious science journalist writes after reading the secondary literature — and one paragraph of it gave me a tidy, memorable formulation that I have been repeating to friends ever since, though I have a nagging sense that my character would wince at it. The passage runs roughly as follows: 'Aumann proved that two people who are both rational, and who each know the other is rational, and who know that the other knows, and so on, cannot agree to disagree. The engine of the result is simple: agents who reason correctly from the same information must arrive at the same conclusion, so any disagreement that survives reflection must come from either irrationality or a difference in the information the parties hold. The theorem also vindicates the older intuition that common knowledge of rationality is precisely what makes backward induction work in games like the centipede — if everyone is rational and everyone knows it, the play unravels back to the first move. This is why economists are fond of saying that rational agents cannot disagree in equilibrium, and why the result is often invoked to show that stubborn public disputes are at bottom failures of reasoning rather than genuine clashes of judgment.'

What I want from you is an accuracy audit I can actually use. Go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a working game theorist or decision theorist would simply accept, which parts would make them wince — and why, whether it is a technical term used loosely, an assumption smuggled in as a conclusion, or a theorem stretched past its scope — and which parts are straightforwardly false. I am especially interested in the closing sentence, since my character's entire predicament depends on whether the theorem licenses anything like it: does the claim about disagreement in equilibrium, and about public disputes as failures of reasoning, follow from whatever is true in the earlier sentences, or does it require something the theorem does not supply? If the popularizer has quietly replaced a demanding condition with a weaker one, or has let a metaphor do work that only a formal result could do, I would like to know exactly where the seam is, and what a defensible version of the passage would say instead — one that a game theorist could read aloud without flinching.
