I am trying to forecast the simultaneous choices of a handful of rivals in a small, stable market, and my method is to shrink the list of actions each of them might take by setting aside any action that looks like a bad bet against the rest. I have been told two things that I want you to assess and then build on. First, that I may sometimes discard an action because it is inferior to a mixture of the other actions available to that party, even when no single other action is plainly better than it -- so that an option can be ruled out by averaging rather than by a straight comparison with any one alternative. Second, that the order in which I discard options cannot matter, and that once I have discarded everything discardable I will be left with exactly the actions a rational, well-informed party could defend.

Please describe the most important implications of this method of pruning for the forecast I am trying to make. I want to know what it licenses me to eliminate and what it does not; which of the two claims I was told survives scrutiny and which has to be qualified; what assumptions about the other parties' rationality, and about their knowledge of one another, I must make for the pruning to be legitimate; and what the surviving set of actions can and cannot tell me about what will actually be chosen -- including whether it may still leave me with many open possibilities rather than a determinate answer. Where the guarantees hold only under special conditions -- say, only when there are exactly two parties, or only when the inferiority is of one kind rather than another -- I would like that boundary drawn sharply, with a small concrete illustration of where it bites.
