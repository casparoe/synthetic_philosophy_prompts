A university panel I sat on last term had to decide whether to leave up a post that would have been unremarkable said aloud to a dozen people, but that a recommendation system had pushed to tens of thousands, and I came away having changed my mind about Mill. I now hold a version of the harm principle in which the object of the principle is not the speech act but its amplification: an utterance as such is protected, but once it enters a distribution channel whose reach is asymmetric and not chosen by its audience, the reach becomes the thing that may legitimately be regulated, because it is the scale of uptake rather than the content that produces the harm. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are: the cases that would break it, the arguments that it collapses back into ordinary content-based regulation, the objection that the utterance/reach distinction cannot be drawn without smuggling content back in, and the charge that it proves too much by licensing any restriction on any widely-heard speech. Tell me which of these a defender would themselves concede and which they would dismiss as merely awkward, and give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into something that risks nothing at all. What I would personally find most persuasive, in either direction, is a case in which identical content distributed at two very different scales intuitively warrants opposite treatment, or a demonstration that no such case can be constructed.
