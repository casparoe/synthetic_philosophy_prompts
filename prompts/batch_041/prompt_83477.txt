Me and a couple of friends have been arguing for weeks now about tight versus loose cultures — you know the idea, some societies demand a lot of conformity and punish deviations hard, others let people get on with their own thing. I've ended up with a position I'd like you to attack properly. Take the role of someone who works on cultural evolution and the philosophy of the social sciences, and before you start answering, spend a bit of space just brainstorming what my view is actually committed to, because I don't want a list of the textbook objections I've already heard.

Here's the position, roughly. I think how much rule-following a society should demand ought to be calibrated to two things: how observable people's behaviour is, and how asymmetric the costs of coordination failure are — I've been calling the second one the 'cliff ratio', the cost of the worst possible failure divided by the cost of a typical needless restriction. Where behaviour is hard to see, I say you're better off with few permissive norms plus heavy ex-post sanctioning than with lots of tight ex-ante rules; where the cliff ratio is high — flying planes, running a nuclear plant — tightness is justified even when it costs people a great deal of liberty. I also insist on splitting three things everyone else lumps together: how many rules there are, how much latitude each one allows, and how hard they're actually enforced. A friend reckons the whole thing is simpler than I think, and that tightness mostly just tracks the public observability of behaviour — cultures get tight because behaviour becomes transparent (dense shared space, gossip, and nowadays logging and ratings), not because of any real threat. If she's right then my story is mostly decoration. I genuinely don't know.

So: what would count against this? Tell me what my position predicts that its rivals don't — the threat story, the observability story, and the boring one where tightness is just a byproduct of inequality or of status competition among would-be enforcers. Two cases I keep going back and forth on: an online community with perfect logging and no existential threat whatsoever that nonetheless became incredibly rule-dense; and a fishing village in real physical danger that stayed loose about almost everything except the sea. Say what each one does to me. And please be honest about whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — if it has, tell me whether that's really a problem here or just what any decent social-science claim ends up looking like.
