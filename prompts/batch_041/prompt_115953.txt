I'm a novelist — three books, the last one a fairly bleak thing about an alignment lab, which is probably why my friend who runs a small interdisciplinary summer program talked me into running a 90-minute session for about 18 people: half MFA writers, half AI-ethics grad students, most of whom have read one pop-sci article about AI safety and nothing else. The topic I somehow volunteered for is low-impact AI, the idea that you try to build an agent whose actions leave the world as close as possible to how it would have gone anyway, so it doesn't wreck things as a side effect of chasing its goal. What I want people to walk out feeling in their bones is a bind I only half understand myself: the whole selling point of an impact measure is that you write it without ever saying what's valuable — the agent is supposed to drop into any world and work for any goal — but "side effect" means unintended effect, and unintended relative to what? If the measure never mentions what we care about, it can't tell a harmless rearrangement from a catastrophe; if it does mention what we care about, you've smuggled in a whole theory of value and the measure isn't doing any work. I want them to run into that wall themselves, not hear me explain it at the end.

I have a rough mechanic in mind and you should feel free to scrap it if you see something better. Each table gets a world: a big sheet of paper with a grid of coloured tokens on it, plus a starting arrangement that counts as how things would have gone if nobody had touched anything. Each table's agent has a goal on a card (something legible and a bit stupid, like get every red token into one corner) and a small budget of moves. Before anyone moves anything, a *different* table writes the impact rule for them — a scoring rule that takes the before state and the after state and spits out a number — under the hard constraint that the auditors may not be told the agent's goal and may not ask. Then the agents play, the auditors score, and we look at which worlds got wrecked and which sailed through. The moment I actually care about is the reveal, when the goal cards get turned over: I want the group to see that the auditors had no way in principle of writing a rule that flagged the thing everybody agrees was a disaster, because the only feature distinguishing it from the intended effect was that nobody wanted it. I'd also like the no-action baseline to be something the auditors have to pick themselves, and to discover the hard way that picking it already commits them to something.

So: build me the runnable version. Rules, materials, the roles (including whether the writers and the grad students should be seperated into different roles on purpose, and which way round), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90, and stakes that don't need money. No lecture up front and no vocabulary on a slide — they shouldn't hear terms like impact regularization or attainable utility until the debrief, if at all, and I can't hand out readings or cite anything, so everything has to come out of the activity itself. Then the debrief, which should walk back through specific moments that happened at the tables and say which part of the problem each one is a picture of, and be honest about which features the game simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud instead (I suspect the real thing lives in enormous action spaces over long horizons and that we'll be showing them something much smaller and tidier, but tell me if I'm wrong about that). Finally, tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and what to do when it does — my guess is that one table's auditors write a rule so vague it flags everything and another write one that flags nothing, and that I'll have to resist the urge to rescue them, but I'd rather hear your guesses than mine.
