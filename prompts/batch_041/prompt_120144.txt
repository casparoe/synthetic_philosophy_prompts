I run a small research desk at a newsroom -- me and two junior staff -- and we keep a short list of claims we treat as settled (MMR vaccine doesn't cause autism, the 2020 election wasn't stolen, that sort of thing). New studies and reports land on us constantly and we've got maybe an hour each to decide what to do: respond publicly, quietly file it, or treat it as a genuine threat to something on the list. There's this old paradox in epistemology that goes: if you really *know* something, then any evidence against it must be misleading, so you're entitled to just wave it off -- which obviously can't be right as stated but feels like it's pointing at something real. Write me a procedure my juniors can actually run, in order, for deciding when it's legit to set contrary evidence aside and when we have to engage with it, grounded in what the epistemology of knowledge and defeat actually says rather than vibes. I have a hunch the distinction between "this shows the claim is false" and "this shows our reasons for the claim were worse than we thought" is doing a lot of work here, but I don't know how to turn that into steps. It has to survive three cases: a big well-run study that contradicts something on the list, a junk study that doesn't, and the nasty one where the new evidence is weak but reveals our original grounds were shakier than we admitted -- I suspect the procedure should force us to engage there even though the claim is probably true, and I want you to be straight with me about where the procedure runs out and its just my call. Keep it to something we can run in an hour, and mark which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure.
