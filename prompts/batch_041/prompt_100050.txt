So I run a small interview podcast about epistemology — basically me and whoever I can convince to come on — and next week's guest is a philosopher whose new book makes a claim I keep turning over. Her line is that the accuracy-first program gets the order of explanation backwards. By accuracy-first I mean the package where credences get assessed by how accurate they are, accuracy is measured by a scoring rule like the Brier score (the proper ones being the ones that reward you for saying what you actually think rather than for hedging), and the governing norm is to pick the credence function with the highest expected accuracy — which is what's supposed to get you probabilism out of a dominance argument and conditionalization out of expected accuracy. She thinks accuracy isn't valuable in itself and then used to justify the norms; she thinks the norm comes first, and the claim that accuracy is epistemically good is something we install by convention — a rule the epistemic community adopts and passes along, which is why it feels like it has authority even though nothing more basic backs it up. She also argues thats a feature rather than a bug: it explains why an accuracy-firster can't answer the question of why anyone should care about accuracy without begging it.

I want to press her on this without letting her just restate the book. Give me nine or ten questions, and for each one that's likely to draw a stock reply, add a one-line follow-up keyed to that reply. Please make sure there's at least one question each on: (1) the order-of-explanation reversal and whether it's circular — if norms come first and the norms are conventions, where does the first convention get its authority; (2) the structural vs. substantive issue, i.e. whether on her view a perfectly coherent agent with absurd priors is doing anything epistemically wrong at all, and what she says to people who think that's the only thing we wanted a theory of credence to deliver; and (3) self-application — what credence her own view says she should have in her own view, and whether a theory whose norms are local conventions can claim to be telling anyone else what to believe.

One more thing I'd like somewhere in there: a concrete case. There's a forecasting lab whose internal scoring convention rewards decisiveness, so a member who says 0.5 about a genuinely chancy event gets marked down for hedging. I want a question that forces her to say whether that member is being epistemically irrational or just living under a different convention, plus a follow-up for whichever way she goes. And if you can fit it in, one question on whether any of this survives when the credences are imprecise rather than precise.

She's sharp and a bit combative, so questions that leave her an obvious exit aren't much use to me.
