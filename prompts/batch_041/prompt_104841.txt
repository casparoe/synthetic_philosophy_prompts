I am preparing a handout for an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of psychiatry, and I want a decision tree that sorts the positions one can take on a single question: does the fact that psychiatric categories loop — that classifying people changes the people classified, and the changed people then change the category — bear on whether those categories are real, valid, or merely useful? The purpose of the handout is to make students answer questions about cases rather than pick a label, so no node should ask anything like "are you a realist or a constructivist?"

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each internal node should be a question on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased as a concrete case or a claim about a case, and answerable by someone who does not yet know which position the answer leads to. Draw the branching questions from distinctions such as these, adding or dropping nodes if the space demands it: whether a classification can change the classified without the change being mere self-fulfilling prophecy; whether the change is confined to what members believe and do or reaches the causal structure that makes the category apply; whether a category whose application conditions shift under pressure can still track a stable dysfunction; whether the reality of a category is settled by its causal role, by its discoverability, or by its usefulness; and whether looping is a defect of a category or simply what it is to classify people.

The leaves should include at least the following positions, each carried by a proponent's name where the position is standardly identified with one and left unnamed where it is not: (i) looping is noise, and categories are real to the extent that they capture a biological dysfunction independent of how people respond to the label; (ii) looping is the mark of a distinctive kind of kind, so human categories are not defective natural kinds but a different sort of thing; (iii) looping is epistemically real but metaphysically idle — classifications change behaviour and self-conception without changing the underlying kind; (iv) looping is one of the causal mechanisms that holds a category together, so a category can be both real and loop-dependent; (v) the category is constituted by relations among its symptoms, so looping can be constitutive rather than distorting; (vi) the reality question is misplaced and categories are instruments to be judged by their consequences; (vii) the categories are harmful fictions; and (viii) the category is real as a social identity rather than as a disorder.

Then do three things. First, run each of five cases through the tree and report where it lands: the rise and subsequent contraction of multiple personality / dissociative identity disorder as a diagnosis; the emergence of pro-ana communities in which anorexia is reframed as a lifestyle; a newly proposed category that I invent for this exercise — call it digital attention fragmentation disorder, defined by smartphone-linked deficits in sustained attention — which has no settled dysfunction and whose members actively seek and contest the label; the broadening of autism criteria together with the rise of a neurodiversity self-description; and the repeated reclassification of grief after bereavement as a disorder. Second, mark any combination of answers your tree permits that no one occupies, and say for each whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Third, flag any leaves your tree cannot separate from one another, and say what further question would be needed to pull them apart.

Keep the result to a handout: a tree of at most six internal nodes and eight to ten leaves, one sentence on each leaf's most awkward commitment, and no more than a page of prose around the diagram. Do not cite literature; describe the positions in your own words, and attach a proponent's name only where the position is standardly identified with one.
