After forty years at the bench I have retired into philosophy, and I have been trying to make sense of epiphenomenalism — the view that the felt qualities of experience are perfectly real but causally inert, mere byproducts of the brain processes that produce them. In a reading group I assembled the following argument against it, and I now find both the argument and my own reaction to it suspicious. (1) To know that I am in a particular experiential state, the fact that I am in it must be appropriately connected to my belief that I am; on the strict reading, that fact must explain the belief. (2) Epiphenomenalism says phenomenal properties have no causal powers. (3) My belief that I am now seeing red is a physical state, and on epiphenomenalism it is produced by brain processes rather than by the phenomenal redness itself. (4) So the phenomenal redness does not explain my belief. (5) So on epiphenomenalism I cannot know that I am seeing red. (6) But I do know that I am seeing red; it is the least revisable thing I know. (7) So epiphenomenalism is false. (8) Yet the standard of knowledge in (1), applied without favor, also denies me knowledge of mathematics, of the past, of other minds, and of tomorrow's weather, for in none of those cases does the fact known cause my belief. (9) I am not prepared to call all of those ignorance, so the standard in (1) looks too strong. (10) If I relax (1) to demand only a reliable connection between fact and belief, (5) no longer follows, since the brain process that produces my belief is reliably correlated with the phenomenal redness — and epiphenomenalism survives after all.

I am attached to (6) and to the general shape of (1), but I will surrender either if that is the cheapest exit. Tell me which step or steps to reject, why they looked sound, and what rejecting them costs.
