I run a reading group in my dept (10-12 people, mostly CS/ML grad students plus a couple of philosophers, 75 minutes, one whiteboard) and next week I'm on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. Don't explain the topic to me -- I know it. Plan the session: order, example, misconceptions, the works.

What I actually want them walking out with is the meta/internal hinge, not "Gödel again" and not "self-trust is impossible." From the outside we can say of a sound system that whatever it proves is true, and that costs us nothing. The obstacle bites only when the agent needs that same fact as a theorem in its own language -- the reflection schema -- and Löb says that a system which proves "if I prove A then A" for its own provability predicate can already prove A, so pasting on "and my proofs are sound" buys you no trust, it just makes the system prove everything. The spine has to be built so that when Löb arrives it feels like the last door closing rather than a trick.

So work out for me:

- what has to come first and what genuinely depends on what. I'm unsure whether they need the provability predicate as a predicate, with the Gödel numbering, before they can feel the reflection schema, or whether they can meet the schema first and only later learn it's a predicate. Which order makes Löb land? I'd rather cut material than rush a dependency.
- the one example that carries it. No Gödel numbering on the board for more than thirty seconds, nothing they'd have to look up, and it has to make the self-reference feel buildable rather than cute. Don't use the parable everyone uses to introduce Löb -- half the room has read it and it turns the whole thing into a riddle. My own draft is a border post that only stamps a passport if its own rulebook says the passport is valid, where the rulebook also contains a page declaring that whatever the rulebook says is valid is valid. Tell me whether that has the right skeleton or whether I've just built a thing that asserts its own authority, and replace it if it's wrong. (Fallback is the successor-agent story, but I worry that hands them the escape hatch too early.)
- the misconceptions, in the order they'll show up, and where in the sequence to head each one off: "so it's just Gödel's second incompleteness theorem again"; "the agent needs more compute / a bigger proof search"; "just verify a hash of your own source and you're done"; and the one I'm most worried about, "we know the system is sound, so the agent can just use that" -- that one *is* the hinge, and I want them to feel the pull of it before I close it.
- one closing question or five-minute exercise that tells me they got the hinge rather than memorized Löb.
- what to openly refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud. Some of them have heard of logical induction and will want it to be the happy ending; I want a line for that which isn't a promse.

Format: The spine (~400 words), The carrying example (~300), Misconception map (~400), The check (~100), What I won't explain (~150).
