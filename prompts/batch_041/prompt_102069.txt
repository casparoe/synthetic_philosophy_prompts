Two friends in my reading group got into it last week about coherence arguments and I've been chewing on it since. It started when one of them, call him Dev, said the money-pump argument basically settles it: if your preferences cycle -- you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A -- then someone can walk you around the loop and take a cut at each step, and any agent that can be bled like that is irrational, so rationality demands transitivity, and the same kind of argument does the rest of the work and you land on expected utility maximization.

The other one, Sam, wasn't having it. She said the pump only works on someone who says yes to each trade in isolation, and a sophisticated agent who sees the loop coming just refuses the first swap. Dev said fine, but then that agent is stuck -- if the options are forced on him he still goes around the loop, and if they aren't he's just frozen, which is its own kind of failure. Sam said the frozen case proves her point: you've shown that cycles make you *vulnerable*, which is a fact about predators and environments, not a theorem about rationality, and "you can be exploited" doesn't entail "you're irrational" unless you already assume the thing you're trying to prove. Dev came back that a cycle means no utility function can represent your preferences, and without that there's no such thing as doing better or worse by your own lights, so you're not even pursuing anything.

Sam said that's just assuming goals have to be real-valued, and that incompleteness is the real problem case anyway -- if she simply doesn't rank A against B, no pump obviously gets her, yet completeness is one of the axioms too, so the argument looks like it proves too much. Dev said incompleteness gets you pumped as well, since you'll pay a bit to dodge a forced choice between incomparables. Sam said that only works if she has specific preferences about being forced to choose, so he's smuggling in extra preferences to save the argument.

They asked me to referee and I cant tell who's got the better of it. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right about what, where each of them is overreaching -- and say who's closer overall?
