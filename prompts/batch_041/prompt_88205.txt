For the past two years I have been compiling a colleague's case-by-case verdicts on extended cognition, and a new case has come up that I would like you to call before she does. She is a philosopher of cognitive science, and her verdicts are always of the form "part of the process, or not" — in every case below, the process is memory.

Her record so far:

1. The standard Alzheimer's notebook case: part of the man's memory.
2. A woman with a mild memory impairment who wears an earpiece that supplies names unprompted as she meets people, and who reports that the names simply arrive: part of her memory.
3. A novelist who keeps an index of her characters' histories and turns to it constantly while drafting, never deciding to consult it, and who says the index is where the book's past is kept: part of her memory.
4. A man whose phone search is always within reach, and who consults it whenever he has consciously judged that he does not know something, then weighs what he reads against his own recollection: not part of his memory.
5. A man who keeps a diary, reads it once a week at a fixed hour, deliberately, and describes it as a record he keeps rather than as his memory: not part of his memory.
6. A man who telephones his brother for facts he needs and accepts what he is told without checking: not part of his memory.

The new case: a woman carries a language-model assistant on her phone and in her earpiece, configured to volunteer facts, reminders, and framings without being asked. She stopped deliberating about its output long ago; its offerings arrive and are used the way her own recollections are. It is reliable, and on the rare occasions it is wrong she corrects it the way she would correct a colleague. She says of it, though, that these are the assistant's views and not hers, and she describes it as an unusually well-informed colleague who happens to travel with her.

Predict her verdict — but give me the reasoning rather than the verdict alone. At least two rules fit her record, so tell me which rules those are, what each of them predicts here, which she is most likely running and why, and which further case would settle the matter. I suspect the new case is precisely where the plausible rules come apart.

Two things I would rather have than a smooth answer. If her recorded verdicts do not in fact admit a common rule, and my list is an artifact of the cases I happened to collect, say so instead of manufacturing a rule that fits it. And if the question as I have posed it conflates two that ought to be kept apart — whether a resource is part of a cognitive process, and whether it is part of a person — say that plainly rather than answering the question as asked.
