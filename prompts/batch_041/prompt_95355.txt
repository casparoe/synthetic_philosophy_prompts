I am a teaching assistant for a second-year undergraduate seminar on probability and philosophy, and I am grading a problem set on Bertrand's chord paradox and the principle of indifference. The seminar has already covered the standard three ways of generating a random chord, so I wrote the following question to see whether students could transfer the lesson to a generator they had not seen. A circular target has radius 1. A chord is generated as follows. First, a direction is chosen by spinning a pointer uniformly over [0, 2π). Second, a distance D is chosen uniformly from [0, 1]. Third, the chord perpendicular to the chosen direction is drawn at distance D from the center. (a) Compute the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. (b) A student in the seminar claims this generator is uniquely neutral because it treats every direction equally and every distance from the center equally. Assess that claim. (c) What does your answer imply about the principle of indifference and about the original question, What is the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?

The student's answer, reproduced without edits, is as follows. (a) The chord length is 2√(1−D²), and the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle is √3. So the chord is longer exactly when 2√(1−D²) > √3, which simplifies to D < 1/2. Since D is uniform on [0,1], the probability is 1/2. (b) The uniqueness claim is false. The classic alternatives are random endpoints on the circle, giving 1/3, and a random midpoint uniformly in the disk, giving 1/4. So neutral does not pick out a unique procedure. The proposed generator is not rotationally invariant, because the pointer selects a distinguished radial direction; the random-midpoint method, by contrast, treats every point in the disk alike and so is the more natural neutral procedure. (c) This shows that the principle of indifference is inconsistent. If the same information—that a chord is chosen at random—supports 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, then the principle cannot be a valid rule for assigning probabilities. The original question has no determinate answer; it is meaningless until a procedure is specified. Once a procedure is specified, as in (a), the probability is determinate. So the right conclusion is that the original question is ill-posed, not that probability is subjective.
