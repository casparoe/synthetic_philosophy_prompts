I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been stuck on this thing I keep calling the long-tail expected value problem. I've heard people call it consequentialist cluelessness, but I don't know the literature. The gist is consequentalism: right action is the one with the best consequences. But for almost any real action, the causal ripples are so sprawling and chaotic that you can't put a non-arbitrary expected value on it. Not just uncertainty—like, the sign of the long-run effect is often unknowable, and second-order effects swamp first-order ones. I see it at work constantly: we tweak a ranking model, and a year later some user cohort is behaving differently in ways nobody modeled, and the downstream effects on who gets seen, who gets hired, etc. are way bigger than the A/B test. So my position is: consequentialism is practically inert as a decision procedure because the required expected-value calculations are hopeless for ordinary agents. It either collapses into doing whatever you were going to do, or it becomes a blank check for rationalization.

What I want is what would count against that. Not the canned stuff like rule consequentialism exists or utilitarians have replies—I mean the real defeators: arguments that the cluelessness is overstated, cases where we can actually rank long-run consequences, formal results about decision-making under deep uncertainty, or ways the view is self-undermining. I also want to know which of these a committed cluelessness person would concede as counting against them versus just dismiss, and whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Like if every apparent counterexample gets absorbed as more hidden ripples, is that a bug or is that just what the view is? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what would personally move someone who already thinks this way. Also, is there a real difference between not knowing the expected value and knowing that the expected value is unknowable in principle? That distinction feels like it matters but I can't tell if it's just semantics.
