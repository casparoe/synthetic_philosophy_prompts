I have no academic background — no degree, no training in philosophy or statistics — and I have spent the last few years teaching myself probability from textbooks and worrying at a single problem, which began when I tried to work out what a positive medical test actually told me about the person who took it. I have ended up with a position, and I would rather have you attack it than explain it back to me.

The position has three parts. First, no evidence is ever certain: whatever I come to believe on the strength of observation — that the display reads 3.7, that the tube turned blue, that the test came back positive — I believe with a probability below one, because instruments, labels and my own attention all fail. Second, it follows that the rule for changing my mind cannot be ordinary conditioning on a proposition, since that requires the evidence to be taken as certain, that is, assigned probability one; the correct rule is the one I have seen called Jeffrey conditionalization, on which I take a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities, hold fixed my conditional probabilities within each of them, and simply redistribute my probabilities across them, so that my new probability for anything is a weighted sum of my old conditional probabilities, weighted by my new probabilities for those possibilities. Third, and more strongly, this is not a technical generalization of conditioning but the only honest description of what any real inquirer does; conditioning is a limiting case that never quite occurs.

What I want is the case against it, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, and I want to know which of these objections someone who holds the view would have to concede as a genuine cost and which they could reasonably explain away. Tell me also what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it is capable of being wrong at all — or whether my third claim is doing no work beyond a claim about how idealized the ideal case is, and whether that matters.

Four things already bother me, and I want to know which are real defects and which are artefacts of how I have set the problem up. (i) When I update twice in a row, once on one set of possibilities and then on another, I seem to get different answers depending on which I do first. (ii) The answer seems to depend on how I carve up the possibilities rather than on what I have learned, so that two people with the same information but different vocabularies would end up believing different things. (iii) If the input to the rule is itself uncertain, where does the first uncertain input come from — does the account not presuppose that I already have probabilities for my own possible experiences, and where did those come from? (iv) The rule holds fixed my conditional probabilities within each possibility, and I cannot see why learning something would not change those as well.

Finally, give me one concrete case that a defender of ordinary conditioning would offer as certain evidence, and tell me whether I should accept it. Do not worry about citing anything; I will not be looking it up.
