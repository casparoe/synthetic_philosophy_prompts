Ok so I run this tiny podcast — more of a reading group with a mic, honeslty — and next week I'm talking to a philosopher who's spent years arguing that we learn how to live mainly from exemplary people, not from rules or from theory. She's basically a modern defender of what Plutarch was up to in the Lives, and she's friendly to the old hagiographers too, the saints'-lives genre, which is definately not a popular position these days. This got weirdly personal for me: I went through a rough stretch last year and reading biographies did more for me than any ethics book ever has, and I can't really say why, which is kind of the whole reason I'm doing this episode.

Can you draft me eight or ten interview questions that actually get into the machinery of the view, not just "so you think stories are important"? Things I want covered, roughly one question each:

- the obvious kill-shot: if a hagiography smooths over all the flaws, doesn't that make the person un-imitatable — what exactly is the reader supposed to copy, and does the genre survive once you admit the flaws?
- why should I trust my own admiration? I've admired people who turned out to be monsters, and half of who I admire is just luck about who I happened to meet. How does the view avoid building ethics on a faculty that's demonstrably unreliable?
- the transfer problem: I've read a hundred stories of courage and I'm still a coward. What's the mechanism supposed to be, and what would the theory predict actually changes me?
- whose lives get held up. The exemplars we inherit are a pretty narrow set, and the canonization is a social and political process. Does the theory have anything to say about that, or is it stuck with whatever gallery a culture happens to enshrine?
- is this a rival to rule-based ethics or just a motivational add-on? If exemplars are supposed to tell us what to do, how do they do it without collapsing back into rules?

Then two things I specifically want to push her on, because I've got half-baked opinions of my own:

- my pet theory is that exemplars don't work by being copied at all — they work by quietly changing what seems possible for me, widening the range of lives I think I could live. Ask her whether that's a friendly amendment or whether it guts the view (if nothing gets copied, what's left of "learning from" a life?). I genuinely don't know which way she'll go, so give me the follow-up for both answers.
- the hagiography thing again but sharper: the old lives of the saints were written to be imitated, and they were also often, let's say, factually loose. Is there a real difference between "a true life that inspires" and "an inspiring story that isn't quite true," or is the theory quietly committed to the story being true when it might not need to be?

For each question give me a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left flat-footed.

One more thing — tell me how confident you are in these, and single out the one question you're least sure is the right way to press her. I'd rather you flag a weak question than pad the list.
