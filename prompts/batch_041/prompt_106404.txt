You are a philosopher of history with a specialty in causal explanation and the historiography of environmental determinism; I am a doctoral student drafting a qualifying essay, and I want you to write the essay itself.

The essay should engage the following controversy: whether it is legitimate to treat non-human forces — pathogens, climatic shifts, volcanic eruptions — as historical actors in their own right, or whether doing so inevitably collapses back into the environmental determinism that our field has rightly abandoned. A colleague has offered a position I find too quick in both directions: she holds that any non-human factor that "makes a difference" to human outcomes thereby earns the label of actor, while her critics insist that such factors can only ever be background conditions and never causes properly speaking.

Before writing, brainstorm the space of positions between these poles and identify what is really at stake in the disagreement — in particular, what work the word "actor" is doing that "cause" or "condition" is not. Then set out and defend a clear criterion for when a non-human factor counts as a historical actor rather than a mere enabling condition; apply that criterion to at least two sharply contrasting cases, one fast-moving and one slow (for instance an epidemic and a gradual climatic trend); confront the strongest objection to your view, including the worry that any such criterion is deterministic in disguise; and arrive at a definite verdict on which side of the controversy is closer to the truth. Argue from concepts and cases rather than from named authorities, and do not leave the conclusion hedged or suspended between the two camps.
