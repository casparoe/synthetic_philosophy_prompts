I'm in my third year and I'm taking a seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and I've come away from last week's session with an argument I can't settle by myself. The question on the table was whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and my classmate Priya and I ended up on opposite sides of the case our instructor put up: a user in a persistent social VR platform whose avatar is subjected to a simulated violent assault. Priya thinks the harm is real — the fear and humiliation are real, the avatar is the user's body in that world, and the response that it is only a game is exactly what lets platform owners off the hook. I've been leaning the other way: what happened was a fiction the user experienced, closer to being badly shaken by a film, and calling it an assault inflates the concept and quietly insults people who have actually been assaulted. Our instructor then made the case harder, in a way neither of us could handle. Suppose the perpetrator turned out to be an NPC, a scripted agent with nobody behind it; or suppose the platform let the user restore the avatar to its previous state, so that nothing in the world's history was lost and no trace remained. Does the harm survive either wrinkle? If it survives, is that because the experience was real, or because the avatar is a real object with real standing in a real social world? If it doesn't survive, then what exactly was the distress about?

I have to write a response paper on this by Friday, and before I commit to a line I want to see the strongest version of the exchange. Please write the dialogue I wish Priya and I had had: a sustained conversation between two equally competent interlocutors — one a realist about the virtual, holding that virtual objects are real objects of a distinctive, socially constituted kind, of the same family as money, borders, and the characters of fiction, whose reality is a matter of shared practice rather than physics; the other an irrealist or fictionalist, holding that virtual objects are not real and that virtual harms reduce to ordinary psychological distress about a fiction — working through the case and the two wrinkles rather than trading slogans.

Make them actually answer each other. When the realist appeals to the analogy with money or with institutional facts, the irrealist should say precisely what the analogy gets wrong; when the irrealist concedes that the distress is real but denies that it is a harm of the relevant kind, the realist should press on what that qualification is doing and whether the same move would dissolve other harms we take seriously, such as harassment by letter, defamation, or a credible threat. Let them disagree about whether the avatar's ontological status matters at all, about whether the NPC wrinkle shows the harm is really about the victim's experience or about a wrong done by someone, and about whether entering a world voluntarily is like getting on a roller coaster. Let each of them concede something real at some point, and let the dialogue end with the disagreement sharper and better located than it started rather than dissolved into a truce. Somewhere around 1500 words is fine.
