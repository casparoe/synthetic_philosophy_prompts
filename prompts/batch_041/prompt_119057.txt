Ok so I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy training, and I've gone down a rabbit hole on this thing people call dirty hands — the idea that a leader sometimes has to do something genuinely wrong to do the job right, and that the fact it bothers them is a good sign, not a bug. I've been poking my gut with cases and I think there might be a rule behind it, but I keep hitting one that breaks it. Can you tell me what principle actually fits, and where I have to give up a verdict?

My gut, case by case:

- Wartime: a general orders a strike knowing it'll kill some civilians, because it's the only way to stop a massacre. My gut: he should order it. But he doesn't get to feel clean about it — if he sleeps great afterward I think something's definately off with him. So: right to do, but stained.
- A doctor lies to a patient about how rough a treatment is, so they'll actually take it and live. My gut: honestly I think this is fine?? Which bothers me, since it's still a lie to someone about their own body.
- I blow off helping my friend move because on the way I come across a car crash and pull a stranger out of it. My gut: totally fine, no guilt, no stain at all. The promise just loses.
- An interrogator tortures a suspect to find a bomb that'll kill hundreds. My gut: not ok, even if the bomb is real and it works. Not just illegal — wrong.
- A CEO needs something grubby done to save the company, and gets a junior person to do it while keeping their own hands clean. My gut: this is worse than if the CEO had done it themselves. Delegating makes it dirtier, not cleaner.
- Someone refuses to tell a lie that would save their friend's life, because lying is wrong. Friend dies. My gut: the refusal was wrong, and "I kept my hands clean" is no defense.

The rule I keep half-seeing: it's ok to do the wrong thing when the person you're wronging would, if they knew everything and were being reasonable, agree to be wronged for the sake of what's at stake — that's why the doctor and the car crash feel fine and the torture doesn't. And separately, the stain shows up when you're actually aiming at the harm, not just accepting it as a side effect — the general isn't trying to kill civilians, they're just in the way, while the interrogator's whole plan is the pain. And the CEO thing is about whose tab it's on: the wrong has to be on your own hands, not outsourced.

But I can't tell if that's one principle or three duct-taped together, and the wartime case seems to need the stain while the car crash case refuses it, even though both are "do a small wrong for a big good." Is there a single principle here, or do I have to drop a verdict? And what does your version say about a case I haven't listed: someone does the wrong thing for a good reason, it works out, and nobody ever finds out — is there still a stain if there's no one around to see it?
