I'm writing the final paper for a course on aesthetics and the epistemology of taste, and I want to avoid the well-worn test cases — the tourist who is told the canyon is sublime, the gallery-goer who reads the wall label before looking. I'd like to read Pixar's Ratatouille through the debate about aesthetic testimony, and I want the reading to be of use to someone who knows the philosophical literature rather than only the film.

The tension I want to work through is this. The pessimist holds that a judgement of taste cannot be justified by someone else's say-so: I can take your word that the wine is corked, but if you tell me it is wonderful, all I have is a reason to go and taste it, because the judgement has to be mine. That is usually cashed out as an acquaintance principle — aesthetic justification requires first-hand experience of the object. The film seems to dramatise exactly this. Remy's taste of strawberry and cheese together is an event no description could hand to anyone else; Ego's ratatouille is a first-person occurrence that undoes him; and the whole apparatus around the restaurant — reviews, stars, a dead chef's cookbook promising that cooking is within anyone's reach — is a machine for getting people to hold aesthetic beliefs at second hand.

Two things complicate that reading, and I want both taken seriously. First, the film grants testimony powers the pessimist does not obviously allow. Gusteau, the dead chef who argues with Remy inside his own head, is inherited authority — a book, a reputation, a voice. And the film ends with Ego's review filling the bistro and Ego himself trading his standing as a critic for a stake in the kitchen. If second-hand taste is worthless, why make a piece of writing the instrument of redemption?

Second, and this is what I would most like tested, I want to try a distinction of my own against the film. Call it the attention view: what aesthetic testimony transmits is not justification for the verdict but a direction of attention — a reason to look here rather than there, which is a real epistemic good even if it leaves the later judgement no better founded. The pessimist would then be right about warrant and wrong about value. Does the film bear this out? Does Ego's review function as a pointer rather than a proof? Or does the film contradict the attention view by insisting that only the first-person event counts, so that whatever testimony does is aesthetically idle? I suspect the Kantian thought that a judgement of taste demands everyone's agreement while resting on nothing but one's own feeling is somewhere in the background, but I don't want that to swallow the paper.

Please don't reduce the film to an illustration of a position, and don't bend the position to fit the film: say where the reading strains. One place I am unsure is the comedy of a rat in a kitchen — a creature who is not a member of the community of taste at all, and whose taste is presented as a gift rather than an achievement. Does that push against the acquaintance principle, or quietly presuppose it? Be explicit about how confident you are in the reading you give, and single out the claim you are least sure of; I would rather have the weak joint flagged than a uniformly confident essay.
