I'm a retired field ornithologist — thirty-odd years of inferring what birds wanted from what they did, which in hindsight is a fairly philosophical way to earn a living — and since I retired I've been reading up on this machine-learning idea of inverse reinforcement learning, where you watch an agent behave and back out the objective it must have been maximizing. A friend of mine in the CS department insists this is basically how we should think about all talk of preferences: behavior is the data, the utility function is the hypothesis, and that's the end of it. I think theres a hole in it, and I want a clean, concrete example rather than a hand-wave.

So: build me a case where an inverse reinforcement learner, watching a single competent human being in an ordinary modern setting, with as much demonstration data as you could ever want, comes back with an objective that ranks two outcomes in the opposite order from the way the human actually ranks them. I've started calling this a quiet reversal — the learner gets the ordering backwards in a corner of the world the person never gets to act in, so nothing in the observed behavior ever contradicts it. Ruled out: noise or thin data, a sloppy or lazy or irrational demonstrator, the person deliberately concealing anything, and any environment built like a trap for learners (no adversarial mazes, no fake sensors, no teleporters). Also, the human has to have a definite and correct view about which of the two outcomes is better — not indifference, not ignorance, not a case where they'd say "I've never thought about it." One paragraph of scenario, realistic enough that I could imagine it happening in a hospital or on a farm or in a school.

Then do the work of showing that it works: what the the learner sees, what it infers and why, and exactly which assumption of the standard setup is carrying the weight. I'd also like to know whether more data ever rescues the learner, and what happens if you weaken the part you've identified as load-bearing — does the reversal shrink into a harmless gap, or does some version of it survive? And tell me how my CS friend fights back: if the learned objective explains every bit of the behavior perfectly, doesn't that make it the right account of the person's preferences, by definition? Last thing, because I'm no longer sure any of this is about behavior at all — say whether the case still goes through if we let the learner ask the person plain questions instead of only watching them.
