I hold the following view about species. Species taxa are real, but they are not kinds: they are concrete, spatiotemporally extended historical individuals — lineages — so that a name like Homo sapiens names an individual much as a proper name names a person, rather than a predicate that organisms can satisfy. On this view, the demand for the single correct species concept is misdirected. The long-running contest among biological, phylogenetic, ecological, and morphological concepts is not a dispute about which definition captures the real extension; it is the residue of a category mistake — the assumption that "species" names a kind. Pluralism about criteria is therefore expected and benign, and it does not amount to the concession that species are merely conventional. I extend the same treatment to higher taxa, on the grounds that genera and families are lineages too.

I would like you to map this view's exposure. What would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how decisive each would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and draw on several different kinds: observations in biology that would be hard to square with species-as-individuals; arguments showing that the view cannot do some explanatory or predictive work that a kind-based account can; cases in which treating a species name as the name of an individual yields unacceptable results — in how we generalize over species, in how we describe their persistence, or in their coming into and going out of existence; and results showing that the "individual" treatment collapses into one of its rivals, whether into plain pluralism or into the deflationary claim that the species problem is a merely verbal dispute that dissolves once the question is dropped. I am also interested in whether the extension to higher taxa is a strength or a liability, and whether the view can account for the roles species are asked to play outside systematics — as the units of conservation law, for instance.

Then give me an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all. Does it predict anything its rivals do not, so that some possible discovery could refute it? Or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — "lineage" left vague, "individual" stretched to fit whatever the cases demand — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?

Tell me separately what a defender of the view would concede as counting against it, as distinct from what critics would press. Where a defeater can be deflected by a friendly amendment, say what the amendment costs. The thing I would personally find most damaging is a demonstration that species do genuine explanatory work in virtue of being kinds; if you think some other line of attack is stronger, say so and explain why.
