I am currently a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on practical rationality, and I am helping my study group prepare for a ninety-minute final essay examination. Every practice question we have been given invites the same canned answer: state Parfit's hitchhiker case, observe that the stranded traveller is better off if he is the sort of person who would pay a driver who cannot enforce payment, and conclude that it can be rational to be irrational. I would like a question that cannot be passed on that basis.

Please write three things for this examination: a single essay question that a strong student could answer in about seventy-five minutes; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty that names the specific moves a top answer must make, allocates marks to each move, and gives a one-line note on how candidates typically get each move wrong.

The question should turn on the following case, which I have invented and which the examination paper should present in full.

Lena is stranded on the near bank of a river, and the only crossing is Osei's ferry. Osei charges nothing and has no means of enforcing payment. He carries a passenger only if, at the moment of boarding, he judges that the passenger is the sort of person who would pay him on the far bank. Osei is an excellent judge of character, and no one can feign the disposition in his presence. Lena does not have the disposition, and she has two ways across. First, she can complete a demanding programme of moral formation, which is known to be reliable and which would cost her a great deal; it would make her into the sort of person who pays. Second, she can post a bond with a guarantor whom Osei trusts; the bond is forfeit unless she pays Osei on the far bank.

The question should ask the student, for each route, whether paying Osei on the far bank is the act that best serves Lena's interests at the moment she arrives there, and whether taking that route was rational for her beforehand. It should then press the crux I care about. The bond is an external commitment device that makes paying instrumentally rational, so does its availability show that the first route contains no genuine irrationality — that what looks like irrationality there is merely the price of a cruder commitment technology — or is something genuinely irrational left over in the first route? Finally, the question should ask what Lena does if the guarantor's fee rises above the cost of the programme, and what that comparison reveals about the relationship between rationality and the availability of commitment technologies.

Please ensure that the question cannot be answered by reciting the standard hitchhiker case, by labelling the parties as causal and evidential decision theorists, or by asserting that the disposition is simply a commitment device without saying what that does and does not explain. The marking scheme should separate an answer that merely observes that the disposition changes what Lena wants from one that explains why that matters — in particular, from one that distinguishes acting against one's interests from acting against one's already transformed preferences.
