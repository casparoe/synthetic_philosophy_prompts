I'm a PhD student in comparative philosophy preparing a seminar paper that reads Borges's short story "The Circular Ruins" through the butterfly dream at the close of the second chapter of the Zhuangzi, and I need a critical reading rather than a summary of either text. What I want is a reading that does real work in both directions — one that neither reduces the story to a warm illustration of the Zhuangzian passage nor treats the Zhuangzi as a footnote to Borges. The obvious move, treating the story as a dramatization of dream skepticism and asking whether the protagonist can know he is not dreaming, is the one I want you to get past quickly; I am after something more specific.

Here is the crux that I keep failing to state cleanly and would like you to press on. Descartes's dream argument is an epistemological exercise: it suspends judgment about the external world in order to locate something indubitable. The Zhuangzi passage, on at least one reading, is not an exercise in doubt at all. It ends by insisting that there must be some distinction between Zhou and the butterfly, and then names the whole business the transformation of things — a distinction affirmed and immediately folded back into a process of change. The word for that distinction is the same one the chapter has been worrying about all along, the making of divisions; so the ending may be a test case for the chapter's larger claim that distinctions are made rather than found. I want you to say what kind of claim that ending actually is: a metaphysical thesis about the instability of the self, a claim about the perspectival character of the waking-and-dreaming distinction, a claim that the distinction between Zhou and the butterfly is real but not the kind of thing that grounds identity, or something weaker still. Then read Borges against it.

The story is peculiar, and this is where I think the pairing gets interesting, because its dream is causally efficacious: the dreamed man is not a private object inside the dreamer's experience but walks out into the shared world, and the dreamer himself turns out, at the end, to be someone else's dream. That seems to break the skeptical frame. If dreams can produce real people, the problem is no longer how to tell dreaming from waking but whether there is any bottom to the regress of dreamers at all. So: does the story illustrate the Zhuangzian point better than a straightforward dream-skepticism reading would, or does it betray it? Borges's ending lands as horror and humiliation, Zhuangzi's as something closer to equanimity, and I suspect that difference matters more than the shared imagery. Say where the story resists the Zhuangzian reading and where the Zhuangzian reading resists the story — including whether the story's obsession with a single creator-dreamer, an origin who dreams his progeny into being, is fundamentally at odds with a text that treats the self as one more phase in a process rather than as a source. Close by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the Zhuangzi passage alone does not, and be willing to conclude that it adds nothing but atmosphere if that is where the reading takes you.
