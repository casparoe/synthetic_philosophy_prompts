I work on planning and model-based control, and I keep having the same argument with two groups of people who refuse to have it with each other. My engineering colleagues treat the frame problem as a representational headache from the early days of logic-based AI — an artifact of writing state descriptions down in first-order logic, which they say disappeared once people stopped hand-coding axioms and started learning dynamics models or writing reactive policies; that nobody on my team has ever had to solve it strikes them as proof that it was never a deep problem. The people in my philosophy of mind reading group say the opposite: that the frame problem is the general problem of relevance, that every finite agent has it, and that anyone who thinks it is solved has simply narrowed the task until the relevance question never comes up. Both camps think the other is answering a different question, and I suspect both are right about that, which is what I want to make precise. So: please write me a decision tree that takes a reader from a commitment they can check for themselves — at any moment, an agent's next move depends on only a small part of everything it knows or could come to know — and leads them, one question at a time, to a named position on what the frame problem is and what would count as solving it. I want the tree built out of the logical space of the debate rather than out of a list of authors, and I want to be able to hand it to someone who has had exactly one AI course and one philosophy of mind course and have them locate themselves without knowing in advance which leaf is the respectable one.

The branch nodes are the whole point, so let me be specific about what I want them to do. Each should be a question about where the difficulty is located: in the world (relevance is a fact about the environment waiting to be discovered), in the task specification (relevance is relative to a goal that the modeler supplies, so the real issue is about who gets to write the goal), in the agent's bounded resources (the difficulty is combinatorial, a matter of computation rather than representation), in the representational scheme itself (any scheme that states facts explicitly must state too many of them), or in the assumption that cognition is representation at all. Along the way the tree should force a verdict on three further things: whether the problem is specifically about representing what does not change or about representing anything tractably; whether a solution must be a formal system that provably yields the right answers or whether a heuristic that works in practice counts as a solution; and whether this is one problem or a family that got conflated — in particular, whether the difficulties usually called the qualification problem and the ramification problem are the same difficulty in different clothes. Please build in three diagnostic cases that every leaf has to answer for. First, the Yale shooting problem: a gun is loaded, time passes in which nothing is done, the trigger is pulled, and a formalism that minimizes change turns out to be equally happy with a history in which the victim is dead before the trigger is pulled. Second, an agent that rebuilds its entire model of the situation at every step, so that it is always right and never assumes anything stays put, and which is therefore useless. Third, an agent with no model at all — a reactive policy mapping the current input to an action, which never asks what has changed and so never seems to face the problem. I want to see which leaves can accommodate all three, and which quietly need one of them to be impossible.

For the leaves, six to eight of them, each labeled with the position's name where there is one and marked unnamed where there isn't, each carrying a single sentence about its most awkward commitment — the price it pays, not a summary of its attractions. I would expect to find somewhere in the tree the view that this is a solvable engineering problem in representation (with its sub-answers: encodings that state what stays the same, nonmonotonic defaults, learned or probabilistic models), the view that it is the problem of relevance and that no amount of representation fixes it, the view that it is a special case of the general underdetermination of theory by data, the view that it is a pseudo-problem generated by a bad picture of mind, and the view that it is a name for several distinct problems that should be pulled apart. Treat that as a checklist of things not to miss rather than a required set: if your branch structure throws up a leaf I have not listed, include it. Two things at the end matter to me as much as the tree itself. First, a note on which combinations of answers are left unoccupied, with your diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, a flag on any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, with an explanation of why they collapse into one another. Keep it to an indented outline, no reading list and no citations, and do not attribute a position to a named author unless you are confident of the attribution — I would rather see unnamed than a guess.
