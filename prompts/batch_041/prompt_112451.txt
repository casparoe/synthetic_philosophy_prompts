Write a dialogue between a frequentist statistician and a subjective Bayesian statistician who have been asked to advise a regulator on how to interpret a reported failure rate of one in a million for a safety-critical system. The frequentist argues the figure is meaningless for this single system unless it names a definite reference class, and that any choice of class is arbitrary; the Bayesian replies that the number is simply a degree of belief, rational whenever it is coherent and well-calibrated against evidence, and that no reference class is needed. Let them press each other on the details: have the Bayesian confront the reference-class problem and the demand for long-run calibration, and have the frequentist say what a long-run frequency is supposed to be a frequency of when the class can be drawn many ways. Then let them weigh the propensity interpretation, on which probability is a physical disposition of the setup itself, and ask whether it is a genuine third option or merely relocates the difficulty onto the single case. Neither should be a strawman: each must answer the other's actual points rather than talk past them. Assume the reader already knows the textbook positions, so skip introductory exposition and go straight to the contested details, ending with a clear verdict on whether the residual disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.
