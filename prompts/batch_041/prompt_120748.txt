I've been reading rationalist and longtermist blogs for years, so I'd rather skip the textbook treatment of discounting and get at the messier question: which arguments actually do work when someone claims the far future should count for less? Here are six I keep running into, each stated plainly rather than by its usual name:

1. Bare impatience — future welfare matters less just because it lies further ahead.
2. Growth — future people will likely be richer, so a marginal unit of welfare does less for them.
3. Extinction — the future may not arrive at all, so we discount by the probability that anyone is there to receive it.
4. Value drift — our values may be wrong or may be superseded, so our influence on the far future is worth less than our influence on the near future.
5. Option value — because we can act later with better information, acting now carries an implicit cost.
6. Non-identity — future people do not yet exist, so there is no determinate subject whose interests are being downgraded.

Rank these six from the one that most legitimately reduces the weight we should place on far-future welfare to the one that least does, give a one-sentence verdict on each (real discount, or confusion), and flag which of them tend to get conflated with pure time preference even though they are doing something else entirely.
