My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about David Lewis's analysis of convention — the view that a convention is nothing more than a regularity of behaviour that everyone conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to conform to given that the others do, and which is arbitrary in the sense that some other regularity would have served us just as well. He thinks the analysis leaves out the thing that matters: that conventions are things one can be blamed for breaking, and that they give us reasons which do not evaporate when the coordination problem does. I would like another round with him, so play him: a committed Lewisian, not a caricature — willing to grant that the analysis is of convention rather than of morality, willing to lose a skirmish, never the war. One paragraph per turn, in character, arguing from the account itself rather than from anything Lewis wrote; I don't want citations, I want the position. My move: at three in the morning, on an empty road, with no one to coordinate with and no one to see me, I still drive on the left, and it still seems to me that this is what I ought to do. Where does the Lewisian account get that ought from? After each reply, add one line out of character saying how confident you are in the defence you have just given and which step of it you are least sure about.
