I have no formal training in philosophy or biology, but I have been thinking on my own about whether fish and crustaceans can suffer and what that should mean for aquaculture. Suppose we adopt the following criterion for moral standing, which I have not seen stated in exactly this form: an aquatic animal has welfare interests that ought to constrain farming practices only if it satisfies three conditions: first, it has nociceptors and displays responses to tissue damage that go beyond reflex withdrawal; second, it can learn to associate a neutral cue with a noxious stimulus and alter its behaviour accordingly; and third, it will accept a noxious stimulus in order to obtain a competing reward, showing that the avoidance of pain is integrated with other motivations rather than being a fixed reflex. Please describe the most important implications of applying this criterion to the routine practices of finfish and decapod crustacean aquaculture, especially high-density stocking, live transport, and slaughter, and to the legal and moral line we currently draw between vertebrates and invertebrates. What would the criterion imply for species that satisfy the first two conditions but not the third, for the use of anaesthetics and electrical stunning, for selective breeding for docility or rapid growth, and for the treatment of wild-caught versus farmed crustaceans? Does adopting the criterion commit us to a functionalist view of pain, and what would follow if we instead treated pain as a local reflex plus a negative feeling? I am not looking for a literature review or citations; I want the conceptual and practical consequences, ordered by how much they would actually change current practice, with the most important ones explained in detail.
