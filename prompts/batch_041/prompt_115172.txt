I'm a fourth-year PhD student in early modern philosophy, and in a few weeks I have to close a seminar on the Scientific Revolution with something I read aloud rather than argue — so what I want from you is a poem. The thing I keep circling in my dissertation chapter is Hobbes's geometry, and I'd like the poem to do a piece of work I can't get into prose. Here is the material it should carry, since you can't look anything up: Hobbes came to geometry late, in his forties, by the story he told his biographer over a copy of Euclid left open in a gentleman's library, and was converted on the spot; afterwards it became the model for everything. Geometry was the one domain where the method of definition and construction was supposed to compel assent — you draw the figure, you derive, and no reasonable person can dissent — and that is exactly why he thought a science of politics built the same way could end the disputes that had just cost the country a king and a civil war. But the last quarter-century of his life was eaten by his conviction that he had solved the classical problems (squaring the circle, doubling the cube, trisecting the angle) and by the mathematician Wallis, Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, who spent those same decades showing in print, again and again, that he had not. Hobbes conceded the first attempt and then produced others, and answered pamphlet with pamphlet until he died at ninety-one. What I want the poem to hold is that this was not merely a failure of mathematics. The one place where reason was supposed to be automatic is the place where he was wrong and could not be shown wrong; and the quarrel was not settled by demonstration but by ridicule, exclusion, and the new guild of the Royal Society — precisely the outcome his conception of method said could not befall a genuine proof. And there is the pathos of the thing: he never wavered. He was translating Homer into English couplets in his eighties.

The form should be doing this work rather than decorating it. Give me roughly sixteen lines of iambic pentameter in rhymed couplets — the couplet being, not by accident, the form he chose for Homer at the very end of his life — spoken in his voice, late, to the man who kept refuting him. The constraint I care about most: the couplets should rhyme cleanly all the way through except the last, which must be a near-rhyme that does not quite land, so that the poem stops and asserts that the demonstration is complete while the form itself fails to close — I want the reader to feel the closure that isn't there. Let the middle of the poem carry the doctrine: the figure drawn, the derivation that cannot be resisted, the model extended from lines to the commonwealth, the hope that this is how disputes end; and let the turn come where he notices that the compulsion he feels is his alone — that no one else is compelled, and that what actually ends a dispute is laughter. The final line can claim the proof is finished, and the rhyme can decline to back him up. Someone who knows the quarrel should recognize the whole situation, and someone who doesn't should still find a good poem about a man who is certain and alone. Please don't make him a crank or the poem a joke at his expense: the interesting thing is that he is right about what a demonstration is supposed to do and wrong about nearly everything else.
