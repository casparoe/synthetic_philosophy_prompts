I have no background in biology or philosophy, but I have been reading popular accounts of the idea — the handicap principle, or costly signaling — that animal displays such as the peacock's tail are trustworthy evidence of the animal's quality precisely because they are expensive, and too expensive for a low-quality animal to fake. I tried to carry the idea over to human beings, and I ended up with an argument that disturbs me, because it seems to turn the principle against itself. I have written it out below in the hope that someone can tell me where it fails, or, if it does not fail, what I have to give up.

(1) A display is trustworthy evidence of some hidden quality only if it is costly, since a cheap display would be copied by low-quality individuals, who would collect the benefit without possessing the quality.

(2) Therefore, whenever a costly display is made where others can observe it, it functions as trustworthy evidence of a hidden quality, whether or not the one who makes it intended it that way.

(3) The life of a working scientist is costly in the relevant sense: low pay, long training, and, above all, the willingness to abandon a cherished hypothesis when the evidence turns against it.

(4) When a scientist publicly gives up a cherished hypothesis, that act is observed by others and is costly, so by (2) it functions as trustworthy evidence of intellectual honesty.

(5) If the act in (4) can be fully explained as an attempt to send that very signal — to be seen as honest, and to collect the reputational rewards of being seen that way — then no further explanation, in terms of a genuine concern for truth, is needed.

(6) Every costly act performed where others can see it admits the same treatment, including the writing and public defense of the handicap principle itself.

(7) So no one's apparent concern for truth need be genuine, and the apparent concern for truth of the theory's own defenders is, by (6), itself nothing but a signal.

(8) A theory whose acceptance is fully explained by the reputational payoffs of the people who advance it gives us no reason to think it is true.

(9) Therefore the handicap principle, extended as above, gives us no reason to think it is true.

I would rather keep (1) than accept (9), since (1) is the whole point of the idea and (9) seems to show the idea eats itself. But I cannot see which of the steps to sacrifice, or why the step I should sacrifice looked so plausible when I wrote it down. Please tell me which premise or transition to reject, explain why it looked fine to an untrained reader, and say what rejecting it costs — that is, what I would have to give up elsewhere in the argument, and whether the surviving version of the handicap principle is still strong enough to explain the peacock's tail.
