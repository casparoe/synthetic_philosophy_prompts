I'm working on a profile of a political theorist for a magazine piece and I need a hand with the part I cant write myself. I'll call her R. — she's mid-career, not famous-famous, but she's got a cult following among the sort of people who argue about this stuff on the internet. The whole thing she does is a newsletter where every couple of weeks she takes one religious arguement that somebody actually made in a live political fight — a floor speech, an op-ed, a letter to a zoning board, an amicus brief — and rules it admissible or not admissible as a public reason. Her write-ups are like 400 words and she has never once stated a general test, which is exactly why I'm asking you and not her.

Her last year or so, roughly:

- A Lutheran pastor testifying against a bill that would cut in-home care funding for disabled people. She says her faith is what got her there, then spends most of her time on waitlist numbers and what institutional care actually costs the state. Admissible.
- A state rep explaining his vote against a bill extending anti-discrimination protections. Scripture is clear, he says, he answers to God and not to us, and he says out loud that he doesn't care whether anyone outside his church can see the reason. Not admissible.
- A Hasidic community asking a town to let it string a symbolic boundary wire on municipal utility poles for Sabbath use. Admissible — R.'s line was that it costs the town nothing and binds nobody who didn't ask to be bound.
- A Catholic hospital system asking a regulator for a blanket exemption from a service it's legally required to offer the general public, on the grounds that offering it makes the system complicit in wrongdoing. Not admissible. Her one-liner was that the reason only works if you already accept the whole theology.
- An interfaith coalition arguing for a bill that expands refugee resettlement. Their spokesperson says her reason is scriptural, then makes the labor-shortage case. Admissible.
- A religious school arguing at a hearing that a state curriculum rule shouldn't apply to its own classrooms. Admissible.
- A city councilman who voted against a rezoning because the developer was going to put a payday lending shop there, and who says his faith is what put him on it. Admissible — R. wrote something like: the evidence did the work, the faith was the engine.

A friend of mine who reads her religiously says the rule is basically "would this reason still work on someone who doesn't share your religion." Another friend says no, it's about who has to live under the rule. And R. herself keeps using this phrase, the swap test — swap the religious premise out and see if there's still a reason standing there. I genuinely can't tell if that's the test she's actually running or just something that happens to fit the cases she's picked, and honestly her one-liners don't all sound like they come from the same test.

Here's the new one. Next month our legislature takes up a bill to run a pilot paying people for kidney donations — ten grand plus lifetime follow-up care. The lead witness against it is a rabbi, and he's upfront about it: his tradition teaches that the body is a trust and not property, that's his reason, and he's not pretending otherwise. He does give the committee the arguments he knows they want for the record — exploitation of the poor, the fact that payment hasn't moved the needle in countries that allow it — but he says right out that those aren't why he's there. And if the bill dies, people on the waitlist keep dying, which is the kind of consequence R. usually insists on putting on the table.

So what does she rule? I care less about the one-word verdict than about which rule she's running, what the other rules that fit her record just as well predict here, and which case would actually tell them apart. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting by ruling one way and what she'd be adopting by ruling the other.

Give it to me as JSON with these fields, exactly these names:

- candidate_rules: array of objects with name (short label), rule (one or two sentences), fits_her_record ("yes" / "yes but strained" / "no, and say which ruling it can't explain"), and predicted_verdict (what that rule says about the kidney case, and why).
- best_guess: object with verdict ("admissible" / "not admissible" / "genuinely 50-50"), which_rule, and why (2-4 sentences).
- confidence: a number between 0 and 1.
- closest_precedent: which of the rulings above is the nearest analog to the kidney case, and where the analogy breaks down.
- what_would_settle_it: array of objects with case (real or made up), separates (which two rules it splits), and predictions (what each rule says about it).
- if_precedents_underdetermine: what principle she'd be adopting by ruling admissible, and what she'd be adopting by ruling not admissible.
- where_my_framing_is_off: if I've got this wrong — if her rulings can't be reconciled with any single rule, or if admissible/not admissible isn't even the right way to carve this up — say so here plainly instead of handing me a tidy rule. I'd rather find out now that I'm chasing a pattern that isn't there than get a neat answer that falls apart when an actual philosopher reads the piece.
