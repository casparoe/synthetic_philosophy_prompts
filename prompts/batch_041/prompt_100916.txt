I serve on the governance working group of a mid-sized online community that is drafting a constitution, and one of our members has taken to quoting Robin Hanson's slogan 'vote on values, bet on beliefs' as though it disposed of our hardest disputes. When another member objected that you cannot cleanly separate what a community values from its beliefs about what will actually work, he said the slogan 'already accounts for that'; when someone asked whether a market's verdict should override the considered judgment of a majority, he said the slogan 'settles that too'; and when a third member pressed him on whether the whole scheme was a normative proposal or an empirical prediction, he waved at the slogan as if the answer were contained in it. I suspect the one sentence is doing several incompatible jobs at once and that some of the readings it invites contradict the others, but I cannot articulate which.

What I want is a disambiguation I can circulate before the next drafting session: the distinct, precisely stated claims that 'vote on values, bet on beliefs' could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each. For every reading, say what work 'values' and 'beliefs' are each doing — whether values are a bare preference ordering, an elected welfare metric, or a substantive theory of the good; whether the vote fixes ends only or also the measure of success; whether 'bet' denotes merely eliciting forecasts, aggregating dispersed information, or a stronger claim that market prices are the best available estimates of consequences. Distinguish, at each step, the descriptive/mechanism-design claim (such a system would function as advertised) from the normative claim (such a system ought to govern), and flag which readings are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, which are empirical bets that could turn out false, and which are incoherent on their face. Then tell me which reading my colleague must be relying on at each of the three moments above, and whether that reading actually licenses the move he makes — in particular, whether any version of the slogan survives the objection that values and beliefs are entangled rather than separable, or whether the slogan simply presupposes the separation it needs.

Lay this out as a menu with verdicts rather than defending a single interpretation, and be explicit about where two readings of the slogan are in tension with one another, since that is the part of the situation I find hardest to put into words.
