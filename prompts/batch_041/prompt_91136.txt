I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm fact-checking a freelancer's explainer about famine before it goes to press. Here is the passage I need audited:

"Sen's great insight was that famines are never really about food. In Bengal in 1943 the harvests were fine; people starved because wartime inflation wiped out their entitlements — their moral claim to a share of what was on the market. Ireland in the 1840s is the awkward case for his famous law that democracies never have famines: there was a free press and a parliament, but the government blamed Providence and let a million people die. The Great Leap Forward is the cleanest case of all, since Beijing kept exporting grain while its own countryside starved, and it is why famine scholars now separate three kinds of failure — availability, entitlement, and response — with the last doing most of the work. The upshot is that famines are political crimes, not natural disasters; Malthus was simply wrong."

The freelancer is competent, and I have seen versions of most of this elsewhere, so I am inclined to let it stand — but I would rather not publish something a famine historian would wince at. Go claim by claim: what is right, what overstates a narrower thesis, what uses a term in its everyday sense rather than its technical one, and what is the freelancer's own tidying rather than anything in the literature. Then tell me whether the political-crime conclusion survives those corrections — in particular whether calling it a crime adds anything to saying policy did it, and whether the three-part taxonomy changes who bears the blame.

If the passage turns out to be substantially fair and I am overthinking it, say so plainly. And if the audit cannot be done well as posed — for instance, if I am assuming there is one settled "Sen thesis" to check the prose against when there is not — tell me that instead of manufacturing a list of errors.
