I am a novelist finishing a book whose central figure is an engineer recruited, in the early 1950s, to adjudicate a wager about whether a machine can hold up its end of a conversation well enough to be mistaken for a person. I have been living inside the founding document of that wager — the paper in which Turing replaces the question of whether machines can think with an imitation game — and the part I cannot get out of my head is its ninth and final objection, the one about extra-sensory perception. It is strange and instructive: the other objections get a brisk paragraph and a shrug, but this one alters the experimental design. If a human interrogator could read minds, no amount of typing would keep the machine from being found out, and the machine could not read back. So the interrogator must be sealed in a room that blocks thought as well as sound, and the machine must be tested on something like guessing cards, where its answers would be fixed by its construction and therefore predictable, while a human's might not be. My engineer does not believe in telepathy. His problem is that he cannot rule it out, and cannot say whether a test that fails to rule it out is measuring thought or merely measuring which side of the glass was born.

I want a sestina for the chapter break. Use the strict form — six six-line stanzas with the end-words rotating through the standard permutation, then a three-line envoi that gathers all six. The end-words, in order of first appearance, are: room, card, guess, machine, mind, proof.

Let the form do the argument. The rotation of the end-words should feel like the permuting of trials in a long experiment, each term returning in a new position, none of them ever retired. The sealed room and the demand for proof should keep colliding until the envoi, where they must finally stand together, and the envoi should leave the matter open rather than resolve it: the machine cannot cheat by thought, the human might, and the protocol cannot tell us which of those two facts it is actually recording. Do not decide whether telepathy is real. Turing did not decide it; he turned it into a design problem, and I want the poem to remain a design problem.

Render the details accurately enough that a reader who knows the paper will recognize the ninth objection without being told its name: the card, the sealed room, the statistics that were taken seriously at the time, the machine's determinism making its guesses predictable, the asymmetry by which one player has a channel the other lacks. A reader who has never heard of any of this should still find a poem about a wager, a locked room, and two parties who cannot be sure what they are proving.

Keep the register dry and fair-minded, the way the paper itself is dry and fair-minded — no Gothic props, no séances, no crystal balls, no jokes at the expense of the believers. Do not quote the paper, do not cite it, and do not put any title, date, or name into the verse. The eeriness should come from the protocol.
