I'm currently taking a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and we've just finished a unit on the Judy Benjamin problem. I want to make sure I've grasped its significance, because the way it was presented seems to me to establish something much stronger than the professor claimed, and I'd like to be corrected if I'm overreading it.

As I understand the case, an agent begins with a prior over a partition of possibilities and then receives information that has the form of a conditional — something like "if A, then B" — rather than learning some proposition outright. She is then supposed to update in the standard Bayesian way. Here is my worry. Conditionalization tells us that on learning a proposition E, one's new credence in X is the old conditional credence P(X | E). So the natural move is to treat "if A, then B" as the proposition that is true whenever A is false or B is true, and conditionalize on that. But when I work through the numbers, learning the conditional actually lowers my credence in A, which seems plainly wrong: information about the connection between A and B should not be evidence against A. And if instead I take the information to be telling me something about the conditional probability P(B | A), then conditionalization cannot even begin, since there is no event to condition on. What made me especially suspicious is that two classmates who each insisted they were simply applying conditionalization ended up with different posteriors, which I take to be a tell-tale sign that the rule is not well defined in this setting.

So the one updating rule that Bayesians rely on either returns an absurd answer or no answer at all, precisely in the cases where the evidence is conditional in form. Doesn't that show that Bayesian conditionalization is inconsistent, or at least radically incomplete, for this whole class of cases? If it doesn't, I'd like to know exactly which step in my reasoning is the mistake, and what the correct treatment of conditional evidence is meant to be.
