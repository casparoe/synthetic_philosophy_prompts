I don't have any philosophy background, I just read this kind of stuff at night and chew on it while I'm doing the dishes. A friend of mine who did a minor in philosophy gave me his version of something called the special composition question (I think the name comes from a guy named van Inwagen?) and I want to know how much of it I should actually believe, because I've been poking at it on my own — like, when I sweep dust into a pile, at what point is it one thing instead of a bunch of little things?

Here's what he told me, pretty much word for word as I remember it:

"The special composition question is just: when do a bunch of smaller things add up to one bigger thing? There are basically only three kinds of answer. Always — that's universalism, everything composes something. Never — that's nihilism. And sometimes. Van Inwagen is the famous sometimes guy. His answer is called organicism: a bunch of things compose something exactly when they're caught up in a life. So you and I exist, and a dog exists, because we're alive — but a table doesn't, and neither does a chair or a planet. There's no table, just a bunch of tiny bits arranged in a table-shaped way. The reason he rules out the other sometimes answers, like 'when the parts are touching' or 'when they're stuck together,' is that those lines are vague — at some point it's neither true nor false that the parts are stuck together enough — and he thinks existence can never be vague. You either compose something or you don't, no in-between. Life is the one relation that gives you a clean cut, because a life is just the parts all working together toward the same goal, like a team. And when he says there's no table, he doesn't quite mean 'there's no table' is false — he means it's a convenient fiction, and whether some things count as composing a bigger thing is really up to how we decide to use words. Which is why most people think the view is dead on arrival: nobody can seriously believe the chair they're sitting on doesn't exist."

That's about the shape of it. My gut says most of it is right but a couple of things smell off — especially the "up to how we decide to use words" part, because I got the impression this guy thinks he's found a hard fact about the world, not a rule about language. But I don't trust my gut, and my friend seems to know what he's talking about.
