Write a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether reading lives can make a person better. The first is a defender of the Plutarchan and hagiographic tradition: she holds that a life is a moral instrument, that its value lies in the pull it exerts on the reader toward imitation, and that a degree of idealization of the subject is not a defect but a condition of that pull. The second writes documentary biography; he grants that lives move readers but denies that they teach, and he presses a specific objection—that one can only imitate what one already recognizes as admirable, so a life can at most confirm moral knowledge the reader brought with them and can never supply anything new. Constraints: both speakers are competent, and neither is a strawman; the dialogue must turn on a concrete case (a saint, a reformer, or a statesman, which you may invent) and on the distinction between a life functioning as a model to be copied and a life functioning as a redescription of the reader's own situation; let the two locate exactly where their disagreement stops being about biography and becomes a disagreement about moral psychology, and have each say what would change his or her mind. No quotations or citations.
