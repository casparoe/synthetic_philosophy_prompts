I'm a science journalist and I've been roped onto a panel at a vision-science meeting next month to defend a line I've been writing about: that the whole Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque fight over the color phi effect -- when two differently colored dots flash in different places in quick succession, people swear they saw the first dot change color halfway across, even though the second color hadn't appeared yet -- is a fight that no experiment can settle. The Orwellian story says the brain rewrites your memory of what you saw; the Stalinesque story says it rewrites the perception itself before you ever had it; and I want to argue the two are empirically indistinguishable, because any test that could pull them apart already has to fix when the subject's report happens, and the report is part of the thing being timed. I've been calling this the two-clock problem: there's the clock out in the world and the clock in the report, and the second one isn't a window onto the first.

The audience is mostly psychophysicists who run Libet-style timing studies and flash-lag experiments, and they're pretty sure the timing of experience is just an empirical question you can measure if you're careful. There's also two philosophers of mind on the panel who I expect will say I've smuggled a homunculus back in.

Prep me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from each side, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is thin. Then tell me the one question I'm praying nobody asks.
