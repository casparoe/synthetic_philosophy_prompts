I'm a science journalist working on a feature about that weird corner where cosmology and philosophy bump into each other — anthropics, the stuff about how you're supposed to reason when you don't know which observer you are. It comes up in fine-tuning fights and the doomsday argument, and I keep running into the same little picture being used to settle everything. A source of mine lays it out like this: think of all the observers who will ever exist, across all the competing theories of the universe, as balls in an urn, and think of yourself as one ball pulled out at random — so if one theory says the urn is vastly bigger than another theory says, then the mere fact that you exist is supposed to be strong evidence for the bigger urn, because you were more likely to get drawn from it. My editor says the picture is lovely but is doing way more work than it looks, and that people who lean on it end up in a silly spot where a philosopher can announce that some perfectly ordinary physical theory is overwhelmingly probable — without doing any experiments — just because it happens to predict a lot more people. She called him the presumptuous philosopher, or something like that, I'd have to check.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I'd like the correspondence laid out — what's the urn, what are the balls, what's the drawing, and what does "at random" actually cash out to in the real case — then the places it breaks down. The things I care about most: whether it quietly assumes you were drawn uniformly (and whether that's a neutral assumption or a smuggled one), whether there's even a well-defined urn or reference class to draw from in the first place, and whether it matters that you couldn't have turned out to be someone else's life — that you only ever get to be you. Then tell me whether the conclusion people use it for, favoring whichever theory has more observers even when that goes off the rails, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was the picture doing the arguing all along. And if the urn is a bad fit, is there a better picture I could put in the piece — or should I just leave it as an arguement that the metaphor can't carry?
