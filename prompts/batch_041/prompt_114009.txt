I retired last year after a career in statistical mechanics, and I have since been enrolled in a graduate program in philosophy. I have been asked to contribute the formal epistemology question to this year's comprehensive examination, and I want it to concern the Judy Benjamin problem. My own experience with inverse problems and maximum-entropy methods has made me doubtful that there is always a single rational way to update a probability distribution when the new information is conditional rather than propositional, but I want students to have to argue for that doubt rather than simply announce it.

Please compose one essay question for a 90-minute examination, together with a model answer of approximately 700 to 800 words and a marking scheme. The question must be self-contained: it may describe the Judy Benjamin scenario in prose, but it must not presuppose that students have read any particular paper, and it must not ask them for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies. It also must not depend on any verbatim quotation.

The question should be designed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the definition of conditionalization or by summarizing the standard Judy Benjamin example. It should require students to do three things. First, they must state precisely what makes the Judy Benjamin case problematic for ordinary conditionalization. Second, they must formulate a general principle for updating on conditional information and explain its rationale. Third, they must apply that principle to a modified version of the scenario that you invent. The modification should add a second piece of conditional information that is individually consistent with the first but that creates a tension for at least one prominent update rule. Students should have to determine whether the modified case admits a unique rational posterior, and if it does not, what that implies for the original problem.

In the model answer, compare at least two approaches to updating on conditionals, such as Adams conditioning, Jeffrey conditionalization, and maximum-entropy updating, but do not let the answer become a mere catalogue. The answer should take a definite position on which approach, if any, is best for the modified case, and it should state the strongest objection to that position. It should also explain what the Judy Benjamin problem reveals about the difference between learning a proposition and learning a conditional, and whether that difference undermines the claim that conditionalization is the uniquely rational update rule.

For the marking scheme, provide five must-hit points, each worth five marks, for a total of twenty-five. For each point, give a one-line description of the move that a top answer must make and a one-line description of the error that typically costs marks. Then add a brief note on what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer, and a final note on how to mark a response that argues the problem is ill-posed rather than attempting to solve it.
