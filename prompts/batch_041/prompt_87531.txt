In De Interpretatione 9, Aristotle introduces the problem of the sea battle. Reconstruct the fatalist argument that the chapter sets out, and state its conclusion precisely. Then explain Aristotle's own response to that argument, being explicit about what he concedes and what he denies. One student submitted the following answer.

---

Aristotle sets up the problem with a singular statement about the future — that there will be a sea battle tomorrow — and asks whether the truth or falsity of that statement now would force the future to be already settled. The fatalist reasons as follows. Either the statement is true now or it is false now. If it is true now, then it is already the case that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, so the battle cannot fail to occur: it will occur of necessity. If it is false now, then it is already the case that there will be no battle, and that is necessary too. Since one of the two must be true now, the battle is necessary either way, and deliberation about whether to fight is idle.

Aristotle's response is to reject bivalence for singular statements about future contingencies. He holds that such a statement is not yet true and not yet false; there is as yet no fact of the matter about tomorrow's battle. This is why he is often regarded as the originator of many-valued logic: he recognises a third truth value, the indeterminate, lying between true and false, which Łukasiewicz later formalised as the value one half. What Aristotle does not surrender is the law of excluded middle: he insists that either there will be a sea battle or there will not, and that one of the two contradictories must obtain, while denying that we can say in advance which of them is already true. His position is thus a denial of excluded middle only in the harmless sense that neither disjunct is as yet determinately the case.

He reinforces the point with an asymmetry between the past and the future. Of something that is, when it is, we may say that it is necessary that it is, since a thing cannot both be and not be at the same time; but this necessity of the present must not be read forward into the future as the claim that whatever will be was always going to be necessary. The fatalist's mistake, on Aristotle's account, is to slide from the statement's being true to its being necessarily true.

I take it that Aristotle's solution is essentially the one Ockham was later to give: the future is kept genuinely open by denying that the truth of a future-tense statement carries necessity with it, and by refusing to treat the future as a branch that has already been selected. Whether this succeeds is another question, since it is not clear what the truth of a future contingent could consist in if it is neither true nor false.
