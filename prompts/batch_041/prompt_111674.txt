I teach a 400-level seminar on value theory, and we've just spent three weeks on the transitivity of 'better than' and on spectrum/continuum arguments, so I'm drafting the take-home final. I need one essay question — 60 minutes, 25 marks — that a student cannot answer by paraphrasing the standard version of the argument, and I want it built around a setup I've found actually makes them commit to something instead of hedging. Here it is: take a sequence of possible lives, each 80 years long. The first contains one genuinely great stretch — let the student fix what it is, six months of deep friendship, or discovery, or whatever — plus about 79 and a half years of mild contentment. Each next life in the sequence shaves a hair off the great stretch and adds a very large amount of the mild contentment in its place, so that by the far end you get a life with no great stretch at all but an enormous surplus of pleasant nothing, day after day of it. Adjacent lives are supposed to be obviously ranked: each looks a shade better or a shade worse than its neighbour, never a big jump, never a tie. I've deliberately made the far end a case where the two ends look like they'd be ranked by a priority/lexical view rather than by straight aggregation, so the students can't just shout "intransitive, QED" and stop. I also want the question to ask them what, if anything, the sequence shows about transitivity specifically, as opposed to about aggregation in general — I keep getting papers that treat those as the same thing.

Could you write me the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 25? For the marking scheme I want, for each item, a one-line note on the mistake that should cost marks. The errors I see most often: students conflating intransitivity with incomparability, students assuming a lexical/priority ordering has to be intransitive, and students thinking a spectrum only ever threatens aggregative views. The A/B line should turn on whether they can keep apart four distinct exits — (a) giving up transitivity outright, (b) giving up the small-step premise that each neighbour is always a bit better, (c) keeping transitivity but going non-Archimedean/lexicographic, and (d) appealing to parity or incomparability — and whether they can say which of those is actually available to them given the way I've set the sequence up (in particular whether the far end being plausible under a priority view rules any of the four in or out). One more thing: please make the question self-contained, so a student doesn't have to name any particular paper or author to do well — I'd rather they reason from the case than cite Temkin or Parfit and think that's the answer.
