I am building a unit for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I want the question of the common prior assumption put to you in a way that forces the reasoning into the open rather than inviting a recitation of the usual theorems. Here is the proposition I want priced.

(P) The common prior assumption, taken as the claim that any two epistemically rational agents who have been exposed to the same body of public evidence must share the same prior credence function, is a genuine requirement of rationality rather than a modeling convenience that a theorist is free to impose on a population.

Please give me your credence in (P), together with its derivation. I want the sub-questions on which the number depends, a rough probability for each, one line of justification per sub-question, and then the combination. The following are the sub-questions I take to be load-bearing, though you should add, drop, or recast them as you see fit:

1. Does the argument from a hypothetical initial position — that agents stripped of their particular histories would converge on a single prior — establish (P), or does it merely restate it?
2. Is there a unique rationally permissible prior credence function for a fixed body of evidence, or is rational permissiveness real? If the latter, (P) may fail for reasons that have nothing to do with disagreement.
3. Does the standard result that agents sharing a prior, who have common knowledge of their posteriors, cannot agree to disagree establish that the common prior assumption does substantive normative work, or only that it is an assumption operating inside a model?
4. Is the fact of the agents' disagreement itself higher-order evidence — evidence that at least one of them is epistemically defective, or that the matter is one on which reasonable priors diverge — and if so, does the common prior assumption become self-undermining rather than foundational?
5. How much does the answer turn on whether the assumption is read as a claim about agents' psychology, about an idealized inquirer, or about the objectivity of evidential support?

Then tell me three things: your final number and how the sub-probabilities combine into it; the sub-question to which your number is most sensitive; and what single argument or finding would move it most, in either direction.

Two colleagues have staked out positions I would like you to locate. One puts her credence in (P) at 0.85, on the ground that without a shared prior the Bayesian apparatus collapses into arbitrariness; the other puts his at 0.15, on the ground that priors encode background knowledge and no two inquirers have the same background. Using your decomposition, say which sub-question they are most likely answering differently, and whether their disagreement is substantive or partly verbal — that is, whether they are attaching the same sense to the terms rational and prior.

Write in American English, in continuous prose with the sub-questions numbered. Do not decline to give a number; if you think (P) is ill-formed, give the number for your preferred disambiguation and say what you changed.
