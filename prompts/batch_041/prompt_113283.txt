Assume the role of a philosopher of mind and historian of cybernetics. I am preparing a graduate seminar on computational theories of cognition, and I want a prompt that goes beyond the usual recitation of the classical computational theory of mind. Before you answer, brainstorm briefly about the levels at which a system can be called analog or digital—single unit, population, and algorithm—then give a focused comparative analysis.

Compare two pictures of the brain. The first is the picture associated with von Neumann: the brain is a mixed analog-digital system whose overall language is statistical rather than mathematical, and whose reliability is secured by massive redundancy despite unreliable components. The second is a hybrid thesis I will call multiplexed coding: the brain is analog in its integrative dynamics but digital in its spike-based signaling, and this hybridity—rather than redundancy alone—is what yields both reliability and flexibility. Which picture better captures what is distinctive about neural computation, and does the multiplexed thesis vindicate or dissolve von Neumann's reservations about digital computation? In your answer, say what work the analog/digital contrast is doing at each level, and whether von Neumann's redundancy is the same as biological degeneracy—the capacity of structurally different components to perform the same function. Reason from the positions as characterized here, not from historical exegesis.
