My friend Dana does Bayesian stats and we spent most of last Thursday arguing, after a seminar, about whether "simpler theories are more likely to be true" is a real constraint on belief or just a habit we've dressed up, and I ended up writing down where I actually stand so I could see whether it hangs together. (1) Simplicity is objective — when one hypothesis is simpler than another, that's a fact about the hypotheses, and any account of inductive inference with no room for it is missing something. (2) The way to make that precise is description length: the simpler hypothesis is the one with the shorter program, and the right prior is the one that weights a hypothesis by 2 to the minus its length. (3) Yes, length depends on which universal machine you pick, but I think that's a non-issue, because any two machines differ by at most an additive constant, so in any comparison that matters the arbitrariness washes out. (4) I think the razor is a claim about the prior and nothing else — once the data come in the likelihood does the work and the prior's contribution fades away, which is the part of the whole picture I'm most confident about, its the cleanest thing here. (5) I also think there's a fact of the matter about what a rational agent ought to believe, and that no computable agent could have it — the Solomonoff prior isn't computable and I'm at peace with that. (6) But when I watch actual scientists, the razor always gets applied inside some vocabulary: you compare two models of the same data, or two particle models, and I can't see what it would even mean to compare "the standard model" against "it's all a simulation" by program length.

Here's the thing I've been trying to sell Dana on, and I don't know if it's a real distinction or just me being tired: the razor as a tie-breaker, which only kicks in when two hypotheses fit the data equally well, versus the razor as a prior, which is always in the game and shapes what you even bother to consider. I only believe the first one; I think people who say they believe the second are usually running the first one at a scale where they can't see it happening.

So which of these can I keep together? I genuinely don't know where the break is, which is why I'm asking instead of just fixing it myself — I've been going back and forth on this alot. Order the tensions by how bad they are, tell me which ones are only apparent, and for the real ones say what the cheapest thing to drop is and what dropping it costs me. And keep the settled parts separate from the parts people who actually work on this disagree about — don't serve me some contested reading of the convergence results as if it were the textbook line.
