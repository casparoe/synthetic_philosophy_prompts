So I'm the parliamentarian for a professional association — about 4,000 members, we vote on bylaw amendments at the annual meeting and elect the board by mail ballot — and I keep getting motions to change how we vote. We use plain plurality right now, everyone hates the vote-splitting, so members keep bringing ranked-choice, approval voting, Borda counts, pairwise/Condorcet-style methods, and every year somebody has a new favorite. And I'm stuck. I half remeber from a poli-sci class that all of these can be gamed — something like: once you've got three or more options on the table and you're not just letting one person decide, there's always some situation where a voter does better by lying about their real preferences — but I have no idea how to turn that into anything I can actually say in a meeting. Right now I just mutter that they're all manipulable and everyone rolls their eyes and we vote the motion down on vibes.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on any proposal that lands on my desk, built on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result and what it really says (not the watered-down version). I need it as an ordered checklist: what to check first, what counts as a dealbreaker, where the theorem genuinely bites and where it doesn't, and where it stops and hands the call to a person because it's a value judgment and not a math question. Concretely, I want to know how to test the conditions in a real election with real candidates rather than in a proof — how do I tell whether we really have three or more distinct alternatives (we have write-ins, uncontested seats, a couple of races where the same person is basically everyone's second choice); how do I tell whether the assumption about unrestricted preferences is doing anything in our case, given that some of our races are obviously one-dimensional; and how do I check whether a proposed method is "dictatorial" or "onto" in the sense the theorem needs, without me being a mathematician. I also want it to be honest about the part where the theorem is basically telling me I can't have what the motion-writers want — I suspect the real answer is "the good method doesn't exist, pick your poison," and I'd rather the procedure say that out loud than pretend there's a clean fix.

I'm not a mathematician, I've got maybe the length of a board meeting to do this, and whatever I recommend the board has to sign off on, so it has to be something a smart layperson can execute under time pressure. Please flag which steps are real inference and which are me dressing up a judgment call as procedure.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A motion to replace plurality with approval voting for the board election. (2) A motion to use a Borda count to rank the bylaw amendments. (3) Our budget-prioritization committee, where the items genuinely do fall along one left-to-right line of "spend more on this vs. spend more on that" and everyone roughly agrees on the ordering — my hunch is the theorem doesn't apply there and the procedure should tell me to stop worrying, but I want you to confirm or shoot that down. And I want one of these worked cases to come out badly for me: I'd guess the proposal I personally like best (some pairwise/Condorcet-flavored method) is going to come out just as gameable as what we've got, which means I can't justify the switch on manipulation grounds, and the procedure should make me say so to the members' faces. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do — the stuff it will get wrong, and the decisions it will quietly push onto me that I won't notice it's pushing.
