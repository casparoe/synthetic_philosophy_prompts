A close friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about mental illness, and I would like a way of working out what we were actually disagreeing about. He had been reading Thomas Szasz and came away convinced that the whole category is a myth — that depression, ADHD and the rest are not illnesses at all but troubles in living, and that calling them illnesses is chiefly a device for excusing people from responsibility or for licensing coercion. I said he was trading on an implausibly sharp line between bodily and mental malfunction, since he was happy to call a broken leg an injury without being able to say what makes it one. We went round this for hours and, I think, kept talking past each other: at various points we were disagreeing about medicine, about blame, about what words mean, and about who gets to decide. I would like a diagnostic instrument that sorts out which of those disputes we were really having, and that lets me place both of our positions on a map. I don't need citations; I need the distinctions.

Please write a taxonomy of positions on what makes a condition a mental illness, in the form of a tree: each branch labelled with the question that separates it from its siblings, each leaf carrying the name of a position (or marked 'unnamed' where nobody occupies it) and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

Requirements:

- Build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Start with the questions, work out which combinations of answers are available, and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Note the combinations nobody occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Every node must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the labels, so no node may take the form 'are you a Szaszian?'. Nodes should be concrete cases or flatly stated commitments.
- The spine should include, at minimum: whether the concept of disorder is descriptive, evaluative, or irreducibly both; whether a failure of some internal mechanism is necessary, sufficient or irrelevant; whether a condition's cause — lesion, learning history, social situation — matters to its classification or only to its treatment; whether the classification turns on distress, on harm to others, on statistical abnormality, or on nothing empirical at all; and whether calling something an illness is a claim about the person or a move within a social practice, of the kind that exempts from obligations or permits detention and forced treatment.
- Include the position that the answer is indexical, that 'is this an illness?' has different answers depending on which practice is asking — licensing a drug, granting a school accommodation, excusing conduct in court, shaping a person's self-understanding. If you think that is a dodge rather than a position, say so and explain why.
- The tree must try to separate three views that are often run together: that health is statistically species-typical functioning; that it is the performance of naturally selected functions; and that a disorder is a harmful failure of a mechanism to do what it was designed to do. If your nodes cannot prise some of these apart, flag the pair and say what further question would be needed.
- Use at least these cases at the nodes, alongside whatever else you require: grief that continues well past the point a culture expects; someone who meets every criterion for a neurodevelopmental condition, values the traits in question, and wants nothing changed; a man whose misery is an accurate response to an intolerable marriage; and a condition whose diagnostic status was reversed by a vote of a professional body rather than by any discovery.
- Aim for a manageable tree, roughly ten to fifteen leaves, rather than an exhaustive catalogue, and do not smuggle in a verdict: I don't want the tree to conclude that one leaf is the sensible one.

Finally, tell me which of your branches are load-bearing and which I could collapse without loss; which single answer at which node would force you to redraw the tree; and where you are least confident that you have represented a position as its defenders would recognise it, rather than as its critics describe it.
