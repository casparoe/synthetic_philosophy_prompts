I am applying for a one-semester writing fellowship at my university's humanities center, and the application requires a two-page research proposal of roughly 1,200 words. My topic is Augustine's account of lying, and I have a half-formed idea I would like you to turn into a proper proposal.

The standard way of sorting definitions of lying distinguishes accounts on which a lie requires a false statement made with intent to deceive from accounts on which the intent to deceive is sufficient and the falsity of what is uttered is not necessary. Augustine is usually placed on the second side: he locates the wrong of a lie in a kind of duplicity of heart, that is, in the will to deceive, rather than in the falsity of what is uttered, which is why he is read as counting certain true statements made with the aim of misleading among the lies, and why he treats even well-intentioned lies as impermissible. What is done less often is to press that criterion against the contemporary phenomenon that negotiation researchers call paltering: selecting and arranging only true statements so as to leave one's interlocutor with a false impression, while asserting nothing false.

My hunch, which you should feel free to argue against but which I want the proposal to commit to rather than hedge, is that Augustine's criterion gets paltering right, and that the interesting consequence is that the falsity condition in the standard definition of lying is doing less work than its defenders suppose. Once one separates the question of what makes an act a lie from the question of when a true statement is deceptive, the second question turns out to be where the real action is, and Augustinian duplicity is a better guide to it than any condition on truth-value.
