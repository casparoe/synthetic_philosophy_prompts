I run a 75-minute session for about 16 undergraduates in a social choice and political philosophy seminar, and I want them to feel the force of Sen's liberal paradox in their hands before anyone names it. Design a staged decision exercise built on a two-issue, two-person template I can replicate across the room: each participant has a private matter that by ordinary lights only they should decide (whether they read a given book, say), plus a meddlesome ranking of someone else's private matter, and the rankings are to be arranged so that when the liberal rule is applied mechanically — each person's preference over their own issue is decisive — the settlement that results is ranked below a different outcome by both parties, so minimal liberalism and weak Pareto collide. Give me the rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no real money, roles for the non-playing observers, a timeline, and the exact point at which I stop enforcing one principle and start enforcing the other. Participants should reach the contradiction by following the rules, not by being told it is coming; they should not hear the name until the debrief. The debrief should map what happened onto the two conditions, explain why the collision is a theorem rather than a flaw in my setup, and be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce: one profile is not a proof, and the impossibility holds for any rule meeting the conditions, not merely for the mechanism we used. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — how to handle participants who insist the meddlesome preferences are simply irrational or who want to dissolve the paradox by declaring some preferences out of bounds, and what to do if the group converges on the Pareto-superior outcome and overrides the liberal rule without noticing they have done anything questionable.
