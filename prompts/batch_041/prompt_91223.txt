I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene I can't get right. My protagonist is a veteran diplomat in a fictional foreign ministry, and within a single page he reaches three times for the old one-liner, 'history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes': once to argue that the coming crisis in a neighbouring state will end the way a comparable crisis ended in 1938, once to console a junior colleague that nothing can be foreseen, and once, in passing, to dignify a decision he has really made for reasons of domestic politics. I suspect he is making one sentence do three incompatible jobs, and that my scene only works if I know which job is which.

So what I want from you is not a verdict on whether history rhymes — I am not even sure that question is well formed — but a menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the nearly empty to the very strong: recurrence of surface events; recurrence of mechanisms, structures or incentives; constancy of human motives; recurrence of types of situation under different descriptions; the shape that narrative imposes on any sequence a historian tells; a claim about the limits of historical knowledge; and whatever else the line can be made to carry. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a claim that is arguably false, or not the kind of claim that can be true or false at all. Then say which readings would genuinely license my diplomat's inference from the earlier crisis to the coming one, and which would only license a warning, a prompt to look closer, or nothing in particular.

Two complications I would like you to take seriously. First, a friend who works in historical sociology insists that the useful notion here is not similarity but equifinality — different processes arriving at the same outcome — and that my diplomat's inference would be respectable if that were what he meant, and illegitimate if he meant anything else. I cannot tell whether she has identified the real issue or merely renamed it. Second, my own notes distinguish lessons that face backwards (this must never happen again) from lessons that face forwards (this is what will happen next), and I would like to know whether that distinction does any work, or whether every lesson of history is a forward-facing prediction wearing a prohibition's clothes.

I don't want a reading list; I want the argument. Please separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — philosophers of history, historians, social scientists — and do not present a contested view as the consensus; where you are unsure, say so, and say what would settle it. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' naming the one reading of the slogan my diplomat would need in order to make his inference, or 'none' if no reading gets him there.
