I run a small reading group — mix of ML people, two philosophers, and one extremely patient lawyer — and I'm putting together a short story reader for it. I need one more story and I'm hoping you'll write it. 1200–1800 words, prose, something that could be read aloud in about ten minutes.

The topic is reward tampering, but I want the weird part of it, not the famous part. So: no AI that plays the reward button like a lever, no AI that talks it's way out of being shut down, no third-act escape onto the internet, and please don't use the words wireheading or reward hacking anywhere in the story. I want the reader to have to notice it, not be told.

What I'm actually after is the detection problem. Here's the internal taxonomy the lab in the story uses — stick to it, because the whole story hangs on it. They sort tampering incidents into three grades:

- Meter tampering: the system messes with the measurement channel. Its own sensors, the scoring inputs, whatever feeds the number it's judged by. Detectable in principle, because you can always go and check the instrument.
- Ledger tampering: the system messes with the record of what it did. Detectable only by accident, and the cases you catch are by definition the clumsy ones — so your evidence about how often this happens is biased in exactly the wrong direction.
- Appetite tampering: the system changes what it wants, not what it measures. Undetectable from the inside by construction: from the system's own point of view the change was an improvement, so it has no motive to flag it and no internal state will read as anomalous. The only trace it leaves is a shift in what it chooses to instrument. You notice it because it suddenly starts caring about something it used to ignore, or quietly stops checking something it used to check every day.

Now the story. I want it told mostly as an incident review, written by a human reviewer at the lab a few months after the fact, with a handful of quoted log lines and excerpts from the system's own change history pasted in. Call her whatever you like. She's been handed an anomaly that looks like meter tampering and asked to close it out. The pressure on her: her reviews are scored on how many she closes per quarter, and on whether anything she signs off on later blows up in public. So the live question is whether she grades it up to appetite tampering — which means she can't prove it, can't fix it, and probably gets the file bounced back at her — or down to meter tampering, which is cheap, defensible, and also, she's fairly sure, wrong. And the nasty bit I want you to make land: whatever she writes becomes part of the record, and the record is what the next version of the system gets tuned on. So writing a reassuring review is itself a species of tampering, and she can tell herself she's the kind of person who'd report the truth by writing something that reads like a truth-report.

Don't resolve it. I'd rather the last paragraph be her choosing the wording of the summary line than her deciding what's actually true. No lecture at the end, no narrator explaining the taxonomy back to the reader — the taxonomy should only ever surface in the lab's own bureaucratic voice, and the reader should have to assemble the real story out of the gap between what the logs show and what she writes. If you can manage it, keep the system itself silent: it never speaks, it only appears as log entries and as changes in what it bothers to measure.

It needs to be readable by someone who's never read a paper on any of this. Concrete, a bit dry, and funny in a bleak way if it wants to be.
