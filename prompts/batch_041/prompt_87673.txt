I'm on the board of a small family foundation — we give out about eight grants a year, $20-40k each, mostly local education and public health with a bit of climate stuff mixed in. We meet quarterly and each proposal gets maybe twenty minutes. We're stuck in a loop. Half the board says you can't possibly know the long-run consequences of anything — the kid who gets dewormed grows up to be a dictator, the community center gets a new roof and then the neighborhood gentrifies — so we should fund whatever feels decent and stop pretending otherwise. The other half says that's just an excuse for laziness, and we should estimate expected value and pick the biggest number. I've read enough philosophy to know both sides have a point and that this is roughly what people mean by cluelessness, but I have no idea how to get it out of the seminar room and into a board meeting.

What I want is a decision procedure we can actually run, in order, on a one-page proposal, in the twenty minutes we've got, with a board that includes a retired accountant, a high school principal, and my niece who's doing a PhD in philosophy. It needs to:

- seperate the two kinds of ignorance we're dealing with — the kind where we can't trace the causal chain at all, and the kind where we can trace it fine but can't put the effects on a common scale — because I suspect they call for completely different responses;
- say what to do at each step, what counts as a good enough answer, and where the procedure is allowed to stop with a yes or a no;
- mark the points where we're just making a value call and dressing it up as procedure, since our minutes have to be honest; and
- include something for the case where the procedure says expected value is uninformative and "fund what feels decent" is unearned — where do we go when both branches fail? I want a tiebreak we can actually apply, even if it's crude.

Then run it on three proposals from last quarter: (a) $30k to expand a deworming program in a country we already work in, where the short-term health effects are solid but the long-run development story is contested; (b) $25k to a local group running free legal clinics for tenants facing eviction, where we can see effects for about two years and then nothing; (c) $40k to a small advocacy outfit pushing a state-level policy that would affect a few million people if it passed, which it probaly won't, and which we honestly can't tell would be good on net even if it did. For each one, tell me what the procedure spits out, and be blunt about which of the three it's going to make me vote against even though I personally want to fund it.

And if there's a step where the honest answer is "this is a coin flip, admit it and flip", say so, and tell me how to do that without it becoming an excuse for every hard call.
