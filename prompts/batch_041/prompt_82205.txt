I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a philosopher of mind who works on the unity of consciousness — about the paper I'm presenting next week, and I want to be ready for the discussion rather than merely right. My claim is that Schrödinger's insistence that consciousness is never encountered in the plural is not a metaphysical thesis about how many minds there are, but a claim about the form of first-person awareness: the subject that knows can never be one of the things it knows, so a plurality of consciousnesses is not something anyone could ever encounter from the inside. Call the two claims subject-singularity and subject-count. I argue that his late philosophical essays, and his invocations of the Upanishads, only ever establish the first, and that identifying his view with Advaita Vedanta — with the identity of Atman and Brahman — quietly imports the second. My friend thinks this makes Schrödinger a confused Kantian and my reading of him merely decorative.

The talk is at a small interdisciplinary workshop: three analytic philosophers of mind, one of whom works on phenomenal unity and on whether unity is a feature of a subject's experience or a fact about how many subjects there are; two historians of physics who admire Schrödinger's physics and regard his late essays as amateur metaphysics; one scholar of Indian philosophy, trained in Sanskrit, who works on Advaita and is allergic to what they call Neo-Vedanta and to Western appropriation of it; and one neuroscientist who studies split-brain patients. You are an expert in both the philosophy of mind and the history of philosophy, with enough grounding in Vedanta to keep Shankara's Advaita distinct from Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism.

Before drafting, think carefully about what each of these people actually cares about, and spend a few lines on which objections will be shared across the room and which are peculiar to one audience member. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session.
