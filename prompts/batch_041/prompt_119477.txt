Two winters ago a driver ran a red light and put me in the hospital for a week. When I filed a claim against his policy, an adjuster told me, more or less, that his client had not caused my injuries — he had merely failed to prevent them — and that the policy covered only what its holder caused. I have no academic training, but I have spent a long time since then thinking about that sentence, and I have come to believe the adjuster was making a real argument, one that many people would endorse if you put it to them plainly: that an act and a failure to act are different kinds of thing, and that only the first can be a cause. I have also come to think the argument is a trap, because once you start asking whether things would have gone differently, the list of things a person failed to prevent never ends, and any action can be redescribed as a failure to prevent something else.

I would like you to write a parody that takes that reasoning seriously and follows it exactly where it goes. Not an essay — a piece of sustained irony, in the form of the policyholder's handbook and customer FAQ for an insurance company whose product covers only harms its customers caused by not acting. Invent the company; name it whatever you like. About 800 words, in the deadpan register of a real customer-facing document: numbered questions and answers, one or two worked examples, a short table of sample claims, and a closing block of fine print.

Two constraints. First, the document must be entirely sincere. Nobody in it may notice any difficulty, and it must never wink at the reader or explain its own joke. Second, the fine print should quietly stake out positions on all of the following, without ever naming them as positions: whether an omission can be a cause at all, and what the company does with the fact that its product would be empty if the answer were no; what it takes to have caused something, and what that test does when the harm was going to happen anyway or when two people each did enough to bring it about; how the company decides which of the countless things a policyholder did not do counts as the cause of a given harm; my own objection that any action can be redescribed as a failure to prevent something else, together with the company's answer to it; and the clause limiting cover to cases where the policyholder had a duty to act, and the sense in which that clause, rather than any theory of causation, is doing the real work. Include at least one worked example in which the same claim is denied twice under two standards that cannot both be correct, and let the document betray no awareness of this. It should be funny to someone who knows the argument well and readable to someone who has never encountered it. No real sources or reading suggestions — the document should stand on its own.
