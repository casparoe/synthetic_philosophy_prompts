I have been reading George Berkeley's *The Querist* and have reached a cluster of queries that I cannot get to hang together. The book supplies no answers; every sentence is a question, and although I take the recurrent formula "Whether X be not Y" to be a way of asserting Y, that device carries me only so far. The queries that stop me are these:

> 4 Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?
>
> 5 Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?
>
> 22 Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?
>
> 23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
>
> 24 Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?
>
> 25 Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
>
> 26 Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

I am not asking for a survey of Berkeley's monetary opinions, and I would rather not be told what commentators have made of him; I want a reading of this passage argued from the text itself, and I am willing to be told that the queries do not in fact cohere. My difficulties are these:

First, what is the ticket a ticket *for*? In query 23 the candidates are "intrinsic value, commodity, standard, measure, or pledge", and Berkeley appears to set them aside in favour of "ticket or counter". But the five rejected candidates do not look like rival answers to one question: a pledge and a measure are not competitors in the way that a commodity and a counter are. Tell me what single question, if any, would make those five genuine alternatives, and what the "true idea" of money amounts to once the alternatives are properly separated.

Second, queries 23 and 25 seem to pull in opposite directions. In 23 the coin itself is the ticket or counter; in 25 it is the *denominations* that are "exponents or denominations of such proportion", while gold, silver and paper are demoted to counters "for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof". If the metals are counters for the denominations, then the ticket is not the coin after all. Which item in Berkeley's scheme is doing the representing, and which is being represented? Is the ticket a coin, or is it the unit of account?

Third, I cannot make "a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty" (query 24) yield a definite sense. What would such a proportion be a proportion *of*, and how does it relate to the older idea that price is the ratio between a commodity and the quantity of money? If price is fixed by demand and plenty, what work is left for money to do other than record the result — and is that the work Berkeley assigns it in query 5, where money is useful only "so far ... as it stirreth up industry"?

Fourth, query 26 seems to be the decisive one, and I want to know how much it commits him to. If the denominations can be "retained, although the bullion were gone", then Berkeley appears to hold that the unit of account is separable from any metallic medium, and that commerce could continue on a purely notional money. Does the rest of the cluster support that reading, or does something in 22–25 require a medium that is itself scarce?

To force the issue, consider two cases. (a) A settlement inherits the money of account of a departed imperial power; it mines no metal, mints nothing, and keeps its books in a unit that corresponds to nothing in particular, settling debts by transfers of claims among its members. (b) A settlement with an identical real output, identical industry and identical tastes, but with a metallic currency. Would Berkeley be obliged to say that (a) is as rich as (b), that both are equally well served in the matter of money, or that (a) lacks something money essentially is? And does his answer turn on the difference between a ticket that is a *claim on an issuer* — whose value depends on the issuer's promise to honour it — and a ticket that is an *admission to a share* of what is currently for sale — whose value depends on the ratio of tickets to goods? My own suspicion is that query 24 fits the second model and query 23 the first, which would explain why the passage reads as it does; but I would rather have the argument than my suspicion confirmed.

Finally, tell me how much weight the interrogative form should carry. If "Whether X be not Y" merely proposes Y for consideration rather than asserting it, the cluster may be a set of exploratory moves rather than a doctrine, and the appearance of conflict may be an artefact of reading rhetorical questions as claims. I would like a judgement on whether that escape is honest here or whether it amounts to declining to find a position in a text that plainly has one.
