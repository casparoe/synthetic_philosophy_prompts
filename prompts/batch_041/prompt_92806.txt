Hi, I need a hand designing something for a class and this has gotten weirdly personal, so bear with me.

I teach a 200-level elective at a community college called Myth, History and Memory. 22 students, 75 minutes, one session. Mostly non-majors, and a few of them get openly annoyed whenever anything sounds like "well actually nothing is real." Last month my aunt sent round a family tree tracing us back to some knight who supposedly fought at a siege in the 1300s, and now half the family talks about him like he's a documented ancestor while my cousin (a librarian, of course) keeps saying he's basically a legend with a coat of arms. We had an actual argument about it at Thanksgiving. So "when does a legend become history" has stopped being a cute seminar topic for me and started being a thing that ruins dinner.

What I want is an activity where they feel it in their gut that the line between legend and history isn't a fact about the past, it's a decision about standards and about who's allowed to say -- and that they will draw the line in different places depending on what standards they've been handed, without ever noticing they were handed anything.

My rough idea, please fix it or throw it out: make up a fictional founding story -- some kingdom or city and a founder who's said to have driven out invaders in, I don't know, the 800s. It has to be invented, so nobody can look it up and so we can engineer the evidence exactly. Split them into groups of 4-5. Every group gets the identical dossier of about 9 "evidence" cards: a chronicle written 300 years later, a fragmentary inscription, a hostile foreign account, a folk song collected in the 1800s, a burial with grave goods, a genealogy that contradicts itself, etc. Each card says what it is and how far after the events it claims to be. The groups have to sort the cards into "could support history" vs "only supports legend" and then put the whole thing on a 5-point dial from pure fiction to established fact.

Then the twist: each group was secretly given a different one-paragraph charter of what counts. One says a source is more reliable if it includes a detail that makes its own side look bad. One says a hostile outsider's testimony outweighs a friendly one. One says physical evidence can settle it on its own. One sets a maximum gap in years between event and source. Same cards, different verdicts. Then hand everyone one new card (say, a newly found inscription) and watch how far the dials jump -- or don't.

End with a ratification vote: the class has to produce ONE official class verdict. I want them to see that what wins is the coalition, not the evidence, and that there's no neutral charter to appeal to. Only after that do we name what just happened, and only then do we run the same dial on Troy, Arthur and the Exodus and catch them reusing whichever charter they were assigned.

Give me the roles/rules, the actual card list, the charters, a timeline for the 75 minutes, the scoring or voting rule, and a debrief that maps each moment back onto the idea. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing they'll argue about the facts instead of the charters) and how to handle it. And be honest with me about which part of the idea this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to just explain myself -- I don't want to oversell it to them. Also I'm not a specialist, so keep the facilitator notes in plain language.
