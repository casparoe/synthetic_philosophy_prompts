Hi — I'm prepping a seminar presentation on the first formulation of Kant's categorical imperative and I've hit a snag I can't get passed. As I understand it, the test is: take your maxim and ask whether it could be willed as a universal law, and "universal law" has to mean a law that every rational agent could will at the same time, otherwise it isn't universal. But then take two maxims that each seem perfectly fine on their own — say mine, "I'll keep my promises," and someone else's, "I'll break a promise when keeping it would seriously hurt someone" — they can't both be universal law, so at least one of them fails. That makes the test not a test of my maxim at all but a test of whether the whole set of everyone's maxims can be made consistent, which is basically a social choice problem, and the verdict is going to depend on which maxims I take as given going in. So is the fix to just count how many agents could will each maxim and go with the winner? That feels weirdly majoritarian for Kant, but I can't see any other way to get a determinate answer. Or is there some tie-breaker rule I'm missing, like the more specific maxim beats the more general one when they collide?
