Not sure if this has a name, and it's been bugging me. There's a move I keep seeing in arguments about historical figures. Someone criticises a figure the speaker admires — Jefferson and slavery, Churchill and the Bengal famine, Columbus and what he did to the people he found — and back it comes: "you can't judge him by today's standards, he was a man of his time." Fine. But the same speaker, same conversation, will turn around and praise that same figure for being ahead of his time, for having the guts to go against the consensus of his day. So the era gets to excuse everything bad and is never credited with anything good, while the man gets all the credit and none of the blame. And it isn't even applied evenly — nobody bothers to say Pol Pot was a man of his time. The shield only comes out for people the speaker already likes, and never for the ones they don't.

Is there an established term for this specific thing? I know there's talk about presentism and not imposing our values on the past, and something about the luck of being born when you were, but as far as I can tell those are about whether context should count at all, not about this lopsided, pick-and-choose way of deploying it. If there's a real name, tell me what it is and how it differs from the nearest ideas; if there isn't, coin one and define it sharply enough that I can tell it apart from ordinary presentism and from plain old special pleading.
