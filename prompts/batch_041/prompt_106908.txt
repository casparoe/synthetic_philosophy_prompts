I have spent the better part of a year on the governance working group of a large member-owned cooperative — about forty thousand members, an elected board, and a stubborn habit of settling every contentious question by referendum — and I have arrived at a settled view that I want stress-tested before I put it into a position paper. Here is the package. (1) Contested policy questions should be decided by conditional prediction markets rather than by referendum or board vote: two markets are opened on the same future date, one on the cooperative's per-member surplus if the proposal is adopted and one on the surplus if it is not, and the proposal is adopted whenever the first price exceeds the second. (2) Markets are the right instrument because they aggregate dispersed information that no committee or electorate could gather, and because they force people to back their beliefs with something they would hate to lose. (3) Whatever the markets are scored against must be a single, published, periodically measured index — I have in mind a composite of per-member surplus, member retention, and an independently audited safety measure — because a market needs a definite settlement value. (4) A decision is legitimate only if every member bound by it has an equal formal say in making it, and only if those on the losing side keep a standing avenue to contest the outcome. (5) I take seriously the thought that any index which is published and used to decide anything will be gamed, so the cooperative should treat its measured index as a fallible proxy for a true objective it cannot measure, and should never let the index itself become the thing it is trying to maximize.

To head off the obvious objection I have been assuming two further things: that the trading tokens would be non-transferable and issued one per member, so the market cannot simply be captured by whoever holds the most cash, and that any member shown to have moved the index by sabotage rather than by genuinely useful action would forfeit a bond posted at the outset.

What I want from you is an audit, not a defence. Tell me which of these commitments actually pull against one another, which pairs only look like they conflict, and how severe each genuine tension is. Rank the tensions rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the package; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. I would far rather be told that two of these cannot be held together than be reassured that they can.
