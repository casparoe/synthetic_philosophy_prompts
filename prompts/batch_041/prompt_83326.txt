I'm running a session for a philosophy of action reading group — mixed crowd, about 18 people, a few grad students, two economists who keep saying "that's just time inconsistency," one emeritus who thinks the whole topic is a confusion, and some undergrads who signed up because they liked the title. 75 minutes, one room, no computers. I want an activity where they feel, in their own choices, the thing the reading keeps gesturing at and never quite pins down: the difference between *changing your mind* about what's worth doing and *failing to do what you judged you should do*. Hyperbolic discounting hands us the first thing on a plate — a clean preference reversal over time — and then everyone acts like they've explained akrasia, and I don't think they have. So I want the game to pull the two apart, physically, in the room, so that afterwards we can ask which of them actually happened to us. Act as an expert designer of teaching games for philosophy, someone who's run things like this and watched them fail. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate mechanics before you commit to one, and say briefly why you killed the others — I'd rather see the rejected designs then get one polished thing with no alternatives.

Design constraints, some of which is me being fussy. Everyone gets a personal account of tokens at the start (call them whatever you like) and at each round picks between a small payout now and a bigger one at the end of the session; tokens buy a small real prize at the end — first pick from a box of decent snacks, a $10 card, something like that — so the stakes are small but not zero, and no actual money changes hands during play. It has to include all four of these, because they're where the philosophy lives: (1) before any round is played, every participant writes down two things privately — what they judge they *should* do across the whole session, and what they predict they will *actually* do — and the facilitator keeps both, sealed; (2) a paid commitment device, i.e. you can pay now to bind your future choices; (3) a paid *release* from a commitment you've already made, priced so releasing is dearer than binding, so we can watch who buys their way out of their own promise and what they say about it afterwards; (4) a group mechanism — either commitments are enforced by the room's willingness to hold people to them, or the group can vote at some point to release everyone — because I'm convinced half of what we call willpower is other people, and I want the game to show that rather than assert it. Bonus if you can work in a naive/sophisticated contrast: give half the room the full schedule of rounds up front and the other half only round-by-round, and see whether knowing what's coming changes what people do *before* the temptation arrives. I need materials, roles if any, a round-by-round timeline with timings that actually adds up to 75 minutes, and a payoff scheme I can explain in two minutes to people who are already suspicious of the whole exercise.

Then the debrief, which is the part I care most about and the part I expect to be hardest. Map specific moments in the game onto specific pieces of the idea — the moment someone pays to release, the moment a pre-play prediction and the actual play come apart, the moment the group vote fails to hold — and give me questions that get *them* to say which of their choices were akratic and which were just them having different preferences at different times, rather than me telling them. If the game can't actually tell those two apart and only makes both feel vivid, say so. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: I don't think 20 minutes of symbolic tokens produces the phenomenology of temptation, and I don't think any of this settles whether the later preference or the earlier one is the "real" one — spell that out and tell me what I have to supply from the front of the room. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm betting someone arbitrages the gap between the binding and release prices, someone treats the tokens as Monopoly money and feels nothing, and at least one person refuses to play on principle) and how to handle each without turning the debrief into a lecture. No reading list, no citations, don't tell me what Aristotle said — I have the syllabus. Just the activity, in enough detail that I can run it Friday.
