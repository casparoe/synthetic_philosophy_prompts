I'm a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on population ethics and the ethics of the long term, and I would like help thinking through the consequences of a toy framework that was handed out in class. The framework is deliberately crude, and the assignment is to work out what follows from it rather than to survey what others have said about it.

The setup is this. An outcome assigns a welfare level, positive or negative, to every slot in an infinite sequence of slots, one person per slot, with each person's welfare fixed independently of everyone else's. Because the welfare in an outcome need not add up to a finite number, outcomes are not ranked by their totals. They are ranked instead by a segment-by-segment comparison: outcome A counts as at least as good as outcome B just in case, for every finite initial segment of slots, the welfare summed over that segment in A is at least the welfare summed over that segment in B. Call this segment-wise dominance.

Three features of the framework seem important to me, and I would like to know what to make of them. First, segment-wise dominance is not a complete ranking: many pairs of outcomes are incomparable, with neither at least as good as the other, and this includes pairs whose totals are equal. Second, it is not neutral about which slot receives which welfare level: two outcomes that differ only by a rearrangement of the same welfare levels across the slots can still be ranked, with one strictly better than the other. Third, whenever two outcomes have finite totals it ranks them in the same direction as simple summing, but it is a refinement of summing rather than a mere extension of it, since it can call one outcome strictly better than another that summing would treat as exactly as good.

Please describe the most important implications of this framework. I am especially interested in (a) whether there can be a best outcome at all, and what a policy of maximizing could amount to when no outcome is best; (b) what becomes of the case for interventions that would make the future much larger or much longer, given that many such interventions will be incomparable with the status quo rather than better than it; (c) whether the framework supplies any reason to treat the order of the slots — as earlier and later times, say — as ethically significant, and so any reason for or against discounting future welfare; and (d) how much of the case for creating additional happy lives survives once creation can leave an outcome neither better nor worse. Where an implication turns on an assumption that the framework itself does not supply, please identify the assumption, and please separate what follows from the framework alone from points that are familiar in population ethics merely restated in this vocabulary.
