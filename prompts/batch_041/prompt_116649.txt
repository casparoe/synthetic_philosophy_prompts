I'm preparing a long-form interview for my science-and-ideas podcast with a philosopher who defends a comparative, consequence-based account of verisimilitude: one theory is closer to the truth than another when its true consequences are more numerous or more important and its false consequences fewer or less important. Her distinctive contribution is a distinction between coverage truthlikeness—how much of the truth a theory captures—and precision truthlikeness—how finely it discriminates among possibilities—and she argues the two can come apart, so a theory can be closer in one respect and farther in the other. I want questions that press her on the machinery rather than let her restate the view. You are an expert philosopher of science and an experienced interviewer. Before drafting, brainstorm briefly for yourself the three or four strongest objections a well-informed critic would raise. Then write eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Include at least one question on the language-dependence objection—the worry that permuting the predicate vocabulary can reverse any verdict of closeness—and a follow-up asking whether her coverage/precision distinction survives it. Include at least one on the child's play problem, where adding true but irrelevant disjuncts seems to inflate truthlikeness, and a follow-up asking which of her two measures blocks it and what that costs. Include at least one on whether truthlikeness is semantic or epistemic: can we ever know which of two false theories is closer, and if not, what work does the notion do for science? Include at least one on scientific realism: does the realist need a metric or only a partial order, and what does her account supply? Keep the questions crisp, non-generic, and free of any request for reading lists or citations.
