I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogosphere for years, and I have just enough amateur probability to be dangerous: I can follow a Bayes-factor argument, I've internalized the norm of noticing confusion, and I've learned to be suspicious whenever a technical result gets summarized as being "just" something more familiar. A small reading group I'm in has been working through Bayesian epistemology, and last week's session was on Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain evidence. The person who led it circulated a one-page handout beforehand, and the rest of the group more or less took it as settled — I nodded along at the time, but something has been nagging at me since, and I can't yet say whether it's a real seam in the summary or just my own failure to internalize the theorem. Here is the substance of the handout, as faithfully as I can reproduce it:

"Ordinary Bayesian conditionalization requires you to be certain of your evidence. Jeffrey conditionalization is the generalization that lets you update when your evidence is uncertain: you take the new probability of the evidence as given, and you reweight the rest of your credences proportionally so that the conditional probabilities within each cell of the partition are left untouched. In the special case where the evidence is certain, it collapses into Bayes's rule, so it is a strict generalization rather than a rival to it. It also has the virtue of being order-independent: because each update is just a linear reweighting of the same partition, it makes no difference in what order you acquire your uncertain evidence, whereas ordinary conditionalization is notoriously order-sensitive. Jeffrey's own motivation was that this is the only update rule satisfying a reasonable set of axioms, so it is the uniquely rational response to uncertain evidence. And since it is really just conditionalization with extra bookkeeping, it inherits the usual diachronic Dutch book argument."

Our group then ran a toy case — two unreliable instruments reporting on the same underlying quantity, with different reliabilities, and a deliberately coarse partition — and concluded from the handout's claims that the order in which the two reports arrive cannot matter, that the result would be the same however finely we carved up the evidence, and that the whole thing shows scientific confirmation can be modeled without ever assigning probability 1 to anything, which they took to dissolve the problem of old evidence as a side benefit. Please also say which of the group's downstream conclusions survive once the errors are corrected, and pay particular attention to the partition-relativity point, since I suspect that is where the coarse toy case is quietly doing work the summary attributes to the rule itself.
