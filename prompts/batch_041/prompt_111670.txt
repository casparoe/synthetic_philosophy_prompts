Two options lie before an agent, and they are equal in every respect that could count in favor of one over the other: equal in expected benefit, equal in risk, equal in the demands they impose on the agent, and equally available. No further inquiry could turn up a distinguishing feature. (The options might be two otherwise identical jobs, two identical routes home, or the two piles of hay in the familiar fable of an animal that starves between them.) The agent must nevertheless act.

Write an essay that takes a stand on whether a choice made under these conditions can be rational, and that defends that stand against the strongest objections to it.

Begin with the two most natural positions. On one side, the thought that a choice is rational only when some consideration favors the option taken over the alternative, so that in a genuine tie reason has nothing to do and any selection is an arbitrary lapse. On the other, the thought that if neither option is better than the other then each is permissible, and a permissible choice needs no further license, so the demand for a tie-breaking reason is simply a mistake. Say which of these survives scrutiny and why, and be explicit about what you take a reason for action to be.

Then complicate the case. Suppose the options are not in fact equal — one is very slightly better — but the difference lies in a respect the agent cannot detect and could never have detected. Call this a hidden tie. Does the hidden tie change your verdict? Consider in particular whether rationality attaches to a choice given the reasons available to the agent or given the reasons there actually are, and whether 'arbitrary' means the same thing in the two cases.

Finally, consider an agent who knows it will face an indefinite series of ties and who will not remember its earlier choices or the reasoning behind them. It may decide each case afresh, or adopt a standing rule — always take the leftmost option, always take the one that comes first alphabetically — and apply the rule without deliberating. Does the availability of a rule change the analysis? If a rule that settles a tie without appeal to the merits of the options can be justified, what justifies it: some feature of the options, or some feature of the agent's situation, such as the cost of hesitation or the value of being the kind of agent that can act decisively?

Close with a verdict: under what conditions, if any, is a choice made without preference rational, and what does your answer imply about the ideal of always acting for a reason?
