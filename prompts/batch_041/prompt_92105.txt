Next month I give a departmental talk defending a view I call partition-relative luminosity. The standard margin-of-error argument against luminosity, and with it the KK principle, assumes the agent's mental states are described at a fixed and fine-grained resolution. My claim is that for any state one can be in there is a coarse-graining of the state space relative to which one always knows whether one is in it, so a restricted KK principle survives the argument intact: if you know that you know, both knowings are indexed to the same coarse-grained partition, and the principle is asserted only for propositions that are unions of cells in that partition.

The audience is a mid-sized analytic epistemology group. Two of them work squarely in the anti-luminosity tradition, treat the result as settled, and will hear my move as cheating. Two work in epistemic logic and will want to know what happens to positive introspection and to the S4/S5 axioms once the knowledge operator is partition-indexed, and whether the restriction makes KK trivial or merely non-classical. One works on hinge propositions and will say I have reinvented the safety condition under a new name. And a visitor from the psychology of metacognition will think the whole debate ignores how poorly people actually track their own first-order states.
