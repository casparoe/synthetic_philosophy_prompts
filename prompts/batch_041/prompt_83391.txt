My half-formed idea: inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is the setup where you watch an agent's behavior and infer the reward function it's optimizing. The usual story treats the demonstrator's policy as a noisy readout of a fixed preference ordering. But humans know they're being watched. If their behavior is going to train a model—or be used as feedback for one—they may choose actions to shape what the model infers about them, not to reveal what they actually want. So the data-generating process is already responding to the inference procedure. The human is, in effect, doing inverse inverse reinforcement learning: modeling the learner's model of them and optimizing against it. This turns preference inference into a fixed-point/strategic problem: the learner's posterior depends on the demonstrator's policy, and the demonstrator's policy depends on the learner's posterior. I want to know when, if ever, the inferred reward identifies the human's actual preferences rather than the human's beliefs about the learner's prior, or the human's desire to appear a certain way.
