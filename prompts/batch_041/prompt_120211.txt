I am the risk officer at a mid-sized mutual insurer. Four times a year a committee of six — underwriters, our actuary, the CFO, none of them statisticians — works through the risk register line by line, and what we write down decides how much capital we hold against each entry and how we price two of our lines. Our house practice is to have two analysts score each risk from 1 to 5 for likelihood, average the scores, and then discuss the average as though it were a probability. Replace it with a procedure a non-specialist can run in fifteen minutes per entry, built out of Keynes's account of probability and uncertainty rather than out of our actuarial habits: that a probability is always relative to a particular body of evidence, so numbers drawn from different evidence are not two readings of one quantity; that the weight of evidence behind a probability is a separate matter from the probability itself, so a firm-looking figure can rest on almost nothing; and that probabilities need not all be comparable, so some entries may not be rankable against the others at all. Give me the steps in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each, where a step returns no number and what to record and do instead, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the committee because what remains is a judgment of values or of nerve. Run it on three entries: a liability exposure never litigated in our jurisdiction, with no close precedent anywhere; a risk covered by twenty years of our own clean loss data; and one where my two analysts return 15% and 40% from substantially different evidence and the committee wants the blended 27.5% in the register. Say which of Keynes's distinctions you had to drop to make the thing usable and what that costs, where the procedure will misfire, and what it cannot do.
