I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on consciousness and the self this term, and we have just finished a unit on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness. The final assignment is a counterfactual one: choose a philosopher who died before any of this work existed, and reconstruct, as tightly as the evidence allows, what he would have said about it. I have claimed William James for myself, and I would like you to write that essay.

I do not want a survey of what has been written on James and the split brain. I want the derivation done properly from his own commitments, with you explicit about which of them are carrying the weight. The ones I take to be relevant are: his treatment of the passing thought as the integer of psychology, together with his refusal to explain thought either by a soul standing behind it or by any aggregation of elementary units of consciousness beneath it; his account of thought as personal, continuous and owned, with the self as knower never recurring and the self as known being empirical, plural and socially distributed; his earlier absorption in cases of divided and alternating personality, which he treated as evidence rather than as curiosities; and the position he reached in his last years, where he gave up on consciousness as a distinct stuff altogether.

The questions I want answered are these. On his criteria rather than ours, does the patient after commissurotomy house one thinker or two, and would he have been willing to say the number out loud? What would he make of the hemisphere that improvises a fluent and sincere explanation for an action it did not initiate — one mind rationalising, or two minds in contact? Does the argument he aimed at the mind-stuff theory, that a plurality of mental facts can never be compounded into a single mind, have any force in reverse, against a single mind coming apart into two? I suspect it does not, and I want that suspicion either confirmed with reasons or defeated. And would his late rejection of consciousness as a special stuff alter his verdict, or leave him saying that the question of how many minds there are is a question about purposes rather than about facts?

Then apply him to a case I have invented, since nothing quite like it appears in what we read. Suppose a patient after surgery reports sincerely and repeatedly that she feels no different — one person, continuous with the woman she was before — while testing shows the two hemispheres giving opposite answers to questions about what she wants, and her left hand acting against her stated intentions often enough that she has taken to speaking of it in the third person, as though someone else were in the room with her. What would James predict she is? Would he treat her testimony or the behavioural evidence as authoritative, and why? And what would he say if a partial reconnection later made the hemispheres agree again, but with a measurable delay between the two sides?

End the essay with what would have had to be true for him to be wrong — the observation that would have forced him to abandon the picture — and with a candid verdict on how much of the disagreement between the one-stream and two-stream readings is a disagreement about minds and how much is a disagreement about words.

I do not need citations or a reading list; the argument is what matters. Please write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write a letter to someone whose judgment one respects.
