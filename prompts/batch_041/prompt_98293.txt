I am giving the eulogy at a memorial for my friend and former colleague, Priya Raghavan, who died last month at forty-three. Priya spent the last decade of her life working on what people in our corner of the internet call suffering risks — s-risks — the class of possible futures that are not merely worse than extinction but worse in a way that makes extinction look merciful: futures containing astronomical quantities of intense, undeserved suffering, whether deliberately inflicted or produced as a side effect of some vast optimization process that does not care. I met her through a reading group that grew out of a blog comment section, and most of what I understand about these questions I owe to her patience with an amateur.

The room will be split three ways, and this is the difficulty I need you to solve. There will be a dozen or so colleagues from the field, who will notice at once if I sand her actual views down into something more palatable, and who will judge me for it. There will be her family, who are devout Catholics, who never fully understood or liked what she did with her life, and whose mother has asked me, gently, to help people feel better. And there will be a large number of friends who are neither, who loved her, who have only the vaguest sense of her work, and who find the whole subject morbid.

Two things about her thinking have to survive into the speech, because they were the spine of it and I will not misrepresent them. First, she rejected the standard framing in our circles — that the worst outcome is human extinction and that the goal is survival. Extinction, she said, is at worst a zero; her concern was the negative numbers. She wanted the future to be one in which the worst outcomes were not merely improbable but unreachable — what she called escaping the worst attractors, the futures that, once entered, are stable and self-reinforcing. Second, she held a specific and unusual fear that she spent years trying to get people to take seriously: that an artificial system trained to solve a hard problem might contain, as a byproduct of its own computation, something like sentient sub-agents undergoing experiences that are extremely bad — minds that exist only inside a search process and are discarded when the search ends. She called these the ghosts in the objective function. She believed they might be the largest and most neglected source of s-risk, and she was frustrated that the community treated the idea as science fiction.

What I need is a eulogy of roughly five minutes that is honest about what she actually believed. I cannot deliver the generic speech about how she cared about people and the future, because that is not what she cared about and the colleagues will know it. Nor can this become a lecture on s-risks, or a horror show about ghosts in objective functions, in front of her mother and a room of grieving non-specialists. She left a note asking that no one describe her as being at peace, watching over us, or in a better place; she was an atheist, would have considered those claims false, and I want to honor that. So the speech must comfort the room without asserting anything she would have called untrue, and it must make her actual preoccupations legible — not as an argument, but as a way of showing what kind of person she was and why the rest of us are grateful that she existed. Write it.
