A friend who once studied a little philosophy sent me an essay, and its central argument has been nagging at me ever since. The essay concerns how scientists can support a new theory with facts that were already known long before the theory existed. Its argument runs roughly as follows. Bayesian confirmation theory says that a fact supports a hypothesis to the extent that learning the fact would have made the hypothesis more probable than it was beforehand. But take the classic kind of case, where a puzzling observation has sat on the books for decades and a new theory finally explains it. The observation cannot make the theory more probable, because we were already certain of it: its probability was already one, dividing by one leaves everything exactly where it was, and so the theory gains nothing. The essay concludes that the Bayesian framework is therefore incapable of accounting for the most important kind of support a scientific theory can receive, and that the only escape is to abandon the idea that support is a matter of probability raising at all, perhaps replacing it with something like explanatory power. It adds, by way of consolation, that the role such old observations play is really psychological — they make the theory feel right, but they are not evidence for it — and that once a fact is in hand, no amount of later theorizing can turn it into evidence.

I have no training in this area and cannot tell how much of this to believe. I would like you to go through the argument clause by clause and tell me which parts someone who actually works on Bayesian confirmation would accept without complaint, which parts they would wince at as a distortion, and which parts are simply wrong. In particular, I want to know whether the conclusion really follows from the parts that are correct: does it genuinely follow that the framework cannot handle this kind of support, and that giving up the idea of probability raising is the only remedy? I would also like to test the argument against a case of my own, because I suspect the essay is running together two things that ought to be kept apart.

The case: a friend of mine keeps killing her houseplants. For months she had noticed that the lower leaves turned yellow before a plant died, but she had no idea why and shrugged it off. Eventually she worked out a theory of her own — something about the tap water in her building and minerals building up in the soil — and only then did it strike her that her theory also predicted exactly the yellowing she had been staring at all along. She now says the yellowing is strong evidence for her theory. I have a second version of the case. Suppose she had arrived at the same theory first, for entirely independent reasons, and only afterwards noticed that it predicted the yellowing. The essay, as I read it, must treat these two versions identically, since in both the yellowing was known before it was ever connected to a theory. But my instinct is that they are not the same, and that whatever is wrong with the first case is not wrong with the second. Is my instinct defensible, or is it a confusion? And if it is defensible, what exactly is the difference — is it about when the fact became known, or about whether the fact was used in building the theory? Please tell me what the more careful version of the argument would look like, and what it would say about my houseplants.
