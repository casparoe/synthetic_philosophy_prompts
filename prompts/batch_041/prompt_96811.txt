Next month I'm giving a paper at a Kierkegaard conference. The audiance is mostly philologists and theologians from a Kierkegaard research centre, a couple of them have spent their whole careers on the pseudonyms and the problem of indirect communication, there's one Hegel person, and the organisers have invited two media studies people as respondents, which is either great or a trap.

My thesis: Kierkegaard's mid-1840s attack on the press isn't really a moral complaint about gossip or a rant about journalists. It's a structural point — a medium that lets anyone address everyone, anonymously and without being answerable for it, manufactures a public that exists as an abstraction but never as anyone who can be held to anything, and that dissolves the conditions for responsibility. I want to argue that the modern heir of this isn't the newspaper but the algorithmic feed, which industrialises the same levelling. My sharper and more dangerous claim is that his critique therefore bites hardest on the most personalised, participatory platforms, not on tabloids.

Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this room will actually make, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would phrase them, with how much damage each one does, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply to each. Flag where a reply is soft. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — I think I know what it is but I want to be sure.

Things to keep in mind: they're allergic to anything that smells like presentism; the pseudonym people will not let me attribute a view to Kierkegaard without pushing on who exactly is speaking and whether the authorship even permits that move; the Hegel person will want to know what work the category of the public is doing that the Phenomenology didn't already do; and the media studies people will come at me empirically, since real feeds fragment and polarise rather than homogenise, and the Corsair buisness will probably come up as a gotcha about him being perfectly happy to use the press when it suited him. I don't want a reading list, just the prep sheet.
