I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in epistemology, and until about six weeks ago the material on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism was, for me, a slightly bloodless set of distinctions I could teach without much investment. Then I spent a fortnight on the receiving end of a workplace investigation, and I watched — in myself and in the colleagues around me — the same bundle of evidence that I would unhesitatingly have called knowledge in a low-stakes setting (is this citation sloppy, is this record-keeping adequate?) curdle into something none of us would have described as knowledge once the question became whether someone's job would survive it. Nobody became stupider or more cautious in any ordinary sense, and no new evidence arrived. What changed was what was riding on the answer. I would like my students to feel that happen in their own mouths, before they hear any of the labels.

So I am asking you to act as an experienced teacher of epistemology and a designer of classroom simulations — someone who has run games with real (small) stakes and knows the specific ways they fall apart. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short scratchpad in which you work out what the activity must accomplish and what could plausibly go wrong with each design choice you consider; only then give me the finished design.

The session is 90 minutes, 14 students, a seminar room with movable chairs. The two views I want them to have run into by the end are these, and I want them stated in my students' language rather than the literature's:

1. Contextualism: whether a knowledge attribution is true depends on the context of whoever is doing the attributing — roughly, on how salient error possibilities are to that attributor — so the same person with the same evidence can be correctly described as knowing in one conversation and as not knowing in another.
2. Pragmatic encroachment (or subject-sensitive invariantism): whether the subject knows depends on her practical stakes — on how much turns, for her, on whether the thing is true — so a subject can lose or gain knowledge when her stakes change while her evidence and the attributor's context stay fixed.

The crux I want them to collide with is that both views predict the same surface pattern, namely verdicts drifting with stakes, and that the only way to prise them apart is to move two variables independently: the stakes for the person who has to act on the evidence, and the stakes for the person asked whether she knows. If the activity can be built so that those two move separately, so much the better. If you judge that cannot be done with real participants in 90 minutes, say why and design the closest thing that can.

What I need from you:

- Roles, rules, and materials. Assume photocopying and a bag of small tokens; nothing that needs money, devices, or software.
- A stakes scheme in points, tokens, or privileges rather than cash, calibrated so that the high-stakes condition genuinely bites without making anyone miserable or resentful.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, including a point at which verdicts are written down individually before anyone can talk, so that we have data rather than a consensus we manufactured.
- A debrief keyed to particular moments: which thing that happened is what contextualism explains, which is what encroachment explains, and what each view would have predicted that we did not observe. The students should do the naming and the arguing; I will supply the labels only after they have committed themselves.
- Your best guess at what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the ways the stakes manipulation will fail to bite, the ways participants will game the rules, the ways the result will be demand characteristics rather than the phenomenon — and what I should do in the moment when each of those happens.
- A frank note on what this activity cannot reproduce, that is, what I will have to say in my own voice because no amount of moving chairs around the room will deliver it.

One constraint on sources: no reading list, no citations, and no named or dated experimental findings. If something from the empirical literature bears on a design choice, describe it in plain language and let me take it on trust.
