You are a philosopher of mathematics with a strong grounding in early modern philosophy and the history of geometry. I would like you to think carefully before answering, and to begin by spending a few lines brainstorming the different ways Hobbes's account of geometric knowledge could bear on a work of fiction, before committing to the most defensible line and developing it fully.

I am preparing a seminar session that pairs Thomas Hobbes's philosophy of geometry with Edwin Abbott Abbott's Flatland, the Victorian novella narrated by A Square, a being who lives in a two-dimensional world and is visited by a Sphere. Hobbes treated geometry as the one branch of knowledge whose certainty is secure, and he located that certainty in construction: we can know the objects of geometry, he thought, because we make them ourselves, and its definitions are ours to lay down by fiat rather than discoveries about a mind-independent realm. He also held that reasoning just is computation with words, and he insisted that philosophy as a whole should proceed by the geometric method. He is also remembered for the embarrassing fact that he spent years convinced he had solved the ancient problems, squaring the circle above all, and for the long public quarrel with a professional mathematician who demonstrated that he had not.

Read the novella through Hobbes's philosophy of geometry. What would Hobbes's constructivism predict about whether a Flatlander could ever genuinely possess geometric knowledge of three dimensions, as opposed to mere faith or hearsay? Does the Sphere's failure to do more than slice, analogize, and display vindicate Hobbes's account of how geometric knowledge is acquired, or does it expose a limit of that account? Where does the novella dramatize Hobbes's nominalism about definitions — the thought that definitions are laid down by whoever holds the authority to make them — and where does it push back, intimating that some structures are not ours to define but constrain us whether we like it or not? I am especially interested in the political layer: Flatland's authorities police what may be thought and taught, and Hobbes's treatment of definition and construction as human acts can be read as making geometric truth a matter of authorized making rather than discovery. Explore whether the novella's satire of intellectual conformity cuts against Hobbes or confirms something he half-acknowledged.

Be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the novella to an illustration of Hobbes, and do not bend Hobbes to fit the story. Tell me plainly where the reading strains and where the honest critic has to concede that the text resists it. End by saying what the novella adds to the philosophical debate about geometric knowledge — what it makes vivid or troubling that Hobbes's prose does not — and give me two or three particular scenes or episodes I should foreground when I teach it. Do not quote from either text; describe them in your own words.
