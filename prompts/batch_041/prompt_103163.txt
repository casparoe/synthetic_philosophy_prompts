I help run a local history reading group, and I have no academic training in the subject — everything I know about the period labels comes from the popular histories we read together and from the plaques in museums. The more of them I read, the less I trust the labels themselves. The stretch we call the Middle Ages appears to have been named by people who came later and wanted some distance from it; the Renaissance seems to mean quite different things in different countries; and I am told the Scientific Revolution was not called that until long after it was over. What I cannot work out is what follows from any of this. When my group argues about whether a boundary such as 1500 is drawn in the right place, are we arguing about a fact, about a convention, or about a story someone is telling? I would like a quiz I can hand round so that people discover which view they actually hold, rather than which view sounds most respectable at the table.

Please write it as a "which theory of historical periods do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case rather than an invitation to pick a school of thought — a date that falls on different sides of a boundary in different regional histories; a single person's life that straddles a supposed turning point; a gallery a museum has to re-label when the fashion changes; a century that one historian files under one period and another under the next — with two or three answer options apiece. None of the questions may be answerable by reaching for a label; each must sort people by what they say about the case. Follow the questions with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions, and give each position a plain-English name and one sentence describing the commitment its holder would find most awkward to defend.

I want the following positions to be reachable as outcomes: (1) the periods are real joints in history, discovered rather than invented, and the familiar three-part scheme is roughly right; (2) the periods are useful tools or coordinates, true only in a derivative sense, to be kept or discarded by how well they work; (3) there is no single correct periodization, only many, each true relative to some purpose or interest; (4) there are no periods at all, and period-talk is at best shorthand for dated events that ought to be dropped; (5) the period terms are primarily value-laden labels that encode a narrative of progress or decline, and their real work is rhetorical; and (6) a hybrid in which the changes in the world are real but the divisions between them are made. If any further position strikes you as occupying a live part of the space, include it; if some combination of answers leads nowhere, say so and explain whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Three distinctions matter most for my purposes, and I would like the questions to probe them rather than to wander: whether period terms answer to something mind-independent or to our interests; whether the standard three-part scheme is the right one or merely one option among many; and whether the terms are descriptive or evaluative. Build the instrument from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then place the positions in it. Where two positions cannot be separated by any pattern of answers, tell me plainly instead of papering over the gap.

The audience is a general-interest reading group with no specialist vocabulary, so keep the language free of jargon, and keep the whole thing to a length one person could complete in ten minutes. End the entire response with a single final line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the one distinction that does the most work in the quiz.
