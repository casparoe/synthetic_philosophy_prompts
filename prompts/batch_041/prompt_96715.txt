I'm interviewing a historian of science next week for my podcast -- he's written a book arguing that Newton's anti-Trinitarian theology isn't a private hobby Newton kept in a drawer, it's actually doing work in the physics -- and I've just spent three hours arguing with my friend Dan about whether that's even coherent, so I need you to help me turn the mess in my head into questions.

Dan's position, which he thinks is obvious: Newton needed absolute space for the spinning bucket and the rotation argument, he needed laws that hold everywhere, and the theology gets bolted on afterwards. Exhibit A is that the General Scholium is an appendix to a maths book. Exhibit B is that Newton kept the heresy secret and the published physics never needs it -- if a commitment has to be hidden from everyone who'd object to it, says Dan, then it isn't doing any work in the mechanics, it's wallpaper. My position, which I definately could not defend at 1am, is that you can't seperate them: the whole point of Newton's God is that he's a free Lord who acts in the world, not a necessary emanation and not the soul of the world, and that shows up in what the physics refuses to do.

The guest's version, as best I can reconstruct it from the book: (a) because God's will is free rather than necessitated, the laws of motion are contingent decrees and can't be derived a priori, which is why he treats them as inferred from the phenomena; (b) absolute space and time are where that free, omnipresent God is -- hence the comparison of infinite space to God's sensory, and the insistence that he's present substantially and not merely virtually; (c) a God who acts freely can go on acting, so the Leibniz complaint that Newton's God has to wind the watch up again is a feature and not a bug; (d) same reason Newton won't feign a hypothesis about the cause of gravity -- the cause is the free agent, not a mechanism; (e) the textual criticism and the ancient-wisdom stuff have the same shape: a pure original truth, in scripture and in the earliest philosophy, later corrupted by the Roman church and by scholastic and Cartesian system-building, so his job is restoration rather than invention.

I want nine or ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give -- he filibusters, so the follow-up has to be sharp enough to cut in. At least one question on each of the things below, and none of them allowed to turn into 'tell us about your book':

- the contingency problem: if the laws are God's free decrees, how does Newton get to treat them as mathematically universal and the Principia as a demonstration? And since Clarke, writing with Newton's blessing, insists that God's will always follows the best reasons, is Newton's voluntarism even strong enough to carry the weight this thesis puts on it?
- the presence problem: how do you get space as the divine sensory without the soul-of-the-world view Newton explicitly denies? What does 'substantially, not merely virtually' add, and does an extended God have parts?
- Dan's problem: if the theology is load-bearing, why does the published physics not need it, and why is the General Scholium so careful never to say the word Trinity? What would distinguish load-bearing from decorative here, and is there anything in the archive that could settle it -- or is the thesis built so that nothing could?
- the philology: he argued that the clause in 1 John that gives you the three witnesses in heaven was a later insertion, and that in the Timothy passage 'who' got changed into 'God'. How does a textual result like that bear on the mechanics at all -- same instinct, or the same argument? And why did he go after Athanasius's character and career rather than just the doctrine? Does where a belief came from have any bearing on whether it's true?
- the ancients: if the true philosophy was ancient, why present the Principia as new mathematics with new laws, and why not cite the ancients as authorities? If the answer is that they had the substance and not the demonstrations, is that a real historical claim or a way of having it both ways?
- the 'subtle spirit' paragraph at the very end of the General Scholium: theological move or physical one, and doesn't it quietly undercut the no-hypotheses discipline?
- what would count against him. I want the last question to make him say what evidence would make him drop the thesis, and I want the follow-up already loaded for the answer 'nothing, because the theology is the background'.

Don't bother with a reading list or citations, I'm not putting them on the show.
