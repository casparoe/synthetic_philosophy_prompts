My flatmate and I have a running arguement that restarts every time one of us reads Dr Seuss's The Lorax to his little cousin, and it's got to the point where I want it settled properly. I'm on the side that says the book is basically propoganda for the intrinsic value of ecosystems, and he — who's been mainlining the wild-animal-suffering literature, the line that most wild lives contain far more suffering than contentment — insists the book has it all backwards. So I'd like a real reading of The Lorax through that debate: the net welfare of ecosystems, and whether habitat destruction actually reduces or increases wild animal suffering.

The obvious move is that the Once-ler's clear-felling is a straightforward harm and the Lorax is there to speak for the trees because they can't speak for themselves. But I want the rival view taken seriously. If the Brown Bar-ba-loots and the Swomee-Swans and the Humming-Fish led lives of net misery — cold, hunger, constant predation, no relief — then the loss of the Truffula forest mightn't be the unalloyed tragedy the book assumes it is. My flatmate keeps bringing up some thought experiment where you'd be permitted to wipe out a habitat if doing so reduced the total amount of suffering, and I can never tell if it's monstrous or just consistent. So: where does the book's moral quietly depend on an unstated premise that wild lives go well on balance? Does Dr Seuss ever actually show us a happy animal, or only animals we're invited to read as happy because they're cute? And where does the story push back — is there something in it the welfare arithmetic simply misses, about place, or belonging, or the fact that the Once-ler destroys the forest for profit rather than for the animals' sake?

I don't want this collapsing into either 'nature is good, so the book is right' or 'nature is a slaughterhouse, burn it down'. I want a reading that's faithful in both directions, that says where the idea illuminates the story and where the story resists the idea, and that ends by saying what The Lorax adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone doesn't — including whether a picture book can even hold the thought that a habitat might be bad for the creatures living in it. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'm not writing an essay, I just want it worked through against the actual scenes.
