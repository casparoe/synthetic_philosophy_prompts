Two nights ago I lost an argument with a friend of mine who edits a dictionary for a living. I said that plenty of perfectly good words have no definition at all, only a web of overlapping similarities, and that this is not a defect in them but how language mostly works; he said that this is vagueness dressed up as philosophy, that a competent lexicographer can write a definition for any word worth defining, and that "family resemblance" is what people reach for when they cannot. We went around it for hours, and I kept invoking Wittgenstein and kept not quite landing him, which is why I am writing to you. Next month I have to run a ninety-minute session in a public library discussion series — eight or so adults, no philosophy background, no advance reading (they will not do it), one retired patent attorney who will demand a test he can apply and one ceramicist who will distrust any test on principle — and I have decided to do language games and family resemblance together. Do not explain the material to me. I know it well enough to be dangerous and badly enough to have lost to a lexicographer. Plan how I should teach it.

What I need is the order the ideas have to come in, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before the next thing can even be heard, and where the temptation to say something false — or something Wittgenstein would disown — is greatest. Tell me which single example should carry the whole session, what it must have and what it must lack, and if you conclude that it has to be games after all, say so and tell me what the obvious choice costs us and how to keep the room from hearing "so there are no definitions, anything goes." I expect a specific set of misconceptions, roughly in the order they will surface. My guess is that the first is "family resemblance means concepts are just vague," and that the second is "a language game is a game played with words," but I want the ones I have not thought of, including whatever they will form about Wittgenstein himself, and for each one exactly where in the sequence I should head it off rather than saving it for the end. I also want a closing question I can put to the room that would tell me whether the idea actually landed, as opposed to whether I got nods.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say out loud that I am leaving it out — I would rather name the thing I am skipping than have the patent attorney discover it and conclude I do not know it. Write it in American English, and give it to me as a plan I can run with a whiteboard, not as an essay I would have to translate into one.
