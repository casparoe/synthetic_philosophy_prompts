Take the 2000 United States presidential election in Florida and analyze the decision faced by left-leaning voters there through the ethics of voting. The facts are common ground, so I will fix them at the outset: Ralph Nader, the Green candidate, drew 97,488 votes in Florida; George Bush carried the state over Al Gore by 537 votes out of 5,963,110 cast; and Florida's 25 electoral votes decided the presidency. I do not want the spoiler narrative retold. What I want is a disciplined mapping of that decision onto the competing accounts of what a vote is and what it is for.

Set out the principal positions: the instrumental account, on which a vote is a causal means to an outcome and its justification lies in its expected effect; the expressive account, on which a vote is a statement, an act of self-authorship, or a way of standing with some people against others, and is assessed by what it expresses rather than what it produces; and the accounts that locate a duty to vote in something other than consequences — in a civic role, in a debt owed to those who secured the franchise, or in a duty not to free-ride on the participation of others. For each, say what the Nader voter faced and what the lesser-evil argument looks like from inside it. Then press on the one place where the arguments tend to go soft. The probability that any single vote is pivotal in a contest of nearly six million is small enough that the instrumental case for voting for the lesser evil cannot be carried by that route at all; state what the instrumentalist must say instead — a rule-based or threshold or assurance-game reconstruction, perhaps — and whether that reconstruction is still recognizably instrumental. And ask whether the expressive voter can be criticized on expressive grounds without covertly importing the instrumental standard the critic claims to reject.

Then reverse the lens. The episode is standardly used as a morality tale about wasted votes, but does it actually support the ethics of voting, or does it present a case the theory never anticipated? Note that the counterfactual on which the whole discourse depends — how Nader voters would have voted, or whether they would have voted at all, had Nader not been on the ballot — is genuinely contested and probably unknowable in the aggregate, yet the moralizing runs ahead of it. Say what it does to the analysis if that counterfactual cannot be had, and whether an ethics of voting that must remain agnostic about it can still say anything about what those particular voters should have done. I am more interested in where the theory falls silent than in where it can be made to speak, so be explicit about which of the accounts survives the case, which is embarrassed by it, and which turns out to have been answering a different question than the one the episode poses.
