Assume the role of a philosopher of language and of public memory who also writes speeches for a living — someone equally at home in J. L. Austin and in the practical business of holding a restive public audience for seven minutes. I need you to draft the dedication remarks I will deliver at a small ceremony next month, and I want you to think hard about the conceptual architecture before you write a single line of it.

Here is the situation. My city removed a bronze statue of a nineteenth-century merchant-benefactor from the plaza in front of the old library — he endowed the library, and he also made a great deal of money in a trade that ruined a lot of people, which almost nobody mentioned for a hundred years and then suddenly everybody did. After two years of hearings, the commission I sit on decided not to replace him with a new hero and not to leave the plinth empty. Instead we commissioned a work that is deliberately not a statue and not a plaque: a low granite bench, cut through with a single question, addressed to whoever sits on it. I will be giving the dedication remarks at the unveiling. The audience will be genuinely divided — some of my neighbors wanted the old bronze back, some think we should have left the space bare, some are furious that we spent money on stone instead of on the library roof, and a few are here mostly for the free coffee. I was on the losing side of one of our votes: I argued for keeping the original figure in place with a caption that told the whole story, and I lost. So the speech has to dedicate, sincerely and without hedging, a thing I partly opposed, to a crowd that contains people who think I got that wrong in both directions.

The philosophy has to do real structural work here, not decorate the remarks. In our deliberations I kept reaching for a three-way distinction, and I want the speech to carry it without turning into a lecture: *honorific* commemoration, which asserts a proposition about someone's worth and asks the passerby to assent; *didactic* commemoration, which asserts a proposition about history and asks the passerby to learn; and a third thing I have been calling *interrogative* commemoration, which asserts as little as it can and instead puts a question into a public space and holds it there, so that the burden of answering falls on whoever walks past. Two further ideas should press on the speech from underneath. The first is that a monument's real addressee is never the person commemorated and rarely the people standing at the unveiling — it is the stranger who will pass this spot in fifty years without knowing any of us, which means my remarks are addressed over the heads of the room. The second, which one of my colleagues pressed hard and which I have not been able to shake, is that a monument is a promise a political community makes to its future self, and that promises of this kind can be renegotiated in public but cannot be quietly abandoned — which is awkward, because by that account we have just broken one, and the ceremony is where we are supposed to say so out loud. I want the tension between those two ideas to be visible in the speech rather than resolved away, because I think that is the honest position and I think the room can feel the difference between honesty and a smooth finish.

Before you write, spend some time thinking through the following and show me your reasoning briefly: which of these ideas can actually bear the weight of a seven-minute speech and which will collapse under it; what the speech is permitted to say once we take seriously that it is addressed to people not yet born; and how a speaker dedicates a monument he voted against without either apologizing for the monument or pretending he has changed his mind. Then write the remarks themselves. Keep them to roughly seven minutes spoken aloud, in a register that a mixed crowd of neighbors will follow without ever feeling talked down to, and with at least one moment of plain, unadorned speech that would survive being remembered by someone who forgot everything else. Do not cite any texts, authors, or dates, and do not put any borrowed phrasing in quotation marks — if you want to invoke an idea, put it in your own words. The question carved into the bench is yours to compose, but it should be short enough to read at a glance and hard enough that a person might sit down to think about it.
