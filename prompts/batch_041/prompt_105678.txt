I'm a graduate student working at the intersection of decision theory and regulatory policy, and a puzzle from a recent seminar has been nagging at me. Consider how environmental and public-health regulators handle small probabilities of serious harm. In one mode, they set thresholds of acceptable risk: an exposure level, or a probability of a fatal cancer, below which they treat the risk as effectively zero and license the substance or activity without further ado. In a second mode, the same kind of agency, and the treaties and statutes it answers to, invokes a precautionary principle: where there is evidence of a possible serious or irreversible harm, it restricts the substance or activity even though the probability is small and the evidence is inconclusive, and it places the burden on the proponent to demonstrate safety rather than on the regulator to demonstrate harm. In a third mode, these bodies run cost-benefit analyses in which harms and lives are valued in money and a small probability is simply multiplied by the magnitude of the harm to yield an expected cost. So one institution treats a small probability as negligible in one place, as an overriding reason to act in another, and as a number to be multiplied and compared in a third.

I'd like you to treat these as practices and ask what philosophical commitments they presuppose — in particular, what conception of rational choice under risk each one only makes sense under. Does the threshold practice commit itself to treating probabilities below some cutoff as genuinely negligible, and if so is that a claim about the world or a decision rule? Does the precautionary practice presuppose that a small probability of catastrophe deserves more weight than a straight expected-value calculation would assign it, or that certain harms must not be traded off against benefits at all (a maximin or lexicographic rule), or merely that the burden of proof should shift? And can a single coherent view — for instance one on which we maximize not expected utility but a risk-weighted expected utility, where the probabilities attached to outcomes are themselves transformed before being weighted by utility — accommodate all three faces, or does the institution hold commitments that cannot all be true at once? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, and say where the practice is underdetermined, so that several rival views would license it.

Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Let the items cover, in order: the conception of rational risk-taking that the threshold practice presupposes; the conception the precautionary practice presupposes; which rival views each of these rules out and where each is underdetermined; whether the commitments of the three practices are mutually consistent; and what would have to change in the practices if the presupposed view were replaced. Within the fifth item, state how confident you are in your overall reconstruction and identify the single point you are least sure of.
