I have no training in philosophy or in medicine, and I've been reading about pain on my own for about a year now. I've reached the point where I don't want another view about why pain is bad; I want to know what any such view would have to get right before I take it seriously. So what I'm after is a specification rather than a survey — the requirements a theory of the badness of pain has to satisfy to be adequate for my purposes, which are practical. I want to know how much weight to give a report of pain when I have to decide whether to believe someone, what to do about a patient who says the pain is unbearable while behaving as if it isn't, and whether to treat an animal's limping as evidence that something is bad for it.

The material that has to be accounted for includes the following.

- The clinical phenomenon that gives the area its name: after damage to certain parts of the brain, people say they can feel the pain — they can say where it is, how sharp it is, how strong it is — but that it doesn't bother them at all. They don't pull away, they don't ask for relief, and some of them show no distress whatever. I gather the mirror image has also been reported, where something is felt as thoroughly unpleasant but the person won't call it pain and can't place it anywhere in the body.
- A case I've been imagining: a drug that leaves the pain entirely intact — same intensity, same location, same quality, and the person's ability to tell one noxious stimulus from another is undiminished — while removing only the unpleasantness. The person is articulate and otherwise normal and describes the pain as just information, like a message arriving. Nothing hurts, in the sense they mean it, although everything else about the pain is exactly as before. Is their pain bad? Must the theory answer that, or is it permitted to call the question confused?
- A second imagined case: a chronic pain, intense and unpleasant, that the person would not accept a pill to be rid of, because they have built a life around it and say it has become part of them. This isn't a case of not feeling it; it's a case of feeling it fully and not wanting it gone. I can't tell whether that shows the pain isn't bad for them, or only that people can want what is bad for them.
- A third: someone reporting agony in a flat voice with a steady pulse, who doesn't avoid the thing causing it and sleeps fine; and beside them someone reporting a mild ache while sweating, shaking, and unable to sit still. When report, behaviour, and body disagree about how bad a pain is, the theory should tell me which of them is the measure, or why the question is misconceived.
- A fourth: an animal that limps and guards a limb, of a kind whose nervous system I'm told lacks whatever the human cases show is needed for the unpleasantness. I don't need the theory to settle what the animal feels. I do want to know whether it must tell me whether anything is bad there, and whether it's allowed to stay silent.
- The ordinary facts: the same injury hurting one person far more than another; attention, fear, and expectation changing pain; a wound going unnoticed in an emergency and becoming unbearable in a hospital bed; drugs that dull the unpleasantness while leaving the sensation alone, and others that do the reverse.

What I want, then:

1. The requirements list itself — what a theory of pain's badness must deliver, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the facts it must be consistent with — each stated as a requirement with the case or consideration that puts it there. Please state each one in a way that doesn't already presuppose an answer; I don't want a requirement reading that pain must be bad because of how it feels, since that is the very thing in dispute.
2. Which of the requirements are genuine and which are simply what one camp happens to want — for instance, whether the demand that badness be tied to how something feels, or instead to whether the person minds it, is a requirement or a prejudice.
3. Any pair or set of requirements that cannot be met together. This is the part I most want. If the list as stated rules out every possible theory, I would rather be told that than handed a theory.
4. Which single requirement does the most to eliminate the views people actually hold, and why that one rather than another.
5. Which requirements are negotiable if I change what I need the theory for — if I cared only about medical practice, or only about how to treat animals — and which would remain no matter what.

I would rather have this than a summary of positions, and I don't want names, dates, or a reading list: I can't get to a library and I won't be able to check anyone's work, so anything resting on my trusting an attribution is useless to me. A table is fine for the requirements if it keeps things compact, as long as the case behind each requirement is still visible.
