I chair the scientific advisory committee of a national environmental regulator, and roughly twice a month we must answer petitions asking us to restrict a chemical, a product, or a practice on precautionary grounds. Our committee of nine spans toxicology, ecology, statistics, and law; we have sixty days from docketing to a written recommendation; our text is read by a minister who wants a defensible decision and by a court that will ask whether we applied our own framework consistently. We may require labeling, monitoring, phased reduction, or conditional approval with triggers, but we cannot prohibit a lawful product outright. What we lack is a procedure. The statute authorizes us to act when there is reason to believe that harm is possible, and nine of us mean nine different things by that. I know the familiar objections — that the principle is vacuous, that it is incoherent, that it paralyzes, that cost-benefit analysis smuggles in values of its own — and I do not want them re-litigated. I want something we can run, and I do not need references or a literature review.

Write the procedure: a sequence of questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the matter to the minister because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. Three requirements must be built in. First, sort each petition into one of three uncertainty types and let the type drive the rest — uncertainty about magnitude (the hazard is agreed to exist at some exposure; what is unknown is whether real-world exposure reaches it), uncertainty about existence (a plausible mechanism and one or two signals, no established hazard in any species), and uncertainty about the model (the agent can alter the system it acts on, so the doubt lies in the class of models rather than in a parameter). Second, require every restriction we recommend to carry an unwinding condition — the observation that would justify lifting it, specified in a form we could actually collect — and a review date, so that precaution does not ratchet. Third, force a substitution check and a status-quo check: for any restriction, name the substitute or the displaced activity and the risk that moves there, and state whether the status quo would pass the standard we are about to apply to the new thing.

Then run it end to end on three petitions: a widely used insecticide whose pollinator effects at field-realistic exposure are contested; a plasticizer with a ready substitute that is itself under suspicion; and a third whose only evidence is a plausible mechanism plus a single high-dose animal study with no identified exposure pathway, so that the procedure, honestly run, tells us to refuse a petition the petitioners regard as the paradigm case for precaution. Finish by naming where the procedure misfires — which steps are judgment dressed as procedure, what it cannot do at all, and how it should handle the fact that the harms our restrictions prevent are unobservable, so that the only side of the ledger the procedure can ever measure is the cost.
