I have no formal training in either biology or philosophy. I run a small reading group out of our public library — the members are intelligent adults, none of them specialists — and we have just spent three evenings on the critique of adaptationist thinking that comes out of the Gould and Lewontin essay on the spandrels of San Marco, along with a couple of the replies from biologists and philosophers who felt the critique went too far. I would like to set the group a written exercise for our next session, and I need help building it properly, because I have never set or marked anything in my life.

Here is why I want the exercise and where I got stuck. The critique is always summarised for us as the observation that not every feature of an organism is an adaptation, and that summary has never seemed to me to be the interesting part. The interesting part seemed to be something about what would even count as evidence for an adaptation story, and about whether a living thing can be carved into separate features in the first place, each with its own story attached. I want the exercise to force that distinction instead of letting people repeat the summary back at me.

What I need from you: a single essay question, with parts if you think that helps, answerable by a thoughtful non-specialist in about seventy-five minutes; a model answer of roughly 700 words; and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks, including a note on what separates an A answer from a B answer and, for each point, a one-line note on how people typically get it wrong. No citations are wanted. The group has no library access and I do not want them guessing at names, dates and titles, so nothing in the question or the model answer should depend on those.

Please build the question so that:

- It cannot be answered by saying that some features are byproducts rather than adaptations, or by reciting the complaint that adaptationist explanations are merely plausible stories. Both of those should be treated as the beginning of an answer, not the answer.
- It requires the group to separate at least three distinct claims that the critique could be making — for instance, a claim about which features exist and how they arose, a claim about how the research programme is actually conducted and whether it could ever be refuted, and a claim about whether the organism can be decomposed into independent traits at all. I want them to say which of these the spandrel example genuinely supports and which of them is the strongest, and to say what someone defending adaptationist reasoning would have to concede for the critique to be answered rather than deflected.
- It includes a second part that applies the same machinery to a case they cannot have memorised. Here is a case I have invented and would like you to use as it stands, without swapping in a famous example: the covered market in our town is carried on a row of stone arches, and wherever two arches meet there is a triangular panel of stonework that nobody drew on purpose, since it simply falls out of how the arches and the roof have to meet. When the market was restored in the 1890s the traders began hanging their painted trade signs in those panels, and today the panels are what visitors come to see: there is a waiting list for them, a rule about who is entitled to one, and a size limit that is fixed by the shape of the panel rather than chosen by anyone. Our guidebook says the panels were designed for the signs.

I want the second part to ask whether the signs are an adaptation, a byproduct, or something that was later taken over for a new purpose, what would settle that question, and — this is the part I am most keen on — where the comparison between the building and an organism breaks down, since I suspect that is where most of the marks belong and where most answers will be lazy.

Please keep the whole thing in plain language. Where a technical word is unavoidable, introduce it and explain it, since the group are readers without specialist vocabulary.
