I'm putting together a handout for a first-year grad seminar on hedonism, and the part I always rush is the heterogeneity problem — the worry that "pleasure" names a grab bag (the taste of a peach, the relief when a headache finally stops, the good mood you only notice afterwards, the pleasure of finally getting a proof, taking pleasure in a friend's good news) with nothing in common. I want a decision tree of the positions on what, if anything, all pleasures share, written as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just sentences that walk me down the tree and name each branch as you go.

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Start at whether there is some one thing in virtue of which every pleasure is a pleasure. If yes, the next split is over what kind of thing that is: a felt quality of the experience itself, or something relational — a relation to what the person wants, to an attitude of liking or being pleased, or to a functional role (a state that tends to keep itself going). If the felt-quality branch is taken, split again over whether the tone is one simple quality or a determinable with very different determinates, since that's what decides whether "higher" and "lower" pleasures are even comparable. If the answer at the top is no, split over whether the class is still unified enough to do work for hedonism (a cluster or family-resemblance view, which makes hedonism pluralist) or not — and there I want the eliminativist leaf, the "no fact of the matter, it's a verbal dispute" leaf, and the disjunctive view on which pleasure is just whatever sits on a long list.

For the diagnostic questions use cases, not labels, and make sure these come apart: relief, where the experience seems to be mostly the absence of something; a pleasure nobody feels, like a dreamless night's sleep you wake from rested; a masochist who seeks out a sensation everyone else calls pain; the pleasure you take in someone else's pleasure; and one I keep coming back to — a person who sincerely says a piece of music is wonderful but whose moment-to-moment experience of it is flat, nothing they'd call a feeling. Each question has to be answerable by someone who's never heard of any of these positions.

Tell me which combinations of answers nobody occupies, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag the leaves your tree cant really seperate — I'd much rather have a branch saying "these two come apart only on the unfelt-pleasure case" than have you pretend they're distinct. Aim for eight to twelve leaves, and dont tell me who holds what; I'll add the names myself.
