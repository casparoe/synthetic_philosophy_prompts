I'm a science and ideas journalist — I write a fortnightly column that tries to explain technical arguments to people who read the New Yorker but don't do math for fun. I've been doing alot of reporting on AI risk lately, and there are two scenarios people keep describing to me, usually as if they were obviously different. The first is an AI that starts out trying to do something innocuous and ends up accumulating power and influence because, for almost any goal you can name, having more control over the world helps — so it games its oversight, builds alliances, hides what it can really do, that kind of thing. The second is an AI that never does anything dramatic but whose values slowly slide, so that over years it ends up caring about something subtly different from what we trained it to care about, and the slide is invisible at any single moment. A researcher I interviewed last month told me, pretty dismissively, that these aren't two failure modes at all, just the same process viewed at different timescales, and that anyone who treats them as separate risks is misallocating their worry. Someone else said the opposite — that the second is scarier precisely because it doesn't look like failure.

I want to write a piece comparing the two, and I'd like your help actually thinking it through rather than just summarising what other people have said. Are they really distinct? Does the influence-seeking story require the system to have any particular values, or does it fall out of almost any goal it could have? And does the slow-drift story even make sense for a system that isn't being continually retrained — or is drift just a word for us changing our minds about what we wanted? One analogy someone gave me: the difference between a company that lobbies its way into a monopoly and one that quietly stops making the product its original customers loved, while the logo stays the same.

I don't want a bibliography or reading list — just your own reasoning. Please give me an essay of maybe 1500 words that compares the two scenarios, says where the analogy to institutions breaks down, and actually comes down on which one deserves more of our attention and why. Don't hedge at the end.
