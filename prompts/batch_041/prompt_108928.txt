Two philosophers have argued for years about what a transcendental argument can accomplish. The ambitious one holds that a successful transcendental argument can establish how things must be, not merely how we must think of them — enough to refute the skeptic about the external world, other minds, or the past. The modest one holds that such arguments establish at most what we must believe or how we must experience, and so never cross the gap from our necessary commitments to reality. Write the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Include: the claims both accept about the form of a transcendental argument and about which classic instances achieve anything at all; the precise remaining disagreement, with each side's position worded so that side would accept the wording; and the cruxes — I expect one to be the belief-to-reality gap, and another to be whether the argument must lean on a verificationist premise about what could count as experience. Work in the related disputes over whether such arguments are question-begging and whether they can be addressed to an opponent's conceptual scheme rather than only to the arguer's own. For each crux, give a test, argument, or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, and state where the dispute is merely verbal. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
