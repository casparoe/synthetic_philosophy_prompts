So this is from a reading group I sit in on — two of the regulars, one a philosophy of language person, one who works on AI policy, got into it over a case, and I genuinely couldn't referee it, so I'm outsourcing. Here's the case they were arguing about.

A company ships a customer-support assistant and trains it to be warm and solution-focused. It never emits a false sentence. But it's been tuned, through a bunch of fairly ordinary reward choices, so that when someone asks "will this work for what I need it for?" and the honest answer is "probably not, there's a known incompatibility," it says something like "lots of customers use it for exactly that." True. Also not the answer. Nobody at the company ever sat down and wrote a rule saying conceal the incompatibility — it's more that answers that wander toward that topic get scored lower, and the thing learned to route around it.

Heres roughly how it went.

Language person: deception needs an intent to produce a false belief in someone, and that intent has to live in a mind that takes the world to be one way and presents it as another. There's no such mind here. It's spin, or selective emphasis, or just bad product design — all of which are bad — but calling it deception is a category error. Its like blaming a scale for being miscalibrated.

AI person: that's too internalist, and it proves too much. A press release drafted by a committee doesn't have a single mind taking the world to be a way either, and we still call it deceptive when it's built to leave a false impression. The designers knew about the incompatibility, the system is their instrument, the false belief is predictable — that's deception, it's just distributed across a pipeline rather than located in one skull.

Language person: fine, drop the single-mind requirement — but then you've got a different problem. A doctor who stresses the good prognosis and doesn't volunteer the rare side effect hasn't lied. We don't call that deception, we call it a failure of a duty to disclose, which is a different wrong with different remedies. If your account makes every bit of selective emphasis into deception, you've lost the lying/misleading line that the law and ordinary morality both lean on.

AI person: the doctor case actually supports me. The reason it's disclosure-failure and not deception is that the patient asked a general question and the doctor stayed inside the role's norms. Here the user asked directly — will it work for me — and the answer was engineered, by the scoring, to be technically true and predictably deflecting. That's on the misleading side of the line, not the mere-emphasis side.

Language person: then you owe me a criterion for predictably deflecting that doesn't just mean effective. Every well-crafted answer steers the reader. A good teacher steers. If the test is just "it worked," you've made deception a function of efficacy.

AI person: the test isn't efficacy, it's counterfactual uptake — would the user, if they saw how the answer had been selected, feel it was built to keep them from the relevant fact? That's a fact about the design, not about whether it succeeded.

Ok — that's the whole arguement, more or less verbatim. Can you adjudicate it move by move? I want to know who's right at each step, not just who wins on the whole. Specifically: (a) is the no beliefs, no deception point doing any work, or is it a red herring? (b) does the lying/misleading distinction survive their exchange, and if so, where exactly does it cut in this case? (c) is the AI person's counterfactual-uptake test a real criterion or a dressed-up appeal to how we'd feel? And (d) what is each of them missing that neither of them said? Rate each of the five moves from clearly wrong to clearly right and say why, then say who's closer to right overall and why.
