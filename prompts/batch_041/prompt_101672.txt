I'm a doctoral candidate in political theory, working at the edge of my field on what an aligned system's value specification ought to look like, and I keep meeting an analogy that is doing a great deal of quiet work in the seminars and policy-adjacent papers I read. I want to audit it before I let it stand anywhere in my argument.

The analogy runs roughly as follows. Specifying the values of a highly capable model is a problem of constitutional design: the specification is the constitution, those who draft it are the founders, the model's ongoing interpretation of its vague clauses is adjudication, and the practical moral is that we neither can nor should pin values down with algorithmic precision, because the great achievement of constitutionalism was precisely to stop trying. Legitimacy comes from the process rather than the text, vagueness is a feature rather than a defect, and the model will fill in the rest in the spirit of the whole. Colleagues use this to license a fairly relaxed posture: design the procedure, write the charter, trust the interpretation.

I want the analogy audited rather than endorsed or dismissed, and I am not after a reading list — I want the mapping and the verdict.

First, the correspondence stated explicitly, as a table: what plays the founders; the act of founding as against the institutions founded; the text; the amendment procedure; judicial review; entrenched rights; the citizenry; and the order's capacity to be wrong and to correct itself over time.

Second, the points at which the mapping fails, separated into those that undermine the conclusion the analogy is being used to license and those that are merely decorative.

Third, the features it smuggles in. My suspicion is that it imports, without admitting it, a bounded citizenry, a monopoly of legitimate force, rulers who are themselves subject to the law and can be coerced by it, and above all an amendment procedure that someone other than the government can actually operate. If the model is simultaneously the government, the judiciary and the constitution, what survives?

Fourth, the amendment problem, where I think the analogy does its most illicit work. Constitutional orders have developed devices against amendments that destroy the order — provisions that cannot be amended at all, and doctrines under which a procedurally impeccable amendment may nonetheless be struck down for wrecking the basic structure — whereas the entire point of the corrigibility literature is that we want the system to remain correctable by its operators, including in directions we cannot now anticipate. Is a charter amendable in any direction still a constitution? Is one that cannot be amended still corrigible? Say which horn you would take, and why the analogy may not have it both ways.

Fifth, neutrality, which is what I am really trying to reach. Constitutions are supposed to be neutral among citizens' conceptions of the good, and liberal neutrality is normally defended as a response to reasonable pluralism within a demos that must live together. If the model's charter must likewise be neutral, neutral among whom — this generation, future ones, non-human animals, the model itself? Does the analogy help here, or merely relocate the difficulty into the neutrality of the founders and of the amendment process?

Finally: if the disanalogies are as serious as I suspect, tell me whether the relaxed conclusion survives at all, and whether there is a better analogy for what we actually want — a system that interprets our values back to us while remaining answerable to us — or whether constitutionalism is simply the wrong template.
