I am a backend engineer by trade with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been working through Book VII of Aristotle's Metaphysics on my own after a friend convinced me it was the place to go for the problem of material constitution — the puzzle of how a lump of clay and the statue fashioned from it can both seem to be present at once, one apparently surviving being squashed flat while the other does not. I have hit a wall at the third chapter and would like help understanding it rather than a summary of what commentators say about it.

As I read it, the chapter runs roughly as follows. Aristotle asks what substance is and proposes to examine what looks like the most obvious candidate: the underlying subject, the thing of which everything else is said while it is itself said of nothing further. By that criterion, he observes, matter appears to be substance. He then runs an argument that seems designed to test the point: if you take away from a thing its attributes, its conditions, and its capacities, and keep removing until nothing further can be removed, what is left behind seems to be nothing but matter — which would suggest that matter alone underlies everything and is therefore the real substance. But he immediately pulls back, saying this cannot be right, since being separate and being a particular this seem to belong to substance more than anything else, and matter has neither. He then leaves the question of which of matter, form, or the composite is substance unsettled and turns to a discussion of form.

What I cannot get past is how the argument is supposed to work. Is the stripping-away step a serious argument that Aristotle endorses, or a reductio he constructs only in order to dismantle it? When he says that being separate and being a particular this belong to substance, is he wielding those as independent criteria that matter genuinely fails, or merely reporting an intuition he intends to explain away later? And how does the underlying subject — the thing that everything else is said of and that is said of nothing else — map onto the clay in my statue case? The clay looks like exactly that kind of ultimate subject, yet it also seems plainly not to be what the statue is, which makes me suspect I have misunderstood what he means by underlying subject in the first place. Please walk through the chapter's moves one at a time, staying close to the text, tell me where the genuine difficulty lies as opposed to where I am simply importing my own assumptions, and finish by saying what would have to be true for the chapter's conclusion to follow — including whether the statue and clay case is really an instance of the problem he is raising there, or whether I am conflating two different questions.
