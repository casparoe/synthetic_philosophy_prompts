Opera audiences and critics have a weirdly uneven tolerance for departures from realism. Nobody blinks when a dying tubercular heroine sings a ten-minute aria at full volume, or when a 55-year-old soprano plays a teenager, or when the orchestra in the pit is treated as inaudible to the characters on stage — that's just how opera works. But move the setting of a Verdi or Mozart opera to, say, a 1970s housing project or a corporate boardroom, and a large chunk of the audience acts like you've desecrated a relic. Same with rewriting the libretto, or cutting a da capo, or casting across race or gender. Some of these get tolerated, some become scandals, and the line between them isn't definately principled.

I want you to work out what philosophical commitments that pattern presupposes. What does it assume about what a musical-dramatic work even is — the score, the score plus the libretto, a tradition of performance, an object with a historical location baked into its identity? What does it assume about which conventions are internal to the art form (singing instead of speaking, the orchestra as invisible, the aria as a pause in time) versus which are just historical accidents of staging that can be swapped out? And what does it assume about the relation of music to drama — is the drama the point and the music its vehicle, or is the music the thing, with the so-called drama largely carried by it?

Bernard Williams wrote a lot about opera: a long piece on the nature of opera, essays on Tristan and time, on Wagner and the transcendence of politics, on manifest artifice in Puccini, on authenticity and re-creation in performance, and a wry one on naive versus sentimental opera lovers. He had a distinctive line on exactly these questions, so use him as a resource — but the main job is the reconstruction. Say what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would all license what audiences actually do. Then tell me whether Williams's own view would license the pattern of tolerance and outrage we see, or whether it commits him to being more permissive (or less) than the average opera-goer. And if we replaced the presupposed view with the most plausible rival, what would actually change in how operas get staged and reviewed? Don't bother with citations, I don't need them — just the argument.
