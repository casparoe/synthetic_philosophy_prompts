I'm giving the toast at the farewell dinner for my colleague and friend Maya, who's leaving a tenured post to work full-time on farmed-animal welfare and is giving away most of her salary to do it. She is, by any measure I trust, a better person than anyone else in the room, me included, and she is also the least fun person to complain to about anything. Take the role of a philosopher who's also a decent after-dinner speaker. Here's my problem: half the room are philosophers who know Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints the way they know their own phone numbers, and the other half are her family, who just think she's wonderful and a bit mad. The thing I can't deliver as a lecture but can't pretend away either: Wolf's worry that a life dominated by moral commitment — and the interesting wrinkle is that Maya is the saint moved by love rather than by duty, which ought to change what's sayable — leaves no room for the personal projects and nonmoral excellences that make a life one you'd actually want, so that the saint ends up admirable but not enviable. I half believe that, and I suspect she half believes it too, which is part of why she's going. So the toast has to actually do this work: it can't canonize her (dishonest), it can't turn into a seminar (loses the family), and the Wolf material needs to shape which jokes land and what I have to leave unsaid, not just get name-dropped. Think through the tension first — admiration vs envy, the saint who is loved vs the saint who is merely approved of — so the thing has a spine, then give me the toast, spoken and warm and funny, starting with the toast itself, no preamble, and no restating the task. Anyways, five minutes, and I'd rather it earn a real laugh at her expense than a sentimental one at mine.
