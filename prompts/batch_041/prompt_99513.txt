In a civics seminar this term, our discussion of whether a school may discipline a student for something said or posted turned on an old saying: a classmate ended the debate by reciting that sticks and stones may break one's bones but words can never hurt one, and our teacher agreed, adding that words are not actions and that only actions may properly be restricted. I suspect that a number of quite different claims are being run together in that exchange, but I cannot articulate them well enough to say which of them is actually doing the argumentative work.

Could you set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that this saying might be asserting? I would like the full range, from readings that are evidently true but inert, through those that are substantive and defensible, to those that are straightforwardly false. For each entry, please indicate whether it concerns the causal effects of words, the nature of the acts we perform in speaking, the responsibilities of speakers, or the reactions of hearers, and whether it asserts something about what is possible, what is permissible, or merely what is prudent.

Please then say which reading my classmate and teacher most likely intended, and whether that reading genuinely supports the conclusion that speech cannot be regulated in the way conduct is. I am especially curious whether any of the readings survive the observation that certain things — a promise, a verdict, an insult, an order — appear to be done in the saying rather than merely caused by it, and whether that observation changes which entries on your list remain defensible. A clear taxonomy with a verdict on each item would serve me better than a general essay on free expression, since I need to be able to point to the specific claim I am disputing.
