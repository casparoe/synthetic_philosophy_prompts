I chair a small community mediation service that the council funds — mostly neighbour rows and the odd planning dispute, two parties in a room, ninety minutes if I'm lucky, no power to impose anything, and whatever they agree has to be voluntary and signed off by both sides at the end. My friend and I got into a long, slightly wine-fuelled argument about it last week. She says compromise is always a betrayal: if you really hold a value, then splitting the difference with someone who denies it is just cowardice with better manners. I think that's too quick, because a lot of what I actually see aren't cases of meeting in the middle at all — they're cases where both sides can end up better off than they started by trading, one giving up something it barely cares about in exchange for something it cares about enormously, and the other doing the reverse. She came back with, fine, that works for preferences like parking spaces, but values aren't like that, you can't put a price on them, and then we just talked past each other for an hour. I want you to take my side seriously and turn it into something I can actually run in the room.

So: write me a decision procedure, in order, with the questions I put to the parties, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me or to the parties because it's a judgment call rather than something the procedure can settle. It has to keep hold of the distinction between two sides meeting in the middle and two sides swapping things they rank differently, and it has to handle the case where a party says some thing simply isn't on the table at all — won't trade it for any amount of the other thing — as opposed to the case where they're only ranking it low and could be moved. Flag which steps are real tests and which are me dressing up a hunch as procedure, and be honest about where the whole thing misfires, especially about whether you can get truthful rankings out of people who think admitting a low priority will lose them the case. Also alot of my cases involve one party who is far more articulate and confident than the other, and I'd like the procedure to say something about that rather than pretend it away.

Then run it on three cases so I can watch it work: (1) a mosque and a residents' association fighting over evening use and parking at a shared community centre, where I suspect a trade is sitting there; (2) an environmental group and a fishing cooperative over a marine no-take zone, where one side treats the reef as non-negotiable; and (3) one I'd rather it didn't answer the way it will — a residents' group and a developer who can both clearly gain by trading on building height against parking, where the trade quietly dumps the noise and the traffic onto a street nobody has invited to the table. I don't want citations, reading lists, or names of philosophers; it needs to make sense to a colleague who's never opened a philosophy book; and please write it in British English.
