Next month I am presenting a paper to a joint session of our department's Chinese-philosophy reading group and a developmental-psychology lab that studies the origins of prosociality, and what I need from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a rehearsal of the argument.

The thesis I will defend is this. The received contrast — Mencius the optimist who holds that human nature is good, Xunzi the pessimist who holds that it is bad — mislocates the disagreement. I argue that both thinkers are nativists about moral capacity: both take human beings to arrive equipped with dispositions without which ethical life would be impossible. What they actually dispute is the direction of fit between those dispositions and the norms. For Mencius the norms are read off the natural responses, the sprouts, so that ethical education is cultivation which lets a native pattern complete itself; for Xunzi the norms are instruments built to break and redirect those natural responses, so that ethical education is construction. On this reading Xunzi is not a pessimist about human nature but a constructivist about ethics, and the chapter usually read as declaring human nature evil is making a claim about the untutored default trajectory of our dispositions rather than about their innate content. I further claim that the reframing dissolves an apparent contradiction in Xunzi, namely that a creature with no moral nature could nonetheless perform the deliberate effort that is supposed to manufacture one.

The room divides into three constituencies whose objections will sound nothing alike. First, Sinologists who read the texts in classical Chinese and are allergic to importing Anglo-American metaethics; they will hear nativism, direction of fit, and constructivism as anachronisms, and they will press on whether the concept of xing can bear the weight I place on it. Second, developmental psychologists who study infants' and toddlers' sociomoral behavior; they will treat the empirical record — infants preferring helpers to hinderers, toddlers helping without prompting — as itself adjudicating the dispute, and they will suspect that nativism about capacity is either trivial or false. Third, one or two colleagues who hold that the Mencius–Xunzi contrast is an artifact of later orthodoxy and that treating it as a live disagreement is retrojection.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones a general ethics audience would raise. Order them by expected damage, likelihood times severity, and phrase each the way the relevant sub-audience would phrase it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede at the outset, what to hold, and which of my claims I should defend preemptively rather than hope goes unnoticed. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me citations or a reading list; I need to answer out of what is already in my head.
