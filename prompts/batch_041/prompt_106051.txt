I work as a software engineer, and I have no formal training in philosophy, but I keep running into a puzzle in how museums handle restitution claims, and I would like someone to work out what the practice actually assumes.

The pattern is this. Rather than return contested objects, a number of museums now offer the communities they came from high-resolution 3D scans, photogrammetric models, or digital archives of them, and describe the gesture as digital repatriation or virtual restitution. The physical object stays where it is. Sometimes the museum goes further and offers the object itself back only on a renewable loan, keeping legal title to it indefinitely. The response from source communities is not uniform: some treat the digital copy as a genuine form of return, at least for certain objects; others regard it as an evasion; and for some categories of material — human remains above all — nothing short of physical return is treated as settling the claim at all.

What puzzles me is that from where I sit, a scan is a copy, and a copy is not the thing. I would never claim to have returned your file to you by sending a duplicate while keeping the original, and I would not expect you to accept that. Yet here the copy is sometimes offered, and sometimes accepted, as a return. I want the implicit theory behind this practice reconstructed: what view of cultural objects, and of what ownership of them amounts to, would make the practice coherent rather than cynical or confused.

Please address, in particular:

1. What does treating a scan as a return presuppose about where an object's value lies — in its unique material substrate, in its reproducible form, in its provenance and history, or in the relationships it sustains? Which of these does the practice commit to, and which does it merely leave open?

2. What does the renewable loan that never ends presuppose about the difference between legal title and possession, and about why possession is supposed to matter morally when title is retained?

3. Why would human remains and inanimate objects fall under different regimes? What does that split presuppose about the kind of value at stake in each — dignity, identity, aesthetic worth, or something else — and is the same underlying theory doing the work in both cases?

4. Finally, tell me which parts of the practice are strict presuppositions and which are underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories would license the same behavior. If you replaced the presupposed view with a rival one — say, that a cultural object just is its material, or just is its form — which parts of the practice would have to change, and which would survive untouched?
