I'm TAing a third-year course on philosophy of action. Question 3 was this:

Q3. (Short essay, ~500 words.) Hyperbolic discounting is often said to explain weakness of will. (a) Explain what preference reversal is, and why a hyperbolic discount curve is supposed to predict it. (b) Taking Davidson's account of incontinence as your philosophical reference point: does the discounting model explain the phenomenon he is trying to account for, or does it only redescribe it in the language of choice? Defend your answer.

And here's one of the scripts:

(a) There's a robust finding that people's preferences over two rewards reverse as the earlier reward draws near: offered, say, $100 tomorrow against $110 in a week, most people take the $100; offered the same two rewards dated a year out, most take the $110. This is what hyperbolic discounting is meant to capture. An exponential discounter applies a constant per-period rate, so her ranking of the two options never changes as time passes. The hyperbolic discounter instead applies a rate that is shallow at short delays and steeper as the delay lengthens, so the near reward looms larger the closer it gets and the patient plan made at a distance gets overturned. The pattern is dynamic inconsistency: the same person, with the same information, ranks the options differently at different times.

(b) Davidson's question is how an agent can do something while judging, all things considered, that it would be better to do otherwise. His answer is that the all-things-considered judgment is not an unconditional judgment but a prima facie one, so the incontinent agent who acts on the tempting option isn't contradicting herself: she acts on an unconditional judgment that favours the temptation, while her conditional judgment favours the better course.

Ainslie's account seems to me to fill this in rather than merely restate it. If the reversal is generated by the shape of the discount curve, we get predictions. The agent should be willing to pay for commitment devices, and to pay more the closer the temptation gets; the Ulysses contract is exactly this, and it works by making the later temptation less attractive to her, so that her short-run and long-run rankings line up again.

Hume would have denied the possibility of weakness of will altogether. If reason is just the slave of the passions, it cannot oppose them, so there is no such thing as acting against one's own better judgment — what we call akrasia is just the strongest passion winning on the day.

If the model is right, then, the gap between judgment and action looks smaller than Davidson supposed: the agent always acts on the option she most prefers at the moment of action. So I'd say the model does explain the phenomenon rather than merely redescribing it, because the discount curve does real work — it tells us when reversals will happen, and how much people will pay to avoid them.
