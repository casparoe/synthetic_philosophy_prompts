I am preparing a proposal for a one-semester, roughly eight-thousand-word paper in value theory, and I have a half-formed idea that I need turned into a proper proposal. The area is the problem of the wrong kind of reasons. The problem, as I understand it, is generated by cases in which there is a reason to have an attitude — to admire a person, to desire a state of affairs, to value something — that does not bear on whether the attitude is correct or fitting. The stock example is a demon who will punish me unless I admire him. Responses divide into those that save the fitting-attitude analysis of value by restricting it to reasons of the right kind, usually reasons that concern the object of the attitude rather than the state of having it, and those that deny that the threat is a reason of the same genus as the ones that matter, or deny that it is a reason at all.

My half-formed idea is that both families of response treat the attitude as a single, unified item — one thing, admiring the demon, against which reasons are weighed and a standard of fittingness is applied. I suspect the cases that generate the trouble are precisely those in which the attitude is not unified but hybrid: it contains a cognitive component, something like a judgment or a construal, and a conative or affective component, something like a desire, an emotion, or a commitment. On this hypothesis the threatening reason attaches to one component while the standard of fittingness attaches to the other, and the appearance that a single attitude is answerable to two incompatible kinds of reason is an artifact of describing the attitude as one thing. If that is right, the problem should be dissolved rather than solved, and what looked like a fact about reasons is really a fact about the unity of the attitudes we invoke.

Please write the proposal. I want it to state a question with a determinate answer — something like whether the problem arises for an attitude just in case that attitude has both a cognitive and a non-cognitive component — and then to do the following: explain why the existing positions leave this question open, rather than merely assuming that they do; set out the method, stage by stage, for what I would actually do, including how I would decide whether a given attitude counts as hybrid and which attitudes I would test (I would like at least one that is plausibly not hybrid, one that is plausibly hybrid, and one that is contested); say why this approach is better than the obvious alternatives; describe what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of what can realistically be done in a semester, including which part I should cut if it proves too large. If you think the half-formed idea is incoherent, say so and propose the nearest version of it that could be made to work.

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not name particular authors or works; refer to positions and arguments instead, and I will supply the references myself.
