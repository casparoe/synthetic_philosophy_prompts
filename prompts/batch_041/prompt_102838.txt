Two philosophers have argued for years about whether beauty is objective, and have now agreed to write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. The first is an aesthetic realist: she holds that at least some works are beautiful, and others defective, as a matter of fact independent of any actual or idealized human response, and that a critical verdict is correct when it tracks that fact. The second is a Humean about the standard of taste: he holds that beauty is a sentiment rather than a quality in objects, that judgments of taste answer to no response-independent aesthetic fact, and that the only standard available — and the only one required — is the joint verdict of qualified judges, the critics who unite delicacy of taste with practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense.

Draft the document their collaboration should produce.

I. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both parties would sign — not platitudes. Cover what is common ground in the problem Hume sets out: the breadth of actual disagreement in taste; that not every verdict is as good as every other; that expertise is real and first impressions corrigible; that the dispute is not between a party who thinks anything goes and a party who thinks beauty is one simple property. Include what each view does not commit its holder to, and any claim about the status of the essay's own test cases that both can accept.

II. Remaining disagreements. State each in wording the party whose view it is would accept as a fair rendering of that view. Address at least: whether the authority of the ideal critic's verdict is constitutive (the verdict makes the judgment correct) or evidential (the verdict is a reliable sign of a fact that would obtain anyway); whether the ideal-critic procedure could in principle diverge from the realist's tracking relation, and what each side holds would then be true; and whether aesthetic properties are response-dependent yet real, response-independent and real, or not real at all — and whether, once a response-dependent realist and the sentimentalist are stated precisely, anything remains of their dispute beyond a disagreement about the word 'objective'.

III. Cruxes table. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — give the crux in neutral wording, each side's position on it, the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about the outcome. Include at least: whether faultless disagreement is possible in matters of taste; whether the true judge is best modelled as a regulative ideal that may never be fully realized or as the convergent verdict of the qualified over time; and how each side would treat a critic whose verdicts are stable, refined, and unprejudiced yet conflict with the deliverances of the other side's favoured method. Among the cases to be run through, use the essay's two examples — the cask of wine from which two tasters each draw out a different foreign object, and the critic who sets a minor poet above Milton — and one of your own construction: a community of judges with a different sensory constitution who apply the ideal-critic procedure as faithfully as we do and converge on verdicts that invert some of ours. For each case record what the realist predicts, what the sentimentalist predicts, and which side, if either, the outcome would embarrass.

Close with two short sections: what each side would count as evidence against its own position rather than the other's, and your own judgment as to whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — if so, which part, and why.

Format the agreed claims as a numbered list and the cruxes as a table.
