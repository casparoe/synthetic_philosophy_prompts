I've been teaching myself this stuff about grading beliefs by how close they land to the truth — you score a confidence level by how near it sits to what actually happened, 1 if the claim is true, 0 if it's false, in between otherwise. What I liked is that you can apparently squeeze the ordinary rules of probability out of it instead of just assuming them. But I built this and now it's eating itself and I cant find the bad step:

(1) A confidence level is better the closer it is to the truth.
(2) So the best possible confidence in a claim is 1 when the claim is true and 0 when it's false.
(3) Any claim is either true or false.
(4) So for every claim there is a best possible confidence, and it's one of the two extremes.
(5) You should have the best confidence you can.
(6) So you should be certain — 0 or 1 — about everything.
(7) But that's plainly mad. Nobody should be dead certain it'll rain tomorrow, and the whole point of the scoring idea was supposed to be that hedging (0.7, say) can be exactly right.

There's a second thing that nags me and I suspect it's the same bug. Take your own confidences and ask which confidences would score best by your own lights. Your current ones always win, because from where you stand they're your best guess about how things are. So the theory seems to say that anybody who's internally consistent is doing fine — including the guy who's 0.9 sure the sun goes round the earth. I want the theory to be able to say he's doing badly.

So which step is wrong, and what do I have to loose to fix it? If it's a real choice, I'd rather keep (1), that being near the truth is the whole story, than keep (6) — but tell me what that costs me and where you think the actual damage is.
