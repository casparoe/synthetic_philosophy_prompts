I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working on decision theory and population ethics, and I've been arguing for weeks with a friend who takes expected value maximization about as literally as it can be taken. You are a philosopher who works on both decision theory and the ethics of the far future, and I'd like you to read a short essay I've drafted for our reading group and tell me what you make of it. Before you start writing, please work through the objections carefully in your head — I would rather have three well-aimed objections than ten perfunctory ones — and then give me your comments in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long and candid email to a colleague. Open with your overall verdict in a sentence or two, then elaborate. Here is the draft:

"Fanaticism in expected value reasoning comes in two forms that are usually run together. The first is the tiny-probability, astronomical-payoff form: a bet with a probability of one in a quadrillion of an outcome worth a quadrillion units has the same expected value as a sure thing worth one unit, and something seems badly wrong with treating these as equivalent, or with taking the bet whenever the probability is nudged upward. The second is the far-future form: if the number of people who might exist later is astronomically large, then a tiny change in the probability of a good outcome for them can swamp any benefit to people alive now, and present-day concerns get lexically demoted. My claim is that only the first is really a problem about expected value. The second is a problem about the size of the stakes, and any decision rule that is sensitive to stakes at all will reproduce the same structure; the fanaticism charge there is really a charge against consequentialism with an enormous denominator, not against the expectation operator. The first is a genuine problem, and I think it is a problem about measurement rather than about aggregation. We do not possess probability estimates that are reliable to twelve decimal places, and a rule that is sensitive to differences at the twelfth decimal place is a rule that is sensitive to noise. So I propose a criterion: before counting a probability-weighted outcome toward the expected value of an option, ask whether a plausible error in the probability estimate — being off by a factor of a thousand in either direction — would change which option wins. If it would, the option's advantage rests on a quantity we cannot estimate, and that contribution should be discounted or dropped rather than used to break the tie. Call this the estimation-error test. It handles the mugging case, since the mugger's probability is a guess, and it does not require us to say that small probabilities do not count, only that unestimated ones do not count much. The obvious objection is that the test is too strong: our estimates of charitable effectiveness are also uncertain by orders of magnitude, and if we discard every contribution that fails the test we will have almost nothing left to compare. I think the test can be repaired by making the discount depend on the ratio of the estimate's uncertainty to the decision-relevant margin, though I am not confident this does not simply collapse into risk aversion, or into a prior over probability estimates dressed up as a principle."

What started the argument with my friend is a pair of cases he insists are structurally identical: a bet that pays off once in a billion, which he values at a billionth of the sure payoff, and a donation with a one-in-a-billion chance of averting an existential catastrophe, which he values the same way. I say the first is a mugging and the second is an ordinary cost-effectiveness calculation; he says the only difference is that I like the conclusion of the second. I would like your comments on three things in particular: whether the distinction between the two forms of fanaticism is a real one or merely verbal, whether the estimation-error test is a genuinely new criterion or a relabeling of something already in the literature, and whether the repair I gesture at in the last sentence can be made to work. If you think the argument fails, please tell me which premise you would give up and what you would put in its place, and if you think the case pair does not actually decide anything, tell me what case would.
