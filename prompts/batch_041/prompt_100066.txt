I am the instructor of a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I am currently grading the final examination. One question asks students to state the independence axiom of expected utility theory and then to analyze a pair of lottery choices modeled on the Allais paradox. The examination question and one student's answer are reproduced in full below. Please evaluate the answer: assign a letter grade (A through F, with plus or minus where warranted) and then supply the specific comments that justify that grade. In your comments, identify what the student has gotten right, and flag every error or imprecision, explaining both why it is wrong and what a correct treatment of that point would say. Where the student's reasoning breaks down, walk through the misstep rather than merely announcing the correct result. Write in American English.

Examination question:

1. State the independence axiom of expected utility theory, and explain in your own words what it requires and why it is central to the theory.

2. Consider the following two decisions. All amounts are in millions of dollars, and you may take the utility of $0 to be zero.

Decision A. Choose between:
A1: $2M with probability 1.
A2: $2M with probability 0.5, $5M with probability 0.4, and $0 with probability 0.1.

Decision B. Choose between:
B1: $2M with probability 0.5 and $0 with probability 0.5.
B2: $5M with probability 0.4 and $0 with probability 0.6.

(a) Which pairings of a choice from A with a choice from B can be represented by a single expected-utility function, and which cannot? Justify your answer with the relevant inequality or inequalities.

(b) Does this example exhibit the Allais paradox? Explain what it shows about the independence axiom, and assess whether it undermines expected utility theory as a descriptive theory, as a normative theory, or both.

Student's answer:

1. The independence axiom governs how a rational agent should compare two lotteries that share a common branch. If two lotteries are identical except along one branch, then the agent's preference between them should depend only on that branch and not on the prize that the shared branch delivers; equivalently, adding the same consequence to both lotteries should not reverse the preference between them. Independence — sometimes called the sure-thing principle — requires that an option that is at least as good as another in every state of the world be preferred to it. The axiom is central because it is what allows preferences to be represented by the expectation of a utility function; if a common consequence could flip a ranking, then no single utility function could reproduce the agent's choices.

2(a). Let u be the agent's utility function, with u($0) = 0. A1 is preferred to A2 exactly when u(2) > 0.5u(2) + 0.4u(5), which simplifies to 0.5u(2) > 0.4u(5). B1 is preferred to B2 exactly when 0.5u(2) > 0.4u(5) — the very same inequality. So the two decisions are governed by one and the same comparison. It follows that the pairings A1 with B1 and A2 with B2 are consistent with expected utility, while A1 with B2 (and A2 with B1) cannot be represented by any utility function.

2(b). This is the Allais paradox. The chooser who takes the certain $2M in Decision A but the gamble in Decision B violates the independence axiom, since the two decisions differ only by a common branch that ought not to matter. What the example shows is that the chooser's preferences are intransitive: A1 is ranked above A2, yet B2 is ranked above B1, so the agent's ranking cycles and cannot be a coherent ordering. The paradox is often taken to show that expected utility theory fails as a descriptive theory, because real agents routinely make exactly these choices. As a normative theory it survives better, since one can argue that independence is a condition of rationality and that the agent who violates it is simply making a mistake.
