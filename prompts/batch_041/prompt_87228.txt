I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on practical reason, and I've been going back and forth with two friends for weeks about cases where you have to choose but you don't prefer either option. I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts and I genuinely can't tell whether they add up to anything. Two identical cans of the same beer in the fridge, I don't care which, I grab one: completely fine, not a lapse, not something that needs deliberation. Two job offers I judge equal on every dimension I care about (stipulate the equality, no hidden difference): flipping a coin is fine, and agonizing for another week would be a defect in me, not conscientiousness. Now change that second case. Medicine vs. music, where I don't think either is better than the other and I also don't think they're equally good — it's not that they tie, it's that there's no fact of the matter about how they rank — and here a coin flip feels close to a failure. I should commit to one and let the commitment make it the thing I care about, or something like that; I can't say what the "something like that" is. Same shape with two people I could see myself building a life with: not interchangeable, not ranked, and "let's flip for it" seems like a category error about what's happening. But then take the organ allocation case — two patients, identical prognosis, one organ, no other relevant difference — where a lottery isn't merely permitted, it's the only decent procedure, and me "committing" to one of them on a personal whim would be monstrous. I also notice that if I take the beer, put it back, take the other, and do that five times, something has gone wrong with me even though no option was ever worse than its rival. And between two equally painful root canals I feel real sympathy for paralysis, whereas Buridan's ass starving between two identical hay piles just seems stupid — the ass has a preference (it prefers eating to dying), so there's no tie at all — except I'm not sure the hay case and the root canal case actually differ, which is one of the things worrying me.

A few more from the list, in case they help. A friend who always takes the leftmost option when things are tied strikes me as doing better than a friend who freshly coin-flips each time, even though the leftmost rule is just as arbitrary; there's something good about having the arbitrariness be a standing policy rather than a fresh act. Choosing a birthday gift for someone when two gifts are equally good: coin-flipping here feels like it undercuts the point of the gift, even though I can't say what gets undercut, since the recipient gets an equally good gift either way. And when I have no preference now but know I'll care a lot in six months, the future preference seems to give me a reason now, so the choice was never preference-free. My best half-formed guess at a principle is that it's about reversibility — arbitrary picking is fine when it's cheap to undo and bad when it isn't — but the organ case kills that dead, because the organ choice is maximally irreversible and the lottery is still the right call, so either the principle needs a clause I can't find or I'm wrong about one of the cases.

So: what principle, or what small set of competing principles, actually captures this pattern? I'd like each candidate stated precisely enough that I can see which of my verdicts it buys and which it has to throw overboard, and I want to know what it says about two cases I haven't listed — say, choosing which of two equally strong candidates to hire (where someone else's claim is at stake but nothing is irreversible), and a case where the options are on a par rather than equal but the choice is trivial and private. Be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle can't accommodate and tell me which one you'd overturn and why. Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up.
