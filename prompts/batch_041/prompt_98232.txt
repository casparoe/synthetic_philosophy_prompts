I run a master's seminar on social choice and political philosophy — 14 students, one 80-minute session — and I want to do Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as an activity rather than a lecture. What bothers me about how it's usually taught is that everyone reaches straight for the veil of ignorance, and the veil is doing none of the actual work: the theorem is a representation result about a social welfare function that satisfies the expected-utility axioms plus Pareto, and the veil-of-ignorance story is at best a heuristic for pinning the weights. Worse, the veil on its own doesnt give you utilitarianism — swap expected value for maximin and the very same veil hands you Rawls. I want them to feel that, not be told it.

So: design me an in-class activity for those 14 students in 80 minutes where they actually make allocation decisions behind some kind of veil and then have to live with the consequences. I want them to feel the pull of the impartial-observer / equiprobable-lottery argument — the tug towards just summing utilities — before they notice that the tug depends on a decision rule they smuggled in themselves. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the real structure: the ex ante aggregation, the cardinal-utility and unit-comparability business that the weights quietly soak up, why equal weights need an extra anonymity-or-equiprobability assumption rather than falling out of the theorem, and the ex ante versus ex post fairness wrinkle (a rule can look fair before the lottery and unequal after it).

Practical stuff: give me small real stakes so it doesn't feel like a worksheet — tokens that convert to something at the end, chocolate or a kitty for the coffee run, nothing needing actual cash. Materials should be printable. Give me roles or rules, a timeline that fits the 80 minutes, and the payoff scheme. No lecture up front; they should build the rule themselves and only find out later what they've committed to. And tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'd rather know now than find out in front of them. Also be straight about which part of Harsanyi the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.

End with a single line beginning 'One-liner:' that sums up in one sentence what the activity makes them feel.
