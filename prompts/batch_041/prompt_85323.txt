I teach a seminar on Aristotle's Politics, and this term I want to end the unit on Book I with a film rather than another article. The unit is on household management versus the art of acquisition: Aristotle's distinction between getting wealth for the sake of the household's needs and getting wealth for its own sake, his claim that exchange begins as barter between households and turns unnatural once money mediates it for profit, his treatment of money as a conventional token rather than something with a nature, his charge that usury is the most contrary to nature of all the ways of making money, and the anecdote he tells about Thales of Miletus, who is said to have foreseen a heavy olive harvest and locked up the presses in advance so he could name his price when the season came. I also spend a session on the natural hierarchy of Book I, since the same book that gives us the economic analysis also argues for rule by nature.

I would like a reading of Trading Places through this material. The Duke brothers' wager is explicitly a heredity-versus-environment experiment, which means the film stages a test of natural hierarchy in something close to Aristotle's own sense, and then lets a street hustler and a disgraced blueblood both turn out to be excellent traders — I want you to say what that outcome does to Aristotle's view of natural rule, and whether the film's apparent vindication of nurture is really a refutation or rather an accidental demonstration of Aristotle's point that virtue is a matter of habituation rather than birth. Then the commodities market itself. Frozen concentrated orange juice futures are exchange value at two removes from any use, money that begets money with no household and no need in sight, and the film's climax is a corner engineered through a falsified crop report rather than through superior foresight — which inverts the Thales story, since Aristotle tells it to show that knowledge could make a philosopher rich if he cared to, while the film shows the money going to whoever controls the information. Does the film, for all that it humiliates the Dukes, end by endorsing exactly the unlimited acquisition Aristotle rejects? Both heroes finish rich and idle in a warm climate, and I want you to sit with that rather than explain it away. Consider too whether the film ever lets use value back in at all, or whether the goods remain purely notional throughout, and what that would mean for Aristotle's claim that exchange is natural only insofar as it serves need.

The reading should be faithful in both directions. Where Aristotle genuinely illuminates a scene or a choice, say so specifically; where the film resists the Aristotelian frame, say that too, and be honest about the anachronism — Aristotle knew nothing of futures contracts, limited liability, or a regulated exchange, and it would be cheap to treat his critique as a direct verdict on a market he could not have imagined. I would also like the reverse contribution: what the film makes visible about the affective life of wealth-getting that Aristotle's categories leave out. He treats the unlimited pursuit of money as a mistake about ends, a kind of category error; the film treats it as a game with winners, losers, race, class, and an enormous amount of pleasure in the winning. Which of those is the better description of what people actually want when they want money? I am teaching undergraduates who will have read the Politics but who may only half-remember the film, so the piece should carry its own plot reminders where they matter and should avoid quoting either the film or Aristotle at length.
