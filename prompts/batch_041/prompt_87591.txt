I'm in a reading group on late antiquity and we keep tripping over the same metaphor. The person who runs it is a classicist by traning but reads a lot of big-picture history, and he keeps saying the Roman empire was basically like a person: it was born, it grew, it hit a vigorous maturity around the second century, then it aged, got frail, and eventually died, and that death is what "the fall" refers to. He uses the picture to make two moves at once. First, that there really was a death, a discrete terminal event, so the whole "it didn't fall, it transformed" crowd is just refusing to call a corpse a corpse. Second, that the decline was basically internal and inevitable the way aging is: you don't blame a ninety-year-old for dying, and you shouldn't go hunting for a single cause like lead in the pipes or a cold snap or some tribe crossing a river, because those are just the pneumonia that finishes off somebody who was already old. I half agree and half suspect the metaphor is carrying both conclusions on its back. Can you audit it for me: give me the correspondance (what plays the role of the birth, the genome, the organs, the aging process itself, the moment of death), then show me where the mapping actually breaks — and be picky about which disanologies matter for his two conclusions versus which are just noise — and then tell me straight whether either conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table.

Two places I especially want you to press. First, whether an empire can both "die" and "live on" in its successor institutions — the eastern half chugging along for another millennium, Roman law and the church and the language outliving the polity — without that being a cheat, and what the organism picture would even say about a creature whose parts keep walking around after the funeral. Second, whether "aging" here names a mechanism at all or is just a redescription of whatever decline we'd already decided had happened; if there's no internal clock, I want to know whether the inevitability claim has any content left. And if a better analogy is available, tell me. A friend keeps pushing something like: collapse is what happens when a society's problem-solving costs more and more for less and less return, the way a business keeps growing its overhead faster than its revenue and one day can't cover the interest — I think it's from an archaeologist, but I don't remember who. Does that do the work better than the aging picture, or does it just swap one set of smuggled assumptions for another?

If the whole framing is confused — if "did it fall" isn't even the right kind of question, or if "fall" is a category we're imposing rather than finding — say so plainly instead of humoring me. I'd much rather be told the question is malformed than get a tidy verdict on a bad question, and if you think I'm over-reading the metaphor and the guy just means something loose and harmless by it, tell me that too.
