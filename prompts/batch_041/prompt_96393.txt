I've been going to a reading group that's slowly grinding through the first Critique, and we got into a fight last week about transcendental arguments that I still can't settle. Here's where I'm coming from: I actually want the Strawson-style argument to work. I want it to be true that features of our experience like experiencing a world of enduring objects that exist whether or not we're looking, and perceiving events in a determinate order we can distinguish from the order in which we happen to perceive them, are only possible if the world really is that way -- that the objectivity of the temporal order is a necessary condition of the possibility of self-conscious experience, not just something we're built to assume. I know the Stroud-ish objection is that this only ever buys you what we must believe and never what's true, but I've always assumed there's some way around that.

Then my friend handed out the following, and says it's the same argument with the subject swapped, and now I can't tell if I'm being trolled:

(1) When I deliberate about what to do, I can't help representing the outcome as genuinely open -- as not already settled by facts that hold independently of my deliberating.
(2) That isn't a feeling I might be wrong about or a habit I could drop; it's constitutive of deliberating. You can't deliberate and at the same time treat your deliberating as an epiphenomenal shadow of a causal chain that already fixed the answer. That combination isn't just false, it's incoherent in the way that promising while believing nobody can be bound by a promise is incoherent.
(3) So the outcome's really being open is a necessary condition of the possibility of deliberating at all -- not just of my representing it as open.
(4) If determinism is true, the outcome isn't open; it was fixed before I ever sat down.
(5) So determinism is false, and I have a kind of freedom that no causal story about my brain could capture.

I can't accept (5), but every step looks structurally like the steps I do accept, which is making me nervous about the steps I accept. So: which step is bad, and what does dropping it cost me? My worry is that if the bad move is the slide from "I can't help representing it this way" to "it is this way," then my external-world argument has the exact same hole and I've just been giving it a pass because I like the conclusion -- which is basically motivated cognition with extra steps. If instead the flaw is something narrower about deliberation -- say, the disanalogy is that experience has representational content while deliberation is a practice, or that "open" quietly means "not yet known" in (1) and "not yet determined" in (3) -- then definately tell me whether that distinction is principled or whether I'm gerrymandering to save the argument I'm attached to. And if you think the argument is actually fine and the real move is to deny (2) or (4) (some compatibilist line where you can deliberate while believing the outcome is determined, as long as you don't know it), say so and tell me what's left of the original argument afterward.
