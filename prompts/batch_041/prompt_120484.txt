I teach a 300-level ethics seminar, 16 students, 75 minutes, and every year we get to Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation objection lands as a shrug. Can you design an in-class thing where they actually feel the squeeze instead of hearing me describe it? They shouldn't hear the name of the problem until the debrief.

Heres the shape I'm imagining. Round one: I hand out role cards making everyone a complainant under some policy. One person bears something grave, a few bear something mid-level, ten bear something mild but genuinely different in kind from the others — not just smaller, or they'll turn it into arithmetic and we learn nothing. They have to adopt a principle governing when the policy may be imposed, and the rule I enforce with a literal gavel is that nobody may justify a principle by citing totals or numbers of people; every defense has to be addressed to the worst-off individual, in terms they could accept from their own standpoint. Round two: I put the gavel down and say, justify it however you like. I want them to watch the room flip. Round three: they must write a tie-breaking rule for cases where complaints are equal in strength, then I spring the case where they aren't equal and see whether they let numbers back in through that door.

Give me materials, timings, the actual wording of the cards, and a way to stop the grave-burden person from folding under social pressure. Then the debrief: map what happened onto the individualist restriction, onto the work the word reasonable is quietly doing in reasonable rejectability, and onto the two escape routes — aggregate inside the principle, or aggregate only in tie-breaking — plus where the ex ante/ex post version of the question changes who even counts as worst off. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the activity cant reproduce: it can show them what the restriction costs but not why anyone should pay it, and I'll have to supply that part myself.
