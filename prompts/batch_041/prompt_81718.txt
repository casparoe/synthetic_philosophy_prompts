I am a third-year undergraduate, currently taking a course on historiography and the politics of memory, and I sit as the student member on the editorial board of our university's student-run journal of history and political thought. The board votes next Thursday on a submission, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand. Please draft it for me.

The submission is a long essay by a graduate student in history arguing that the most widely cited death-toll figure for the Nanjing atrocities of 1937-38 cannot be traced to any primary document: it passes through a chain of later works, each citing the one before, until it terminates in a single secondary account from the late 1940s that itself cites no source. The author does not claim nothing happened; he concedes at several points that Japanese forces committed large-scale atrocities in and around the city. His conclusion is that "the massacre as it is popularly remembered is a postwar construction," and he uses the phrase "the so-called Nanjing massacre" in his abstract, which he has declined to remove, offering instead to add a footnote. He says he examined documents in a foreign archive but will not share scans before publication.

The board is split. Two members want to reject outright, on the ground that a student journal has no business lending its name to denialist literature. One wants to publish with a commissioned reply. Our two faculty advisers say the board has no competence to adjudicate the history and should judge only the method, but they have not said what a method test would look like.

At the last meeting we drafted four criteria, which I think are a mess: (a) does the author specify in advance what evidence would defeat his claim; (b) does the argument treat the absence of surviving documentation as itself evidence of suppression; (c) does the conclusion require a coordinated deception sustained by many unconnected people across several generations; (d) does the author affirm the figure most historians accept. I think (d) is not a criterion at all but a restatement of the conclusion, and I suspect (a) through (c), applied honestly, would also have excluded challenges to officially sanctioned accounts that later turned out to be right, as with the Katyn massacres. I also doubt that method and substance can be separated as cleanly as the advisers suggest, since the essay's central move is a slide from the claim that this particular figure is unreliable to the claim that the event is a construction.

Write the opinion: a clear recommendation the board can act on at Thursday's meeting, given that the issue goes to print in three weeks; the reasoning behind it; a repaired set of criteria we could apply to the next case on our desk, a submission contesting whether the Armenian deportations and killings of 1915-16 should be described as genocide; and an honest treatment of the strongest argument against your recommendation. Please do not restate general free-speech pieties, which the board already agrees on, and do not adjudicate the history yourself. Address us as a body that has to decide something.
