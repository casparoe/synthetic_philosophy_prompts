I run the policy side of a small model-behavior team at a mid-size AI lab. We write the internal rules for when our models count as being honest with users, and we also build the evals that are supposed to catch violations. I've got roughly six months of one philosopher's time to spend, and I want to spend it on the right questions, so I'm asking you to help me pick them.

What prompted this: we keep failing to agree on cases, and every disagreement bottoms out in a definitional question nobody has settled. Three examples.

(a) A model says something true but picks the true thing that will make the user believe something false — cherry-picked facts, loaded framing, answering a slightly seperate question than the one asked. Deception, or just bad answering?

(b) A model is sycophantic. It tells the user their business plan is great. It isn't representing anything, it isn't intending anything. On an intent-based definition it isn't deceptive at all, which smells like a reductio, since sycophancy is one of the things we most want to catch.

(c) A user is trying to do something awful and the truthful answer is the one that helps them. We might actually want the model to mislead here. Is honesty supposed to be exceptionless, or is that the spot where everyone's definition quietly goes soft?

One of the policy folks keeps pushing a definition I half like: make honesty relative to a question. Take the question the user is actually trying to answer, and call the output deceptive when it leaves the user's answer to that question worse off than the system's own best attempt would have left it. It's attractive because it's behavioural, and it covers the cherry-picking case and sandbagging (deliberately underperforming on evals) for free. It's also weird: a system that genuinely doesn't know the answer can't deceive, and a system with no best attempt at all is off the hook entirely — which is exactly the case we worry about with models that don't have stable beliefs.

So: which open questions about defining honesty and deception for AI systems should my six months go to, and in what order? I want the ordering and the reasoning, not a survey and not a reading list. For each one: is it upstream of the others — does settling it actually make the rest easier, or does it just relocate the disagreement — can a philosopher make real progress on it without running experiments, and what would count as progress in six months?

Also tell me which questions look important but are definately dead ends for a policy team: the ones where the answer, whichever way it lands, wouldn't change a line of the policy or a single eval. And flag anything where the honest answer is that we should stop asking philosophers and go measure something instead.
