Write a satirical piece in the form of an in-universe mail-order catalogue, roughly 700–800 words, from a fictional firm that supplies explanatory equipment to philosophers of biology and of mind. The target is a style of explanation, not a person or a doctrine: the habit of accounting for a phenomenon by positing something that was never assembled, and its mirror image, the habit of skipping the intermediate levels altogether.

Background you may take as given, and should treat as the shared knowledge of the firm and its customers. A crane is a sub-process that does its lifting out of machinery already in place: it builds the next thing from parts left lying around by the previous thing, so nothing has to be lifted from outside the system. A skyhook is a lifting device that works from above and presupposes no prior machinery at all — it accounts for a phenomenon by invoking something that came ready-made. Greedy reductionism is the shortcut in the other direction: it declines to construct the intermediate levels and explains the top level directly in terms of the bottom one, so it, too, does no assembling.

The catalogue's register is deadpan commercial: cheerful, boastful, faintly bureaucratic, with prices, part numbers, a warranty, a returns policy, testimonials, and a single complaint from a dissatisfied customer printed together with the firm's reply. Never wink at the reader. The piece should read as parody only because the merchandise is impossible and the copy is entirely unembarrassed about why.

Requirements:

1. At least six distinct products, each standing for a recognizable family of explanatory moves that writers in these fields have actually made — a force that steers evolution toward complexity, intentionality treated as primitive, normativity as underived, consciousness as an extra ingredient added to an otherwise complete system, laws of nature as commands issued to matter. Do not name any real person, work, or position, and do not merely recycle those five illustrations; at least three entries should be your own inventions within the same families. Each entry must specify, in marketing terms, the exact explanatory task the item performs and why no crane could perform it.

2. A warranty clause built around the notion of a load-bearing mystery: the item functions only for as long as no one asks how it functions, and the guarantee is voided the moment a customer explains it.

3. One customer complaint alleging that the device turned out to be a crane in disguise, followed by the firm's reply, which should concede nothing while making the point that anything whose operation can be described has already been reclassified — and that this is precisely why the hooks ship without instructions.

4. A budget line from the same firm, aimed at customers who find the intermediate levels tedious: the shortcut that delivers the finished phenomenon with no assembly whatever. A reader should be able to see, without being told, that the cheap line and the premium line promise exactly the same thing.

5. A closing note to the trade, in which the firm addresses the awkward question of what lifts the first crane, and answers it by declining to answer.

Constraints: no citations and no names of real people, books, or doctrines; no quotations; no authorial commentary, footnotes, or breaking character. A specialist in the debate should find the parody fair — every product should correspond to a move someone has actually made, and the humour should come from the structure of the move rather than from the absurdity of the customer. A reader who has never encountered these terms should still be able to follow the piece.
