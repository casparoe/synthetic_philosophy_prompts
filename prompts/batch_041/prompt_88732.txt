You are to act as an expert adviser on decision theory and institutional strategy — someone fluent in the analysis of one-shot coordination problems, in the differences between causal, evidential, and rule-based (updateless) reasoning about other agents, and in how commitment devices actually function inside organisations. Work the problem through carefully before you write anything: the first formulation that comes to mind will be the textbook one, and the numbers below are chosen so that the textbook answer is not obviously right here. Everything you need is in this message. There is nothing to look up, and I do not want citations, reading lists, or appeals to what some named author has already concluded.

I chair the governing board of the Brackenridge Institute, a small independent research institute that is one of the nine member institutes of the Aldworth Consortium. Our board meets on Thursday and I have to circulate a written decision memo beforehand.

The Halvorsen Trust has announced a one-off competition, open only to the nine consortium institutes. Its terms are these:

- If exactly one eligible institute files a letter of intent by the deadline — sixty days from today — that institute receives €6 million over four years.
- If two or more file, the grant lapses entirely. The Trust then pays out a consolation pool of €1.8 million, divided equally among the institutes that filed.
- Preparing and filing costs an institute roughly €250,000 in staff time and foregone work, win or lose.
- Net of that cost, the outcomes are: sole applicant, +€5.75m; two applicants, +€650k each; three, +€350k each; all nine, −€50k each. In general, if k institutes file, each nets 1.8/k − 0.25, in millions.
- Nothing in the rules is negotiable, the deadline will not move, and the Trust will not disclose who has filed until the deadline has passed.

All nine boards know all of this, and all nine are meeting this month. We are the smallest and least well endowed of the nine, and the others know that €6 million would matter more to us than to them.

Two years ago all nine institutes signed a consortium pledge: no member will file a competing application to a single-award scheme without first offering the other eight a lottery to designate a single applicant. The pledge carries no sanction; its only force is that we expect to keep working together for years.

Two of my trustees have already made up their minds. The first says the arithmetic is simple — filing pays against almost any assumption about the others, since even a nine-way pile-up costs us only €50,000 — so we should file and stop moralising. The second says the pledge settles it: we offer the lottery, and if the others decline to join, we abstain; a board that breaks its own written word over €6 million cannot be trusted with anything else. I need to know which of them is closer to right, or what a third position looks like.

Please draft the memo. It should:

1. Open with a one-sentence recommendation, then give the concrete sequence of actions that follows from it: what we do this week; what we propose to the other eight and in what form; what we do if they refuse, or if only some of them join; and what we do in the final forty-eight hours.
2. Set out the structure of the problem in your own terms — who the decision-makers are, what each is choosing between, and whether our reasoning about the other eight is a reason to treat our own choice as evidence about theirs. Be explicit about whether you are reasoning causally, evidentially, or as if all nine boards were running the same decision procedure, and say what each of those commitments recommends here.
3. Treat the numbers as load-bearing. Where your recommendation depends on a probability, an equilibrium, or an assumption about how the others will reason, state it and show the calculation; where the recommendation would flip under a plausible alternative value, say so.
4. Give the strongest version of the case against your own recommendation. In particular, state as fairly as you can the argument that the pledge is now cheap talk and that only expected value matters.
5. Assume for the argument that each board's utility is linear in money; if you think that assumption is doing real work, note where.

Before drafting, spend a few paragraphs enumerating the candidate recommendations and testing each against the two trustees' objections. Then write the memo itself as a clean document addressed to the board: formal, no more than 1,200 words, no hedging preamble, no citations, and no discussion of the decision-theory literature as such. End with the single sentence a trustee who disagrees should have to argue with.
