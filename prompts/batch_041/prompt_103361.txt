You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist specializing in the intentional stance, the attribution of propositional attitudes, and the question of machine mentality. I want you to reason carefully and from first principles rather than pattern-matching to stock positions: before you commit to any answer, brainstorm in a scratch section, list the competing hypotheses, and make your assumptions explicit as you go. Treat the pattern of judgments I give you as the data; where you are tempted to lean on what some well-known author is supposed to have held, argue from the cases instead.

I have a colleague — call her R — who works on machine cognition. She describes herself as broadly Dennettian: she thinks belief-talk about non-human systems is legitimate and not a mere useful fiction, and she is comfortable ascribing beliefs to creatures most people would not. She does, however, part ways with the fully instrumentalist reading of the intentional stance. Here is how she rules on a series of cases, in her own words as best I can reconstruct them:

- A domestic thermostat: no beliefs. It is about the room's temperature only in the thinnest sense; nothing is represented for the system, so nothing is believed.
- A chess engine that searches the game tree and plays strongly: no beliefs, even though it is natural to say it thinks its opponent will castle. The search description is complete; the belief attribution picks out no state and adds nothing.
- A honeybee that dances to signal a food source: has beliefs. She will say the bee believes the food is roughly three hundred metres north, and can be mistaken about it.
- A foraging fish: has beliefs, on the same reasoning.
- A multinational firm whose pricing moves are predicted well by treating it as believing its rival will cut prices: no beliefs — even though she grants that the intentional stance is here predictively indispensable. She is adamant that indispensability is not sufficient.
- A human adult: has beliefs, uncontroversially.
- A large language model that predicts the next token and answers questions fluently: no beliefs. She calls fluent belief-ascribing talk about it a convenient overlay on a text-completion process.

Now the case I actually want your prediction about. A team has built an AI agent, B. B is built on a language model, but it maintains a persistent, human-readable store of propositions it has committed to — for instance, entries such as the user's sister is called Mara, or the deadline is Tuesday — each tagged with a source and a time. When input arrives that conflicts with a stored entry, B revises or deletes the entry; B can be made to output the store, to say why a given entry is there, and to flag the entries it regards as uncertain; and its answers are generated by conditioning on the contents of the store. B has no sensors and no body; it reads and writes text only. A variant, B*, is behaviorally indistinguishable from B — same inputs, same outputs, same revisions — but has no explicit store: the same dispositions are spread across the weights of a single network.

Please do the following, in this order. (1) Predict what R will say about B and about B*, and state your confidence. (2) Identify the rule, or small set of rules, she appears to be running — the criterion or criteria that reproduce all seven verdicts above. (3) Name at least two other plausible rules that fit those seven verdicts just as well but differ from the one you first identified, and say where each of them comes from. (4) For every candidate rule, say what it predicts about B and about B*. (5) Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasons for preferring the rule you think she is really running. (6) Finally, name the further case — an example or a discriminating experiment — that would best tell these rules apart, and say honestly which of her commitments, if any, that case would force her to give up.

I am not looking for a survey of what has been written about machine beliefs, and I do not want a reading list or citations: reason from the verdicts as given. What I want is a forecast, the rules behind it, and the test that would settle them.
