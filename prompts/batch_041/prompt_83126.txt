I sit on my state's Board of Pardons and Paroles as one of its two citizen members — I taught shop at a vocational high school for thirty-one years and have no legal or philosophical training, though I have read a fair amount on my own since we began hearing capital cases. On Thursday the board votes on the recommendation the governor has formally asked us for in the case of a man who has been under sentence of death for nineteen years. The legislature abolished the death penalty six years ago, but only for crimes committed after the repeal, so he and seven other men remain under the old law; our statute directs us to weigh the interests of the victim's family, the safety of the public, and the possibility that the prisoner can be reformed, and to send the governor a written opinion stating what we advise. The facts are not in dispute. He was twenty-two and drunk when he shot a night clerk during a robbery; the jury that convicted him fixed the sentence at death; by every account he has been a quiet and useful prisoner since; he has now written to us himself asking that we not stand in the way of the execution; and the victim's two surviving children have written to opposite effect, one asking us to let it proceed, the other asking us to commute the sentence so that she can stop attending hearings. I want to draft the opinion I will circulate to the other four members before the vote, and I want it to do something I have not managed in my own head, which is to keep two questions apart: what we owe this particular man, and what we are entitled to say about the death penalty at all, given that it is no longer our law and that we are not the legislature.

What I keep coming back to is an essay Albert Camus wrote against the guillotine in the late 1950s, which I read years ago and will now mangle for you. His argument, as I carry it around, is not that killing is simply wrong, but that the state's killing is a different thing from any killing it punishes. The condemned man is told the date and then held in a cell for months knowing it, which is something no murderer in private life does to his victim, and it was the waiting rather than the drop that Camus thought made the practice monstrous. He also held that the deterrent argument collapses on its own terms: executions are no longer public, so whatever example they set is set in secret; death is the fate of everyone, so threatening it adds little; and murders of passion are not deterred by anything — so what remains is not punishment but the state's quiet disposal of someone it has decided is beyond repair. Then he made a point that sits oddly beside all this: in a society that no longer believes, the older justification for taking a life — handing the condemned over to a judgment he cannot escape but might yet be reconciled with — has evaporated, and the state is now pronouncing an absolute sentence without any authority that anyone holds absolutely. There is one further piece of the essay I remember, and it bears directly on our prisoner's own request: as a stopgap while full abolition was still being argued for, Camus suggested that a condemned man might be offered a drug he could administer himself. I am not asking you to tell me what Camus really said or to cite him; I am asking whether the argument, as I have it, has any grip on a case like ours. One of my colleagues has been needling me on precisely this and I cannot answer him. If it is wrong for the state to kill a defenseless man who has been in its power for nineteen years, why is it any better for the state to keep him in a cell until he dies of old age, which is also a death the state has arranged for him in advance, only more slowly? He says my position proves too much, and that followed out it either requires letting him go or amounts to choosing the gentler-looking of two ways of killing him.

Please draft the opinion itself, about two pages, addressed to the governor's office and written so that the two families can read it. It should state the recommendation in the first sentence — commute, deny, or commute on stated conditions — and give a time frame the governor's office can actually work with, since we are told the execution is scheduled for the spring. It should deal honestly with the strongest thing on the other side, namely that a jury of his peers fixed this sentence under a law that was valid when he broke it, and that a pardon board which second-guesses a lawful sentence commits a small mischief of its own. I would rather the document not rest on religious premises: one member of the board opposes the death penalty because he believes life is given and cannot be taken back by us, and while I respect that, I do not think it can carry a paper issued by a secular body, so the opinion should say why the argument has to be made on other ground. It should also face squarely what our prisoner's own request to die does and does not change about our authority to kill him, and whether the answer differs once the killing would be at his hand rather than the state's. Finally, please do not hedge at the end. Tell me what to advise, and if my colleague's objection about life in prison cannot be answered, say so in the memo rather than papering over it.
