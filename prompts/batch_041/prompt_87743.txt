I'm a novelist — literary fiction, four books in, and I've spent more of my life inside the guts of a single manuscript than I care to admit: moving scenes around, renaming people halfway through, keeping a story bible so I don't contradict myself 300 pages in. So I know exactly how a character gets made — they're a bundle of descriptions and backstory and tics and whatever the plot needs them to have been, and then somehow they become a person you can't change without the whole book collapsing. Which is why when a friend tried to explain Kripke to me over drinks — names as rigid designators, Hesperus is Phosphorus being necessarily true if it's true at all, this necessary a posteriori business — I kept nodding and thinking, isn't that just a character across drafts? So here's what I want. Take Kripke's picture of names and necessity and restate it in my vocabulary — drafts, versions, the story bible, continuity, retcon, canon, the gap between what a name points at and what its supposed to mean — as faithfully as you can, and don't flatter me by making my craft terms sound deeper than they are. Then give me the residue report: what carried over clean, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no home in how novelists talk at all. And tell me whether the leftovers are a hole in my way of thinking or a sign Kripke's picture is quietly assuming something an author never has to deal with.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
