The President will deliver an address to a joint session of the legislature on 14 October, the sixtieth anniversary of the last of the removals, and I am drafting it. Please write the address: roughly 1,300 words, ten minutes at the podium, in the register of a head of state speaking to a nation rather than of a philosopher writing an essay.

The occasion. Between 1938 and 1967, agencies of this state removed some fourteen thousand children from a minority community of about two hundred thousand people and placed them in institutions and with families of the majority, on the theory that the community's language and way of life were unfit for a modern nation. The Commission of Inquiry, reporting after six years, has recommended that the state apologize and that a reparations framework be enacted. The President signs the framework immediately after the address; both chambers vote on it the following week. The gallery will hold surviving members of the community and their families, several of whom have publicly rejected the framework as a settlement that will extinguish their claims. The chamber will hold members who maintain that no one now living is answerable for what officials of a previous constitutional order did. And the transcript will be read into the record of a case pending before the regional human rights court, whose registry has requested it.

What makes this hard, and what I want the address to do. The Commission distinguishes three things the state might do: acknowledge that the removals happened and were wrong; assume responsibility for their continuing effects; and apologize. My legal advisers are content with the first two and anxious about the third, since an admission of moral responsibility is easily read as an admission of legal liability. My deeper difficulty is not legal. The chair of the community's council told me privately that she does not want an apology from us: we are not the ones who took the children, and she would rather have an account, from the people who now hold power, of what they mean to do about a wrong they did not commit. So the question the address must answer is whether an apology offered by a state that did not do the thing is a genuine second-personal address to the wronged, or a performance staged for the gallery — a way of collecting the credit of remorse without incurring the costs of repair. The Cabinet has left the question to me, and I would rather the address take a position than split the difference.

So: if the answer is that the state can apologize, write an apology — unhedged, without the conditional and passive constructions that have become the genre's tell, and without apologizing for anyone's feelings. If the answer is that it cannot, then the address must say so as its central act, and the refusal must be a substantive moral claim rather than a procedural dodge, and it must leave the council's chair with something she can accept. Either way the address must not promise anything beyond the framework, must not be heard as a condition on the vote, and must not treat the wronged as material for the state's own moral improvement. It should be intelligible to someone watching at home who has never read the Commission's report, and it should not embarrass us before the court. It is a state document, not a lecture: no theorists, no citations.

Please write the address.
