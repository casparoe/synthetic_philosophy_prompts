I am reconstructing, for a graduate seminar on well-being, an argument that a student produced in response to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. I put it on the board, invited objections, and found I had none I believed. It runs as follows.

(1) Hedonism is a substantive thesis about well-being only if "pleasure" names a single property — one thing that all and only pleasures have in common; call this the one-tone assumption. Otherwise "maximize pleasure" is either an ill-formed injunction or a list of goods in disguise, with no unifying rationale.

(2) Whatever that property is, it must be either phenomenal (a felt quality present in every pleasure) or non-phenomenal (being liked or desired, or occupying some functional role).

(3) It is not phenomenal. Set side by side the pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of finally seeing the point of a proof, the pleasure of a joke landing, and the pleasure of a headache easing — the last of which appears to be a subtraction and nothing more. Nothing is felt in common across all four, and for every candidate quality there is a pleasure that lacks it. A hot bath can even be pleasant in one respect and scalding in another at once.

(4) Nor is it non-phenomenal. If pleasures are unified by being liked or wanted, what unifies them lies in the subject's attitudes rather than in the experiences; and we like these experiences because they are pleasant, so the account reverses the order of explanation. Cashing out the attitude without appeal to pleasure or well-being is either circular or changes the subject.

(5) So no property is shared by all and only pleasures.

(6) By (1), hedonism is therefore not a substantive thesis about well-being.

(7) The reasoning generalizes: emotions, memories, and beliefs are as heterogeneous as pleasures, so (5) and (6) prove there are no such kinds either. The student took this as confirmation — pleasure is one more pretend kind — but I cannot have it. I am no more prepared to say there are no emotions than to say "the absence of pain" is empty.

I am attached to (1) and to (3) in roughly equal measure, which is why I am stuck. So tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what giving it up costs. If you reject the one-tone assumption, say what unifies pleasures instead and why that is not a list with a label stuck on it; if you keep it, say what the shared quality is and how it survives the relief case and the mixed cases. You are a philosopher working on well-being and the metaphysics of mind: think the problem through carefully before you answer, then take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose from. No citations are needed — I want the argument, not the literature — and I would rather have your honest confidence than a hedge.
