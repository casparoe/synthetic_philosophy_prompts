I convene a graduate seminar that treats science fiction as a laboratory for problems in game theory and decision theory, and I am assembling a short anthology of original stories for the unit on interstellar deterrence. Please write me a story of roughly two thousand words built on the dark forest hypothesis, but I want it to earn its place by doing intellectual work rather than by rehearsing the familiar mood of cosmic dread, so work with a specific variant of the idea rather than the standard summary. In the usual telling, the galaxy is silent because concealment is cheap, detection is fatal, and the first civilization to be seen is the first to be destroyed, so the equilibrium is one of hiding. Set your story in a galaxy where that has turned out to be only half true: hiding fails in the long run for anyone who becomes technologically visible, and what actually maintains the silence is that any weapon capable of reaching and destroying a distant civilization also advertises the position of whoever fires it. The galaxy is then not a forest of hunters but a standoff among exposed marksmen, and the dramatic question becomes what it would take for one of them to judge the shot worth taking.

For the setting: a small listening station, far from its home world, crewed by a handful of people whose task is to watch a quiet sector and to obey a standing rule that nothing detected may ever be acknowledged. Include a cryptolinguist, a station commander who has begun to doubt the doctrine, and an astronomer whose instruments have turned up something anomalous. The story should turn on inference under uncertainty — no faster-than-light travel or communication, no omniscient narrator who simply knows how galactic sociology works, every exchange one-sided, every reply arriving to a civilization that may no longer exist, and the crew's own reports home themselves taking centuries to matter. I would like the argument to be reconstructible by the reader from what the characters say and do: someone who finishes the story should be able to state the standoff model and the reason it may be breaking down. I would also like at least one character to press the objection that the doctrine is unfalsifiable, that any pattern in the silence can be read as confirming it, with another character answering seriously. Do not resolve things with a revelation about who the hunters are, with a benevolent alien voice, or with a twist in which the humans turn out to be the monsters; end on a decision made with insufficient information, and let the reader feel the weight of the structure rather than the shock of a secret.

House style: no lectures delivered in dialogue, no infodumps about galactic history, technology kept internally consistent with the physics you have committed to, and an ending that is quiet rather than apocalyptic. Give it a title. The story should be legible to a reader who knows the formal literature on deterrence without quoting it or gesturing at it.
