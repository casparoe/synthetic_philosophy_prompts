I cover science and ideas, and I am at work on an essay about how the vocabulary of justice behaves in disputes over borders and jurisdiction, where the same act is a crime on one bank of a river and an act of war on the other. I keep returning to a cluster of fragments in Pascal's Pensées that I find gripping and, the longer I sit with them, opaque. I would like a careful reading rather than a summary, and I would rather you stay inside the text than place Pascal on a map of positions. In the section my translation titles "Justice and the Reason of Effects," he writes:

"On what shall man found the order of the world which he would govern? Shall it be on the caprice of each individual? What confusion! Shall it be on justice? Man is ignorant of it. ... Nothing, according to reason alone, is just itself; all changes with time. Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it. ... We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end."

A few fragments later:

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical."

He then concludes that the two must be joined, so that justice is backed by strength or strength is made to serve justice. In the same section he gives us a man on the far side of the water whom it is heroism rather than murder to kill, because that man's ruler and mine are quarreling; he gives us children saying "This dog is mine" as the beginning and image of all usurpation; and he remarks that when the question is whether to kill so many Spaniards, the only judge is an interested party, so there ought to be a third who is disinterested.

What I cannot make cohere is this. If man is ignorant of justice, and custom creates the whole of equity, and the authority of law rests on its acceptance rather than on its merit, then in what sense is might without justice tyrannical? Tyrannical as judged by what? The first half of the "Justice, might" fragment says it is right that what is just should be obeyed and necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. I want to know what work "right" is doing there, given everything that precedes it — whether it is a normative claim, a claim about fitness or propriety, or a concession that the word survives only as a piece of rhetoric.

Second, what does the section's title contribute? If there is a "reason of effects" — a justification that runs through consequences rather than first principles — does it give Pascal a way out of the circle, or does it only describe the circle from inside? And if the joining of justice and might is the answer, is that a solution or a renaming of the problem?

Third, the counsel to conceal the origin of law. Is that political advice, a description of how legitimacy actually operates, or an ironic unmasking? And note the awkward position of the writer: Pascal is himself tracing law back to its first principles, which he says destroys it. Is the fragment doing the thing it warns against, and does that self-reference change how I should read the warning?

Fourth, the disinterested third party. Given the anthropology of the rest of the section, who could occupy that position? Does the figure quietly reintroduce a standard of justice that the earlier fragments deny, or is disinterestedness itself just another effect?

Finally, tell me what the passage leaves genuinely undecided. I would like the strongest reading on which Pascal's position is coherent, the strongest on which it is not, and an honest account of which fragments each reading has to explain away. I am not interested in the standard line that this makes Pascal a proto-realist about power unless the text compels it; I want the internal mechanics.
