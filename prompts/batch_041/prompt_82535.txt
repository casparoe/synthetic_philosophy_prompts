I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the last four years I've sat as the lay member on the heritage and commemorations panel of a mid-sized English borough council. Our remit is narrow but strange: we approve or refuse plaques, museum labels, street renamings, and, occasionally, formal statements in which the council asserts a contested historical claim as fact. I've started to suspect that the panel runs on a doctrine none of us could state, and next month's agenda has a case I think will expose it, so I'd rather have a prediction and an analysis than a vote.

Here is our actual track record, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it. We approved a plaque for the legend that a medieval bridge was designed by a monk, even though the parish records name a documented mason, on the condition that the label say "according to local tradition." We approved, as plain fact, that a factory chimney was pulled down by its own workers during an 1897 dispute, on nothing but the testimony of three elderly residents and a photograph of the crowd, the firm's archive having burned. We approved, over a petition of some four thousand signatures, a statement that the borough's Victorian benefactor drew income from a slave-trading firm, because the ledgers were unambiguous. In the case of a wartime shelter collapse, where the official inquiry recorded twelve deaths and local memory insists on forty, we declined to amend the figure but funded a memorial that describes the day without a number; our historian later showed that the forty figure first appears in a 1950s anniversary feature. We refused entirely a request to commemorate a witches' well once it was demonstrated that the well, the witch, and the curse all originate in a novel published in 1974. And we approved a claim that a local suffragette was force-fed in prison, resting on her own later memoir and nothing else. Our clerk likes to say that the record answers what happened and the borough answers what it means, though I've never seen us apply that line consistently.

The new case is this. A residents' association wants the council to declare as fact that a 1919 rent strike turned violent when police fired into a crowd and killed a demonstrator. The official inquiry found that no shots were fired and attributed the deaths to a crush at a locked gate. One contemporary newspaper report, however, claims shots were fired, and the family tradition in three households is emphatic; the historian advising us has satisfied himself that this tradition derives neither from that newspaper report nor from any later dramatisation or piece of fiction. The association is asking for both the factual declaration and a plaque. I'd like you to predict how we will rule if we are being consistent with our own record, to give me the most defensible reading of the rule we have actually been running, to set out at least two rival readings that fit the same history equally well and say what each of them predicts here, and to name the single further case you would put on a future agenda in order to decide between them. Please write your answer in British English.
