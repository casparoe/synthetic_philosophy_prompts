After some thirty years as an experimental physicist—statistical mechanics, and latterly the thermodynamics of small systems—I have retired and, perhaps imprudently, taken up the philosophy of science in earnest. I have settled on a proposition and would like your candid credence in it rather than a tour of the literature: that Humeanism about laws of nature is correct. By this I mean that the laws of a world supervene upon, and are nothing over and above, the totality of that world's non-modal, non-nomic facts—its Humean mosaic—the laws being whatever systematization best balances simplicity against strength, or some suitably refined compression-based surrogate for that balance. The anti-Humean insists instead that laws govern, or are grounded in primitive modality, powers, or relations of necessitation; I want to know how likely it is that the anti-Humean is wrong. Please give me a single number between 0 and 1, and then decompose it into the sub-questions on which it actually rests, assigning each a rough probability with one line of justification, and showing how the terms combine into the final figure. I would expect the decomposition to include at least: whether the governing conception is genuinely incoherent or merely unparsimonious; whether the simplicity–strength tradeoff can be made determinate without covert appeal to modal or pragmatic facts; whether a purely non-modal base can underwrite the counterfactual and explanatory work we ask of laws; and whether the supervenience base is itself well-defined, i.e. whether such things as forces, dispositions, and symmetries can be characterized without modality creeping back in.

To keep this from decaying into a recitation of the standard positions, I should like you to fold in a case I have been turning over myself. Imagine a world whose complete non-modal history is finite and which, as it happens, admits two systematizations exactly tied on simplicity and strength: one yielding a small set of local, time-translation-invariant regularities, and another yielding a single global regularity that encodes a vast and otherwise inexplicable correlation between distant regions. The first feels to us like a world with laws and the second like a world with one enormous coincidence; yet on the Humean account nothing beyond the balance of simplicity and strength is available to break the tie, and that balance is silent. I want to know whether this tie-break problem should lower your credence in Humeanism, whether you think some further Humean resource—a preferred language, a particular compression measure, a Best System permitted to be partial or to privilege locality—dissolves it, and by how much it moves the number.

Finally, tell me which single sub-question your figure is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument—ideally something a working physicist might actually encounter, rather than a fresh intuition pump—would shift it most. I am less interested in being told that the matter is unsettled than in seeing precisely where the weight sits, so that I can tell whether my own residual anti-Humean sympathies, which I suspect are simply the habits of someone who spent a career treating equations as though they pushed things around, are doing any real work.
