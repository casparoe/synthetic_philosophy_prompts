I want a sestina about middle knowledge, and I'd like the form to carry the doctrine rather than merely illustrate it. The doctrine: prior to any decision to create, God knows what each possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances it might be placed in; that knowledge doesn't make the actions happen (nothing has yet been decreed), and the creature's own nature doesn't make them happen either (they are free) — hence the standing objection that nothing whatever makes these conditional truths true. So let the six end-words be settled before the poem begins and return in every stanza regardless of what the circumstances of that stanza are; let the poet's only freedom be the ordering and placing of them, which stands for the decree, arranging circumstances without settling what anyone would do in them; and let the envoi, where all six crowd into three lines, be the single act of knowing all of them at once, before anything exists. Keep it concrete — one invented person and one particular thing they would or wouldn't have done — rather than a poem assembled from jargon. Don't settle the objection at the end, and don't paper over the disanalogy: I am the one choosing the end-words, and the doctrine says nothing of the kind about God, so let that awkwardness be visible rather than hidden.

Give me the poem, then a section headed 'Notes on the choices', of about 150 words, on where the form and the doctrine meet and where they come apart.
