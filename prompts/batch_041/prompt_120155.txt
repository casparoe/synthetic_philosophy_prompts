For forty years I worked as a physicist, and since retiring I have been teaching myself philosophy — slowly, and with the clumsiness of a man accustomed to questions that are settled by measurement. One puzzle in decision theory has held me up for months, and I have concluded that what I want is not another proposed solution but the specification that any solution would have to satisfy.

The puzzle is this. Expected-value reasoning instructs me to choose whatever maximises value multiplied by probability, summed over the possibilities. It is then a short step to cases in which an outcome of enormous value, arising with a probability so minute that no observation could ever distinguish it from zero, still outweighs any finite loss incurred by acting on it; and from there to a case in which a stranger may extract from me whatever he pleases by threatening a harm of inconceivable magnitude on a probability of equally inconceivable smallness. As far as I can follow the discussion, one either embraces these conclusions, or patches the theory with thresholds and caps, or abandons the whole manner of reasoning — and none of the three has convinced me. Having spent much of my working life on series whose sums depend on the order in which one adds them up, I suspect the trouble lies in what we are asking of the theory rather than in any particular proposal.

So write me the desiderata. What must a decision theory deliver in order to count as adequate here? I want the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it: the intuitive verdicts it may not simply overrule; the structural properties it must preserve, whether of consistency among preferences, of comparability between options, of the treatment of infinite and of vanishingly small magnitudes, of behaviour when probabilities are unknown rather than known, or of the plain requirement that the theory tell an agent what to do; and the distinctions it must not collapse. Mark which of these are genuine demands and which are merely the habits of one school. I am chiefly interested in whether any pair of them is jointly unsatisfiable — if the impossibility lies in the requirements themselves, that is the result I most want, and I would rather have it than a verdict on which extant theory prevails. Tell me also which single requirement excludes the most candidates, since I suspect I have been treating a parochial demand as a universal one.

Kindly write in British English; forty years of journals have left me set in my spellings. And I am not asking for a reading list — I should rather have your reasoning than your references.
