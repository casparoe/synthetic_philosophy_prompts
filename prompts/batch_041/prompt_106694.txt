Our sixth-form philosophy society has spent the last two meetings on a handout about the impossibility of agreeing to disagree: the claim that two people who begin from the same probability judgements, update correctly on their evidence, and each know what the other now thinks about some question cannot knowingly end up attaching different probabilities to it. At the end of the last session Rowan and Amara carried the argument out into the corridor, and I never established who came off better. The occasion was our school's chances in the county cup, which they had both followed through the same five matches: Amara put the chance of our winning it at about four in ten, Rowan at about seven in ten, and each professed to find the other's number inexplicable. Rowan's line was that this is precisely what the result forbids — since they had watched the same matches and are both careful reasoners, at least one of them must be mishandling the evidence — and that more generally any persistent disagreement between well-informed people has to be a defect in one of them. He added that he did not regard the shared starting point as a hidden extra assumption at all, on the grounds that a difference in starting probabilities can only reflect a difference in what the two people had already lived through, which is simply a difference in evidence again.

Amara's reply was that the result is a conditional and not a verdict: it says that if two people share a starting point, then common knowledge of what each thinks forces agreement, which tells you only where a disagreement must have come from — a difference in starting points, a difference in evidence, or a failure of common knowledge — and locating the source is not the same as convicting either party of irrationality. She also insisted that there is no single rational starting point for them to share, since the obvious recipe (treat every possibility as equally likely until evidence arrives) yields different answers depending on how one describes the possibilities, so two rational people may begin in different places with neither at fault. Rowan then produced two further points he had read and which I was in no position to assess. First, he said the framework has a betting counterpart: if two people share a starting point, they cannot both regard a bet between them, made on the strength of private information, as profitable — and, conversely, that this impossibility of agreeing on a bet's profitability holds exactly when a shared starting point exists, which he took to show that the shared starting point is a genuine and checkable constraint rather than an idle stipulation. Second, he said the result also implies that two honest reasoners who simply keep announcing their probabilities to one another must converge, so disagreement is unstable and Amara's position is untenable for anyone willing to keep talking. Amara answered that the convergence works only if both parties carve up the possibilities in the same way and take the same description to be the question at issue, and that in their own case they had never agreed on what was being priced at all — she meant the chance of winning the cup, whereas he may have been pricing the chance of at least reaching the final — and that arriving at a common number would in any case not make the number correct.

I should like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general verdict: which of the claims each of them made are correct as they stand, which are overreachings, and what both of them are leaving out. Please be explicit about what the result does and does not establish, and about whether the betting counterpart helps Rowan or in fact cuts against him.
