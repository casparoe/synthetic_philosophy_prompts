I chair the board of a federation of about thirty worker co-ops (two farms, a bakery, a machine shop, a print shop, a couple of software outfits, a few others) and next month we vote on whether to fund a two-year pilot of an internal planning system some of our members built. Here's the setup, roughly. Every transfer between member co-ops -- grain from the farm to the bakery, machined parts from the shop to whoever needs them, hours from the software co-op -- gets a price off a central ledger. Those prices aren't bargained; a bureau computes them from input-output coefficients that each co-op submits every quarter, running an iterative solver until supply and demand for each category roughly balance. There are around 400 intermediate categories. Adding a new category takes a board vote and about a year to get into the ledger, so in the meantime people improvise and keep private tallies. The bureau audits maybe one submission in twenty, and a co-op that lowballs how much of a scarce input it uses gets charged less for it. Nobody can go bankrupt -- losses get absorbed federation-wide -- and nobody's pay or bonus is tied to their own co-op's surplus. Consumer goods stay as they are: money for most things, but housing and the big durables go by waiting list and lottery, so there's no price there at all. One board member, a lapsed economist, says this is Lange's answer to Mises with better arithmetic and that it'll fail for the reason Hayek gave -- not because the equations can't be solved but because the knowledge those prices are supposed to carry doesn't exist anywhere in a form anyone can submit, and because a number nobody has to act on isn't doing the work a real price does. Another member says we're small enough that the bureau can basically just ask everyone what they need, so the dispersed-knowledge objection is a problem for national economies and not for us. The co-ops pushing it say the pilot itself will tell us whether it works, and they've offered to run it on just eight co-ops first.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Land on something concrete -- approve, approve with conditions, or reject -- and if it's not a flat no, say what the conditions are, who's responsible, and when we review. Take the strongest version of the losing side seriously instead of just telling the Hayek people they won, and be honest about where the disagreement is really located. I'd also like it to say what the pilot would actually teach us: what result counts as the system working, and what would count as the knowledge problem biting in a way that no amount of better software fixes. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I know the literature well enough and I'd rather have the argument. Keep it under two pages, no exec-summary bullet salad, and write it so the three co-ops that are on the fence can vote on it rather than just feel talked at.
