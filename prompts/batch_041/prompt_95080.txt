For the better part of a century philosophers have argued about Hempel's ravens and, so far as I can tell, nothing has been settled. The hypothesis is that all ravens are black; a black raven is an instance of it; and since that hypothesis is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens", a white shoe -- itself a non-black non-raven -- would seem to be an instance of it too, which is the paradox. The optimists accept that the shoe confirms, only very weakly; the pessimists deny that it confirms at all; a third party says the question is malformed because confirmation is comparative, so the shoe confirms only relative to a body of evidence that renders almost nothing likely; and a fourth holds that the equivalence condition simply cannot be kept alongside the intuition that irrelevant evidence confirms nothing. Each reply is well known to the others and none has moved anyone in decades. My own hunch is that this is not one dispute but two wearing a single name -- a real one about the logic of confirmation and a verbal one about whether "confirms" is to mean "raises the probability of" or "is an instance of" -- but I don't trust the hunch. Diagnose the impasse for me: is it verbal, a hidden formal question about which of our conditions on confirmation can be jointly satisfied, a clash of pre-theoretic intuitions about what confirmation is for, or several parties answering different questions under one heading? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than in a survey of the literature. Then, given that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress -- a new adequacy condition, a formal result about joint satisfiability, a stipulation about how to regiment "confirms", or a reason to think that none should be expected. I would also like your verdict on a case I have not seen discussed: a white shoe known to lie in a sealed box that no one ever opens, against the same shoe seen directly. If the optimists are right that both confirm, is the difference one of degree, of kind, or merely of our psychology? Please answer in British English.
