I am a retired computational neuroscientist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy, and I am applying for a single-term visiting fellowship; I need a two-page research proposal on which computations matter morally. My half-formed idea is this. Talk about the moral status of artificial or nonhuman systems keeps sliding between three different things — the algorithm a system is said to run, the physical implementation that runs it, and the description under which an observer credits it with running that algorithm at all — and I suspect that once one takes seriously how many different computations any physical system can be said to implement, depending on how its causal structure is carved, the appeal to what the system computes loses its grip and has to be replaced by something narrower. I would like the proposal to pin down which of the three is supposed to carry the moral weight and why; explain why the familiar functionalist answer and the familiar biological answer both leave that open; set out an approach in which I isolate a few candidate constraints — counterfactual sensitivity that survives redescription, integration, or a system's own use of its internal states — and test whether any of them is both relevant to moral status and determinate; say what would count as a real result and what would count as the project failing; and give a realistic plan for a single term, including where I would stop if the question proves too large for the time. I do not want citations or a literature review; I will supply those myself. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
