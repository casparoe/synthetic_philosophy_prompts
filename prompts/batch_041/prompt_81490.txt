I teach a jurisprudence seminar in which I pair Kafka's In the Penal Colony with the Hart–Fuller debate, and I want a reading of the story that uses Fuller's eight principles of legality as a genuine interpretive instrument rather than a checklist to be ticked off. The colony's apparatus kills a man who has never been told the charge against him; the officer who tends it is the last believer in a regime whose founding commandant is dead; the machine finally breaks down and destroys its devotee instead of granting him the illumination it gave its victims. I want you to ask, scene by scene, whether what we are shown is a legal system in Fuller's sense at all — where the story dramatises the demands of promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, constancy, and congruence between official action and declared rule, and where it makes those demands look like the wishful thinking of a theorist who has never watched a state die. Then press the other way: does the story also embarrass Hart, by suggesting that a system can be perfectly positivist in form and still be nothing but an instrument of terror? And where does Kafka resist the jurisprudential reading altogether — where is the story about guilt, embodiment, or writing rather than about legality? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, that ends by saying what the story adds to the argument which the exchange itself cannot supply, and that does not pretend the eight principles were written with this colony in mind.
