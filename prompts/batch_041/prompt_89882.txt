I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized software company, with no philosophy training to speak of. I've been asked to give the eulogy at a memorial for a colleague and close friend who died in March at thirty-six; we shared a payments team for six years, and outside work he spent most of his free time on a family of arguments he called infinite ethics, which he treated less as a hobby than as the thing that made his life make sense. His version of it, as he explained it to me more times than I can count, ran roughly like this. If the universe is spatially infinite, or if there is any real chance that some civilisation continues for infinitely long, then the total amount of value in the world is infinite; and once a total is infinite, adding or subtracting anything finite leaves it unchanged, so his death, a child's birth, a cure, a massacre each contribute the same finite amount to an infinite sum, which is to say, in the end, nothing. He thought that was only the beginning of the trouble. The deeper point, in his telling, was that comparisons themselves stop having answers: to say that one world is better than another when both contain infinitely much good, you have to choose some way of matching things up between them, and different defensible ways of matching give opposite verdicts, so the question of which world is better is not merely hard to answer but malformed. I have never been entirely sure the two halves of that view fit together, and he always insisted they did. He found the whole thing liberating rather than bleak, and he lived accordingly: he genuinely declined to rank his projects, his friendships, or his years against one another, and he would not say that one cause or one person mattered more than another.

What I need is a eulogy of five or six minutes for a room of roughly sixty people. His parents will be there, and they are devout in a way I am not, and they will be expecting some acknowledgment that he is at peace. A dozen people from the reading group he ran will be there too, and they knew this material far better than I do and will notice immediately if I quietly help myself to a comparison he denied. The remaining forty are coworkers who are simply grieving and should not have to sit through a seminar. The difficulty is that the standard equipment of a eulogy is exactly the equipment he rejected. I cannot say that his life added up to more good than harm, or that the world is poorer without him, or that his work will keep paying off, or that his death is a loss in some larger ledger; and if I say none of those things I am not sure I have a eulogy at all. So please do not hand me a menu of approaches and leave the choice to me. Commit to one line: say in a few sentences, with reasons, where the significance of a life lives if it does not live in a sum or a ranking, answer the obvious objection that I am smuggling back in precisely the comparisons he denied, and then write the speech on that basis, in plain spoken language, without jargon and without quoting his own writing back at his friends. If the speech can only work by taking some liberty with his view, tell me at the end where it does so and how large the liberty is.
