I have been reading about the color phi phenomenon and the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction that Dennett draws in Consciousness Explained, and I would like to know how much of the following account I can trust. It is the gist of a long essay on time and the brain by a science writer whose work I generally respect, and I have reproduced it as faithfully as I can:

Two differently colored dots are flashed in quick succession at two positions a few degrees apart. Subjects do not report two dots. They report a single dot that travels across the gap and changes color partway through its journey, and, remarkably, they report the color change as occurring before the second dot was presented at all. Dennett takes this to settle a long-standing dispute about the timing of consciousness. The visual system cannot have known what color the second dot would be until it appeared, yet the experience of the color change is dated to an earlier moment, so the brain must wait for the second stimulus and then assemble the whole episode, motion and color change together, presenting it to us as something that has already happened. Dennett calls this the Stalinesque model, after Stalin's show trials, in which the record is falsified before anyone is allowed to see it: the brain edits the experience before it reaches consciousness, so what we take to be a real-time stream is really a finished film spliced together after the fact. The rival Orwellian model, on which the experience is genuine but the memory of it is rewritten afterwards, is ruled out by the experiment, because subjects place the color change too early. The upshot, for Dennett, is that there is no fact of the matter about when a given content enters experience, and therefore that consciousness as we ordinarily conceive of it, a single inner show running in real time, is an illusion.

My instinct is that the description of the effect is more or less right and that the trouble, if there is any, lies in the philosophical gloss, but I do not know the literature well enough to judge. Please go through the account sentence by sentence and tell me what a specialist in the area would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, and what is actually wrong, and in particular whether the experiment does the work the writer claims for it, how Dennett himself uses the Orwellian and Stalinesque labels and how he thinks they are related, and whether the concluding claim about consciousness follows from anything true in the account.
