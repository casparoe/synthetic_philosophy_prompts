I am producing a limited-run podcast on the formal prehistory of artificial life, and the guest for our fourth episode is a computational biologist who has spent the past decade building and defending an extended version of the typogenetics system that Douglas Hofstadter introduced in Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979). In the original system, strands are strings over the four letters A, C, G and T; enzymes are strings of amino acids, each amino acid being specified by a duplet of letters; and an enzyme acts on a strand by searching for the duplet that encodes its first amino acid and then executing a fixed sequence of operations — cutting, deleting, switching to the complementary strand, moving left or right, copying, inserting — as dictated by the remainder of its amino-acid sequence. Hofstadter's point was that some strands, once translated and their enzymes allowed to act, end up producing copies of themselves, and that this loop is supposed to illuminate the relationship between syntactic manipulation and semantic self-reference, and thereby the DNA–Gödel isomorphism.

My guest has gone further. She has built what she calls second-order typogenetics, in which the duplet-to-amino-acid mapping is not fixed but is itself encoded on a designated master strand, so that the code can be rewritten by the very enzymes it specifies. She claims that this modification makes the system a better model of the origin of the genetic code, and that it also sharpens the Gödel analogy, because the code becomes an object that the system can in principle represent and modify — a formal system that can talk about its own syntax. I am sympathetic but unconvinced, and I want the interview to be genuinely adversarial rather than celebratory.

Please draft eight to ten questions for me, each followed by a short follow-up that I can deploy if she gives the answer I expect. Order them so that the pressure accumulates. At a minimum, I want questions that press on the following, though you should add others if they sharpen the sequence:

1. Whether self-replication in ordinary typogenetics is really self-reference in the Gödelian sense or merely a fixed-point construction, and what would have to be true of second-order typogenetics for the stronger claim to hold.
2. Whether making the code mutable genuinely deepens the analogy or simply relocates the fixed point from the strand to the code-plus-strand pair, so that the same explanatory gap reappears one level up.
3. The genotype–phenotype relation in typogenetics: the enzyme is read directly off the strand, with no ribosome, no transfer RNA and no separation of template from catalyst. Ask whether that directness is a virtue, because it isolates the logic of self-reference from biological contingency, or a vice, because it makes replication too easy and leaves the system unable to distinguish a self-replicator from a mere self-copier.
4. Whether second-order typogenetics can support open-ended evolution or only a bounded space of code variants, and what that implies for her claim about the origin of the genetic code.
5. The role of the arbitrary duplet-to-amino-acid mapping. If the mapping is arbitrary, where does semantic closure come from; if it is not arbitrary, what constrains it?
6. Whether the system's self-replicators are robust to the kinds of noise that real self-replicating systems face — misincorporation, parasitic strands, resource limitation — and whether robustness or fragility is the better sign that the model has captured something real.
7. What exactly she takes the Gödel analogy to be an analogy between: a strand and a Gödel number, an enzyme and a proof, self-replication and a self-referential sentence, or something else. Press her to commit.
8. Whether the fact that typogenetics is a designed system, with a designer-chosen alphabet and mapping, undermines its use as a model of how self-reference could emerge in a system that was not designed for it.

For each follow-up, key it to the answer she is most likely to give, and make the follow-up harder than the original question rather than merely a request for elaboration. Please write in British English, and do not include questions that ask her to summarise her book or to explain what typogenetics is, since the audience will have had a primer.
