A colleague of mine who works on AI governance has been defending the idea of a coherent extrapolated volition — in his telling, the preference profile that a person, or a whole population, would converge on if it knew more, reasoned better, and had longer to grow up together — against the charge that treating such a thing as authoritative is paternalism in fancy dress. His argument leans on an analogy I have seen used in several other contexts, and I would like it audited rather than argued with.

The analogy is the Ulysses contract. Odysseus knows that the Sirens' song will make him want to steer for the rocks; he therefore has his crew bind him to the mast and instructs them to disregard, and if anything tighten, his later entreaties. On my colleague's reading, this shows that we already grant authority to a person's better-informed preferences over the preferences he will actually have at the moment of choosing, and that binding an agent — or a civilization — to what it would want on reflection is continuous with an ordinary, uncontroversial practice rather than a new species of coercion. He anticipates the objection that the idealization is unauthorized: he concedes that Odysseus bound himself, whereas a parent who overrides a child's present wishes for the sake of her later good is not acting on the child's authorization at all, and he says this is a strength rather than an embarrassment — authorization is not what makes the practice legitimate.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I cannot yet say precisely where it slips. So please audit it on its own terms, with the following pieces:

1. A correspondence table. For each of the two source cases — Ulysses, and the parent with the child — say what plays the role of the present self, the later or idealized self, the act of binding, the party with standing to enforce the binding, and the preference that gets overridden. Then line those elements up against the extrapolated-volition proposal, stating explicitly what would have to correspond to what for the analogy to go through.

2. The breakdowns. Where does the mapping fail? Separate the disanalogies that bear on the intended conclusion from those that are merely picturesque, and say why the ones you set aside do not matter here.

3. The smuggled freight. Which features of the Ulysses story does the analogy quietly import that the extrapolated-volition proposal does not supply — determinacy of the later preference, ex ante self-authorization, a single unified will, prior knowledge of what the temptation will be — and is any of them doing the justificatory work?

4. The verdict on the conclusion. Does my colleague's conclusion — that idealization of this kind is legitimate because continuous with a practice we already accept — survive the disanalogies? If it does not, say what would have to be true either of the idealized preferences or of the authorizing subject for a repaired version of the argument to work, and name a better analogy if there is one.

Work from the structure of the cases; I am not after a reading list.

End with one final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <label> — <short clause>

where <label> is one of HOLDS, HOLDS-WITH-REPAIRS, or FAILS, and the clause names the disanalogy or missing condition that decides it.
