I'm part of a small forecasting club that meets in a pub back room once a month. We each submit probabilities on a set of questions (will X happen by Y, etc.) and the organiser scores us with a Brier-type rule. The wrinkle is that he's added a bonus for what he calls bold calls: if you give a probability below 0.2 or above 0.8 and you're right, you get extra points; if you're wrong, you lose the same. I've noticed that my honest credences cluster around 0.6 to 0.7 on most questions, so under the bonus I'd do better, in expectation, by reporting 0.81 on the ones where I'm 0.65 confident, and 0.19 on the ones where I'm 0.35. My mate says that's just gaming the scoring rule and that if I care about being accurate I should report what I actually believe. But another friend says the whole point of the club is to maximise our accuracy score, and if the scoring rule rewards boldness then boldness is what accuracy amounts to here. I definately feel there's a real distinction between the accuracy of my beliefs and the accuracy of my reported numbers, but I can't articulate it. I also worry that if I report 0.81 when I'm 0.65, I'm lying in a way that corrodes the club's whole purpose, even if the lies are systematically truth-tracking.

Could you answer as someone who knows both formal epistemology and the practical design of scoring rules? Start by brainstorming a few ways to frame what's going on, then tell me what I should actually do. I'd like you to seperate the question of what credence I should have from the question of what number I should report, and to say whether the bonus changes the former at all. If you think the organiser's bonus is a bad rule, explain what it gets wrong in terms of incentives and truth-conduciveness, and what a better rule would look like. Also, if you can, say something about whether my unease is a genuine epistemic norm or just a hang-up about fair play. I'm not asking for a reading list; I want the argument.
