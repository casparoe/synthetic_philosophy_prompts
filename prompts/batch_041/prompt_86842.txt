For the past decade I have covered nuclear strategy and arms control — second-strike forces, crisis stability, the offense-defense balance, the security dilemma, launch-on-warning — and I am now writing about the dark forest hypothesis, the argument that any civilization that reveals its location is destroyed, so that hiding, and striking first when a rival is detected, is the only rational policy. Restate that argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, then give me the residue report. Which elements carry over cleanly — the chain of suspicion as a security dilemma, technological explosion as a decisive offense advantage, the fatal broadcast as the forfeiture of a survivable second strike? Which require distortion, and precisely what gets bent in the process? And what has no counterpart in strategic thought at all, given that the tradition I know is built on signalling: on threats that can be communicated, read, and believed, and on adversaries who can be deterred rather than merely preempted? Treat the residue as the interesting part, and end with an explicit verdict: does it expose a blind spot in the vocabulary of deterrence, or does it show that the dark forest is a stipulation dressed as a derivation — one that assumes, as no strategist I have ever interviewed would, that every sufficiently advanced civilization reaches the same conclusion by the same reasoning?
