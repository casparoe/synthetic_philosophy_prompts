Write an in-universe labor arbitration award, roughly 800 words, issued by the Careenium Labor Relations Board in the matter of the Simmball Guild v. Simm Local 1.

Background for the frame: the careenium is the thought experiment in which a great many tiny magnetic balls careen around an enclosure, clumping into higher-level structures whose configuration tracks regularities outside the walls; the analogy's central question is which level does the pushing — the balls themselves, or the clumps they form. (Sources spell the two levels variously, e.g. simms or symms for the balls and simmballs or symmballs for the clumps; pick one spelling and stay with it.) In the dispute you are to invent, the Guild has filed a grievance claiming that the clumps — the symbols — are the genuine causal agents and that the balls merely execute their instructions; the Local has filed a counter-grievance claiming exclusive jurisdiction over all pushing, on the ground that every displacement in the enclosure is a ball-on-ball collision and that a clump is a pattern, not an agent.

Use the standard form: appearances, stipulated facts, findings, conclusions, remedy, and a short dissent. Keep it deadpan — no winking, no asides to the reader, no jokes that break the frame, and no quotation marks around anything from a real published work; every quoted phrase must be spoken by an invented witness, party, or board member. The comedy should come from getting the metaphysics right, so that a reader who has followed the downward-causation debate laughs rather than winces. The award must therefore engage, without settling them by equivocation, at least these four points. First, causal exclusion: if every motion is already fixed by the ball-level facts, any clump-level cause looks either redundant or identical with the ball-level cause. Second, the counterfactual test for causation, along with the objection that intervening on a clump while holding the balls fixed begs the question. Third, multiple realizability — build into the stipulated facts an experiment in which the balls are swapped out one at a time, the clump persists, and the Guild's expert argues this proves the clump cannot be the balls, while the Local's expert argues it proves only that the clump is a pattern. Fourth, the boundary problem: who draws the edge of a clump, and what follows for clump causation if the boundaries are drawn by an interpreter. Add a brief concurring note on gerrymandered clump boundaries.

The majority should rule, and the dissent should disagree, such that a specialist can identify which live position each opinion is defending. Do not let the board resolve the matter by declaring that both sides are right on their own level; if any opinion takes that line, it must argue for it, and the dissent must say what the line costs. Close with a remedy that follows from the majority's causal holding rather than from a joke about paperwork. The piece should not depend on, cite, or quote any real book or author; everything in it should be intelligible to a reader who knows only the thought experiment and the shape of the debate.
