I've been going back and forth with a friend about pure time preference, and we keep talking past each other, so I want your credence with the reasoning laid bare. The question isn't about market interest rates, inflation, or ordinary impatience about consumption, and it isn't about the discount we apply when a benefit might simply fail to materialize. It's this: strip away all uncertainty, hold the affected parties fixed, and ask whether a benefit or harm delivered later can be discounted relative to an identical one delivered sooner simply because it is later. My own credence that no such discount is ever rationally permissible — that pure time preference is rationally impermissible — is around 0.35, and I suspect my friend is near 0.85 for the opposing claim, but I care less about the number than about where the number comes from.

Please give me your credence and decompose it, and feel free to re-carve the joints if mine are wrong. At minimum I'd want terms for: (1) whether personal identity across time is robust enough that a future experience is mine in the sense that gives its utility an equal claim, and if a reductionist view of the self is correct, whether that pushes toward or away from discounting; (2) whether the intrapersonal case and the intergenerational case actually come apart — that is, whether the verdict for an individual discounting her own future could differ from the verdict for a policymaker discounting future generations, and if so which way; (3) whether the causal asymmetry between earlier and later — that earlier states can shape later ones but not the reverse, and that later people often owe their very existence to earlier choices — is a genuine justifying reason or merely a description of the order of things; (4) whether any reciprocity or contractarian condition (future people cannot reciprocate, cannot be parties to an agreement) has real purchase here or is doing illicit work; and (5) whatever else you think belongs. One line and a number for each term, then how they combine into the final figure, and which term the figure is most sensitive to. I'd also like your guess at where the gap between my 0.35 and my friend's 0.85 most plausibly lives — which single term is carrying the disagreement.

To make this less abstract, here's a case I invented and can't resolve. Two otherwise identical people each stand to receive the same benefit; one receives it in ten years, the other in a hundred. Neither knows anything about their circumstances that bears on the benefit's value, and we've stipulated away every uncertainty, including whether either will still exist — so the only difference is temporal distance. Now suppose I add a fact: the person at a hundred years exists only because of choices made by the person at ten, so the later claim is downstream of the earlier in a way the earlier is not downstream of the later. Does that existence-dependence give the earlier claim any genuine priority, or is it a red herring? If your answer to that moves your overall credence, tell me by how much, and tell me which single sub-question my 0.35 is most hostage to. Finally, what would have to happen — an argument, a case, a consistency result — to move your own number by more than 0.15, and in which direction?
