I'm a TA running the Friday discussion section of a 200-level philosophy of mind course — sixteen students, fifty minutes — and last week a student said, about a study we'd just walked through, that she knew the numbers said one thing but she didnt beleive it, and then got irritated when I said that was an odd thing to say, because I couldn't actually give her a reason. I want the whole next section built around that. So: design me a game they play where they hit the wall themselves before anyone says "Moore's paradox." It needs real stakes in the scoring — give everyone private information that's worth something — and at least one round where the only move that pays is to state a fact about the world together with a claim about whether they believe it, in a way they find they can't say out loud even though the whole sentence would be true. Don't let it collapse into a game about catching liars. Nobody hears the name of the paradox until the debrief, and the debrief has to line up what happened with the omissive and the commissive versions so they see a sentence can be true and still unsayable. Also be straight with me about how it'll blow up the first time (no budget, fifty minutes, and they'll game anything with points) and about what a game like this can't get across and I'll have to say myself.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items — setup and materials, rules and payoffs, the moment it bites, the debrief, and what'll go wrong plus what the activity can't reach — one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up.
