I'm a fifth-year PhD student in political theory, and in three weeks I'll present a paper at a small workshop on democratic legitimacy. I've argued the paper to death with my own cohort and I don't need help tightening the prose; I need a Q&A prep sheet. The room is small and I know roughly who's in it, and I'd rather walk in having rehearsed the objections I'll actually get than hope to improvise.

The thesis I'll defend is this. The case for universal, equally weighted suffrage is instrumental rather than intrinsic: political equality is valuable because of what it tends to produce, not as a side-constraint on institutional design. For a narrowly bounded class of decisions — referenda that turn on technical matters where the competence distribution among citizens is both wide and asymmetric (fiscal sustainability, grid decarbonization, the design of a vaccination schedule) — I argue we should replace equal weighting with a modest competence weighting: every citizen's vote counts at least one and at most three, and the multiplier is earned through a certification whose content is not written by experts or legislators but by a randomly selected citizens' assembly, re-constituted for each such referendum. My thought is that letting a sortition body author the competence standard dissolves the regress of who gets to decide who is competent. The mechanism is deliberately modest — capped weights, a guaranteed floor, and the weighting applies only to a closed list of referendum topics that itself requires supermajorities to amend.

Here is the audience, as precisely as I can predict it. Two deliberative democrats, both of whom treat legitimacy as arising from inclusive justification rather than from accuracy, and who will be alert to anything that creates a two-tier citizenry. One formal theorist who knows the jury-theorem literature cold and will go straight for the independence and competence assumptions. One empirical political scientist who studies political ignorance and turnout, and who will want to know about take-up, manipulation, and self-selection. And one historian of political thought with a critical-theory bent, who will ask who has historically been judged competent and by whom.

Give me the prep sheet. I want the objections ordered by how much damage they'd do if I fumble them, phrased the way each of these four would actually phrase it in their own vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a frank note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front rather than defend, what I should hold firmly, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — and be honest about whether the empirical premise my whole argument leans on is the thing I should be most afraid of.
