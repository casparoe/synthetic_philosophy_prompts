I have been reading the rationality blogs and comment threads for the better part of fifteen years, and the argument I keep returning to is whether a failure to act can count as causing what follows. A friend of mine, who has the same reading habits and now runs a small reading group on action theory, has been working the question for a decade. His verdicts on individual cases are far steadier than any principle he will state out loud, so I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule he is actually running. Here is the track record, as I have reconstructed it from our conversations.

1. A lifeguard on duty reads a paperback while a swimmer drowns within easy reach; she was paid to watch and could have reached him in seconds. His verdict: her inaction caused the death.
2. A parent does not feed an infant for a week, and the infant dies. Caused.
3. A physician on shift omits a standard, effective treatment, and the patient dies of the condition the treatment would have cured. Caused.
4. An employer is legally required to fit a machine guard, does not fit it, and a worker loses a hand in the unguarded machine. Caused.
5. A strong swimmer relaxing on the same beach — not a lifeguard, no relationship to the drowning swimmer — notices the swimmer and could have saved her at no risk, and does not move. His verdict: this inaction did not cause the death, though he calls the man a heel.
6. A wealthy person does not give to a charity that would have used the money to save a named child's life. Not a cause.
7. A person does not forward a warning that, forwarded, would have prevented an injury. Not a cause.
8. Someone deliberately distracts the lifeguard so that she will not look up, and the swimmer drowns. His verdict: the distractor caused the death, even though the distractor's own act injured no one directly.

He is plainly not a naive counterfactualist — cases 5 through 7 have the counterfactual dependence and still come out as non-causes — but I can think of more than one rule that fits everything above, and I would like to know which one he is running.

Now the new case. A hospital requires that a blood transfusion be authorized by two senior physicians jointly; the protocol is written so that neither can authorize alone, and the patient is harmed only if the transfusion is not given. On the night in question, both physicians are paged, both let the page go unanswered, and the patient is harmed. Had either one signed while the other did not, the transfusion would not have happened and the harm would have occurred just the same. Had both signed, the harm would have been averted. Did either physician's omission cause the harm?

I would like four things. First, the candidate rules that fit the track record; I suspect there is more than one, and I want to see the space rather than a single guess. Second, what each of those rules predicts about the new case, including whatever disagreement there is about how to individuate the omission — one compound lapse, or two separate ones. Third, your best guess at his verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading of him over the alternatives. Fourth, the cleanest further case to put to him that would separate whichever rules survive the new case. And a closing word on why this matters to me, which is the ordinary rationalist worry: if his rule turns my own unacted-on failures into causes, I want to know exactly which failures; and if it does not, I want to know what work the word cause is doing in the cases where he says it applies.
