I've been kicking around a version of program equilibrium that I havnt seen anyone actually work out, and I want you to argue for the surprising side of it.

Recap of the setup, since I don't want you leaning on a half-remembered version: in a program equilibrium each player submits a program before the game starts, and a program is allowed to read the other player's program and condition its move on what it finds there. A pair of programs is an equilibrium if neither player could get a better payoff by submitting some other program, holding the other's fixed. The standard payoff of the whole idea is that the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma gets a cooperative equilibrium anyway: both submit the program that cooperates if the opponent's program is the very same program and defects otherwise, and neither can gain by swapping in anything else, even with no repetition and nobody around to enforce anything.

Here's the wrinkle. Take that same setup and make inspection cost something. To condition on the other program you first have to pay c (compute, time, whatever), and you're always free to skip paying it and pick your move blind. My gut says this should be the end of the cute cooperation result — if looking is expensive, just don't look, defect, and pocket the temptation payoff. I want you to argue that my gut is wrong.

Specifically, argue for this: the cooperative program equilibrium survives any inspection cost below a threshhold, the threshold is exactly the gap between the mutual-cooperation payoff and the mutual-defection payoff (c < R - P in the usual R, P, T, S notation), and above that threshold the only equilibrium left is everyone defecting blind. Walk through the deviation analysis properly — in particular explain why the temptation payoff T never shows up in any equilibrium here, and why the cooperator can't be profitably undercut by a program that inspects and then defects anyway.

Then do the harder part, which is whether this is a real vindication or a bit of a swindle. At c just under R - P the cooperative equilibrium is barely better than mutual defection, so is 'cooperation survives' doing any work, or is it a knife-edge artifact of a model where you get to condition on exact program identity? Press on whether the result holds up when inspection gives you a noisy or partial read of the other program rather than a perfect one, and say whether adding more players changes the threshold or just multiplies the same logic. End with a verdict I can actually defend in an argument: does costly inspection tell us anything about cooperation between real agents that can see each other's code, or is the whole thing a fixed-point trick that dissolves the moment the model gets realistic?

Keep it concrete and work the actual payoffs where it matters. I don't need the history of the idea or who said what — I need the argument.
