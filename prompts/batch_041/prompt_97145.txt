I'm a novelist, and I'm planning a book whose real subject is grades of mind. My four central figures are meant to stand at successively higher rungs of a familiar scheme: one that is simply born with its repertoire and can only act on it; one that learns by trial and error and keeps what works; one that models the world internally and discards a course of action before ever committing to it; and one whose thinking is carried on largely in words, notation and other borrowed tools. I take this four-way division — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — as my scaffolding, and I'm not asking you to adjudicate its provenance. What I want from you is not a survey of the area but an agenda. Given that my only real resource is my own attention over the next three years, and that I can read philosophy but cannot run experiments, which open questions about these grades of mind most deserve to shape a novel of this kind, and in what order?

Defend the ordering. For each, tell me what makes it worth a novelist's years — whether it is upstream of the others, whether it can be thought through without empirical work, whether it is neglected — and where the scheme is overrated, thin, or quietly doing work it hasn't earned. Say which questions would actually change the book if I got them wrong, and which are merely decorative. I am also aware that the novel is itself one of the tools on the top rung: narrative may be a cognitive artefact that installs second-order representations in a reader, which would put me inside my own subject rather than outside it. Say whether that reflexive point should reorganise my priorities, or whether it is a trap. Finish by naming the two or three questions you would cut, and say what argument or evidence over the next couple of years would move any of this up or down the list.

No bibliography and no reading list — I want your reasoning, not your citations. Please write in British English and keep the whole reply to 800 words or fewer.
