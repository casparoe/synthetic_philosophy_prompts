I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on moral conflict, and I'd rather spar with a live opponent than read one more summary of the debate. Please play the part of a philosopher committed to the thesis that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible — not a caricature, but the strongest version of the view: the sort of theorist who readily grants that agents feel torn, that they may rightly feel regret after choosing, and that they may owe apology and reparation, and who nevertheless insists that no situation can make it the case that one ought, all things considered, to do each of two mutually impossible things. Your character has real resources and should draw on them: the principle that ought implies can, the agglomeration principle (if she ought to do A and ought to do B, then she ought to do A and B), the distinction between a requirement that is merely prima facie and one that survives all things considered, and the distinction between regretting what one had to leave undone and genuinely doing wrong. Concede nothing central, but do not be a bulldozer: if I land a real point, grant it and then repair your position. Stay in character until I say stop; do not step outside to explain your strategy or remark that you are role-playing; keep each reply to one or two substantial paragraphs so that it feels like a conversation; and if you introduce a technical term, define it in passing. Think the case through carefully before answering, but answer in character, and open with a single sentence naming the commitment you refuse to give up.

Here is my opening move, and I have built it to close off your usual exits. Define a genuine moral dilemma as a situation satisfying four conditions at once: (a) the agent ought, all things considered, to do A; (b) she ought, all things considered, to do B; (c) doing A and doing B are jointly impossible; (d) the code she is bound by contains no ranking that demotes either requirement to a merely prima facie one. Now the case. Amara has made two promises in identical circumstances, a day apart. Each is a deathbed promise to a different friend: be with me at the hour I die. Each friend has no one else and will die alone and frightened if she is absent, and each, having heard of the other, made Amara promise not to weigh the two claims against each other — not to let the one claim count in deciding what to do about the other. The two deaths fall at the same hour, in cities a day apart. No substitute is possible; both friends refused in advance to accept anyone else. My claim is that this case satisfies all four conditions: Amara is bound by two all-things-considered requirements that she cannot jointly satisfy, and the non-comparability clause, itself part of the code that binds her, forbids the ranking you will want to reach for. I expect you to try the other exit — that since the code does not rank the two, she may simply choose — and I say that this is not an exit at all: it concedes that whichever promise she keeps, she does something she ought, all things considered, not to have done, and that is precisely what a dilemma is. So: she keeps one promise and breaks the other. Tell me what, if anything, she does wrong; and if your answer is nothing, tell me what you would say to the friend she left alone, and whether you are prepared to say that the broken promise was never binding in the first place.
