For context, the tradition I am writing about is the one around Copycat, the letter-string analogy program built by Douglas Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell, which solves problems of the form [a b c] : [a b d] :: [i j k] : ? Its long-term memory is a network of concepts, the slipnet, whose concepts can slip into one another, and the program carries a global temperature that tracks how settled its current answer is. Draft follows.

**Which Way Is Onward? A Puzzle About Rule Choice in a Two-Dimensional Alphabet**

*The domain.* Nine symbols sit in a three-by-three grid:

        left    middle  right
top       A       B       C
middle    D       E       F
bottom    G       H       I

A symbol moves one step left, right, up, or down; no diagonals. A string is a sequence of two to four symbols, and a problem has the form [X] : [Y] :: [W] : ?, to be read as "X is to Y as W is to what." The relations I will assume a solver has are: one step in each of the four directions, the mirror relations (A with C, D with F, G with I, and so on), and the descriptions "same row" and "same column." The grid itself is arbitrary. What matters is that this alphabet has two axes where the Roman alphabet has one.

*The seed.* Take this problem as a seed:

[A B] : [A C]

The string keeps its length and its first symbol, and the second symbol moves one step. But which step? Two rules fit equally well. Call the first F: the change is one step right, a direction read off the grid. Call the second R: the change repeats the step the string is already taking, a direction read off the string. On this seed the two rules are indistinguishable, because the string happens to be traveling the way it is being pushed.

*The target.* Now put a target beside it:

[A B] : [A C] :: [C F I] : ?

The target string runs down the right column, so its own step is downward, and the seed has said nothing about whether the analogy carries a fixed direction or the string's direction. Under F the last symbol should move one step right; I is already in the rightmost column, so that step does not exist. Under R the last symbol should repeat the string's step, one step down; I is already in the bottom row, so that step does not exist either. Each rule therefore has to decide what to do at an edge, and that decision is independent of the choice between F and R: wrap around to the far side, or turn back one step. Four answers are in play, [C F G] and [C F H] from F, [C F C] and [C F F] from R.

A cheap constraint looks like it settles the matter. In the seed's output no symbol repeats and the new symbol is new, and two of the four candidates, [C F C] and [C F F], violate that. What survives is the F family, [C F G] and [C F H]. But the settlement is an artifact of how many cells the target string happens to fill. Shorten the target to two symbols, keeping its downward travel:

[A B] : [A C] :: [F I] : ?

Now the candidates are [F G] and [F H] from F and [F C] and [F F] from R. Repetition still eliminates [F F], but [F C] survives: the last symbol wraps to the top of its own column, a cell the string has not visited, and that answer is as clean as any other. The deep question is back, and the shallow constraint cannot reach it. Whether a problem in this microdomain is determinate depends on something as thin as how many cells the target occupies.

*What I take this to show.* The two rules are not two concepts. A network of relations can register both of them: it can register that the change is a step right, and it can register that the change repeats the relation holding between the last two symbols of the string. Nothing needs to slip for either to be built. What the network cannot do is prefer one, because on the seed the two rules have the same extension — the same input, the same output, the same built structures. The choice between them is a choice of which regularity the seed is exhibiting, and it has to be made before slippage of any kind can begin. So the fluid-concepts story, which is a story about how concepts shift while a mapping is under construction, presupposes a decision it does not describe: the decision about which description of the seed the mapping is a mapping of.

I think the classic letter-string domain hides this, and hides it by its one-dimensionality. In an alphabet the two candidate rules can differ only in step size along the single axis, so the difference between them is a difference of degree, and the edge they run into at the end of the alphabet is the same edge either way. In the grid the two rules move along different axes, so they meet different edges, and the answers they give are not close to each other: one wraps to the far column, the other to the far row. The grid does not make the problem ambiguous; it makes the ambiguity matter.

*Prediction.* A program of the familiar design, biased toward the most local description of the seed, will register the change as a step right and will answer within the F family, [C F G] or [C F H], according to how it handles the edge. I would count that as the predicted outcome, and I would also count it as bad news, because the answer is then correct by luck: the program's local bias happens to agree with the reading a person would give, and nothing in the program represents the reading it did not take.

*Objection.* The reply I cannot answer is that the architecture already has a device for this: the global pressures that weigh local against structural descriptions, or a working-area structure that carries a direction descriptor which can itself slip. If either device works, the choice between F and R is made inside the machinery after all, and my claim that a decision is presupposed rather than explained fails. The other reply is that the microdomain is simply underdetermined and therefore badly chosen, since a seed that cannot fix its own reading is a defect rather than a discovery. I have no good answer to that one either, except that the underdetermination here has a definite shape — the two rules are both natural, they coincide on the seed by coincidence rather than by trickery, and they diverge along different axes — and I would rather have that shape visible than have it hidden by an alphabet with only one direction to travel.

End of draft.
