Draft: The usual way of telling the sea-battle story is that if it's true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is already fixed, and if it's false, then it's already fixed that there won't be one; either way, deliberation is an illusion. But I think that argument only gets its grip because we slide between two different notions of truth. Call them settled truth and scheduled truth. A proposition is settled true if the facts that make it true are already in place at the time of utterance. A proposition is scheduled true if it is true at the time of utterance but its truth-maker lies in the future — it's not that the future event is already real, but that the proposition has a truth-value that will be grounded when the event happens or fails to happen. Under this distinction, future contingents can be scheduled true without being settled true, and bivalence is preserved without making deliberation pointless. The catch is that I need a principled account of what it means for a proposition to have a truth-value before its truth-maker exists. I try to say it's like a promissory note: the note is real now, but its value is redeemed later. Is that just a metaphor that collapses under pressure? And does the view actually save deliberation, or does it just relocate the problem into the semantics? I also wonder whether the distinction maps onto anything in the actual debates about branching time or whether I'm reinventing a wheel badly. A friend who teaches logic said it sounds like a view with a long history, but I can't tell if that's a criticism or a compliment. Any comments on accuracy, interest, and what to cut would be great.
