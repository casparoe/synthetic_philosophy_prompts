I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of economics, and my advisor has asked me to write a short state-of-the-field memo that does not merely survey the literature on utility but actually argues for an ordering: which conceptual questions about revealed preference and the meaning of utility are most worth someone's next two or three years, and why. I'd like you to write that memo. The binding constraint is that I have roughly one person's worth of effort — my own — and no experimental budget, so I care less about a comprehensive map than about a defended ranking. For each question you put near the top, I want to know why it is upstream of the others rather than downstream, how someone working conceptually rather than experimentally could actually make progress on it, why it has not already been worked to death, and what argument or development over the next couple of years should move it up or down the list. I am especially drawn to questions that the field currently treats as settled by convention rather than by argument, since those are the ones I suspect are quietly load-bearing.

To make this tractable, here are three framings that have been circulating in our reading group; my sense is that the ranking depends on which of them you take seriously, and I don't need you to declare a winner so much as to let the prioritization be sensitive to the disagreement. One colleague insists that revealed preference theory is best read as a family of representation theorems — claims about when a pattern of choices can be represented as maximizing something — and that most of the apparent paradoxes dissolve once you stop sliding between that modeling claim and a metaphysical claim about what preferences really are. A second colleague thinks the interesting gap is normative rather than metaphysical: choice reveals what an agent will trade, and nothing guarantees that what an agent will trade tracks what is good for them, so the real question is what work the word utility is doing when it is asked to serve simultaneously as the thing that explains behavior and the thing that grounds welfare. A third has been arguing, half-seriously, that the deepest unresolved issue is the asymmetry between utility as a summary of an agent's choices and utility as a target the agent is trying to maximize — that once an agent can represent its own utility function and act on that representation, these two roles come apart in ways the standard framework was never forced to confront. I want you to say which of these framings, if any, actually generates questions worth working on rather than merely redescribing the same ones, and to flag any of the three that you think is a verbal dispute dressed up as a substantive one.

So: give me an ordered agenda of no more than five open questions, and defend the ordering explicitly, stating the criteria you are using — importance, tractability for a philosopher, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer — and the trade-offs among them. Where two questions are entangled, say which is upstream. Where a question looks important but would turn out to have no consequences for how anyone actually models, measures, or appeals to utility, say so and demote it. And close by telling me which single question you would bet my next two years on, and what would have to be true for that bet to be a mistake.
