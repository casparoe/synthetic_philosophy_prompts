So I've been chewing on sortition lately — filling at least some political offices by lottery instead of election. The standard pitch is that a random sample mirrors the population and that nobody can campaign or buy their way in. Great. But here's the wrinkle I keep running into: the people who actually show up when you send them a jury-style summons, and the people who say yes, are not a random sample of anyone. As far as I can tell they skew older, more conscientious, more civically dutiful, and more likely to already have firm opinions about whatever they're being asked to decide. So a plain equal-probability lottery over the whole adult population, run in the real world, quietly selects for exactly the sort of person who would've shown up to the town meeting anyway. The mirror gets warped at the exact moment you reach for it.

So here's the design I want you to argue about. Call it the weighted lot: every citizen gets a nonzero but deliberately unequal chance of selection, weighted inversely to their political engagement. The more you vote, donate, post, attend, canvass, the smaller your chance of being drawn. People who never think about politics get drawn the most. The rationale is that the assembly should mirror the distribution of preferences and life-experiences in the population, and raw engagement is the single strongest predictor that it won't.

Argue for or against the weighted lot. I want a definite verdict, not a survey of pros and cons — pick a side and defend it, including what you think the strongest objection to your own position is and why it doesnt sink it. And dont hedge your way out at the end; if you're genuinely torn, tell me which way you'd actually go and why the other side loses.
