I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, and only in retirement have I had the leisure to read philosophy seriously; the question that now occupies me is one I never had to answer while I was working, namely: when natural selection builds something, at what level is it building? I would like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this, and I would like to supply the concrete case myself, because the textbook examples have been worked over so often that both sides can recite their lines without thinking.

Here is the case, which I stipulate rather than defend. Imagine a shallow-reef invertebrate that grows as a colony, with two features. First, the larvae of genetically distinct parents frequently settle on the same patch of rock and fuse, so that a single colony is a chimera, its tissues a mosaic of more than one genotype. Second, some colonies secrete a compound that neutralizes a poison produced by a common predator; the compound diffuses through the water and protects neighbouring colonies as well, and secreting it is metabolically expensive, while non-secretors in the same neighbourhood do just as well and pay nothing. Within a single chimeric colony, meanwhile, some cell lineages proliferate in the germline at the expense of others, so that the colony's own reproduction is quietly biased toward a subset of its founders.

I would like the two interlocutors to be these. One holds that selection is, at bottom, always selection of genes, and that talk of colony-level or group-level "adaptations" is at best convenient bookkeeping — that the appearance of higher-level design dissolves once you actually track the genes through the generations. The other holds that between-group selection is a real causal force rather than a redescription, and that some traits are adaptations for the persistence of collectives in a sense that cannot be cashed out at the gene level without losing the explanation. Let them disagree, in particular, about my chimeric colony: about whether the secretion is an adaptation for the reef, for the colony, or for nothing but the genes; and about whether the chimera even counts as an individual, given that its own germline is a site of internal conflict.

A few constraints. Both should be fully competent: the gene's-eye advocate should not be a caricature of reductionism, and the multilevel advocate should not be a sentimental holist. Each should answer the other's actual argument rather than talking past it, and each should concede a point where the other has earned it. Let the exchange turn at least once on whether the formal partition of selection into within-group and between-group components is a causal decomposition or merely an accounting identity — I take it that is where the real disagreement lives. End in a genuine impasse or a partial meeting of minds rather than a knockout; if one side clearly wins, you have probably cheated. Roughly a thousand words, and no need for names, dates, or references — I want the argument itself, not its pedigree.
