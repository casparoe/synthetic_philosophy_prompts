Analyze the deception practiced by the residents of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon -- a Protestant village in Vichy France that sheltered several thousand Jews and routinely lied to Vichy and German officials about the refugees in their midst -- through Augustine's account of lying. I want the mapping to run in both directions. First, take Augustine's distinctions seriously: the lie told to harm, the officious lie told to help, the lie told in jest; the claim that every lie is a sin, with some venial and some mortal; and the difference between speaking falsely and merely concealing or failing to disclose. Which of these does the villagers' conduct fall under, and does his absolute prohibition, which allows no exception even to save an innocent life, really forbid what they did? Second, press the theory with the case. Does Le Chambon expose a gap in Augustine -- for example, that he has no place for the question of whether the officials had any right to the truth, or that his ranking of lies by the harm they do sits awkwardly with his insistence that the malice of a lie lies in the will to deceive rather than in its consequences, or that his handling of the biblical precedents that seem to reward deception (the Hebrew midwives, Rahab) does more work than the principle does? End by saying what the episode shows about Augustine's view: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated. Keep the history to the salient facts; this is an exercise in moral theory, not a history of the village.
