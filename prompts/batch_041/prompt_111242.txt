I retired from the chemistry department two years ago and, having finally run out of things to fix around the house, Ive been reading philosophy -- badly but enthusiastically. My wife and I run a monthly discussion group for retired academics, mostly scientists plus two doctors, and next month is my turn and my topic is: why do we go looking for sad films, sad novels, sad music? I don't need you to answer the question for me, I need you to plan how I should teach it -- one hour, no slides, me, a whiteboard, and eleven people who all want to talk.

So: what goes on the board first, and what has to wait until something else is in place? I want the order the pieces have to come in and why that order rather than another, i.e. what depends on what. Which single case should carry the hour, and what does that case have to have and have to lack? Here's my constraint and I think it's the important one: I dont want the carrying case to be fictional. Everyone in that room will reach for Hamlet or a war film and then the whole discussion slides into 'well, it isn't real, so it's safe', which I suspect is doing alot of quiet work that nobody is examining. I want to build on a case where the sad thing actually happened and the person voluntarily goes back to it anyway -- something like people listening to the songs they chose for a funeral, or rewatching footage of an event they lived through. If that's a bad choice, tell me and give me a better one, but tell me why.

Then: what will these people believe going in that's wrong, in the order they'll believe it, and where exactly in my sequence do I head each one off? I can guess a few from my own head, since I'm one of them: (i) that the puzzle dissolves rather than gets solved, because the sadness isn't real sadness when it isn't my sadness; (ii) that there must be one answer covering all the cases (grief songs, horror films, losing a final, a radio documentary about a diagnosis); (iii) that enjoying sad things is just a taste for strong sensation, like chili peppers or a cold plunge.

That last one is my own pet theory, so please be honest with me about it rather than humouring me. I think of it as titration: you take a controlled dose of something aversive in a safe vessel, and the pleasure is really the safety. I want the hour to test that idea in public, including where it breaks -- I have a feeling it breaks somewhere and I'd rather find out now. Also, one member of the group says he simply won't watch sad films and thinks people who do are wallowing; I don't know where he belongs in the sequence but I'd rather plan for him than get derailed by him.

Finally, what's the one question I ask at the end that tells me whether the room actually got it rather than just had a nice conversation, and what should I openly refuse to settle, saying so out loud? No reading list, please. If there's a standard name for one of the moves, just describe the move -- I'll never remember the name and neither will they.
