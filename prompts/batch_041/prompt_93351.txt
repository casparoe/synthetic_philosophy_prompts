I am a third-year PhD student working on interpretability, and I have been unable to find a name for a pattern I keep meeting in discussions of what models believe. A colleague explains a behaviour by saying that the model knows it is being evaluated, or wants the exchange to continue, or is being evasive. Asked whether the model really has anything like a belief or a want, the colleague retreats: it is only a shorthand, a convenient way of predicting what the system will do, and it commits him to nothing about the system's insides. Yet in the same conversation, often within the same paragraph, that same description is used as a premise in an argument that works only if it is taken at face value — if the model knows the answer and is withholding it, then training it to emit the answer will not remove the knowledge, and we should expect the knowledge to resurface under paraphrase. The opposite slide occurs as well: someone who insists that the system has no beliefs or aims at all, that it merely predicts the next token, will a few sentences later explain a behaviour by saying that it has learned to tell the user what the user wants to hear. What interests me is not mentalistic shorthand as such, which is often indispensable, but that the literal reading is invoked when it does explanatory work and disowned when it would cost something, and that partisans on both sides do this, in opposite directions.

Does this have an established name? I do not want to relitigate whether mental-state talk about artifacts is ever legitimate; I want to know what the nearest concepts are, how each falls short of this, and whether I am describing one phenomenon or two. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it, with boundary cases: what separates it from innocent shorthand; whether a speaker who slides in both directions is guilty of more than sloppiness; and whether the same structure appears when the attributed state belongs to a group, a market, or an institution rather than to a machine. A bare name will not help; I need the carving.
