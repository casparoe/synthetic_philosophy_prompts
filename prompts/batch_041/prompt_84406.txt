I constructed the following argument for a seminar on value theory, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether it is a proof or a trick. Its conclusion is one I am not willing to accept.

The case. You have to choose between two jobs. A is the job in your hometown: steady, unremarkable, tolerable pay, and it leaves you twenty minutes from your mother and from the friends you have had since you were six. B is the job two thousand miles away: more interesting work, better money, no one you know, and two visits home a year. You have been thinking about this for the better part of a year. Your considered verdict is that A is not better than B and B is not better than A. You also resist the verdict that the two are equally good: you would not be content to settle the matter with a coin flip, and you take that reluctance to be more than squeamishness.

The argument.

(1) Two options are comparable only if exactly one of three things holds: the first is better than the second, the second is better than the first, or the two are equally good.
(2) A is not better than B, and B is not better than A.
(3) Suppose, for the sake of argument, that A and B are equally good.
(4) Let A+ be A with a single small improvement: the same job, except that it pays nine hundred dollars a year more.
(5) A+ is better than A.
(6) If A and B are equally good, and A+ is better than A, then A+ is better than B.
(7) A+ is not better than B. Nine hundred dollars a year does not make the hometown job better than the job two thousand miles away.
(8) So A and B are not equally good.
(9) So A and B are not comparable at all: they are incommensurable.
(10) Nothing in steps (3) through (8) turned on these particular jobs, or on nine hundred dollars. Run the same reasoning on any pair of options you decline to rank: take one of the pair, find some respect in which it could be marginally improved, and you will have an improvement that is real but too small to tip the balance. Some such respect can always be found, since any option can be made marginally better in some way. So no pair you decline to rank is ever a pair of equals; every such pair is a pair of incommensurables.
(11) So "equally good" is very nearly an empty relation, and incommensurability is everywhere.
(12) And between incommensurables there is nothing to choose on, since a reason to take X over Y would have to be something in virtue of which X is better than Y.
(13) So in every hard choice there is no reason to choose either way, and choosing is arbitrary, which is to say irrational. Much of a human life is therefore irrational.

I am attached to (2) and to (7); those are the verdicts that started this, and giving either up feels like abandoning the phenomenon I wanted explained. I am also unwilling to accept (13). Tell me which step or steps to reject, why the step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. If the honest answer is that something has to be added to the machinery — a further comparative relation, a different account of what "equally good" is, or a different account of what a reason for choosing is — say what the addition has to do and what it must avoid. Be explicit about which of these moves is settled and which is a live dispute among people who work on the question, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. No citations or reading list: I want the diagnosis.
