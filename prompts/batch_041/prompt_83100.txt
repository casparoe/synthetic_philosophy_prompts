I am putting together a handout for a graduate reading group on decision theory and longtermism, and I want it to be a diagnostic decision tree rather than a prose survey. The topic is expected value fanaticism: the cluster of views according to which a sufficiently large payoff can make it rational or morally required to pursue an option whose probability of success is arbitrarily small, and the cluster of objections that this makes expected value maximization absurd. The group has split between people who treat Pascal's mugging as a decisive proof that we must take tiny probabilities of astronomical goods seriously and people who treat it as a reductio of the whole framework, and I want the handout to show exactly where their commitments diverge. The tree should begin from a starting commitment that most participants will share—for finite, well-understood prospects, expected value maximization is the correct decision rule—and then branch through the substantive commitments that force or block fanaticism. Each branch node should be a question about one of those commitments, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which named position it leads to, and no node should simply ask whether the reader accepts fanaticism. The nodes I especially want represented are: whether value is bounded or can be infinite or lexically superior; whether probabilities can be arbitrarily small yet still sharp and comparable; whether the decision rule is applied choice-by-choice or to a whole policy or life; whether the relevant comparison is ex ante expected value or ex post realized value; whether the agent may discount tiny probabilities as a matter of rationality or only as a matter of psychology; and whether the problem is better treated as one of imprecise probabilities, moral uncertainty, or the topology of the value space rather than as a problem about probability at all. Use concrete cases at the nodes where they help—Pascal's mugging, the St. Petersburg game, a long-shot intervention that might avert an astronomical catastrophe, a series of individually negligible chances to do enormous good—but do not let any node depend on a citation.

Aim for twelve to sixteen leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of a position, or a descriptive placeholder if no one defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I would like the tree to include at least these positions as leaves: strict expected value fanaticism; a threshold or probability-discounting view; bounded utility; risk-averse expected value; domain restriction, meaning expected value maximization over finite values but not over infinite or lexically superior values; imprecise-probability dissolution; moral-uncertainty hedging; satisficing; maximin; lexical threshold; and a view that accepts fanaticism for goods but not for evils, or vice versa. Also include a leaf for the position that the real problem is diachronic rather than synchronic—each step in a sequence looks acceptable, but the sequence is not—and a leaf for the position that fanaticism is simply a reductio of expected value maximization, so the starting commitment must be abandoned. Where two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, say so explicitly and explain what extra distinction would be needed. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, mark it as unoccupied and diagnose whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. No bibliography, reading list, or citations; the instrument should stand on its own for someone who cannot look anything up.
