I need one essay question for the final of an upper-level course on Descartes and his critics, and the topic is the cogito. The question has to sit on the seam that most students slide over: the certainty of a present episode of thinking is one thing, the existence of a thinker is a second, the persistence of that thinker through time is a third, and the claim that the thinker is a substance whose whole essence is thought is a fourth. The received answer — that the cogito is a performance and not an inference — leaves all four fused together, and I want a question that makes that answer insufficient by itself.

Two constraints on the question itself. First, it must not depend on the candidate's recognising any commentator or critic by name; wherever an objection is needed, put it in plain words inside the question. Second, it must force candidates to say what work the notion of a substance is doing in the step from a thing that thinks to a thinking substance, and what would be lost if that notion were dropped; and it should make them choose between the reading on which that step is an inference and the reading on which it is a single act of intuition, and defend the choice. Allow sixty minutes; the paper is closed-book; the question is worth twenty marks.

Deliver your response as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing remarks. Item 1 is the question exactly as it will appear on the paper. Items 2 through 5 are the four moves a full-mark answer must make: for each, state the model treatment in a sentence or two, give the marks it carries (the four totalling twenty, with the first two together worth less than half), and add one line naming the mistake that should cost a candidate those marks. Order the four so that the first two are within reach of a competent script, while the last two are what separate a first from a merely good one, and so that a script making only the first two cannot pass.
