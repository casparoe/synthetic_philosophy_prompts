You are a philosopher of science with expertise in the epistemology of forecasting and in the theory of technological change — someone who has thought hard about what it takes to justify a claim about a one-off, unprecedented event. Please take your time; I would rather have an unhurried answer than a quick one, and I would rather be told that my question is badly posed than be given a tidy answer to the wrong question.

Let me explain where I am coming from. I spent forty years in condensed-matter physics and retired eight years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy, and the debate over AI takeoff speeds is the first thing I have encountered outside my old field where the arguments have the same shape as the ones I used to make — scaling relations, feedback loops, the question of whether a transition is sharp or smeared out — but where I cannot tell what the standard of evidence is supposed to be. So I would like to submit my own view for an audit.

Here is the view. Whether we get a hard takeoff — a discontinuous jump in capability that outruns any social response — depends almost entirely on which of two feedback loops dominates the process. The first is reflexive: better cognition produces better cognition, because designing better cognition is itself a cognitive task. The second is physical: better cognition must be cashed out in the world, through fabrication, energy, data, and above all through experiments whose results the world has to supply. I claim the physical loop binds. Thinking faster does not bring a fabrication plant online sooner, and it does not make the world answer a question sooner, and I see no reason to expect either constraint to yield to cleverness within the relevant horizon. So my forecast is a fast industrial revolution — decades, not hours — and the discontinuity people fear is, I suspect, an artifact of describing the process at the wrong level, much as a phase transition is sharp only in the thermodynamic limit and smeared out in any finite sample.

What I want from you:

First, what would count against this? Not the standard list of worries about advanced AI — I have read enough of those to know them by heart, and they are not what I am asking for. I want the observations, arguments, or formal results that should move someone who starts where I do, ranked by how decisive they would be, with a clear separation between the ones that would genuinely count and the ones that would be merely awkward.

Second, I suspect there is a soft spot I have not found. If the binding constraint is on the software side rather than the hardware side — if the returns to algorithmic insight are large and the returns to raw computation are modest — then my physical-loop argument does not bind at all, because better thinking buys better algorithms without any plant and without any experiment. Is that a real escape hatch or a confusion? What would have to be true for it to matter, and how would I know if it were true?

Third, what does my view predict that its rivals do not? If it implies the same things under fast and slow takeoff alike, it is not doing any work, and I would like to know whether it is doing any.

Fourth, consider an objection I cannot decide whether to take seriously: that my sense that iteration must take time comes from imagining a system that needs many trials, and that a system sufficiently better than us at the relevant science might get the answer on the first attempt, in which case there is no iteration bottleneck at all and my "decades" is an artifact of projecting our own trial-and-error regime onto it. Is that a serious objection, or does it assume what it needs to prove?

Fifth, and most important to me: tell me honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every possible course of events — fast, slow, or intermediate — can be absorbed by adjusting which loop I say was binding, I want to know that, and I want your judgment on whether that is a fatal defect for a forecast of this kind or an acceptable cost.

Before you answer, spend a paragraph brainstorming: enumerate the candidate defeaters and the strongest reply to each, and only then sort them. I would like the result ordered by severity rather than presented as a flat catalogue, and I would like you to flag anything I have offered as a problem for myself that is not actually a problem — the reverse of what I asked for is welcome if it is true.
