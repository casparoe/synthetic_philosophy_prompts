I am a sixth-form student in England and have entered an essay competition run by the philosophy department of a nearby university. The title I have chosen is what John Stuart Mill would have made of the precautionary principle. He plainly never wrote about modern environmental or public-health regulation, so the essay cannot be a report of his opinions; it has to be an argument about what he would have said, defended from what he did write. Please write that essay for me, at about 900 words and in the formal register an essay competition expects, so that I can see how the case is properly made before I rework it in my own words.

Three things I would like you to keep apart, because our discussion group found that they tend to get bundled together. The principle is sometimes put forward as a claim about evidence — that whoever proposes a new activity, rather than whoever wants to restrain it, should bear the burden of proof. Sometimes it is put forward as a claim about value — that losses we cannot undo deserve a different weight from losses we can. And sometimes it is put forward as a claim about procedure — that a decision of this sort ought to go through an assessment, a licence, or a moratorium rather than being left to whoever acts first. Say which of these Mill could accept, which he would reject, and why his reasons for one do not carry over to the others.

I am also bothered that the principle is nearly always stated as a restraint on acting, as though not acting were not itself a decision with consequences. Our teacher calls this the baseline problem: you can only identify something as a threat once you have fixed what would otherwise have happened, and the background state may be dangerous in its own right. I want the essay to say whether Mill's account of when interference with someone is justified can be stated without quietly assuming a baseline, and whether the answer helps the case for precaution or damages it. A related question, if you have room: a rule that consistently blocked new activities would also block the remedies for the dangers it was meant to avert, so say whether the principle can be given a form that does not undermine itself.

One more thing, which you should feel free to tell me is mistaken. I have a vague memory that Mill, somewhere in his political economy, says he would not much mind if the growth of production came to a stop, and that he disliked the thought of the natural world being consumed for the sake of more output. If that is right, it may make him friendlier to precaution than his reputation suggests. Do not quote him, though: I cannot check quotations, and my teacher will ask for references I do not have. Paraphrase his views, do not invent words for him, and do not send me a reading list. Use one or two cases an A-level student would recognise, and make sure at least one of them is a case where precaution and Mill's defence of liberty pull in the same direction rather than against each other.

Give me a verdict rather than a survey, and do not simply tell me that he was a liberal and would therefore have been against it; the interesting question is which parts of it he would have taken up. Which version of the principle would Mill have accepted, where would he have refused to go further, and which of his commitments does most of the work in your answer? End by saying what you would need to find in his writing to overturn the prediction.
