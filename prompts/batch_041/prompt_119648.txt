I'm a high school student, and over the past year I've been reading my way into the metaphysics of material objects — in particular the debate over what philosophers call the special composition question, which is just the question of when some things add up to, or compose, a further thing. I've ended up with a view of my own, and before I defend it at my school's philosophy club I want to know exactly how exposed it is.

My view has three parts. First, restriction: some things compose a further thing only if they are jointly caught up in a single self-maintaining causal process — a process that actively keeps itself going and maintains a boundary between itself and its surroundings. Second, sufficiency: whenever they are, they do compose something, with no further condition. Third, non-arbitrariness: the criterion is supposed to mark a real division in nature rather than a convenient one, which is why I picked self-maintenance over spatial contact, being fastened together, or being stuck to one another. I deliberately did not write "biological" into the first part; I meant self-maintenance literally, so on my view a candle flame, a tornado, and a star are composite objects, whereas a chair, a statue, a water molecule, and a rock are not.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this — not a general survey of the composition debate, and not a summary of what critics of restriction in general tend to say. I already accept that "self-maintaining" is vague and that there will be borderline cases, so a defeater that only says the criterion is vague will not move me unless it shows that the vagueness does damage my rivals avoid.
