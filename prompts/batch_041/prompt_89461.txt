My colleague retired mid-semester and I've inherited her intro-to-ethics handout, and before I put my name on it I want a second pair of eyes on one section. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reproduce it:

Egalitarian theories of distributive justice disagree about the currency of equality. Welfare egalitarians — the utilitarian tradition is the obvious example — say the thing to equalize is welfare, whether that's pleasure over pain or preference-satisfaction. Resource egalitarians say it's external resources, and Rawls is the standard case, since his difference principle licenses inequalities only when they leave the worst-off group better off than strict equality would. Capability egalitarians, following Sen and Nussbaum, say it's capabilities: the set of things a person is really able to do and be. The decisive objection to welfare egalitarianism is Dworkin's expensive-tastes argument: if welfare is the currency, someone who cultivates a taste for champagne and caviar can demand a subsidy from the rest of us, which is absurd. The decisive objection to resource egalitarianism is the disability objection: a wheelchair user needs more resources than an able-bodied person to reach the same level of mobility, so equal resources deliver unequal functioning. Sen's capability approach is meant to answer both objections at once, since it measures what a person can do rather than what they want or what they happen to own. Nussbaum's version adds a list of ten central capabilities and treats them as thresholds a decent society has to secure; Sen's stays comparative and refuses any list.
