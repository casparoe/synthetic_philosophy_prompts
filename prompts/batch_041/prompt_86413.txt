I'm a third-year PhD student and I've got into an argument with the person I share a flat with — a statistics postdoc who reads a great deal of decision theory — that neither of us can settle, and I'd like a ruling from someone who genuinely works on the mathematics of betting rather than from a financial adviser or a friend who will simply tell me to be sensible.

Here is the situation. A friend of mine is starting a small business and has offered me the chance to buy in at the outset, once and only once, on terms that amount to this: with probability one half my stake triples, and with probability one half I lose the entire stake. I have about £25,000 in savings, which is everything I own apart from furniture, and there is no second round, no way to buy in later, and no market in which I could sell the stake on. My flatmate says I should put in roughly a quarter of my savings — a quarter, he says, is what falls out of an argument about the rate at which my wealth compounds over time, as opposed to the average of the outcomes across imaginary copies of me — and that materially more than that is reckless and materially less is timid. What I cannot get past is this: I can see why a quarter would be the answer if I were going to be offered this same bet again and again for the rest of my life, and I can see why staking everything would be the answer if I cared only about the expected value of my wealth, but I cannot see what makes a quarter the answer to a bet I will be offered exactly once. When I press him on it he says I am averaging over the wrong thing, that the quantity that matters is how fast my wealth grows along the single trajectory that is my actual life, and that this is still the right way to think when there is only one bet, precisely because there is only ever one of me. That sounds to me as though it might be quietly assuming a repetition that isn't there, but I cannot put my finger on where.

Two smaller things nag at me as well. First, my savings are a strange denominator for this decision, since I expect to earn several times that amount over the course of my career and none of that future income is in the account, or sellable, or hedgeable. Second, my own income comes from the same small sector as my friend's business, so a bad outcome for the sector is a bad outcome for me twice over. I would like to know whether these change the arithmetic or merely the psychology.

Please answer as an expert in decision theory and the theory of optimal betting, and be willing to tell me plainly that one of us is confused. Before you write, work out what is actually doing the work in the argument for a quarter — whether it is a theorem about rationality as such, a theorem about a particular way of measuring value, or a heuristic borrowing authority from a case that does not apply here — and then tell me which of the three it is. Open with a two- or three-sentence verdict on who has the better of the argument, then give the reasoning, including what would have to be true for a quarter to be exactly right and what the honest answer is if it is not. If the truth is that the question has no answer until I say how much a pound is worth to me at different levels of wealth, say so plainly and say what follows from each choice. No more than 450 words.
