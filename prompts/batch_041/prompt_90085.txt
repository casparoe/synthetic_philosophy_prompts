I'm a postdoc in computational cognitive science and in a few weeks I'm giving a talk at a mid-sized neuroscience-adjacent workshop where I defend a fairly unpopular line about the free energy principle — the FEP, Friston-tradition — and I want to walk in ready for the question period instead of improvising at the whiteboard. You're a philosopher of cognitive science who's sat through a hundred of these talks and knows both the maths and the sociology of the field. Think it through carefully before you start writing, and give me the prep sheet, not a rehash of my own argument.

My thesis, roughly, is that the FEP is caught in a vacuity-or-triviality squeeze. As a general mathematical framework it's unfalsifiable, because you can redescribe pretty much any persisting system as minimising free energy after the fact; but the moment you make it testable by actually specifying the generative model, you've just done ordinary Bayesian modelling and lost the grand unifying claim. I add a second prong, what I've been calling the boundary problem: the Markov blanket formalism is supposed to tell you where the agent stops and the world starts, but it either makes every statistically persistent pattern into an agent or gives you no criterion at all, and I don't think you can wriggle out of that without smuggling in a prior notion of the organism that the framework was supposed to explain. To make it concrete I'm going to run a little ladder on a slide — a flame, a whirlpool, a thermostat, a bacterium, and a person — and ask where the framework draws the line and on what grounds, since I suspect the standard appeal to "the complexity of the generative model" just moves the bump under the rug. I also want to say something about "self-evidencing" smuggling in a teleology that the maths doesn't license, but I'm definately less sure of that prong and might drop it.

The room is the problem. It's mostly computational neuroscientists who build active-inference models and use this stuff daily, so they'll be annoyed, and they know the formalism better than I do. There are a couple of philosophers of science, one of whom is a hard-line falsificationist who'll love my first prong and then turn it straight back on my second. Give me the objections I'll actually get from these people — not the ones I'd get from a philosophy of mind reading group — ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply in their vocabulary and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, and which single reply has to land for the talk to survive.
