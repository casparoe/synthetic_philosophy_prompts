Question. Explain the is–ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, and say how the two are related. Illustrate with a short example.

Answer. Hume's is–ought gap is the observation, at the close of Book III of the Treatise, that moral writers habitually pass from premises about what is the case to conclusions about what ought to be, without explaining how this new relation can be derived from the other. The gap is therefore a point about entailment: no set of purely descriptive premises, taken on its own, yields a normative conclusion. Hume named this the naturalistic fallacy, and Moore later gave it its sharpest form with the open-question argument, which shows that "good" cannot be defined by any natural property, since for whatever natural property we propose we can still intelligibly ask whether the thing that has it is good. And because no descriptive fact can settle a moral question, the gap shows that moral claims are not really truth-apt: they express attitudes rather than state facts. Consider the inference from "this act causes needless pain" to "this act is wrong." It does not go through unless we supply the suppressed premise that acts causing needless pain are wrong — which is exactly the kind of smuggled normative premise Hume was warning about.
