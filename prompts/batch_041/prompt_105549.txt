I'm in a small reading group that has spent the better part of a year on decision theory, and we have reached a standstill on the question of Kelly betting that I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. Roughly half of us are convinced by the geometric, ergodic picture: for a process that compounds — a sequence of bets made against the same bankroll, a portfolio rebalanced over time — the quantity that governs what actually happens to you is the growth rate of your wealth, the expected logarithm, and so the correct policy is to wager the fraction that maximizes that growth and to decline any bet, however favorable its arithmetic expectation, that risks a drawdown from which compounding cannot recover. The other half are equally convinced that this is a confusion. Their line is that maximizing expected log wealth is simply expected utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function; the decision theory has not changed, only the utility function has, and if you are a log-utility maximizer then Kelly sizing is just what your theory already prescribes, while if you are not, then refusing a positive-expected-value bet is nothing but an unargued injection of risk aversion. We keep rehearsing these two speeches past each other. What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse: is this a verbal dispute about the word 'rational' (or about whether 'utility' is fixed by preference or by something like wealth); a substantive disagreement about which average — the ensemble average across possible worlds or the time average along a single realized path — should guide an agent who gets exactly one path; a disagreement about the idealization itself (whether the ensemble of parallel copies is even a coherent object to reason from, whether the relevant process is ergodic, whether the agent is one trajectory or a distribution); or two groups answering different questions under one heading — a descriptive question about which strategy grows wealth fastest versus a normative question about what one is permitted or required to do? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the residual disagreement is not about the mathematics, which both sides accept, but about whether the time-average/ensemble-average distinction is a fact about the world that constrains rational choice or merely a fact about one particular representation of the agent's situation; if I am right, say so and say why, and if I am wrong, tell me which of the other readings it actually is.

Make the diagnosis concrete by engaging the cases we actually fight over: the bet that wins fifty percent or loses forty percent with equal probability, which has positive arithmetic expectation and negative expected log growth; the single all-or-nothing wager that a Kelly bettor declines and an expected-value maximizer accepts; the purchase of insurance, which is negative-expected-value protection; and the familiar observation that even committed growth-maximizers bet only a fraction of the Kelly amount in practice, which the other side reads as an admission that risk aversion was doing the work all along. For each, say what each side is committed to and where, if anywhere, one side is covertly helping itself to the other's premises. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal result about when time and ensemble averages coincide or diverge and what that implies for choice, a reformulation that both sides could accept as the real question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the disagreement bottoms out in something neither side can argue for. I do not want a survey of the literature or a bibliography; I want the anatomy of the disagreement and a verdict on what kind of disagreement it is.
