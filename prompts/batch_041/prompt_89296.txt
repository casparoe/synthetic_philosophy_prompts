I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the philosophy of time, and a friend from the physics department has been needling me with an argument he insists is airtight. He sets it out as follows. (1) Take the event of my reading this sentence right now, and take any event that is spacelike separated from it — say, some event on a distant star. Special relativity guarantees that there is an inertial frame in which those two events are simultaneous, and it guarantees that no inertial frame is physically privileged over any other. (2) An event is real just in case it is simultaneous with something real, and there is no further fact of the matter about which simultaneity class is the real one. (3) So the distant event, being simultaneous with my present in some frame, is real. (4) Since the same reasoning applies to every event in my past and my future, every event in the spacetime manifold is equally real. (5) So the felt difference between past, present, and future — that some things have happened, some are happening, and some have not yet happened — is a feature of me rather than of the world. (6) Therefore nothing is ever really 'now', time does not really pass, and my deliberation about what to do is no different in kind from my memory of what I did: in both cases I am merely reading off a static block.

He thinks this conclusion is not merely acceptable but forced, and he tells me that anyone who resists it is either secretly denying relativity or secretly denying that distant events are real — neither of which I want to do. For my part, I am entirely happy to grant that special relativity is true and that simultaneity is frame-relative, and I can even make my peace with the block universe as a picture of the world. What I cannot accept is step (6): that passage is a mere illusion and that 'now' picks out nothing at all. Yet every time I try to say where the argument goes wrong, I find myself sounding as though I am rejecting (1), at which point he declares victory, and I suspect I am being cornered by rhetoric rather than by logic.

So please tell me which premise or step I should reject, exactly why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it commits me to. If the honest answer is that I really must give up the objectivity of the present, then say so plainly — but if there is a cost either way, I want it itemized, and I want to know which of the moves in the argument is actually doing the work.
