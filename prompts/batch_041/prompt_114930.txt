I am assembling, before I read any more of the literature, a specification of what a theory of personal identity would have to deliver in order to be usable in the uploading and gradual-replacement debates. No reading lists, no citations — argue from the cases themselves. Treat the theory's job as practical: it is supposed to say when a replacement or copying procedure is a treatment and when it is a death, what may be done to the original and to any copies, and when two stages of a process are stages of one person.

Give me the desiderata as a list — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the constraints it must not violate — each with the case or consideration that motivates it. The cases I want on the list, at minimum, are these; add any you think indispensable and any you think idle.

- The Replacement Case: a person's neurons are swapped one at a time for units that reproduce the input–output profile of the tissue they replace, with no interruption of behaviour and no reported discontinuity.
- The Swap: the same substitution done in a single step under anaesthesia.
- The Two Jars: the original tissue from the Replacement Case is not destroyed but kept functioning in a vat alongside the replacement, both awake.
- The Half-Upload: replacement stops at roughly sixty per cent, leaving a person whose memory and dispositions are a partly integrated mixture of biological and artificial parts.
- The Rewrite: replacement proceeds alongside a slow substitution of the person's memories with false ones, so the endpoint is functionally continuous but informationally discontinuous with the start.
- The ordinary cases the theory cannot afford to get wrong: dreamless sleep and general anaesthesia, and the fact that a living person is already turning over their molecules and most of their cells across a lifetime.

Then require the theory to respect the distinctions I would want respected: numerical identity versus survival; what matters versus what identity is; continuity of causal process versus continuity of information; a fact versus a convention; a determinate answer versus a case in which there is nothing to be right about; and survival versus the availability of a copy.

Tell me which of these desiderata are genuine requirements and which are the parochial preferences of particular camps — psychological-continuity, bodily-continuity or animalist, further-fact, perdurantist or four-dimensionalist, closest-continuer — and say explicitly whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable; if so, that is the main thing I want from you. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to rule out otherwise attractive theories.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one-line verdict on whether the list can be met, or which requirement you take to be decisive — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question.
