I cover ideas for a living and I'm working up a piece on the abolition of slavery in the British empire as a stress test for the base-and-superstructure claim, the Marxist picture where the economic base explains everything else. I dont want the set-piece version of the debate (Williams saying slavery was killed off once it stopped paying, the other side saying it was still profitable and the evangelicals did it) — I want the distinction underneath it. People mean at least two different things when they say the base explains the superstructure: one is that economic interests cause the ideas, the other is weaker and more interesting, that however the ideas arrive, the economy selects for the ones that keep it running. Run the abolition case through both and tell me which survives, using the details that don't fit the moral story: the £20 million paid to the slave owners, borrowed, and I think still being serviced into the 2010s; the apprenticeship scheme that kept the plantations staffed for a few more years; and the timing, the trade in 1807 but the institution only in 1833, when the same arguments had been on the table for decades. Where does the case break the theory, and where does the theory see something the moral story can't? Then tell me what would change your mind — what would the episode have to look like for you to say the base really did do the work?
