In my Theory of Knowledge class my teacher has been ruling on claims about the past, and I have been keeping a list. She calls pseudohistory the claim that Atlantis was a real maritime empire whose memory survives in Plato and whose destruction the dialogues record; the claim that the Egyptian pyramids were built with extraterrestrial help; and the New Chronology associated with the mathematician Anatoly Fomenko, which holds that the events we assign to ancient Rome, Greece, and Egypt actually happened in the Middle Ages and that the history of everything before roughly 1600 was fabricated. Her stated reason is similar each time: the claim cannot lose. The lost island is always somewhere not yet excavated, the missing alien artefacts prove how well the cover-up has worked, and the documents that contradict the chronology are forgeries.

She is careful in the other direction as well. She does not call pseudohistory the argument that the weathering of the Sphinx enclosure shows the monument to be far older than dynastic Egypt; she thinks it is probably wrong, but its defenders say in advance what would refute them. She does not call pseudohistory a local amateur's contention that a runestone in our town museum is a genuine medieval Norse inscription; he is probably wrong, but he argues in the open and would withdraw the claim if the runes turned out to be modern. And when I asked about a famous fossil forgery from the early twentieth century, she said it was a fraud rather than pseudohistory, because the forger had to hide from the evidence and was eventually caught by it.

The case we are arguing about next week is this. A research group at a major university, with the usual credentials, publishes in the ordinary peer-reviewed journals of its field. It maintains that a particular ancient population collapse was caused by one specific event. When the signature that event should have left fails to turn up at a newly excavated site, the group does not abandon the claim; it adds a qualification — the signature was not preserved there, the site was disturbed, the event affected only part of the population — and carries on. Each qualification is individually defensible, and the group members are not cranks; they are simply never wrong for long.

Predict my teacher's verdict on that case. I care less about the verdict than about the rule she is actually running. State the criterion that best fits her earlier rulings, name at least two other criteria that fit those rulings equally well, and say what each of them predicts about the new case. Give me your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for preferring that reading. Then tell me which case on the table — hers or mine — genuinely forces a choice among the criteria, and which move a defender of the research group would make to insist that no line is being crossed at all.
