You are a philosopher specializing in decision theory, with expertise in the debates over causal, evidential, and updateless decision theories. I want you to write an essay on a controversy that arises at the intersection of counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. To keep the discussion from recycling the standard examples, I will specify a variant that I have designed. Your essay should analyze this variant, weigh the arguments for and against paying, and reach a justified verdict on what the agent should do. It should also say what the variant implies for the broader dispute between the decision theories.

The variant: A blackmailer sends you a letter. The letter says that before sending it, the blackmailer flipped a fair coin. If the coin came up heads, then the blackmailer has already donated $10,000 to a charity you strongly support, and will continue to do so only if you now pay $100. If the coin came up tails, then the blackmailer has already destroyed $10,000 worth of resources that you care about, and will destroy another $10,000 unless you now pay $100. You do not know which side the coin landed on. You are a member of a community of agents who face similar letters, and your decision is public: others will observe whether you pay and will use your choice as a precedent for their own decisions. You care about the total welfare of the community, not just your own. The blackmailer is known to be truthful about the structure of the offer, but you have no independent evidence about the coin. This is a variant of XOR blackmail, with the addition of public precedent and community welfare.

In your essay, address at least the following: (1) What does causal decision theory recommend, and why? (2) What does evidential decision theory recommend, and why? (3) What does updateless decision theory recommend, and why? (4) How does the public-precedent element change the analysis, if at all? (5) Is there a difference between the first-person question of what I should do and the policy question of what policy agents like me should adopt? (6) Does the fact that the blackmailer's coin flip is already settled matter? (7) What are the strongest objections to each recommendation?

Reach a conclusion: say whether you should pay, and justify it. Do not end on a non-committal note. If your verdict is conditional, state the conditions clearly.

Epistemic requirements: Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic. Do not present a contested view as the consensus. Be explicit about which steps in your argument are controversial and which are relatively uncontroversial. If you are uncertain, say so and explain why.

Begin by brainstorming the main considerations and possible lines of argument. Then write the essay.
