Need help settling an argument with a friend before I lose another weekend to this. I'm building a little automated-science toy — you feed it some data, it ranks candidate hypotheses, and I wanted the ranking to come from an actual prior instead of vibes. The obvious move is to weight hypotheses by how short they are to describe, in the Kolmogorov-complexity sense, on the theory that this turns Occam's razor into a theorem instead of a taste. My friend says I'm fooling myself. Here's his argument, written out the way he gave it to me.

(1) If "simplicity" is going to be precise at all, it just means "shortest description" — shortest program that spits out the hypothesis. There's no other non-arbitrary notion on the table.
(2) But "shortest program" is only defined relative to a fixed universal machine, i.e. a choice of instruction set / description language. Change the machine, change the lengths.
(3) Fine, says the standard line: any two universal machines give lengths that differ by at most some constant, so the machine "doesn't really matter." That's the invariance theorem.
(4) Look at what the theorem actually licenses, though. Since I get to choose the machine, then for any two hypotheses H and H' I care about, I can build a machine that hardwires H as a super short program and makes H' pay full freight for its length — so under *my* machine, H comes out simpler. And I can run the same trick the other way, hardwiring H'. Both orderings are on the table.
(5) So for any two hypotheses that are anywhere near each other in complexity, "H is simpler than H'" has no machine-independent answer. The comparison is a fact about my choice of machine, not about the hypotheses.
(6) Therefore any prior that says "prefer the simpler hypothesis" is just whatever prior you smuggled in through the back door with your machine choice. It's a convention wearing a lab coat.
(7) So your toy's ranking is arbitrary and "Occam's razor is a theorem" is a shell game.

I'm stuck because I can't find the joint. (4) looks airtight to me — I can literally sit down and write the machine he's describing — and if (4) holds then (5) seems to follow, and then (6). But the conclusion has to be wrong, because I'd bet anything that "the straight line through these points is a simpler hypothesis than the degree-100 polynomial that also fits them" is a fact about the hypotheses and not about my instruction set. For what it's worth I already tried to dodge him by switching to a two-part code / description-length tradeoff and he just said that's the same trick in a nicer suit, and I couldn't tell wether he was right.

So: where does it break? Which step do I reject, why did that step look so good, and if I have to give something up, what's the cheapest thing to sacrifice — because I really don't want to give up the straight-line-vs-polynomial intuition, and I'd also rather not give up (3), since without it I don't see how the whole Solomonoff story hangs together at all. Tell me what I'm paying, and to whom.
