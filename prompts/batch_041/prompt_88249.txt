I am a novelist, currently at a residency in upstate New York, and I have been trying to turn a philosophical irritation into a short essay. The irritation is this: when readers say a character acted out of character, they seem to be appealing to a fact about what that character really wanted, even though the character is only a partial specification in a text. My draft argues that what a fictional character truly wants is best modeled as the coherent extrapolation of their actual desires under full information and ideal reflection, and I call this narrative extrapolated volition. I borrow the structure of idealized-preference views: an agent’s real preference is not what they occurrently want but what they would endorse after learning all relevant facts, correcting logical inconsistency, and imagining relevant alternatives. For fictional characters, I argue, this cannot be a psychological discovery; it is a constraint the author imposes and the reader ratifies. The author’s authority is not to report hidden facts but to construct a hypothetical agent whose preferences remain stable under the story’s counterfactuals. I then defend three claims: first, narrative extrapolated volition is not reducible to what maximizes the character’s welfare, because a character can be authored to prefer self-destruction; second, it is not the same as what the reader wants, because the reader’s idealization runs on different information; third, it is not the same as what the author wants, because the author can be surprised by what the constructed agent would endorse. I also consider the objection that fictional characters have no counterfactual mental life, so the whole notion is a category mistake, and I reply that the same is true of any idealization: we do not need metaphysical facts, only a procedure for extending a partial specification.

I would like your comments on this essay, written in American English and in a formal but direct register. Please assess the accuracy of the philosophical machinery, especially whether I have conflated idealization, which makes preferences consistent, with extrapolation, which extends them under improved epistemic conditions. Is the analogy to coherent extrapolated volition illuminating, or does it obscure the difference between a person and a character? Is my third claim about authorial surprise coherent, or does it secretly reintroduce authorial intention as the real standard? Does the category-mistake objection have more force than I allow? I am not asking for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want an assessment of the argument’s soundness, originality, and clarity.

Finally, tell me which parts are dead weight. I suspect the welfare clause is a distraction and that the reader-ratification idea is doing more work than I admit. If you think the essay’s central concept collapses into ordinary talk about character consistency, say so plainly and explain what, if anything, survives. Please organize your comments as follows: (a) accuracy of the philosophical machinery; (b) whether the central distinction is interesting enough to carry an essay; (c) specific suggestions on the three claims and the category-mistake objection; and (d) a verdict on whether the essay should be pursued, revised, or abandoned.
