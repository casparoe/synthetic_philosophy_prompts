I'm leading a session of a small reading group on anthropic reasoning — we've already done Sleeping Beauty and the doomsday argument, and the group has hardened into a self-sampling camp and a self-indication camp that no longer hear each other, so I want to put a film in front of them instead of another paper. The film is Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006), adapted from Christopher Priest's novel, and the part I care about is its central mechanism: the machine Angier commissions from Tesla, which duplicates a man while leaving the original intact, so that one Angier takes the applause on stage while the other drowns in a locked tank beneath it. He performs the trick nightly, and the film ends by revealing rows of water tanks holding the corpses of his duplicates. The other half of the film is Borden, whose secret is that Borden is two identical twins living a single life between them — each performs as the magician, each plays the assistant Fallon while the other is on stage, and each of them regards himself as the man. Angier, for his part, admits at the end that after so many duplications he is no longer certain which of them he is.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the film through the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher — criticism, not a plot summary, a review, or a survey of the literature. I want to know which assumption the film's narrative logic actually presupposes, and at which junctures. Climbing into the machine every night looks like a bet, and the two assumptions price it very differently: under self-sampling, once the run has gone on for a while almost every Angier-moment is a drowning, so he should expect to be the man in the tank; under self-indication, the machine's multiplication of observers is itself evidence in his favour, so there will always be an Angier left to take the bow. Is the film dramatizing the presumptuous philosopher's inference or refuting it — and does it matter that his machine makes the profligate hypothesis verifiable, so that the extra observers can literally be counted in a storeroom, in a way the cosmological case never permits? I'd also like you to take the twins seriously as a case the technical apparatus handles badly: one identity, two observers, and no clean way to specify the reference class, which is precisely where the doomsday argument is usually attacked. Where does the film's treatment of Borden illuminate that difficulty, and where does it merely trade on our intuitions about brothers?

Two further things I'd like pressed. First, the film seems to blur two questions I keep wanting to separate: whether the man who takes the bow is the same person as the man who stepped into the machine, and which of the resulting observers he should have expected to be. Call them the continuation question and the sampling question. Is that a distinction with real work to do, or is it the self-sampling/self-indication debate in costume? Angier's grief, and his confessed uncertainty about his own identity, seem to require an answer to the continuation question, and I want to know whether the anthropic literature requires one too, or whether it can stay silent on it and still answer everything it is asked. Second, be honest about where the film resists the reading: it is also about obsession, sacrifice, and the anatomy of a magic trick, and I would rather have a reading that says so than one that turns Angier into an illustration of a decision problem. Close by saying what the film shows that the arguments cannot, if anything. Along the way, tell me which three or four scenes I should put in front of the group and what I should ask about each. No quotations or citations are needed; assume I know the film and the debate, and that the group will forgive you for not having read Priest.
