I write novels, and I keep running into self-deception as a craft problem — I've got a character in the current book who's made of the stuff, and my editor and I keep disagreeing about which of my other people actually count. So here are my gut calls. Tell me if theres a principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

The husband who never opens the bank statements and says, sincerely, that they're fine money-wise: fooling himself, yes.
The wife who says her husband isn't an alcoholic, he just drinks alot: yes.
The guy who says he isn't afraid of flying and gets on the plane with his palms sweating: no. That's his hands not knowing what his mouth knows. Not the same thing.
The mother who believes her son is a genius when every teacher has told her otherwise and she's read every report: yes, and this is the clearest one to me.
The man who says he never wanted the promotion anyway, after he was passed over: no. He's changed what he wants, not what he believes.
But the woman who says she always wanted to stay in this town, after the move fell through: yes. And I can't tell you why that's different from the promotion guy, because structurally it looks identical.

The principle I'd tentatively put forward: you're fooling yourself only when you'd flinch to hear your own reasoning laid out in one go, by someone who knows the subject, with the case against you stated at full strength. The belief survives because you never let it be said out loud like that. Test it against the cases — I think it somehow earns the town/promotion pair, but I want you to show me how, and if it can't, which verdict has to go.

My wife reckons I've got the whole thing wrong, that it isn't about believing the false thing at all, it's about arranging your life so you never have to find out, and that if she's right then half my verdicts are about something else entirely. So also tell me: does your principle make self-deception a kind of believing, or a kind of not-looking? Because I don't see how you decide to believe something you know isn't so, and if you can't do that, I don't see how the thing ever gets started.

And what does it say about a couple who've both quietly agreed never to mention the affair, without either of them ever saying so; someone who knows perfectly well they're fooling themselves, says so, and keeps doing it; and the one I keep going back and forth on — the man who sincerely believes he's forgiven his father. Then tell me which of my verdicts above you'd have to overrule.
