I share an office with a philosopher who works on markets and morals, and our reading group has spent the past year on the ethics of commodification, so I have accumulated a small file of her verdicts. She thinks a regulated kidney market — generous compensation, careful screening, but with most sellers drawn from poor regions and selling out of financial desperation — should stay illegal. She thinks selling one's vote in a national election should be illegal. She thinks paying a stranger to take the blame and the penalty for your traffic offence should be illegal. She thinks the American arrangement of paying plasma donors is fine. She thinks paying someone to stand in line on your behalf, the way lobbyists pay people to hold places in the queue for court arguments and public hearings, is fine. And she thinks reselling a concert ticket for whatever the market will bear is fine. When I press her on the reasoning, she reaches for two different vocabularies and I can never tell which one is doing the work. Sometimes the complaint is that a market preys on people who have no decent alternative — she will call such a market noxious — and sometimes it is that the thing being bought and sold is the sort of thing whose value is spoiled by having a price at all — she will call such a good contested. Both vocabularies seem to fit all six verdicts, which is part of what confuses me, and I suspect the fit is doing less work than she thinks.

A colleague of ours in the economics department has now floated a scheme, and I want to know what she will say about it before Thursday's meeting. To fund field stations and their conservation work, a trust would auction the right to name each newly described species — a cave beetle, a deep-sea snail — to the highest bidder, with the winning name printed in the formal description and the proceeds going to the scientists who did the describing. The species have no commercial value to anyone, nobody is being coerced or misled, and the sums raised would be large next to what these stations can get from grants; the scheme exists precisely because the ordinary funding has dried up. I expect she will object, but I cannot tell whether she will object on the ground that somebody is being wronged or on the ground that something is being debased, and that is the question I actually care about.

So: what will she say, and more importantly, what do her six verdicts commit her to? Which rules are they jointly consistent with — and are there rules I have not thought of that fit them just as well? What does each of those rules predict about the naming auction, which rule should I bet she is actually running, and what is the strongest case for one of the others? If the rules come apart on the auction, what further case should I put to her, one where the candidate rules point in opposite directions, to find out which one she is really using? I would much rather have an answer that marks clearly where people who work on commodification actually agree and where they are talking past one another than one that hands me a contested position dressed up as the settled view.
