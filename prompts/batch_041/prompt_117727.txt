Right, I teach the third-year/MA seminar in philosophy of medicine and I'm writing the final, and the naturalism vs normativism unit is the one where the whole class can recite "Boorse says disease is value-free biological dysfunction, the normativists say it's value-laden all the way down, and Wakefield splits the difference with harmful dysfunction" and then falls apart the second you push them. So I want one essay question (90 minutes, 20 marks) that they can't answer by reciting that, and that they can answer without having read anything except the question itself.

Here's the material I want built into the stem. Case A: an industrial toxin has, for two generations, held everyone in a valley to about 60% of the lung function you'd see in comparable populations elsewhere; nobody in the valley has species-typical lungs, and no one in the valley is "below normal" relative to anyone else in it. Case B: a rare structural variant, present in maybe one person in a thousand, harmless, no loss of function, no symptoms, nobody would call it a disease. I want the question to force them to see that statistics get you nowhere on their own: if they think A is a disease (or a disorder, or at least a bad state of a body that medicine has a reason to care about), then statistical abnormality isn't neccessary for disease, and B shows it isn't sufficient either. So whatever is doing the work in the naturalist's account has to be function and species-typical design. Then the crux: if the valley's lungs are dysfunctional because they fall short of the species design, you owe an answer to which environment the design is supposed to operate in, and fixing that looks like a normative choice about what counts as a proper or natural environment for a human. I want them to say whether the naturalist can pin that down non-normatively, and if not, whether it sinks naturalism outright or whether the naturalist can concede something modest and still hold the line — and where that leaves the harmful-dysfunction hybrid (naturalist, normativist, or neither?).

Couple of constraints for you. Put the two cases in the stem so a student who's read nothing can still do well; name Boorse and Wakefield if you want, but don't let the marks depend on knowing who said what, and don't ask them to cite anything — no reading list, no internet in the exam room.

Give me (1) the question itself, with parts, since it's 90 minutes and 20 marks; (2) a model answer of around 600-700 words, the kind of thing a strong student writes rather than a lecture, and no citations in it; (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points, 4 marks each, with one line per point on the specific way candidates usually blow that point — I'd definately want the A/B line to be about whether they see that the statistics can't be doing the work, not about name-dropping.

Last line, after the marking scheme, just: \answer{...} with a short phrase naming the single move that separates an A from a B on this question, and nothing after it.
