I'm a software engineer with no training in philosophy, and I've been reading about the money-pump arguments against cyclic preferences. I'd like my verdicts on a set of cases turned into a principle, if one exists. Here is what I think, case by case.

1. A colleague ranks three jobs in a cycle — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — and will pay a small fee whenever she holds one and is offered her current favourite. I think she is irrational, and that the fees she pays are a genuine loss rather than a trick of framing.
2. The same colleague with the same cycle, offered all three at once and unable to trade afterwards: I lean towards saying there is nothing irrational about her yet, though I'm unsure.
3. A man whose preferences shift predictably over the day — orange juice in the morning, coffee in the evening — and who at each moment prefers what he prefers then: not irrational at all, even though a well-timed offer of a swap could extract money from him.
4. Someone who pays £5 now to bind a choice he knows he will otherwise make badly tomorrow: rational, and I would do the same.
5. Someone who accepts £5 now to alter what he will choose tomorrow, in a way that leaves him worse off: irrational.
6. Someone who declines to rank two options at all, saying he simply has no preference between them, and who therefore cannot be traded round in a circle: not irrational.
7. Someone who revises her preferences on learning something new, and who would have paid beforehand to have chosen otherwise: not irrational.

My tentative principle is that a person is irrational just when some sequence of trades she would freely accept leaves her certainly worse off. But that seems to condemn the juice-and-coffee man along with the cyclic chooser, and I want to keep both verdicts. Is there a principle that gets all seven, or nearly all? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically, note which of my cases forces each clause of it, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it implies for a case I haven't listed: a chooser with a genuine cycle who has resolved in advance to refuse every swap and does refuse them. I can't decide whether she is rational for guarding herself or irrational for having preferences she must guard herself against. I would also like to know whether the irrationality lies in the preferences, in the trades, or in their combination — that is, whether rationality is something a person has at a moment or across time. Please answer in British English.
