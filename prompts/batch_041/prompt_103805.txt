I'm a grad student who got dragged into a fight about wild animal suffering at a reading group and now I have to actually write something, so I want you to build the strongest case for a position rather than help me survey the territory. The position: converting a mature, species-rich habitat into a simpler, human-managed one — clear-cutting forest for pasture, draining a marsh to make a fish pond, that kind of thing — typically reduces the total amount of suffering experienced in that area rather than increasing it, so the environmentalist reflex that habitat destruction is a welfare catastrophe for animals is at best unproven and plausibly backwards. That sounds gross when I write it down, which is part of why I want it argued properly instead of softened.

The reason I dont want the stock version: everyone reaches for the fact that most wild animals die young and badly, and then stops, and that's way too quick. So use the framework I've actually been working with instead of swapping in your own. Call a load the total quantity of negatively-valenced experience inside a bounded area over some stretch of time, say a decade — so it comes out as a product of how many sentient lives get lived there, how long each of them lasts, and how bad the average moment of each is. Call turnover the rate at which those lives get replaced. My hunch is that the load in a mature forest is dominated by turnover rather than by per-moment intensity: staggering numbers of short-lived, high-mortality animals — invertebrates, nestlings, small rodents — each living a brief and mostly bad life, whereas a well-managed pasture might support far fewer, longer-lived animals with much better average moments. If that's right, destroying the forest could lower the load even while it wrecks the ecosystem, and ecosystem health and animal welfare would come apart in a way that most conservation talk can't accommodate.

So argue for it, and argue for the version you actually think is most defensible — pick it and commit, don't hand me a menu. That means telling me what has to be true for the load comparison to come out the way I want: which empirical conditions it actually hinges on, and the two or three places it's most likely to break. I also want you to say what happens to the idea of the net welfare of an ecosystem as a moral object — is that even a coherent thing to aggregate over, or does my load framing quietly smuggle in a utilitarian arithmetic that the ecosystem-level vocabulary can't support, in which case the whole question was misposed to begin with? And if you end up thinking the position is just wrong, say so flatly and give me the best replacement rather than a both-sides wrap-up; I'd much rather be told I'm wrong with reasons than get a balanced survey.
