Okay I keep having this argument with people and I want an actual number instead of vibes. Heres the setup. A planning board sees every transaction, every inventory level, and every machine reading in the economy in real time, runs one big optimisation overnight, and posts the resulting shadow prices as the prices everyone faces. It also lets citizens freely resell consumer goods to each other, so theres a secondary market for anything it mispriced. Wages and job assignments stay administrative — the board sets them, people dont bid for them. Assume the board is honest and the computers are genuinely up to it; dont fight me on that part, its not the interesting bit. I know this is the old Mises/Hayek/Lange fight, im asking the version where the computers actually exist. Whats your credence that after thirty years this economy gives its people a standard of living at least as high as a market economy of the same size and starting point? I want it decomposed, not just a number: how much rides on the computation being feasible at that scale, how much on local or tacit knowledge that never makes it into the data, how much on incentives for effort and for innovation, how much on whose preferences get aggregated and how, and how much on the fact that 'comparable market economy' is smuggling in the benchmark. Give me a number and one line for each term, then the combination. Then tell me which term Im most likely wrong about, and what would actually move it — a case, an argument, whatever. For reference Im at about 0.2 and my friend who does mechanism design is at 0.65; where do you think we actually disagree?
