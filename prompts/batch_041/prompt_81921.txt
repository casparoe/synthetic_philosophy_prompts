Ok so I had a genuinely ridiculous two-hour argument with my flatmate last night about Sen's liberal paradox — the one where if you give two people even a sliver of say over their own business, and you also accept that when everyone prefers X to Y society should too, the social preference ends up going round in a circle. He kept insisting it's just a dressed-up version of "freedom and efficiency trade off," and I kept saying no, it's much stranger than that, and then at 1am I couldn't actually rebuild the example to prove it, which was annoying. I'm teaching the upper-level social choice seminar in the spring and I want a final-exam question on this, because I'm pretty sure half the class would also just say "freedom vs efficiency" and stop there.

Here's a little case I built to hang the question on — check it isn't broken: two flatmates, Ada and Ben. Ada's thing is the living-room wall: she can paint it blue or leave it white. Ben's thing is music: he can play it loud or keep it quiet. Three relevant states:

a = blue wall, quiet
b = white wall, quiet
c = white wall, loud

Ada's ranking: c > a > b. Ben's ranking: b > c > a. Ada is supposed to be decisive over the wall, i.e. over the pair {a, b} (music held quiet); Ben is decisive over the music, i.e. over {b, c} (wall held white). If minimal rights means "each of them gets to settle their own pair," and you add weak Pareto, you get a P b (Ada picks the wall), b P c (Ben picks the music), and c P a (both prefer c to a, so Pareto), so the social ordering cycles. That's the paradox in miniature, right?
