I chair the human-subjects panel at a university, and I have to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting. A behavioral economics group wants to run a one-shot field study in our largest residential college, whose roughly 1,100 members are adults living on site; members would be told only that they are taking part in a study of group decision-making. An outside foundation has placed $44,000 in escrow, and over a one-week window each member may submit at most one claim on it. If exactly one member claims, that member receives the whole sum. If more than one claims, one claimant is drawn at random and receives $44,000 divided by the number of claimants. If nobody claims, nothing is paid and the money reverts to the foundation. Half the college would receive, with its invitation, an extra sentence observing that every other member faces the same choice at the same moment; the other half would receive a neutral administrative sentence, and which sentence is the intervention would not be disclosed. The investigators say the point is to learn whether a brief reminder can move people from thinking about their own chance to thinking about the group's outcome, and they call the study harmless: the money is new, claiming costs nothing, and nobody ends up worse off than before. The foundation offering the pot also funds the principal investigator's laboratory. Two of my members would approve the protocol as written; a third says it is indefensible to set neighbours against one another in a community that has to keep living together afterwards.

Please draft that memo: a concrete disposition — approve, reject, or approve with stated conditions — with the reasoning for it, a fair treatment of the strongest case for approving as written, and a date by which the investigators must be told. State how confident you are in the recommendation and identify the point you are least sure of.
