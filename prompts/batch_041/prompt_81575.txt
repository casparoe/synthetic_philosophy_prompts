Construct a thought experiment that separates two accounts of what it is for a physical system to implement a computation, and does so in a way that bears on the question whether the brain is a computer.

The two accounts to be separated are these. On the mapping account, a system implements a computation just in case there exists a mapping from its states and state transitions onto the states and transitions of a formal description, under which the system's transitions correspond to the computation's steps. On the mechanistic account, a system implements a computation just in case its components are organized so that their causal interactions themselves carry out those steps: the correspondence must track the system's causal joints rather than merely be available. The accounts may not come apart in both directions. If they cannot, say which direction is closed and construct the case in the direction that remains.

Specifications for the case:

1. A single, fixed physical system. Its physical description, its behaviour, and its functional profile are held constant; the only thing that varies across the two verdicts is the account of implementation. The accounts must not disagree because they are describing different systems, nor because one of them is permitted to redraw the system's boundary while the other is not. In particular, do not let the case turn on treating part of the environment as the system's memory unless you can show that the disagreement survives with the boundary held fixed.
2. The system must be one that a reasonable person, on first hearing about it, would be inclined to call a computer for independent reasons. The standard triviality case, in which a rock or a wall counts as implementing every finite automaton by an arbitrary state-to-state mapping, is excluded; if your case is a variant of it, state precisely what has been changed.
3. Finite states, ordinary physics, at most one paragraph of description. No infinities, no oracles, no unphysical idealizations, and no machine whose computational status is stipulated rather than shown.

After the case, do the following in order. State whether the system implements the computation under each account, and why. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the accounts come apart, and say what becomes of the case when that feature is removed. Say whether the same feature is present or absent in the brain, that is, whether the case transfers to the original question, and be explicit if the transfer fails. State the strongest objection available to a defender of the account that comes out badly, and whether it can be met without abandoning that account. Finally, say honestly whether you succeeded: if no such case can be built without the two accounts collapsing into a verbal dispute, say so and explain what that would show.

The case is meant to support the claim that the question whether the brain is a computer has no determinate answer until an account of implementation is fixed. A case whose verdicts are stipulated rather than derived from the accounts, or which trades on the accounts being vague at the point of disagreement, therefore does not count.
