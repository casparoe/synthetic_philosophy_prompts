Contract law and platform design both treat a click on "I agree," a typed name on a signature pad, or a thumbs-up emoji sent in reply to an offer as capable of forming a binding agreement, and they treat an automated trading algorithm's transmission of an order as a binding offer or acceptance even when no person formed any intention at that instant. Yet the same systems set aside an apparent agreement when a party was joking, mistaken about what the document said, under duress, or lacked capacity, and they generally refuse to treat silence as acceptance unless the offeror said in advance that silence would count. What conception of speech acts does this pattern presuppose? Does it require that promising, offering, and accepting be constituted by public convention rather than by the speaker's intention, or can an intention-based account explain why the unread click and the machine's output bind while the joke does not? Which rival accounts of performative utterances does the practice rule out, and is it internally consistent, given that private facts such as joke, mistake, or incapacity can defeat an act that was, by every public mark, performed? Finally, say what would have to change in how platforms obtain consent, or in how courts evaluate automated transactions, if we adopted a purely convention-based or a purely intention-based view.
