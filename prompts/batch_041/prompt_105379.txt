I am presenting a paper on the wrong kind of reasons at a value theory colloquium next month and need a question period prep sheet.

The paper defends three claims. First, the wrong kind of reasons problem is not a local embarrassment for the fitting attitude analysis of value but a fatal one: if being valuable is being a fitting object of favouring attitudes, the analysis owes an account of which reasons bear on fittingness, and no account both admits the reasons that make ordinary value judgements true and excludes the threat of a demon who will punish you unless you admire him. Second, the reason no such account exists is that the right kind and wrong kind distinction is not a distinction between the contents of reasons at all, but between their roles: a reason is of the right kind only when it can be the basis on which the attitude is held, rather than a reason to see to it that one holds the attitude. Third, this basis condition cannot be applied to reasons one at a time, as a static filter on contents, but only to the route by which a reason enters the history of an attitude, and the case that shows this is one I constructed for the paper.

The case: a donor offers my department a large endowment on a condition she is firm about. I am to come to admire a particular painting, a competent but unremarkable landscape, and my admiration must be unbought — she will withdraw the funds if she learns I admire the painting because of the money, since she wants the admiration to rest on the painting's merits. The money is thus a reason I have for being in a state that, by her stipulation and by the nature of admiration, can be correctly held only on the basis of reasons about the painting. Suppose it works. The money moves me to look at the painting properly, the looking makes me see something, and I now admire it, and admire it fittingly. The reason that started the process was state given, the reason I now hold the attitude for is object given, and I know perfectly well that I would never have looked without the money. The paper argues that the fitting attitude theorist cannot say what is wrong with the money's role here without giving up the claim that fittingness is the value making relation, and that the repair is a process condition on right kind reasons.

The audience: a value theory colloquium, about ten people, most of whom work on fitting attitude analyses and take it as settled that value is analysed in terms of fittingness; two of them are committed buck passers who will hear any weakening of the fittingness claim as a concession. One Kantian thinks the whole debate treats attitudes as states to be assessed rather than as exercises of reason and will say my case is the toxin puzzle dressed in aesthetics. One formal epistemologist will want the process condition stated as a constraint on conditional probabilities or on a representation theorem, and will ask whether my account is just the old object given and state given distinction relabelled. One philosopher of emotion works on guilt and will press the basis condition with cases in which an unfitting emotion is nonetheless the right response and the fitting one would be morally worse. One person has written on reasons for desire and will ask whether my process condition even applies to conative attitudes, since desires lack the correctness conditions admiration has. Nobody will be hostile, but they will be quick and technical.

Write the prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from these five, phrased in their own vocabularies and with the emphases each will bring, ordered by expected damage, with a separate note where the order in which they will actually arrive differs from that ranking. For each objection: a two minute reply, what in the reply is soft, and whether I should concede, hold, or split. Then tell me what to concede in my opening remarks before anyone asks, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the question period, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me the objections a generalist audience would raise, and do not give me a survey of everything anyone has said about the problem; I want the five people in this room. Finally, if the case is incoherent as I have stated it — if there is no consistent description under which the money is both the reason for which I came to look and not the reason for which I now admire — say so plainly, because I would rather find out from you than from the Kantian.
