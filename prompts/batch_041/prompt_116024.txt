I teach Bostock v. Clayton County in a jurisprudence unit on statutory interpretation precisely because it frustrates the textbook contrast between textualism and purposivism: the majority opinion is avowedly textualist and reaches the liberal result, while the principal dissent reaches the conservative result and charges the majority with departing from the text. I want the case worked in both directions, and on the crux rather than at the level of slogans.

The crux is not whether ordinary public meaning governs -- both opinions accept that -- but what the ordinary public meaning of the statutory phrase is. The majority reads it as imposing a but-for causal condition on the outcome, so that any reliance on the employee's sex in the decision is sufficient however the employer labels its reason; the principal dissent reads it as identifying the ground on which the employer acted, and denies that orientation or gender identity is that ground. Use the distinction between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by it on the first question, and the philosophy of action -- Davidson's claim that reasons are causes -- on the second: does the causal theory of action collapse the two readings into one, or show that they were never in genuine conflict?

Then say what each theory holds the Court to have confronted and to have been required to do, which features of the case each explains and which it misdescribes, and whether the fact that two opinions both resting on ordinary public meaning reach opposite results shows that the method underdetermines outcomes or only that one of them misapplied it. Close with a definite verdict: should the text/purpose divide be recast as a divide over semantic versus communicative content, or does the older contrast survive? Defend that verdict against the strongest objection you can formulate rather than surveying the options.
