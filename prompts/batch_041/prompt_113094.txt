I'm presenting at a small decision theory workshop next month and I need to be ready for the question period. The room is mostly causal decision theorists who are deflationary about intention — for them the toxin puzzle is basically a solved problem about correlated decisions — plus two people who work on intention and planning in the Bratman style (plans as elements of practical reasoning, not just predictions about yourself), and one game theorist who works on commitment and cheap talk. Nobody there likes evidential decision theory, and a couple of them get visibly annoyed by anything that smells like making deliberation self-referential.

Here's the thesis I'm defending. The standard reading of Kavka's toxin puzzle says the interesting thing is that you can rationally intend to drink the toxin (so as to collect the million) even though, once the money is safely in the bank, drinking is not rational. I want to argue that this is incomplete at best. My line: an intention only does the work it does in that puzzle if it's credible, and credibility is never free. In the original case the cost is the toxin itself, so the case is fine — but the popular gloss, the one where you form the intention, collect the prize, and then renege, quietly treats the intention as both credible and costless. Once you take the cost seriously, the case stops being a puzzle about intention and becomes a self-commitment problem, and the real question is what can make a self-commitment credible when there's no third party around to enforce it.
