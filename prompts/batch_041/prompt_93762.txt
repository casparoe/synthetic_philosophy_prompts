So I'm in a small reading group that's doing Leibniz on the side, and I've realised the phrase 'the best of all possible worlds' is doing about four different jobs for four different people in the room and nobody, me included, can say what it actually claims. My flatmate says it after every minor disaster, meaning roughly 'everything happens for a reason', fate, chin up. My aunt means it seriously when the problem of evil comes up: her move is that if there were a God who could have made a better world he would have, so this must be the best one, so complaining about the way things are is just complaining about how they had to be. And a friend in the group waves it around as a reductio — obviously absurd, look at the news. Meanwhile Leibniz somewhere says that each possible thing has a right to claim existence in proportion to the perfection it involves (Monadology, I think?), which I can't even parse, and someone told me the whole point of his 'theodicy' was to show that God still deserves praise rather than to explain why there's suffering, which doesn't sound like the same project as my aunt's.

Can you lay out for me the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say what it commits you to, whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false, and which of my little cast of characters probably has it in mind? I especially want: what 'world' is supposed to range over, and whether 'possible' is doing real work (I gather possible worlds are whole runs of everything that exists rather than planets, and that things in one world have to be compossible with each other, but I don't know what actually turns on that); whether 'best' means a maximum, or only something weaker like unsurpassable, or best-given-some-constraint like containing free creatures; whether it's a claim about the whole or a claim about each particular event (does it entail that my friend's flat tyre was for the best?); whether the comparison is even well-defined when there are infinitely many candidates — someone in the group insists you can always improve a world by adding one more happy creature, so there can't be a best, and I don't know whether that's a knock-down or a confusion about infinite value; and the reading where 'best' just means 'whichever one God happened to pick', which would make the slogan trivially true and completely useless, and I want to know if Leibniz could accept that. Also — is there a reading on which it isn't a factual claim at all but an attitude, or a way of trusting providence?

The thing I most want is a verdict on which reading, if any, actually gets my aunt her conclusion, i.e. which one licenses 'so stop complaining' — I suspect most of the readings that are true are too weak to do that work and the ones strong enough to do it are ones nobody could believe, but I want you to tell me instead of me guessing. A menu with verdicts, please, not an essay on Leibniz's system, and flag any two readings that can't both be held at once.
