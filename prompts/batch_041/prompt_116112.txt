ok, I've been reading around in the free will / moral responsibility stuff for a few months now and I keep bouncing off the same thing: every paper is an arguement about one case, and it never adds up to anything you can hold in your hand. So before I read another one I want the spec instead. Give me the desiderata for a theory of moral responsibility -- not free will in general, the narrow thing: what has to be true of an agent for them to be a legitimate target of blame or praise. What does any adequate theory in this area have to deliver? For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it's non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp. A list is fine, a table is fine.

Things I want in there, though add your own if I'm missing something:

- the cases it has to get right. You know the standard set: the guy coerced at gunpoint, the kleptomaniac, the brainwashed convert, and the Frankfurt-style case where the counterfactual intervener never actually has to do anything because the agent does it on his own -- the whole point of that one being to kill off the idea that you have to have been able to do otherwise. But I've got two of my own below and I mostly want the list built around those, since I think the standard set is what lets everyone talk past each other.
- the distinctions it can't collapse. Candidates: excusing someone (they did it, but it doesn't count) vs exempting them (they're not in the responsibility game at all); being the author of your action vs being the one who has to answer for it; being responsible for an action vs being responsible for the character it flows out of; and degrees, responsible vs responsible-to-this-extent. Tell me which of these do real work and which are just verbal, and whether any of them is really doing the job of a seperate requirement.
- the results it has to be consistent with. Determinism may be true or false and the theory shouldn't presuppose either way. Moral ignorance is real: people do terrible things out of false moral beliefs they aren't culpable for picking up. Luck is real: two equally careless drivers, one hits a kid. And responsibility seems to attach to omissions and to things done by many hands, not just to single agents pushing single buttons.

My two cases:

Case 1, the wrong button. Jones is a decent person. He's been told, by a source he has every reason to trust and no way of checking, that pressing the button will divert a flood away from a town and into an empty valley. It will actually kill a hundred people in the valley. He presses it. Also, Black has been watching; had Jones shown any sign of not pressing it, Black would have forced his hand. So Jones couldn't have done otherwise, and he acted out of a false moral belief he isn't culpable for. Is he responsible for the hundred deaths? My instinct is there's nothing to blame him for and yet the act is more his than anyone else's. So if the theory says there's no responsibility here, I want to know what it thinks it has explained; if it says he is responsible, I want to know for what.

Case 2, the self-made man. At 20, Jones deliberately and culpably makes himself ruthless: he takes a course of treatment he knows will strip out his scruples, because he wants the career. At 40 he betrays his friend, and the betrayal flows from exactly the disposition he installed. He wavered for a second at 40; the treatment would have kicked in and made him do it anyway. So he's a Frankfurt case whose counterfactual intervener is his own past self. Blame him? And if yes, is the tracing back to 20 doing any work, or has the clause about the treatment kicking in made the tracing idle? I suspect the second, and I want the desiderata to force that issue rather than let a theory quietly pick whichever half of the case suits it.

Then the part I most want:

- is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable? My suspicion is that exonerate the manipulated agent and don't be a sceptic about ordinary responsibility can't both be had, because none of us chose our constitutions and the line between a cult upbringing and an engineered one looks arbitrary; if you drop the first you owe me a principled difference, and if you drop the second you're a sceptic. Say whether I'm right, and if so which requirement you'd rather lose and why.
- which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories? One requirement, named, plus the theories it kills.
- which requirement is doing the least work -- the one that sounds like a requirement but that everyone satisfies trivially, so it's basically decoration.
