I'm a second-year PhD student in ancient history and I'm the TA for a big undergrad survey called Democracy Ancient and Modern. I get my own discussion section, about 20 students, 75 minutes, an ordinary classroom with movable chairs, no budget for anything. Next week it's my turn to run the Athens session and I want to do something better than the usual "look how the mob behaved" conversation. Heres the thing that got me here: in the spring I sat on my department's admissions committee and we voted to make an offer to a candidate, and within about 48 hours half the room had quietly changed its mind, but nobody would say it out loud for another week and a half. I was the one who finally moved to reopen it, and it cost me something socially that I still feel every time I see two of those people in the hallway. Ever since then I've been stuck on the Mytilene vote, because the more I read it the more I think the interesting problem isn't that a mass assembly can get furious — of course it can — it's how a mass assembly ever manages to un-decide anything, and who pays for being the one to say out loud that the room got it wrong. I want an activity that makes my students feel that in their bodies, not hear me say it. So: you're a veteran designer of classroom simulations who also knows Athenian democracy cold. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate mechanics and tell me which one you'd pick and why, then give me the full build. Think carefully about the timeline, since 75 minutes goes fast.

Here's the material I want it built on, so you don't have to go look anything up. In 427, Athens' ally Mytilene on Lesbos revolts and is besieged and taken; the assembly, in a fury — a Peloponnesian fleet had actually turned up in support, and Mytilene wasn't even a tribute-paying subject, which made the betrayal worse — votes to put every adult man to death and enslave the women and children, and a ship goes out with that order to the general in the field. The next day brings repentance. What makes the second vote happen at all is that the Mytilenian ambassadors who happen to be in Athens, plus their Athenian friends, get the question put again — and the presiding officials agree to it because, as our source has it, they can plainly see that most citizens want some excuse to reconsider, but nobody wants to be the person who moves it. In the second debate Cleon, who carried the original decree, argues for keeping it, and Diodotus argues against. The thing I care most about: Diodotus never pleads for mercy. He argues from imperial advantage — the common people of Mytilene had nothing to do with the revolt, they were armed by their own oligarchs and immediately turned on them and forced the city to surrender, so killing them means killing your benefactors and guarantees that the next time an oligarchy revolts the commoners will be on its side; he says he'd rather swallow an injustice than put to death people it's in his interest to keep alive. So the decent outcome comes out of a speech that refuses to talk about desert at all. Then the vote to reverse is nearly a tie, almost equal hands. And the actual rescue is a second trireme chasing the first, which had roughly a day and a night's head start; the Mytilenian envoys on board lay in wine and barley cakes and promised the crew rewards if they made it, the winds happened to be favorable, the first crew wasn't hurrying on such a horrible errand, and the second ship arrives just as the general has finished reading the decree aloud and is about to carry it out.

What I want back: a complete runnable design. Don't tell the students it's Athens — run it as an unnamed coastal city-state and reveal it in the debrief. Round one should give them only the furious version of the facts, a clock, and a vote. Round two should hand them the new information on cards, and it needs a real reversal mechanic where reopening the question requires someone to publicly move for it and visibly pay for it — standing, points, a card other players can spend against them later, whatever you think works. Give me the rules, the exact wording of the role cards and information cards, a printable one-page handout, materials that cost nothing, and a minute-by-minute timeline. I want the second vote to come out close rather than by consensus — force it with a secret ballot — and I want the round-one outcome to hang on something contingent, so that if they get saved they can see they were saved by luck and by a bribe and not by the institution working. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto three cases: Mytilene, where the correction came just in time and only barely; the Sicilian expedition of 415, where the assembly never reopened the question — the man who tried to talk them out of it failed, then tried to scare them off by inflating the size of the force he'd need, and that backfired into an even bigger vote, and the fleet's sponsor was recalled mid-expedition and the whole thing was annihilated in 413; and the trial of Socrates in 399, where the verdict was narrow and there was no way to un-say it once it had been carried out. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each one — including what to do when twenty nice nineteen-year-olds won't vote for a massacre in round one, because I'm pretty sure that's the first wall I'll hit. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce and I have to supply in my own words: the scale of a sovereign body of thousands with no parties and no representation, the fact that my students know it's a game so their anger is theater, and the fact that no token cost really models what it costs to be the person who tells the assembly it was wrong. American English please, and no bibliography or reading list — I have the texts, and don't quote them at me.
