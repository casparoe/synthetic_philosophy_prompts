I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science with a decent background in decision theory and measure-theoretic probability, and I'm at the point where I need to commit to a dissertation area. I've settled on Dutch book arguments — more precisely, on whether they can bear the epistemic weight that philosophers have asked them to bear — but the field as I've absorbed it feels like a collection of well-worn set pieces: the synchronic argument for probabilism, the diachronic argument for conditionalization, the various replies that each argument is either question-begging or merely pragmatic. I don't want to write the fourteenth paper on whether coherence is constitutive of credence. What I'd like from you is a genuinely defended agenda for someone in my position: the open questions in and around Dutch book arguments, ranked, with the reasoning shown rather than asserted.

To make that concrete, here are the candidates I've been circling, though I don't want to be confined to them: (i) whether the diachronic Dutch book for conditionalization can be reconstructed so that it doesn't already presuppose that the agent's preferences are additive or that she is logically omniscient — the whole family of "package deal" and clever-bookie worries; (ii) whether countable additivity, or regularity (assigning zero only to genuine impossibilities), can be defended or undermined by Dutch book considerations; (iii) whether the accuracy-first program and the Dutch book tradition are rivals, complements, or the same argument with the pragmatics stripped out; (iv) what an incoherence result could even look like for an agent who isn't logically omniscient, i.e. whether there is a usable notion of Dutch-bookability for bounded agents; (v) whether anything can be said about credences conditional on probability-zero events, or about improper priors, in these terms; and (vi) whether the Dutch book arguments for reflection and self-trust add anything over the accuracy-based ones, or vice versa. If the most important question is something I haven't listed, say so and explain why I'm missing it — that would be the most useful thing you could tell me.

For the ranking itself, I want the criteria made explicit and traded off against each other: which questions are upstream of which, in the sense that settling one would settle or dissolve several others; which are tractable with the tools currently on the shelf (decision theory, measure theory, formal epistemology) as opposed to needing a conceptual move nobody has made yet; and which are neglected merely because they're unglamorous. Fix the purpose clearly — this is one term of reading-group time plus roughly two years of writing, aimed at a contribution to formal epistemology that a philosopher with my background could actually finish, and secondarily at people working on bounded rationality.
