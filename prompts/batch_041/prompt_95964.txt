I'm an undergraduate in a course on Kant's political philosophy, and we have just finished the First Supplement to Perpetual Peace, the part where Kant says the guarantee of peace is furnished by nature and then adds the notorious thought that even a people of devils could solve the problem of founding a state provided they were clever enough. In seminar a classmate kept invoking the idea that nature guarantees perpetual peace, and he used it to wave away another student's objection that the whole project is a utopian fantasy: if nature guarantees it, he said, then it is going to happen, and the hand-wringing is pointless. I doubt that is what Kant means, but I cannot say precisely what he does mean, and I suspect the phrase is doing several different jobs at once.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the guarantee of perpetual peace could be making, and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false, and what it would license. I would like the menu to run from the weakest readings to the strongest: for instance, a bare empirical prediction that peace will in fact arrive eventually; a claim that natural mechanisms such as war, commerce, and the dispersion of peoples operate as means to a lawful order independently of anyone's moral intentions; a teleological claim about nature as a purposive system with an end; a regulative or practical claim that the guarantee is not a forecast at all but a warrant for hope, without which the duty to work for peace would be pointless; a deterministic claim that the outcome is fixed whatever we do; and a reading on which talk of nature is a deliberate substitute for talk of Providence, adopted for reasons of epistemic modesty. Add any further reading the phrase invites, and say what difference the devils remark makes to the question whether peace requires moral improvement or only a constitutional mechanism that arrays self-interest correctly.

Then tell me, for each reading, whether it would actually support my classmate's complacency, and which reading, if any, supports the opposite use: that the guarantee is what makes the duty to promote peace rational rather than futile. I want to be able to say in section what the guarantee can and cannot be asked to do.

Please keep the whole reply under 800 words, organized as a numbered list of readings, each with a short verdict and a one-line note on what it licenses. No citations are needed; I just want the distinctions and the verdicts.
