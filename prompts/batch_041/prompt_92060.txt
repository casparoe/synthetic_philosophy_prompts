I am a retired field ecologist — thirty-five years on fisheries and coastal ecosystems, now four years into a late and entirely self-taught education in moral philosophy — and for the past year I have chaired the ethics advisory committee of a modest grant-making foundation. The committee's unexpected and increasingly frequent chore is to vet what the foundation's small matching service calls moral trades: arrangements in which two people who disagree about what matters each agree to do something the other wants, so that each ends up with more of what he or she values, measured by his or her own lights, even though each must thereby do something he or she thinks should not be done, or at least should not be done by them. I understand the general shape of the proposal and I am persuaded it is not nonsense; what I lack is anything I can hand to five non-philosophers — a lawyer, a retired economist, a trustee, a local physician, and me — so that we reach defensible answers within the ten days we allow ourselves and can write those answers down for applicants who will be angry with us. We see two or three proposals a month, from individuals and from small organisations, our decisions go into a public log, we have no power to enforce anything (we either list a trade on the service or decline to list it), and one trustee's standing worry, which I share, is that by listing a trade the foundation becomes a party to it.

Please write the procedure. I want it as a sequence of questions to be asked in order, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, where the sequence stops with a no, where it stops with a yes plus conditions the parties must accept, where it must be handed to a human being because what remains is a question of value and not of fact, and a short closing section on what the procedure cannot do at all. Please keep in view the distinctions I suspect do the real work and that we are most likely to flatten: between a party doing something they take to be wrong and a party procuring someone else to do it, since the second may be barred by their own view even where the first is not; between a trade that genuinely improves each party's position by their own valuation and one that merely looks symmetrical in dollars; between a moral view that can license trading at all and one whose constraints forbid acting against a first-order judgment whatever the gain, so that for such a party there is nothing to trade; between a trade that touches no one outside it and one that uses other people, or the public, as its material; and between a trade that is merely distasteful to me and one that is wrong. I do not want citations, a reading list, or the names of articles — the committee will not read them and I cannot verify them. I want any technical term you introduce defined in a sentence that the physician and the trustee can both use. Be explicit about which parts of the underlying theory must survive translation into a checklist and which can be dropped without loss, and about where the procedure will misfire and a person will have to decide.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, showing your work question by question, and tell me plainly where it returns an answer I will not like. First: two donors, one who ranks global-health charities far above animal-welfare charities and one who ranks them the other way, each pledging five thousand dollars to the other's preferred organisation, money they would otherwise have given to their own. Second: a doctoral student who believes animal experimentation is wrong proposes to take over her department's teaching and the laboratory's tedious data work if a postdoc takes her share of the animal work; the postdoc is willing and her supervisor says the arrangement would suit the lab. Third: a committed vegan, who believes eating meat is a serious wrong, offers to eat a steak at a public dinner in exchange for a wealthy acquaintance's five-year gift to an animal sanctuary; the acquaintance regards the whole fuss about animals as sentimentality and would enjoy the spectacle. I would have guessed that the second is plainly acceptable and the third plainly not, and I am asking in part to learn whether the procedure agrees with me. Finally, say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the one point in the procedure about which you are least sure.
