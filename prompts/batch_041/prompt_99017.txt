I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been drafting a short piece on the frame problem for a reading group. I'd like a critical response, and specifically I want three things: (1) where my technical and historical claims are wrong or shaky, (2) whether the distinction I propose at the end is a genuine contribution or just a rebranding of something that already has a name, and (3) whether my claim about machine learning holds up. Here is the draft.

Everyone meets the frame problem as a puzzle about logic. You describe a situation in facts, you let an action occur, and now you have to say which facts survived. Naively you need one axiom per unaffected fact — the wall is still red, the cup is still on the table — and the list explodes. The standard fix is a frame axiom: a single rule saying things normally stay as they are unless something changes them, with the awkward consequence that the system will cheerfully conclude the cup stayed put after I moved it, unless it is told otherwise. I take this to be the narrow problem, and I think it is genuinely solved, either by writing the right axioms or by accepting a default logic and managing the exceptions.

What I think is not solved is the wider problem, which is not really about logic at all but about relevance. A system facing a decision has to know which of the indefinitely many true statements about the world bear on that decision, and this is not something you can simply read off the world. My move is to say the frame problem is at bottom the problem of not knowing what to ignore: any agent, whether it reasons with symbols or with weights, must carve a relevant situation out of an irrelevant universe, and the carving is a choice the agent makes rather than a fact it discovers. This is why I think a large learned model does not escape the problem. It may never need a frame axiom, because it learns correlations instead of stating them, but it still has to be trained on situations that somebody else already decided were the relevant ones; the choice of what to ignore has merely moved from the axioms into the dataset.

So my thesis is that there are two frame problems and only one of them is technical — the narrow one about representing persistence, and the wide one about the selection of relevance — and the wide one cannot be solved by better representations, only displaced. I call the wide one the relevance frame, and I claim it is a distinct problem from the frame problem as normally stated. Please tell me whether the narrow/wide split as I've drawn it matches how the issue is actually understood, whether the relevance frame really is distinct or collapses into the ordinary problem once you state it carefully, and whether I'm right that learning only relocates the difficulty.
