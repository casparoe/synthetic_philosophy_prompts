**The examination question**

Consider the following compressed argument for the claim that the Everettian (many-worlds) interpretation recovers the Born rule.

(1) The universal state evolves unitarily and never collapses, so the measurement problem cannot be dissolved by collapse; every term in the post-measurement superposition continues to exist and to evolve.
(2) Decoherence explains why those terms do not interfere with one another, and why an observer associated with one term can never detect another.
(3) Decoherence therefore delivers a preferred basis — the basis in which the terms are the branches.
(4) Given a preferred basis, the only remaining question is how to distribute a probability measure over the branches.
(5) Gleason's theorem states that on a Hilbert space of dimension three or greater, the only measure on subspaces that is additive over orthogonal decompositions is the one induced by the squared amplitudes. Hence branch weights must be given by the squared amplitudes.

Assess this argument. Does each step follow from the ones before it? Is there a step at which the argument assumes what it is meant to establish? And does the conclusion, if sound, give the Everettian what she needs — namely, a reason to expect her experience to conform to Born statistics — or does it deliver something weaker? In your answer, discuss at least one decision-theoretic and at least one non-decision-theoretic approach to Everettian probability.

**The student's answer**

The argument is in good shape, with one qualification at step (3) and a caveat at step (5).

Steps (1) and (2) are standard and correct. If the Schrödinger equation is exact then nothing collapses, and the appearance of a single definite outcome has to be explained rather than postulated. Decoherence supplies the explanation: once the apparatus is entangled with a macroscopic environment, the off-diagonal terms of the reduced density matrix are driven to negligible values, so the branches cannot interfere with one another and an observer in one branch can never detect another. What is left over is exactly the question the argument identifies: where does probability come from in a theory that is deterministic and unitary?

Step (3) is essentially right, though it is worth being precise about the sense in which decoherence picks out a basis. The branches are not arbitrary: only states that are stable under environmental monitoring — the pointer states — survive as branches. So the preferred-basis problem is solved dynamically rather than postulated.

Step (4) is the crucial step, and I would want to slow down here, because a preferred basis does not by itself give you a probability measure over branches. This is the gap that the decision-theoretic literature is designed to close. Deutsch's argument treats the Everettian agent as an expected-utility maximizer who faces a branching future, and derives that her preferences are representable by a utility that is additive across branches, with weights proportional to the squared amplitudes of those branches. Zurek's decision-theoretic treatment reaches the same conclusion by a different route, and Wallace later recast the whole thing as a derivation from axioms of rationality rather than from any particular decision rule, so that the Born weights come out as the only rationally permissible weights. The Born rule is thus a theorem about rational preference rather than an extra postulate.

Step (5) also goes through, though the theorem has to be read carefully. Gleason's theorem says that on a Hilbert space of dimension three or more, the only measure on subspaces that is additive over orthogonal decompositions is the one induced by the squared amplitudes. So if the branch weights are assumed to be such a measure, they must be the Born weights. What Gleason's theorem does not tell you is that there must be a measure at all: it answers "which weights?" rather than "why weights?". That is the sense in which the Everettian gets uniqueness rather than existence.

A last point about why this is not just a formal trick. If we count branches and ask in what fraction of them a given outcome occurs, we should expect the Born rule to emerge in the limit by the law of large numbers, because the branches whose relative frequency is close to the squared amplitude vastly outnumber the others. So the weights are not imposed on the branches from outside; they track how the branches are distributed. Putting the pieces together, steps (1) through (4) are sound and step (5) secures uniqueness, so the argument does give the Everettian the Born rule.
