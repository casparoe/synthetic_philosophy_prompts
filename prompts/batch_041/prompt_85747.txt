I am a second-year PhD student in comparative ethics, writing a chapter on Buddhist ethics, and I would be grateful for help with a taxonomic problem. The consequentialist readings of the tradition seem to me basically right, and I have been trying to reconstruct the position in its strongest form. The karmic framework assesses deeds by what they ripen into; Buddhist ethics is therefore a consequentialism whose distinguishing features are its unusually long payoff horizon (rebirth) and its unusually wide circle of beneficiaries (all sentient beings). The disagreements with utilitarianism then reduce to questions of scope and discount rate: whether animals and hungry ghosts are to be counted, and how heavily distant future lives are to be weighted. Two apparent obstacles seem to me harmless. The tradition's insistence that action is intention (cetanā) is no objection, since a bad intention is simply one of the causes that produces bad results — intention is an input to the consequentialist calculus, not an alternative to it. And the fact that karma is a natural law rather than a divine command, which might be thought to make it a sanction rather than a criterion of rightness, seems no more damaging here than it is for any naturalistic utilitarianism. My one residual worry is that ripening is agent-relative — my deeds ripen for me — which makes the view sound like a long-horizon egoism, a label I would rather avoid. Is the reconstruction defensible as it stands, and if so, which variety of consequentialism should I attribute to the tradition?
