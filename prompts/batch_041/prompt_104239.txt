I run the species-status review at a state natural heritage program, and I need help turning the whole "what even is a species" mess into something my staff can actually run.

Context: maybe three or four times a year somebody petitions us to recognize a new species -- usually a split of one of our tracked taxa into two or three. This matters more than it sounds. Our tracking list drives which populations get surveyed, which get grant money, and occasionally which get emergency protections when a site is about to be bulldozed. A split means a form that nobody was watching suddenly has a name and a file. A lump means something quietly winks out and we never record it. So the decisions are load-bearing, and we get to make them with a biology master's, not a systematics PhD.

The part that breaks my brain is that the systematists themselves don't agree, and it's not just that they have different data. We'll recieve two letters, both competent, one saying obviously two species, one saying obviously one, and when I dig in they're not even arguing about the same thing -- one is talking about whether the populations interbreed, the other about whether you can diagnose them, another about whether each one is a clean branch on a tree. And nobody will say out loud that some of this is a choice about how finely we're willing to slice.

So: write me a decision procedure. I need the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure flat-out stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call dressed up as a fact. Keep in mind the constraints: I have about three weeks per petition, two staff, a committee of three outside biologists who have to sign off, and a lawyer who wants the same steps followed every single time so we can defend it in a hearing. The procedure has to survive systematists disagreeing, not assume they won't.

Format it like this:

- The part of the species debate that actually bites here -- about 250 words, the minimum I need to not embarrass myself
- The procedure, in order -- numbered steps, and for each one say what a sufficient answer looks like, plus whether it's a fact question or a judgment call
- Where it stops and where it hands off -- the no's, the handoffs, and who signs off on each
- Two or three worked cases -- take a woodland salamander split into three on the basis of a few percent divergence in one gene, a coastal vs. inland songbird split on plumage and song, and a petition to split a hatchery trout stock; tell me which one the procedure will answer in a way I won't like
- Where this will misfire -- about 200 words, honest about what it can't do

Don't worry about citing anyone or giving me further reading, I won't have time for it anyway.
