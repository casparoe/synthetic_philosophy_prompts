I serve on the advisory board of a small foundation that supports philosophy-and-medicine work, and we have committed the next three years of our grant budget -- enough for roughly one postdoc and a partial second, with no money for clinical trials -- to the definition of death: brain death, circulatory death, and the contested territory between them. Our aim is not to advance the literature for its own sake but to come out the other side with arguments and frameworks that could plausibly change how hospitals write protocols and how legislatures write statutes. What I need from you is a funded agenda, not a survey, and please do not give me citations or a reading list; I will be reading this offline, and I want your judgment rather than your bibliography.

Begin by laying out what you take the genuinely open questions to be, including any I am not seeing. Among the candidates: whether death names a biological kind at all, or is better understood as a legal status with a biological alibi; whether the operative criterion should track the capacity for consciousness, the integration of the organism as a whole, or something else again; whether the irreversibility condition is doing any real work once interventions can restore circulation in a body already declared dead under some other criterion; how the dead-donor rule constrains what we are permitted to say about donation after circulatory death; and whether the concept should be abandoned altogether in favor of a bundle of narrower statuses -- no longer a person, no longer a bearer of interests, no longer an appropriate source of organs. Two of my colleagues have offered frames that I would like you to assess rather than assume. One argues that our difficulties are an instance of a much more general problem -- assigning a sharp boundary to a graded biological process -- and that we should ask whether the solutions other fields use for the onset of life, the edges of a species, or the boundaries of an ecosystem transfer to death. The other thinks that is a category mistake, because death, unlike speciation, is a status we confer rather than a fact we discover, and that the real question is who has standing to confer it -- families, physicians, or the state -- with the metaphysics largely decorative. Then rank the questions. I want the reasoning more than the ranking: which questions are upstream of which, such that settling one would settle or dissolve several others; which are tractable for people working with conceptual tools rather than fresh clinical data; which are neglected for reasons that have more to do with institutional interests than with difficulty; and which look profound but would not change anything downstream, since I would rather fund a distinction that alters how a hospital committee deliberates than one that only matters in a seminar. Close by naming the single question you would fund first, the case for it against the runner-up, and the evidence or argument that would move it down your list.
