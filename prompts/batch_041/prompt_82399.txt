I'm running a 90-minute session of a reading group on epistemic rationality next month -- 14 people, mostly MA students, one big table, no projector, so everything has to live on paper and a whiteboard. We've just done the basics of credences and coherence, and what I want them to walk out having felt (not just agreed with) is the accuracy-based argument for probabilism: the one where, if your credences violate the probability axioms, there's always some coherent set of credences that is at least as accurate as yours in every possible world and strictly more accurate in some, so if accuracy is the thing you want from your credences you can't stay where you are. I have a rough plan I'd like you to turn into something actually runnable. I hand out sheets with six numbered items, all about one roll of a die I've tampered with, which I've also rolled twenty times beforehand with the results on the board so nobody can just write 1/6 six times and hide. The items are deliberately linked -- two of them are the same proposition in different clothes (something like 'the die shows an even number' and 'the die does not show 1, 3 or 5'), one is a conjunction of two others, one is the disjunction of those same two -- and the links are scattered so they're not obvious. Everyone writes six numbers in silence. Then I collect the sheets, redistribute them at random, and each person becomes the auditor of someone else's sheet: they may replace the numbers with any set that respects the logical relations, using a crib sheet I give them with the repair arithmetic spelled out. Then we roll, score everybody twice (their own sheet and the repaired one) with the quadratic score summed across the six items, and the repaired sheet beats the original for everyone who wasn't already coherent -- which I'm betting is most of them, and the ones who tie become a control group, which I suspect is worth a line in the debrief. I half-remember that for the quadratic score the repair that always wins is the nearest coherent set of credences, some kind of projection, but I don't trust my memory there, so check me. The thing I want them to see: the repair used no information about the die, it was fixed before the roll, and it wins in every possible world rather than just this one, so it isn't a lucky call or a bet someone was tricked into -- it's just a better attempt at the thing they were already trying to do.

What I need is the full design: the exact six items and why each link is the one to use, the repair crib sheet in a form a 22-year-old can apply in three minutes without a spreadsheet (or tell me if that's impossible and it needs precomputed tables), the timeline, materials, how to run the audit round so it doesn't feel like a gotcha or like the auditor is cheating by rewriting someone else's opinion, how to score and display the results, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the machinery -- why the repair exists, why it doesn't need to know how the die falls, why fixing someone's own numbers is legitimate rather than outside interference, and why this is not just the dutch book argument in a lab coat (that distinction is really the reason I'm doing this at all, and if the activity can't bring it out I'd rather know now). I'd also like a short second round where I swap in a crude score that just pays you your credence in whatever turned out true, because I have a hunch the whole phenomenon evaporates and that would show strict propriety, not accuracy as such, is load-bearing -- but test the hunch before you build on it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect at least one person submits a boring already-coherent sheet, at least one refuses the repair on the grounds that it isn't thier opinion, and the arithmetic eats twenty minutes), and be straight with me about what this can't do. My main worry is that it shows an outsider can always repair your sheet, which is not yet the claim that you're irrational for not repairing it yourself -- the agent has to see her own credences as a worse means to her own end from the inside, and I don't know whether the activity can stage that or whether I just have to assert it.
