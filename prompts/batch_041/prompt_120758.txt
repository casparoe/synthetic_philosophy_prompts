I'm preparing a short paper in the philosophy of mind and would like substantive comments on the argument rather than on the prose, the framing, or the presentation. Everything the argument needs is stated below, so please work from the text in front of you: I am not asking for reading suggestions, and I would rather not have the answer spend time attributing positions to other authors. What I want is an assessment of whether the argument works.

The setting is the teleosemantic programme for naturalizing content. On views of that family, an internal state's content is fixed not by what causes it but by its proper function — by what it was selected to do — and one widely used variant fixes content by what the downstream mechanisms are supposed to do with the state, rather than by what the state is supposed to track. I accept that general shape. What follows is a puzzle that I think this variant cannot handle as it stands, together with a fix I am not confident in.

The case. Consider a small animal of my own invention, the reed-diver, with a single internal state, D, that fires when a certain pattern of shadow and vibration reaches its eye. D has two downstream consumers, and both are currently under selection; the habitat is mixed, so the animal still meets predators at ground level and still meets predators stooping from above. The first consumer, the Bolt system, was selected to use D to trigger an immediate dart into the reeds. Its proper function is to save the animal from a predator at a distance of a few metres, so it succeeds only if D carries the information that a predator is at a distance. The second consumer, the Crouch system, was selected later, after the population moved into more open ground; it uses the same D to trigger a freeze under cover. Its proper function is to save the animal from a predator directly overhead, so it succeeds only if D carries the information that a predator is overhead. Same state, same neurons, same firing pattern, two consumers with different conditions of success. If content is fixed by what consumers are supposed to do with a state, and these two consumers are supposed to do incompatible things with it, then D has two contents and the theory supplies no principled way to choose between them. Call this consumer conflict.

Three escapes, and why I think each fails. First: say D's content is the disjunction, a predator either at a distance or overhead. This makes both consumers correct, but at the cost of the explanatory payoff that motivated the view — a disjunctive content is one that neither system can use to discriminate the two situations, and each system's proper function is precisely to discriminate them. Second: fix content on the producer side, by what D was selected to track. That is either the proximal pattern of shadow and vibration, which is the wrong grain and makes the state about photons rather than predators, or it is the distal condition, which reintroduces the disjunction. Third: declare one consumer primary and the other parasitic. That is not an escape but a promissory note, since no criterion of primacy has been given.

My proposal. Primacy goes to the consumer whose proper function is presupposed by the other's. The Crouch system was selected as a modification of an already-contentful state, not as a redefinition of it: it has a proper function only because D was already a state carrying information about predators at a distance, and it is selected to add a response to that state rather than to give it a new subject matter. So D represents that a predator is at a distance, and the Crouch system systematically uses a state with the wrong content — which is a design flaw, not a second content. Generalizing: content is fixed by the earliest consumer in the selection lineage whose function is to act on the state as a representation of a distal condition, and later consumers inherit content rather than confer it.

What I want from you. First, tell me where the argument is doing real work and where it is only relabelling a familiar indeterminacy worry in new vocabulary. Second, does the presupposition criterion in my fix have any content beyond the order of selection? If it collapses into the older consumer wins, is that fatal, or is lineage order a tolerable primitive? Third, is the reed-diver case under-described in a way that makes the puzzle look sharper than it is? In particular, tell me whether the verdict changes if there are three consumers, if two are selected simultaneously so that there is no lineage order, or if one consumer's proper function is to inhibit the other rather than to act on the state. Fourth, what is the strongest objection I have not anticipated, and what should I concede in reply? Fifth, flag any distinction I have leaned on that the case does not actually support, and name the one sentence you would cut. If the fix fails outright, say so plainly and tell me whether anything within the same teleosemantic commitments could replace it, or whether the puzzle shows that content cannot be fixed by consumer function at all.
