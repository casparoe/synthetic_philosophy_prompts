I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, but I hang around the ecology building a lot and I've somehow ended up as the informal "research agenda" person for a small foundation that just came into a pot of money and wants to spend it on conceptual work about wild animal suffering -- theory, welfare biology, decision-relevant philosophy, not fieldwork and not another camera-trap study. Concretely: they can fund maybe six to eight people for three years, and they've told me they'd rather fund five things that could actually change how they spend their intervention budget later than twelve things that are merely interesting. So I have to hand them a ranked list with reasons, and I want to make sure I'm not just recycling the obvious stuff everyone says at EA meetups (the "nature is red in tooth and claw" bit, the "should we intervene at all" bit).

Here's the rough list my reading group came up with, and honestly I suspect at least a couple of these are duds but I can't tell which: (1) whether the typical life of a wild animal is net negative or net positive, and whether that question is even well-formed given there's no counterfactual baseline for "the wild"; (2) the death-rate point -- for r-selected species almost all individuals die young, so how much of the total suffering is concentrated in the dying rather than in the living, and whether that changes what counts as a good intervention; (3) the replacement / density-dependence problem -- if you save individuals, do others just take their slots, making only population-level interventions matter; (4) moral weight across the size and complexity spectrum, i.e. whether a million insects count for more than one deer, and whether any principled scaling rule could settle it; (5) whether ecosystems or species can be welfare subjects at all, or whether that's a category error conservation ethics has smuggled in; (6) the measurement problem -- whether any field proxy for suffering is trustworthy enough to license intervention, or whether we're stuck with theory-only reasoning for decades; (7) intervention risk, especially irreversibility, e.g. gene drives, and whether "we can't undo it" should dominate the analysis; (8) whether the whole area is better understood as a bet on future tractability -- is the right move now to build theory so that when the tools arrive we're not starting from zero.

So: give me a defensible ranking, with the criteria made explicit (I care about leverage -- how many downstream questions and budget decisions hang on the answer -- more than about how fun the question is), tell me which of my eight are overrated or malformed, and say what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would move something up or down the list.
