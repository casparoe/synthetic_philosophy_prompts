I am a software engineer on a team that trains and ships a large language model, and I have no formal background in philosophy. I would like your help working out what our training pipeline is philosophically committed to, because the longer I look at it the less confident I am that its parts belong together.

Here is what we do, as concretely as I can put it. We show human contractors pairs of model responses to the same request and ask which one they prefer; we fit a single reward model that maps any response to one number; and we optimize the model against that number with a penalty that keeps it from drifting too far from a reference checkpoint. The reward model is in fact two models, one trained on preference data collected under the heading of helpfulness and one under the heading of harmlessness, whose scores we combine with fixed weights, currently around seven to three, and I have never met anyone who can tell me where those weights came from. Independently of the reward, we maintain a short list of request categories the model must refuse outright no matter how much reward compliance would earn, along with a few pattern filters, and we clamp the reward so that no single response can score spectacularly. For reporting we average the reward over a fixed panel of roughly forty contractors and put that one number on a dashboard the whole company watches; when it rises, we say the model has improved.

What I want from you is an account of what this arrangement presupposes about value and about ethics, and where it is only one option among several. Does scoring responses on a single scale presuppose that the goods in play — being useful, avoiding harm, and whatever else the contractors are actually tracking — are commensurable, that they can be exchanged at a fixed rate, and that a cardinal number is the right representation of them? What does the refusal list presuppose, and is it consistent with the weighted sum, or is it a kind of rule the weighted sum cannot express? If the two look inconsistent, I would like to know whether the conflict is real or only apparent, given that one could redescribe the refusals as extremely large weights. What does the practice presuppose about whose preferences are being aggregated, and is averaging forty people's scores into one number something the pipeline needs or merely something it does? What does capping the reward presuppose about the scale it is capping? I would like you to separate what the pipeline strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, to say which rival views of value it quietly rules out and which it leaves open, and to tell me what would have to change in the pipeline if we adopted a different view — for instance if the goods at stake are not commensurable, or if preferences cannot be added across people. If part of what looks like a tension inside the pipeline turns out to be a matter of wording rather than substance, please say so and say why.

Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; a long email is the right shape for this.
