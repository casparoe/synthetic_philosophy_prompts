I'm a high school junior who runs our school's philosophy club, and I need to design a single one-hour meeting, for about a dozen students with no background in decision theory, that makes them feel the absent-minded driver problem in their bones rather than just hear about it. The idea I want them to run into is this: a driver on a highway passes two exits, cannot tell which one he is at because he forgets having passed the first, and has to decide at each one whether to exit or continue, where the best outcome comes from exiting at the second and the worst from exiting at the first, with never exiting being a mediocre fallback. Design the activity so players must commit to a plan in advance and then act at an exit they cannot identify, using a payoff scheme in points rather than real money, arranged so that the plan that looks best from the outside and the move that looks best from inside the moment pull against each other. Give me the rules, materials, and payoffs, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto why the best overall policy turns out to be a randomized one and why "at any exit, continuing just seems better" still feels compelling even though it leads to the worst policy. They shouldn't hear the term absent-minded driver until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it and how to handle that, and be honest about which feature of the problem the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to explain myself.
