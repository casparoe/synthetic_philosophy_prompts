My friend Priya is an economic historian and she cornered me at a conference bar last month with an argument she says she's given up trying to escape, and now it's stuck in my head too. She works on why some places got rich and others didn't, and she's convinced the whole literature is a house of cards. Here's her chain. (1) A causal claim is only as strong as its identification: you have to rule out the confounders. (2) Randomisation is the only thing that really does that, because it's the only way to guarantee the two groups differ in nothing but the treatment. (3) Nobody randomised the last five hundred years. Countries weren't assigned their institutions, their coastlines, their soil, their religions or their work ethics by a coin flip, and we can't rerun it. (4) Natural experiments are the consolation prize -- a border drawn here, a colonial power swapped there, Korea, the two Germanys, Samoa carved up between two empires, the mortality rates of colonial settlers -- but each one has a live confounder (the border follows the mountain range, the coloniser picked the place for what was in the ground, the partition was caused by the war we're trying to explain) and each one leans on an exclusion restriction that nobody can test: you need the causal theory in order to license the instrument that's supposed to establish the causal theory. (5) So for every piece of evidence in the literature there's some confounder that can't be ruled out. (6) Therefore no causal claim here is warranted -- not that institutions matter, not that geography matters, not that culture does. (7) By the same reasoning we can't know that the Black Death raised wages, or that the printing press raised literacy, and we definately can't know that smoking causes lung cancer, since nobody randomised cigarettes either. (8) But we do know that smoking causes lung cancer. So something's wrong with the arguement -- and yet I can't find a premise I'd be happy to give up.

Priya's response is that the culprit has to be (2) or (3), and that means biting the bullet: there's no non-experimental causal knowledge at all, and the epidemiologists just got lucky with a big enough effect. I don't want that. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (4) exactly as she states it -- I think those natural experiments really are as contaminated as she says -- and I'd rather keep (8), which is non-negotiable. So which step is doing the illegitimate work, and why does it look so innocent sitting there between the ones around it? And tell me the price of cutting it: what do I have to give up about what historical evidence can do, and is there a version of (5) or (6) I could still keep after the cut? I don't want a reading list, I want to know where the load-bearing beam is and what it costs to saw through it.
