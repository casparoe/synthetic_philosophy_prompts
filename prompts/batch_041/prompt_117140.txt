The prof gave me a 10-point rubric and told me to leave point-by-point comments, but she's out of town and I'd rather not wing it on the conceptual stuff. Question (10 pts). (a) [3 pts] Williams's lorry driver case: two drivers, both driving carefully, and one of them — through no fault of his own — fails to see a child who runs into the road, and the child is killed; the other drives home without incident. Explain what kind of luck is at stake here and why this case is supposed to threaten the principle that we're only morally assessable for what is under our control. (b) [4 pts] New case: two residents in the same hospital, working the same shift, each independently makes the same dosing error with the same drug; the first resident's patient has an undiagnosed enzyme deficiency and dies, the second resident's patient recovers fully. Say what kind of luck this is, whether it changes what each resident deserves, and then assess this: the second resident, on hearing what happened to the first resident's patient, begins to feel guilty about her own near-miss. Is that guilt appropriate, misplaced, or something else? (c) [3 pts] Does the reality of moral luck give us reason to give up holding people morally responsible? Argue for a position.

Student's answer. Nagel introduces the problem of moral luck, and Williams gives the lorry driver as the illustration. Two drivers are equally careful, but one fails to see a child who runs out and kills him, while the other drives home fine. This is resultant luck, since the difference between the two drivers isn't in what they did or in what kind of people they are, but in how things turned out. Nagel calls the assumption that we're only morally assessable for what's within our control the control principle; the lorry driver case seems to violate it, because we hold the first driver responsible for a death that it wasn't in his power to prevent. On part (b), I think the hospital case is circumstantial luck, because the difference between the two residents lies in the circumstances they happened to face — one of them happened to draw a patient with the enzyme deficiency, the other didn't. So the resident whose patient died gets judged much more harshly than the one whose patient lived, even though their actions were identical. But since both residents could have double-checked the dose and didn't, both are fully responsible for what happened to their patients: the first is responsible for the death, the second for the near miss. As for the second resident's guilt, Nagel's notion of agent-regret is what we feel when we've caused harm even without fault, so it makes sense in the lorry driver's case but not really here — the second resident didn't cause any harm, so her guilt is basically a misplaced hangover, something she should be talked out of. On part (c), I think most philosophers agree that if moral luck is real, our practices of blame and punishment are unjustified, because we're blaming people for things outside their control, and since our characters are themselves a product of circumstantial luck, even our deepest commitments aren't really our own. Williams thinks the whole institution of morality is compromised by this, and Nagel agrees the problem is fatal rather than just awkward. So the honest position is that we should stop holding people responsible and replace blame with something forward-looking, like risk management.
