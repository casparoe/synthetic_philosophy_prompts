I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, with enough of a mathematical background that formal work doesn't scare me, and I'm drafting a two-page prospectus for a departmental fellowship. The topic sits at the intersection of Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor. The received picture, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: assign each hypothesis a prior proportional to 2 raised to the negative of the length of its shortest program on a universal machine, update by Bayes, and the simpler explanation is then favored automatically; Occam's razor becomes a theorem rather than a rule of thumb, and the dependence on which universal machine you pick washes out up to an additive constant. What nags at me is that the simplicity being formalized here is purely a matter of how long a hypothesis is to state, and is entirely insensitive to how hard the hypothesis is to use — how much computation it takes to extract predictions from it. Two hypotheses can have identical shortest-program lengths while one delivers its predictions after a handful of steps and the other only after an astronomically long computation; a compactly stated but chaotic or pseudo-random-looking law sits next to a boring one as equally simple. My suspicion is that scientific practice does not treat these as equally simple, and that there is a real question whether the razor practitioners actually wield tracks description length at all, or instead tracks something closer to how much work the hypothesis does for you once you have it.

I'd like the prospectus built around this. Make the central question precise enough that it has a determinate answer — something along the lines of: can a Solomonoff-style prior be the right formalization of scientific simplicity, given that program length ignores computational cost; and if not, what minimal modification restores the correspondence without collapsing into arbitrariness? Candidate modifications I want weighed against each other include a runtime or resource penalty folded into the prior, a depth-like measure that distinguishes a short program that runs fast from a short program that runs long, and a two-part code that charges separately for describing the model and for the computation needed to generate predictions from it. Then explain why the existing positions leave the question open rather than settled: what the standard defenses of the received picture are, and exactly where they stall when the cost of using a hypothesis is put on the table. Lay out a method I could actually execute in a single semester — what I would formalize, which toy cases or counterexamples I would construct to force the candidate razors apart, what the concrete deliverables are, and what outcomes would count as the project failing (for instance, if every candidate modification turns out to be machine-dependent in a way the original prior is not, or if the distinction I'm drawing turns out to have no bite on any case where the razors disagree). Finally, give me an honest read on scope: whether this is a term paper, a dissertation chapter, or the kind of trap that keeps growing the longer I stare at it. Don't bother with citations or a literature review — I'll supply those myself; I need the argumentative skeleton, not the bibliography.
