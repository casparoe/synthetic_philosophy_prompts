I am a final-year undergraduate currently taking a course in philosophy of mind, and I have come to accept the causal exclusion argument in roughly its standard form: mental properties supervene on physical properties, the physical domain is causally closed, and physical effects are not systematically overdetermined by both physical and mental causes; therefore mental properties are excluded from making a causal difference to physical outcomes. I would like you to tell me what would count against this position. I am not after a survey of the debate; I want a map of its exposure. Which premises, if false, would most cleanly dissolve the conclusion, and how live is each failure?

Please work through, at minimum: the rejection of causal closure; the rejection of supervenience in favour of some other mind-body relation; the acceptance of widespread overdetermination; the reconception of causation as difference-making rather than production; and the suggestion that causation is itself interest-relative or explanatory rather than a feature of the world. For each, tell me what the world would have to be like for the escape to succeed, and which of these routes a committed exclusionist would concede as a genuine cost versus treat as a bullet to bite.

I am also worried about a reductio: if the argument works against mental properties, does it not equally exclude every higher-level or special-science property, and is that a consequence the exclusionist can accept or a sign that something has gone wrong? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and give me an honest assessment of whether it has been insulated by qualification to the point of unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Of the escape routes, the difference-making reconception is the one I personally find most persuasive, so treat it seriously and tell me whether it genuinely avoids the conclusion or merely relocates it.
