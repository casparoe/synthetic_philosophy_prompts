Please explain a specific conceptual problem in the theory of incommensurable values, rather than surveying the literature. Begin from the idea that two options can stand in a relation that is neither better, nor worse, nor equal — parity — and set out carefully how parity differs from equality, from incomparability, and from the epistemic and semantic readings of these notions (vagueness, indeterminacy, and mere ignorance of a comparison that does exist).

Then explain the small improvement argument and say precisely what it needs in order to work. Use the following case as the test, and treat it as a case I have invented: a pianist must choose between five years spent mastering a body of work she loves but that almost nobody will hear, and five years spent building a school of young players where there is none. Suppose neither option is better than the other, and that they are not equal. A patron offers a modest improvement to the first — one extra year, a better instrument. Is the improved option now better than the second? If not, what has been established, and what does the argument presuppose about how improvements work? Distinguish an improvement that fails to tip the comparison because the values are open-ended from one that fails because it is not in the currency of the comparison at all. Then enlarge the improvement: suppose it is enormous — ten extra years and a lasting reputation. Does parity survive, or does the enlargement show that the options were comparable all along, separated by a high threshold?

Finally, say what this implies for the choice itself. If the options are on a par, is the pianist's decision rational, arbitrary, or something else? Does it need a justifying reason? And what should be made of the regret or sense of loss that may properly persist afterwards — is such residue evidence for the incommensurability thesis, or against it?
