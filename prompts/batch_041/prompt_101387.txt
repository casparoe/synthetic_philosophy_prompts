I have been trying, on my own and without any training in the subject, to work out whether a moral disagreement between two people can be a positive-sum game the way ordinary trade is meant to be, or whether it is always at bottom a zero-sum contest over a fixed amount of "getting one's way." I would like a number rather than a tour of the literature.

First, a distinction I have found useful. Call a compromise easy when each side simply gets more of something the other side does not care about, so that nothing either side values is surrendered — two people who want different things and can each help the other get them. Call a compromise hard when each side has to give up something it genuinely values in order to receive something it values more, judged by its own lights. Easy compromise is not very interesting; the real question is hard compromise, and whether it is generally available.

Here is the claim I want a credence for. Take two people who each hold a fairly settled set of moral values, who are each internally consistent, and who each regard the other's values as badly mistaken but not unhinged. Claim: for such a pair there almost always exists a hard compromise — a package of sacrifices and gains — that each party would rank above the unchanged status quo when assessed by its own values, so that the arrangement is a genuine improvement for both rather than a shared loss. Please give me your credence that this is true, and decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on, with a rough probability for each and a note on how they combine, then the resulting number.

The sub-questions I can see, though I expect you to add or correct some, are these: whether an ordinary person's values are fine-grained enough to rank whole packages at all, rather than being all-or-nothing on a handful of items; whether people tend to have one overriding priority such that any deal touching it is unthinkable, which I suspect is the single thing that would kill the idea outright; whether what one side is willing to give up happens to be what the other side wants, so that there is a match of wants rather than two people each holding what the other already has; whether agreeing to the trade corrodes the very value being traded, so that a cause traded away for a price is no longer being upheld; and whether the two sides can trust and verify the bargain once struck.

My own number is about 0.6; a friend I argue with puts it nearer 0.15, on the grounds that serious moral commitments are not the sort of thing that can be swapped at all. Tell me your number, tell me which sub-question the final figure is most sensitive to, and tell me what I could actually learn — an argument, a thought experiment, or a pattern from real negotiations — that would move the number most, and in which direction. If you think my friend and I are really disagreeing about something other than the claim as I have stated it, say what.
