Could you give me comments — mainly on whether the argument actually goes through, whether I'm mischaracterizing anything, and whether the distinction I'm leaning on is a real one or just me renameing stuff that already has a name.

Here's the gist. Everyone talks about multipolar traps and races to the bottom as if the defining feature is that no single agent can unilaterally stop. Prisoner's dilemma, arms races, tragedy of the commons, whatever. And the usual diagnosis is: the bad outcome is stable because each party's best response to everyone else is to keep doing the bad thing. Coordination failure, basically.

My claim is that this lumps together two structurally different things, and the difference is what actually matters.

(1) Symmetric coordination traps. Everyone would be better off if everyone stopped, and the payoff structure is roughly mirrored across agents. Classic PD, fisheries, overfishing, that kind of thing. Here you genuinely need mutual assurance, a binding commitment device, or a change to the game itself.

(2) Ratchet traps. The bad outcome is stable not because each agent prefers defecting given what others do, but because there's a one-way ratchet: any agent who unilaterally de-escalates loses relative position and gets selected against, while the agents who remain in the population are the ones who didn't de-escalate. So the equilibrium is maintained by a selection/composition effect on the population of agents, not by the mutual-best-response property. The example I use is engagement-optimizing recommender systems. It's not that any one company prefers the arms race to a world where everyone backs off — it's that companies who back off lose users and get outcompeted, so the population of surviving firms is enriched for aggressive optimizers. Same shape for a lot of the enshittification stuff maybe, and arguably for certain AI training/compute races.

The reason I think the distinction is load-bearing: in a ratchet trap, a single agent can unilaterally exit the bad dynamic, it just pays a cost and gets selected out — which means interventions that change who survives (not who prefers what) are the relevant lever. In a coordination trap, unilateral exit is basically self-defeating and pointless. So "get everyone to agree" vs "change the selection pressure" map onto the two cases, and I think confusing them is why a lot of proposed fixes for races-to-the-bottom are aimed at the wrong target. A treaty works for (1) but is nearly useless for (2) if the ratchet keeps re-populating the bad actors.

Objections I anticipate: (a) am I just redescribing the difference between a game-theoretic equilibrium and an evolutionary/selection explanation, i.e. is (2) just (1) with a dynamic and replicator dynamics bolted on? (b) Is the distinction stable, or does every ratchet trap collapse into a coordination trap once you include staying in the game as a move? (c) Does my claim that a single agent can exit assume something false about irreversibility — maybe de-escalation is actually irreversible because of lock-in (infrastructure, data advantages, switching costs), so it really is a one-way door and not an exit at all.
