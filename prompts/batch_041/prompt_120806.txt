I've been sitting in on a Buddhist study group run by a nun — Tibetan trained, but she teaches the Pali suttas too — and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts on the cases people bring in, because they don't fit any of the boxes I know how to use. Roughly:

- A student lied to a border guard about what was in her bag to get her uncle's medicine through. The medicine was banned, the uncle was sick, nobody got hurt. Verdict: no fault at all, she'd call it good.
- Another student lied to her own doctor about how much she drinks, out of embarrassment. Verdict: fault, and a real one. Her gloss was that the drinking isn't the issue, the issue is that the student would rather be thought well of than be treated.
- The hospice case: a doctor gives a terminal patient a dose she knows will probably shorten his life, aiming only at his pain, at his request. Verdict: no fault. She stressed two things — the doctor wasn't aiming at his death, and she wasn't using him for anything.
- The soldier: kills a man who's about to throw a grenade into a market, saving a lot of people. Verdict: fault, no hedging. Someone asked about the lives and she said she wasn't asking how many he saved. The mind he did it with — terrified, furious — was, for her, the whole of the act.
- The builder: lies on a permit application, which gets a project approved that ends up employing most of a poor village. His wanting to help was genuine; he also got rich. Verdict: fault, but a smaller one than the soldier's. You did a good thing and you also did it for yourself, and the second part isn't nothing.
- The captain: the old story about the captain who kills a bandit to stop him robbing and murdering the merchants he's travelling with. Verdict: blameless — and she was emphatic that this one has nothing to teach us, because the captain was blameless for the reason that he'd have gone to hell for those merchants gladly. The same act by one of us, she's said, would wreck us.

She also keeps drawing a distinction I can't get a grip on: an act can be a transgression, she says, and you can still not be at fault for it. I don't know who's keeping the books in that picture, or what the transgression is doing if nobody's on the hook for it.

Half the group thinks she's a utilitarian in robes, since the state of mind seems to do all the work — but she faults people for doing the most good, so thats not it either.

Next week's case is this. A student runs a clinic in a district where a foreign foundation funds vaccine programs only for districts with a declared outbreak. Hers has no outbreak yet, but the district next door does and it's clearly coming; if she certifies that hers has one, the foundation moves immediately, the kids get vaccinated months early, and a lot of them live who otherwise wouldn't. She does it purely for the kids. She doesn't want the post that comes with running the program and expects to be miserable in it, but she takes it anyway, because turning it down leaves it to someone who'd botch it, and it comes with a salary she doesn't need but doesn't refuse. The form needs a second signature, so she signs her supervisor's name; if it ever comes out he's the one who wears it, and honestly she barely thinks about him, he's a formality to her.

What does she say about that one? I want the rule she's actually running, the other rules that fit her record just as well, what each of them predicts here, which one I should bet on and why, and what case I should put to her next to tell them apart. I care alot less about the verdict than about the rule.
