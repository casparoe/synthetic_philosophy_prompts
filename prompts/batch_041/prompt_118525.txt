A three-person group — one philosopher of language, one contracts lawyer, and one engineer — has two years, a shared budget, and no appetite for collecting new data. The engineer is building a system that tags utterances with an illocutionary force; the lawyer wants to know when those tags should track legal consequence; the philosopher wants an account that survives both pressures. Their single applied question is this: under what conditions does an utterance — or a keystroke, a signature, a nod, a synthesized voice — perform a determinate act of the kind that creates a commitment, transfers authority, or changes a normative status? What I want from you is an agenda for those two years: which open questions about illocutionary force are most worth settling first, in what order, and why.

Five puzzles currently sit in the group's way. Treat them as candidates rather than a closed menu: add whatever else belongs, and say explicitly if any of them is a distraction.

1. Medium neutrality. A raised hand at auction, a nod, a signature, a thumb on a reaction button, and a spoken acceptance all appear able to do the same kind of thing. Is force attached to words, to speaker intention, to conventional procedure, or to social recognition — and can one account cover the verbal and the non-verbal cases without collapsing into the claim that anything counts as anything so long as the community goes along?

2. Artificial and corporate speakers. A chatbot assures a user that its operator will keep their data private; a company issues an apology that no employee personally endorses; a cloning tool fabricates a statement by someone who never made it. Is there a speaker in these cases at all? If force can be constituted by institutional role rather than individual intention, what constrains which systems can perform which acts? And in the fabricated case, whose act, if anyone's, has been performed?

3. Indeterminate and deniable force. Some utterances seem designed to leave their force open: a passive construction that locates an error in no agent; a conditional apology that apologizes only for the addressee's reaction; a remark that can be retracted into a joke if it lands badly. Is indeterminate force a genuine category, or is a determinate act always performed and hearers merely disagree about which? If it is genuine, can the ambiguity itself do normative work — creating commitments the speaker can later disown?

4. Non-uptake and refusal. A promise the addressee refuses to acknowledge; testimony heard but not admitted as testimony; a resignation submitted to someone who declines to accept it. Is uptake constitutive of the act, a condition on some of its effects only, or irrelevant? The engineer needs an answer, because the classifier must decide whether to tag an utterance that nobody on the receiving end treats as what it purports to be.

5. Direction and address. Some accounts treat a speech act as essentially directed — a second-personal claim on a particular addressee; others treat it as a public change in the standing commitments of a conversation, with no essential addressee. Which notion is more fundamental, and does the choice make any difference downstream — to whether one can promise something to a person who will never hear it, or to whether an utterance addressed to no one can bind anyone?

For each question, address why it is or is not upstream of the others, how far it can be advanced by armchair argument within two years as opposed to needing the prototype or the case law, and what finding would move it up or down the list. Then give the ranked agenda: what this group should take up in year one, what in year two, and what it should deliberately leave alone. Close with the two or three claims you would stake most on, and the considerations that would most change your mind. I want the reasoning that generates the ordering, not a survey, and no reading list — everything you need is in this message.
