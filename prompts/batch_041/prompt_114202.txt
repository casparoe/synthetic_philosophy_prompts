A friend who TA'd a phil of language course gave me this gloss on Russell and I want to know how much of it to keep before I repeat it anywhere:

"Russell's problem was that a phrase like 'the present King of France' has to mean something for the sentence around it to mean anything at all, which is why Meinong ended up saying there are non-existent objects. Russell's fix was to say the description isn't a name: 'The present King of France is bald' really says that there's exactly one present King of France and that he's bald. So on Russell's view the sentence is neither true nor false — it just fails to express a proposition, since there's nothing for the description to latch onto. The general lesson is that definite descriptions never refer; 'the' turns out to be a quantifier like 'some' or 'every', an 'incomplete symbol' that has no meaning on its own. And that's why Strawson's objection — that we'd say 'there is no king of France' instead of 'that's false' — is generally taken to have refuted the analysis."

I get the quantifier paraphrase, and honestly the "neither true nor false" part feels right to me too, since there just isn't a king. So where does this go wrong? Go clause by clause: what Russell actually held, what's a harmless simplification, what's a straight misattribution, and whether the Strawson point really kills the theory or is doing something else entirely.
