Gould and Lewontin argued that biologists are way too quick to assume every trait is an adaptation. Their example is the spandrels in the dome of St Mark's in Venice, the triangular spaces between the arches. Nobody put them there on purpose — they're just the unavoidable gaps you get when you sit a dome on round arches, and the mosaics got added later. The lesson is that alot of biological traits are like that: side effects of other stuff rather than adaptations, and a spandrel is basically a trait that got repurposed for something it wasn't originally for. Gould and Lewontin called the habit of seeing design everywhere the Panglossian paradigm, after Dr Pangloss in Voltaire's Candide, and argued that adaptationist explanations are unfalsifiable — whatever you find, you can always cook up a story about how it was selected for — so the whole programme isn't really science and the traits actually worth studying are the non-adaptive ones. The blog also mentioned that critics have said the cathedral analogy backfires, since the spandrels of St Mark's were designed by the architect in the first place.
