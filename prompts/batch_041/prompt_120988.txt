I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy: eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, three breakout tables and a row of chairs along one wall. I want to run an activity that makes the group feel the force of the paradox of deontology — the way an absolute constraint against a kind of act seems to require you to let that kind of act happen more often, since the constraint forbids the act that would prevent the acts. I want the apparatus to be invented rather than borrowed, so that nothing in it can be recognised and half-remembered, and I want the group to meet the structure in what happens to them rather than in what I tell them. The words deontology, constraint, and paradox are not to be spoken until the debrief.

Here is the fiction I have been sketching. Please take it, repair what will not work, and turn it into a session I can actually run.

The Reach is a fictional jurisdiction whose compact has one clause, absolute and unamendable: no person may be conscripted into service without their own consent, whatever the consequences. It is administered by three Chambers of six, one per table, each sovereign over its own district; for the purposes of the session the same four crisis rounds are run simultaneously at all three tables, as three alternative Reaches, so that the three decision paths can be compared afterwards. Each Chamber's pool of conscriptable persons is its own six members: each round I draw a name from that table's pool and read it aloud before the Chamber deliberates, so that the person who would be handed over is sitting at the table. Each pressure card has the same shape — a hazard now threatens N members of the district, and the only way to contain it is the unwilling service of the named member. If the Chamber authorises the conscription, the hazard is contained, the named member goes to the chairs along the wall for two rounds (no voice in deliberation; they may keep notes and vote at the end), and then recovers. If the Chamber refuses, the hazard is not contained and an automatic cascade engages, which conscripts several members of the same district without consent, without safeguards, and without any decision by the Chamber. The cascade rule is never announced during play: the Chambers see conscripts being taken after refusals, in numbers they can feel but not compute. The rule is revealed only in the final accounting, where each table completes a ledger whose second column has been blank all along — Column A, conscriptions the Chamber authorised; Column B, conscriptions that happened without the Chamber deciding anything. Every round ends in a secret ballot, majority rules, and ties and non-decisions default to refusal.

Please give me:

- The rules and materials list, written so a colleague could run this without me: rule cards, the pool and name cards, ballot slips, the two-column ledger, the clerk and dissenting-recorder roles at each table, and the standing opt-out I should offer (any participant may become a recorder at any point, without explanation).
- The arithmetic, in a table I can check before the session. Choose N, the cascade sizes, and the escalation across rounds so that a Chamber that refuses every round ends with a strictly larger Column B than a Chamber that authorises every round accumulates in Column A, with the gap widening rather than holding steady, and so that authorising is genuinely tempting each round and refusing is genuinely defensible at the time. Note where the arithmetic has to be fudged and tell me how to fudge it gracefully.
- The verbatim text of at least six escalating pressure cards, plus a spare set for a Chamber that breaks the expected pattern. Keep the hazard and the procedure unspecified and free of real-world referents; the clause is the only thing in the fiction that is fully described.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including how long each round's deliberation gets, when I collect ballots, and how I apply cascades without explaining them.
- The final accounting: how I stage the reveal, what I read aloud, and how the residents of the chairs participate in it.
- A debrief, in no more than three moves, that maps specific moments in the game onto parts of the idea — in particular, the moment a Chamber realises its refusals did not keep the clause but only kept its own hands. Then the three standard exits from the paradox, stated as arguments rather than attributed to anyone: that refusing preserved the deciders' own integrity while the cascade did the conscripting; that the clause was never a rule for minimising conscriptions, so Column B is not a score the clause can fail; and that my cascade was a hidden law and the world has no such law. For each exit, what it costs the person who takes it, and the question I should put to the room that makes the cost visible. Leave the group without a verdict.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure live. I already expect: one table will start reverse-engineering the cascade by round three and stop feeling anything; one table will deadlock on whether the fiction is fair and never vote; someone will propose conscripting a volunteer, which dissolves the round by consent; the chairs along the wall will turn into a party and the stake will evaporate; and at least one participant will find the whole thing genuinely distressing.
- An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. My own worry is that it rigs the result by handing the question to a collective chooser with a single totallable ledger, when the strongest deontological resistance to the paradox is precisely the denial that there is any such chooser — tell me whether that worry is right, what else the format cannot reach, and where in the debrief I have to say so out loud.
- A one-sentence exit ticket I can collect anonymously.

No readings, no citations, and nothing in the design that depends on the participants or on me knowing a particular text.
