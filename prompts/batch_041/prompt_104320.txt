I cover science and ideas for a general-reader magazine, and I'm assembling a package on the long argument over unrealistic assumptions in economics — specifically the habit of invoking Milton Friedman's 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics," and the doctrine Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist, to wave away assumptions that everyone agrees are false. Friedman's own text attaches conditions his popularisers drop, and philosophers of economics have for decades sorted false assumptions into kinds: those that treat some factor as negligible, those that restrict a theory to a domain where the factor is absent, and those that are openly heuristic — known to be false, not asserted as approximately true, and held in place only until a better theory arrives. My instinct is that the third kind is where the popular defence quietly comes apart.

For the package I'd rather have one satirical artefact than an essay about satire. Please write the model-documentation page of an invented quantitative consultancy — name it what you like — that sells behavioural forecasts (job changes, divorces, turnout, church attendance) from a model whose agents are fully informed, forward-looking expected-utility maximisers facing no frictions. Aim for about 700–800 words, in the deadpan register of a corporate transparency notice: an assurance that the assumptions are not meant literally, a line about the model having been validated against three decades of data, a note that the firm does not claim its clients are rational, only that they behave as if they were, and a short taxonomy sidebar in which the firm cheerfully files its own assumptions under the very category its critics say the defence cannot reach.

Two constraints. First, keep it fair: let the firm state the strongest version of its case, so that a methodologist reads the argument rather than a strawman — the humour should come from following the doctrine to where it actually leads, not from making the firm stupid. Second, stay deadpan; no winking asides, no signalling that the page is absurd. A reader who knows the literature should end up recognising the standard objections — that success on a stable sample cannot license extrapolation or policy use, that the doctrine can make a model unfalsifiable along the one dimension that matters, and that the as-if claim smuggles in a mechanism it refuses to name — without any of them being stated outright.

Finally, after the artefact, add a short editorial note (a paragraph or two, clearly not part of the page) saying how confident you are that the piece is faithful to Friedman's actual position and to the standard objections to it, where it takes liberties with the doctrine or leans on a contested reading, and the single point in it you are least sure you have got right.
