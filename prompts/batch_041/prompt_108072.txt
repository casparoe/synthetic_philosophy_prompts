I'm part of a small philosophy-of-mind reading group — five of us, we meet in whoever's kitchen is biggest, and next week I'm the one who has to open the session. Last time we spent the evening on a thought experiment that I'd like your help with, because the room split roughly down the middle and I honestly couldn't referee it. The setup is this: a vast enclosure, stadium-sized, packed with an astronomical number of tiny identical balls — the group called the box a careenium and the balls simmballs — bouncing off one another and off the walls. Tune the walls and the starting arrangement just so, and a standing pattern in the balls implements a one-bit memory, so that the whole contraption can be rigged to answer simple yes/no questions in a way that depends on what it has stored. Nothing in the box knows what a bit is, no single ball holds the memory, there is no little person inside reading anything off; and yet the pattern persists, can be read from the outside, and reliably drives what comes out the far end. What divided us was whether the pattern — the symbol, the remembered bit — pushes the substrate around, or whether the simmballs simply push one another and the symbol is only our way of describing the pushing.

Two of the group, Nadia and Owen, went three rounds at the whiteboard while the rest of us ate. (1) Nadia: the pattern is a cause. The bit the careenium has stored is what makes it answer as it does; the simmballs are the medium, not the mover. Compare a chess program — we say the choice of move caused the piece to move, and nobody insists that the transistors caused it while the program merely described them. If you want to predict or control the careenium, the level that pays is the register, not the collisions. (2) Owen: nothing pushes a simmball but other simmballs and the walls, and the stored bit is just a summary of an enormous number of collisions. Point to anything the meaning does that the collisions do not already do. Give me a complete micro-physics of the box and every motion follows from it without ever mentioning a bit; if the register were a cause as well, each effect would have two sufficient causes, and we would have to admit that the physical story is incomplete — the very price the thought experiment was supposed to help us avoid paying. (3) Nadia: then use the test we use everywhere else. Intervene on the register, nudge whatever implements the bit from 0 to 1, and the downstream answer changes; nudge a simmball in a way that leaves the register untouched, and nothing downstream changes. That is the difference-making test for causation, and it is the only test the special sciences have. Stronger still, the register-to-answer relation is invariant across micro-realizations: any swarm that implements 1 yields the same answer, and that invariance is what earns a level its causal credentials. (4) Owen: your test is too generous — every individual simmball passes it too, since nudging any of them changes the trajectory, and so does every disjunction of micro-states and the sum of the box's contents. When everything counts as a cause, the word stops discriminating. And the invariance you are proud of is itself a micro-fact: it holds because the states that implement 1 are physically alike in exactly the respects that matter downstream. You have explained a macro-regularity by a micro-regularity and then called the macro-regularity a cause. (5) Nadia: you cannot take that line without losing the special sciences wholesale. The hurricane caused the flood; the price rise caused the shortage; the gene caused the disease — each of those is a pattern over micro-events and would fail your test, since a complete micro-story makes each of them redundant. If the register is not a cause, nothing above the level of elementary particles is, and the careenium was supposed to teach us precisely the opposite: that a pattern can be thoroughly real without being a further ingredient in the world. You keep treating real as one more thing, which is the single move the thought experiment was built to block. (6) Owen: I will grant that the register is real in that sense — stable, projectible, worth its keep in prediction. What I deny is that it does any pushing. All the pushing is simmballs, and there is a complete account of the pushing that never mentions a bit. What is not verbal, though, is whether the register-invariance could be derived from the micro-story: if it could, the symbol level has no independent grip on anything and all the explaining is done below; if it could not, then I have lost and there really is something the symbol level adds. At that point we broke up, and neither of them had budged.

So: please adjudicate the exchange as it actually happened, move by move — for each numbered step, say whether it is right, wrong, or right but for the wrong reason, and rate it accordingly, then say what that move should have been if it went wrong. After that, tell me whether the disagreement is substantive or finally verbal, which of the two has the better of it overall, and what both of them are missing that would change the shape of the question. I would rather have the verdict than a reading list: I don't need citations or suggestions for further reading, and I would like you to reason the thing out rather than gesture at who usually gets cited in this area.
