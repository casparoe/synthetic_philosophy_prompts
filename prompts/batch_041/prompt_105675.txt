I maintain the rulebook for a 200,000-member online community: roughly forty rules, revised quarterly, enforced day to day by volunteer moderators with no training in logic. A growing share of the rulebook consists of rules about rules — how rules are proposed, amended, interpreted, and overridden — and we keep drafting ones that tie us in knots: a rule that appears to apply to itself, a rule requiring every decision to be reviewed by a body whose own decisions need review, a rule that seems to authorize its own repeal. I do not need an essay on Russell's paradox. I need a procedure the rules committee can run on any proposed or existing rule before it goes into the handbook.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as an answer to each, the point at which the procedure halts with "rewrite this rule," and the point at which it halts with "this is the committee's call, not a logic problem." For any rule that looks self-referential, it must separate four cases: a genuine contradiction, a mere infinite regress, a rule that is only ever vacuously satisfied, and an ordinary conflict between two rules that a stated priority order would settle. It should also say when to bar a rule from applying to itself, when to stratify the rulebook into levels, and when to narrow a rule's subject matter — and what each repair costs us in meaning or coverage, since several of these rules only do their job if they reach everything, including the rules.

Then run it on three: (1) "Any rule that imposes more burden than it prevents is repealed." (2) "Every enforcement decision may be appealed, and every appeal is decided by the rules committee, whose decisions may themselves be appealed." (3) "No rule takes effect until the committee has approved it" — which this rule never was. I expect at least one of these to come out against me. Close with what the procedure cannot do.
