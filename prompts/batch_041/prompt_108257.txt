My friend Dana and I burned like two hours on this at a diner last night and I honestly can't tell who won, so I want to write it out and actually settle it.

Her case: lying and misleading are the same wrong. Either way you're trying to get someone to believe something false, and the truth of the individual sentences is a technicality. She thinks the misleading version is actually worse, because you make the other person do the deceiving themselves, and because you keep your hands clean — you get to say "I never said that," which is a second insult stacked on the first. She says the liar at least puts their own credibility on the line, whereas the misleader gets to use the truth as a weapon and then act indignant when you catch on. She called it lying with a receipt (I think there's a name for this, paltering or something).

My case: then there's nothing left of what's wrong with lying in particular, and half of ordinary life gets condemned along with it — magicians, surprise parties, bluffing at poker, telling a host the stew was so hearty when it was awful. Some of that is clearly fine and some of it clearly isn't, and the only thing separating them looks like whether the words you said were true.

So write me an essay that settles it. Weigh the best arguments on both sides, but don't end on "it's complicated" — land on a verdict: is there a moral difference between lying and misleading, and if there is, where exactly does it live (in the falsity of what you said, in what you intended, in what the hearer ends up believing, in what you can be held answerable for afterward)? If there isn't, say so and own the consequences. And actually resolve these two, don't just wave at them: (1) a false statement to someone who knows it's false — sarcasm, or a bluff at a poker table; (2) a misleader in a setting where the person being misled has in some sense agreed to it, like a magic show or a surprise party. If the difference is real, it has to do some work in those cases.
