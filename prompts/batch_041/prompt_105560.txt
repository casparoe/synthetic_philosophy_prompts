I'm the safety-and-quality lead at a company that makes industrial lifting gear -- cranes, hoists, rigging -- and a chunk of my job is writing up incident investigations, maybe fifteen a year, everything from a near-miss where a load slipped a few inches off the floor to a fatality. The findings section of those reports is basically a list of 'we know X' sentences. We know the sling was undersized. We know the operator bypassed the limit switch. We know the brake seized because of water getting in. Legal reads them, the regulator reads them, and after a bad one a plaintiff's lawyer reads them back to me three years later in a room with a court reporter.

I keep getting into fights here about how strong the evidence has to be before I'm allowed to write 'we know'. Some of my colleagues say it's the same standard every time -- either you've got the evidence or you haven't, and it doesn't matter whether we're talking about a scuffed paint job or a guy in the hospital. I've been reading around this thing called pragmatic encroachment, which says that's not how it works, that how much evidence you need before you know something shifts with what's at stake. And there's contextualism, which is a cousin of it, somthing about 'knows' changing meaning with the conversational context, though I gather the two views aren't the same thing.

Can you turn this into an actual procedure I can run? I want the questions to ask in order for a given finding, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and a rule for where the report stops short of 'we know' and says 'the evidence indicates' or 'we could not determine'. I've got about an hour per finding and no philosophy training, and legal has to sign off on the wording. Things it has to handle:

- Same evidence, different stakes. A visual inspection plus a maintenance log looks like knowledge after a near-miss. Does it look like knowledge after a fatality?
- Whose stakes? Mine writing the report? The guy on the shop floor? His family? The reader in the deposition?
- The timing problem. If 'knows' depends on context, then a sentence can be true when I write it and false when it's read back to me in court. Does that mean I should just write to the highest foreseeable stakes, or does that throw away the whole idea?
- The differnce between the bar for the claim being true, the bar for me putting it in the report, and the bar for us acting on it -- shutting a line, recalling a product. I suspect my colleagues are smearing these three together and I want the procedure to keep them apart even if it turns out they usually track each other.

Then run it on three findings: (1) a near-miss where a load slipped and the log shows a missed inspection; (2) a fatality where the physical evidence burned up and all I've got is two conflicting witness statements; (3) a case where the evidence is genuinely solid but the finding lands on our biggest customer's maintenance crew.

Also tell me straight where the procedure can't decide anything and I'm just making a values call, and where it'll misfire -- I'd rather find that out now than in a deposition. And please don't hand me a reading list, I need the thing itself.
