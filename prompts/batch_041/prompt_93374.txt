I retired last year, after thirty years in experimental physics, and have spent the time since reading philosophy on my own and with a small fortnightly reading group. I came to the subject late and without formal training, and I have noticed that the habits of my old trade — above all, the insistence on asking whether a quantity is well defined before asking for its value — are not always the habits philosophy rewards. The exchange I am about to describe is one in which I genuinely cannot tell whether the question at issue is well defined, and I would like you to adjudicate it for me.

The occasion was a discussion of a principle that two members of the group took opposite sides on. Stated roughly, the principle says that the thing to do is whatever you would endorse if you were fully informed, fully coherent, and had deliberated as long as you liked — the verdict, as it were, of your ideal counterpart. The question was whether that principle can bear the weight placed on it, and whether one has any reason to follow such a counterpart at all. Neither party is a philosopher by profession, but both are careful, and neither was obviously losing.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

Rowan: The principle is the only one that escapes arbitrariness. If I say 'do what seems best to you now', I license every prejudice and every mistake; if I say 'do what your fully informed, fully coherent counterpart would endorse', I have not asserted a standard, I have defined what correctness is. So deferring to that counterpart is not a leap of faith; it is what 'ought' means.

Iris: Then it owes us an answer to a question it cannot answer — which of my attitudes survive the idealization. My preferences are partly made out of my beliefs, so idealizing away my ignorance does not leave my preferences untouched. The fully informed counterpart is not me with the errors sanded off; it is a different agent who happens to share my history, and nothing in the definition guarantees that this agent cares about the things I care about, or about me.

Rowan: That conflates correcting a preference with replacing it. Suppose I want the stuff in the glass because I believe it is gin and it is in fact bleach. The counterpart still wants what I wanted — not to drink bleach — because what I wanted all along was the gin. Idealization reveals the content of my existing concerns; it does not substitute new ones. The counterpart is me, made consistent.

Iris: That works where the belief is incidental. But for the things that matter most — whom to love, what work to give one's life to, whether to have children — my attitudes are not a residue that survives once the beliefs are stripped away; they are partly constituted by a particular way of seeing the world. Strip that away and you do not get the same concern purified; you get a different concern, or none. And if the reply is that those attitudes were never errors, then the idealization has no grip in the cases that matter, which is a strange place for a standard of conduct to sit idle.

Rowan: Even if idealization is hard or indeterminate in the difficult cases, in the plain ones it still yields an answer, and it would be perverse to refuse to follow it there. We defer to idealized versions of ourselves all the time: I take my future self's interests as reasons, and I trust my considered judgments over my three-in-the-morning ones, and I am not wrong to.

Iris: The future-self comparison is disanalogous exactly where it needs to be analogous. My future self is downstream of me rather than a limit I can never reach, and I already have reason to care about his interests. There is also a regress. If the standard is 'what my ideal counterpart would endorse', why stop there? My ideal counterpart, being ideally coherent, would presumably tell me to do what a further idealized counterpart would endorse. If the first idealization is privileged, it is privileged by something other than idealization, and that something is doing the real work; if it is not privileged, the standard never issues a verdict. Either way it fails to be what it claims to be: a procedure that settles what to do without importing a substantive commitment. And there is the further difficulty that if the counterpart is the limit of an endless deliberation, then no actual stage of me occupies that limit; it is not obvious that any stage has reason to adopt the limit's preferences, or that the limit is a determinate thing rather than a direction in which preferences drift without ever settling.

Neither would move, and I did not intervene.

What I want from you is not a verdict on which of them is more congenial but a point-by-point adjudication of the argument as it was actually made. For each move, say whether it is sound, mistaken, or right in part, and briefly justify the rating. Say where a term is being used in two different senses (I suspect that 'idealization' and 'endorse' are each doing double duty), and where one of them is attacking a position the other does not actually hold. Say what, if anything, both are missing, and whether the dispute is substantive or turns entirely on how the principle is defined. Please be explicit about where the burden of proof lies at each step. Finally, say whether the principle can be restated so as to meet the strongest objection raised, or whether it should be abandoned, and what a version of it that survives would look like. You need not cite any literature — I would rather have the arguments themselves than a map of who said what, and in any case I have no way of checking a bibliography.
