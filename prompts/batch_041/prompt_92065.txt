For a graduate seminar on Moore, I need a problem that cannot be answered by reciting Principia Ethica. In its final chapter, Moore treats the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection as the great goods, while the same book insists that the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts. Assume both doctrines are his, and argue that the second, taken at full strength, makes the first untenable as a ranking of two goods: what the chapter presents as two goods are really one compound good, so that any verdict weighing aesthetic enjoyment against personal affection as independent items is incoherent. I do not want the familiar objection that organic unity is trivial because a whole is just another object with its own value; I want the harder claim that it fuses the two goods. Give the argument as exactly five numbered steps, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, and in each step mark whether you take it to be uncontested exegesis or a contested interpretive move, so that a reader can see where the settled reading of Moore ends and the disputed one begins. Write in American English.
