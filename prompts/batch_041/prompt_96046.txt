I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about when it is legitimate to explain a social outcome by appeal to a collective — a firm, a class, a market, a culture, an institution — rather than to the beliefs and actions of the individuals involved. Going case by case and trusting my reactions, I get verdicts that I cannot get to add up, and I would like you to find the principle behind them, or else tell me that there isn't one and which verdicts have to go.

Here are my verdicts. A firm moved its plant offshore to escape a union drive: fine. France sought to humiliate Germany at the peace conference: fine, even though a great many French people wanted nothing of the kind. The hierarchy's opposition to contraception shaped policy in several countries: fine. Consumers balked at the price rise and sales collapsed: fine, and I take this to be nothing over and above a lot of individuals reacting. The currency fell because the market lost confidence in it: not fine — the only evidence for the confidence seems to be the fall. The depression helped the extreme right: fine. The working class was betrayed by its leaders: not fine, since the working class never decided anything and its members disagreed about everything. The party's discipline came from its organizational structure rather than its ideology: fine. The region's culture resisted industrialization: not fine; I cannot see what I would be asserting beyond a pattern of individual refusals, and when I try to make it more than that it turns into a claim about a group mind. The Wehrmacht's rigid command structure hampered improvisation: fine, even though I can't state this as a fact about any individual's beliefs or desires. The bureaucracy expanded because it needed to: not fine. Smallholders had an interest in inflation, so they tolerated it: fine. The bourgeoisie supported the extreme right because it was in its objective interest: not fine, since the interest is being read off the outcome it is supposed to explain. The banks lobbied against the rule because they stood to profit from its absence: fine.

My tentative principle is that collective talk is legitimate just in case it can be replaced, without loss of truth or causal relevance, by talk about individuals. That handles the consumers, the smallholders and the banks, but it seems to me simply false of the firm, the French state and the Wehrmacht, where the replacement loses the very thing doing the explaining. So I suspect a second clause is needed, and I don't know what it should say.

What I want from you: the best principle or small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough to apply to cases I haven't thought of, with the specific verdict above that motivates each clause; your verdicts on three cases I haven't settled — whether "the election turned on the economy" is an aggregate claim or a holistic one, whether "the housing market's speculative dynamics" is legitimate given that no one intended the dynamic and no body decided on it, and whether your principle extends to collectives that are not made of people at all, such as a colony or a hive; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should give up. If two principles fit equally well, say which is simpler and what turns on the choice.

Open with the principle in two or three sentences — I want the bottom line before the defence — then the rest. Keep the whole reply under 800 words.
