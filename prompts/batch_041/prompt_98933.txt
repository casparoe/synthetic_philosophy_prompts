You are a philosopher specializing in metaphysics and the philosophy of language. I am writing to you with an argument that a colleague and I constructed for a graduate seminar, and which I now find suspicious because its conclusion is absurd. Please think carefully about it and, before you settle on a diagnosis, brainstorm at least three candidate points of failure. Then, in continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way one would write a careful email—tell me which step you reject, why that step looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. I would also like you to say which of the standard responses you find least costly, and whether the idea I describe below as a mode of combination can be saved.

Here is the argument.

(1) A proposition is a unified entity: it is not a mere collection of its constituents but something that says that things stand in a certain way. (2) For any proposition, its unity must be accounted for. (3) If the unity of a proposition is itself a constituent of that proposition, then the proposition contains a further item—call it the unifying relation—that must be unified with the other constituents. (4) That further unity would require yet another unifying relation, and so on without end. (5) If, instead, the unity of a proposition is not a constituent, then either it is a further entity (which would make it a constituent, contradicting the assumption) or it is a non-entity: a mode of combination, a form, or a way the constituents are arranged. (6) But a non-entity cannot bind the constituents unless it is related to them, and any relation is an entity, which returns us to the regress. (7) Therefore, either way, the constituents of a proposition are never unified. (8) Therefore, there are no propositions. (9) But there are propositions: for example, that the cat is on the mat, that two is even, and that if it rains the ground will be wet. (10) So something must be wrong with the argument.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (9). I also want to keep the idea that the proposition that the cat is on the mat differs from the proposition that the mat is on the cat, so the arrangement of constituents matters. What I cannot see is where the regress stops. Some friends tell me the regress is benign, because an infinite chain of unifying relations is not vicious. Others tell me that the constituents are incomplete in a way that makes them combine without any external binder. Others say that propositions are not structured at all, and that talk of constituents is a useful fiction. I want to know which of these moves, if any, can be accepted without giving up the idea that a proposition's truth-condition is determined by its constituents, and what each move costs. Please give me your diagnosis.
