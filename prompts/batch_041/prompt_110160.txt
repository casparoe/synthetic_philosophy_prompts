I retired last year from a career in experimental physics, and I have been spending my new leisure reading philosophy in a somewhat unsystematic way. In a reading group on ancient political thought, a friend—a former economist, now also an amateur philosopher—presented the following reconstruction of Aristotle’s views on money and exchange, and I cannot decide whether it is a fair criticism or a caricature. He put it this way:

(1) For Aristotle, exchange is natural when it serves need: each party gives what the other lacks, and money is merely a conventional measure that facilitates this. (2) Aristotle also insists that acquiring money for its own sake, and especially lending money at interest, is unnatural, since money is barren and its end is not need but accumulation. (3) But in any market exchange, the seller acquires money, and the merchant who buys in order to sell again does so precisely for the sake of money. (4) Therefore, by Aristotle’s own standard, the everyday commerce of the polis is unnatural. (5) Yet Aristotle treats the polis as the natural completion of human life and cannot consistently condemn the exchange on which it depends. (6) So Aristotle’s account of money proves too much and must be rejected.

My friend takes this to show that Aristotle’s economic remarks are incoherent. I suspect the argument trades on a distinction I am not seeing, perhaps between money as a tool of exchange and money as an end, or between household management and retail trade. But I also worry that the distinction may be doing more work than Aristotle can license. Which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost? If I want to preserve the core of Aristotle’s view—that there is something natural about exchange for need and something corrosive about the endless pursuit of money—which premise can I give up without losing that core? Another friend suggested the repair is to distinguish natural acquisition from wealth-getting by the limit internal to the activity: natural acquisition has a limit set by need, whereas wealth-getting does not. That sounds promising to me, but it also seems to make the difference psychological rather than economic, and I would like to know whether it actually rescues Aristotle or merely relabels the problem.
