I instruct an undergraduate seminar in ethics, and two students stayed behind after class to argue at length about a hypothetical I had put on the board. The case was this: a reactor's coolant can be routed along only one path, through a small maintenance room in which a technician is at work and from which, given the emergency, he cannot be extracted in time; sending the coolant through will scald him fatally, whereas not sending it will cause a containment failure that kills hundreds in the adjacent town. The operator, knowing the technician will die, routes the coolant, saves the town, and the technician dies. I had offered it as a candidate for the doctrine of double effect and left it at that. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Maria contended that the case fails the doctrine's condition on means. The town cannot be saved unless the coolant passes through that room, and the coolant's passing through that room is precisely what kills the technician; since the good effect is unattainable except through the occurrence of the fatal effect, she said, the death is a means rather than a mere side effect, and the act is impermissible.

(2) Dev answered that this conflates causal necessity with being a means. What saves the town is the coolant's drawing off heat, not the technician's death; the death contributes nothing to the cooling and is merely an unavoidable accompaniment. If "you could not have done it otherwise" sufficed to make something a means, he said, then every foreseen side effect would count as a means and the doctrine would forbid any act with a foreseen harmful consequence, which would render it useless.

(3) Maria replied that the distinction was doing less work than Dev supposed. In the familiar contrast he was leaning on, the terror bomber uses the civilians' deaths to break the enemy's will, whereas the tactical bomber's bombs destroy the target and the civilians die beside it. Her case, she said, contains no analogous instrument that does the saving independently of the harm: the very event that saves is the event that kills, so its structure is nearer the terror bomber's than Dev would allow.

(4) Dev said his case is structurally like the tactical bomber's, and that the only difference Maria had identified was closeness — how directly the harmful effect lies on the causal path to the good. Closeness, he granted, may bear on how culpable the agent is, but it does not change what the agent intends, and the doctrine, he insisted, is about intention, not about how near the harm falls.

(5) Maria then added a detail: the operator had earlier been passed over for promotion by the technician and privately felt relief when the man died. If the doctrine is truly about intention, she argued, this shows the operator did not merely foresee the death but welcomed it, so even on Dev's own reading the act should fail.

(6) Dev responded that this proves too much. On that standard no one could ever satisfy the doctrine, since no one can govern stray feelings; what matters is what the operator was aiming at when he deliberated, and his plan was to save the town. Wanting a person dead, he said, is not the same as killing him as a means.

I was not prepared to referee this on the spot and told them I would think it over. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of (1) through (6), state whether the move is clearly mistaken, clearly correct, or only partly right, and give the reason. Then say which of the two, if either, holds the better overall position on this case, and identify what both of them are leaving out.
