I teach a graduate seminar on dispositions and causal powers, and in our last meeting a student presented a reconstruction — assembled from the finks-and-masks literature, she said, though I have not been able to trace it to any single source — which she now suspects proves far more than anyone should accept. I have reproduced it below with the steps numbered, tidying the prose but not, I hope, the reasoning:

(1) A disposition is individuated by its characteristic manifestation together with the circumstances that elicit it: for a thing to be disposed to M when C is for it to be such that, were C to obtain, M would occur.
(2) A thing can possess a disposition, meet the eliciting circumstances, and still fail to manifest it. A fink removes the disposition at the very moment the circumstances arrive; a mask blocks the manifestation while the disposition remains in place. Nothing about either device is exotic, and no disposition is immune to interference of this kind.
(3) So for any disposition and any eliciting circumstance, it is possible that the disposition is present, the circumstance obtains, and the manifestation does not occur.
(4) So no disposition guarantees its manifestation conditional: the conditional with which (1) identifies the disposition can fail while the disposition is present.
(5) Hence nothing is such that, were C to obtain, M would occur, in virtue of its disposition.
(6) Since being disposed to M when C just is being such that, were C to occur, M would occur, and by (5) nothing is such that, were C to occur, M would occur, nothing is disposed to M when C.
(7) But M and C were arbitrary, and a property that answers to no manifestation conditional is not dispositional at all.
(8) So nothing has any dispositional property: nothing is fragile, soluble, magnetic, or irritable.

I cannot accept (8) — it would gut the explanatory vocabulary of the special sciences and of ordinary life alike — but I am finding it harder than I expected to say precisely where the argument goes wrong. I am confident of (2) and (3); the fink and mask cases are real and I would not surrender them. What I want to avoid is the familiar move of rejecting (1) and quietly reinstating the simple conditional analysis, which I take to be dead for exactly the reasons (2) supplies. Nor do I want to be told merely that the conclusion is absurd; I already know that, and I need to know what licenses it.

So: which step should the student give up, why did it look compelling in the first place, and what does giving it up cost her? If the answer is that (1) must be replaced rather than merely qualified, tell me what account of what fixes a disposition's identity she then owes us, and show that it survives the fink and mask cases that started the trouble. If instead the fault lies in the passage from (4) through (6), tell me exactly what the fallacy is and why the repair does not collapse back into the simple conditional analysis. And I would like the alternatives priced: what must each of the standard positions concede in order to escape (8) — a refined conditional analysis carrying an interference clause, a non-conditional account on which dispositions are individuated by their essences, and the categoricalist line that the conclusion is true but harmless because talk of the dispositional is a manner of speaking? A short verdict at the end would help me hand this back with comments.
