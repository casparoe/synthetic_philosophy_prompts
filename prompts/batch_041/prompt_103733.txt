I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been roped into doing a short set at our department's end-of-year revue, which is mostly in-jokes for people who have sat through the same logic and metaphysics seminars I have. I want to write a joke about the Liar paradox and the theories of truth that have been built to contain it, and I need it to be genuinely funny to someone who knows the material — the punchline should turn on the actual content of a position, not merely on the fact that a sentence saying of itself that it is false is a strange thing to encounter. What I don't want is the standard gag where the philosopher pronounces the sentence false and the sentence smugly announces that it told him so; that version has been told to death, and at best it earns a pity laugh from people who are just pleased to recognize the reference.

So give me a joke, or a short piece of spoken material, that only lands if the audience understands the machinery. It could be built around the contrast between a hierarchical response in the spirit of Tarski and a groundedness or fixed-point response in the spirit of Kripke, around what a paracomplete theorist and a dialetheist would each say when pressed, around the revenge problem, or around the way a contextualist keeps quietly changing the subject; the choice is yours, but it should commit to one or two of these and get the details right. Assume the audience knows the basic setup, has heard of the main positions, and will notice if you cheat. It's for a spoken set, so no visual props and nothing that depends on typography, and please keep it short enough to actually tell — a setup and a punchline, or at most a three-beat exchange, rather than an essay. If it helps, you can add a sentence afterward explaining what the joke is doing and which position it is taking a jab at, since I'd like to be able to explain it to the two people in the room who took the ethics track.
