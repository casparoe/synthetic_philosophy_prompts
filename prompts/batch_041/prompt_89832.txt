Question 3. The covering-law model of explanation holds that to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected, by exhibiting it as the conclusion of an argument whose premises include at least one general law together with statements of particular conditions. Its critics hold that historians explain by telling stories rather than by subsuming events under laws. (a) State the model's requirements, including its account of the relation between explaining and predicting, and its treatment of explanations that appeal to probabilistic rather than universal regularities. (b) State the strongest version of the narrativist objection — the version that does more than observe that historians happen to lack laws — and distinguish the sense in which a narrative is a way of presenting material from the sense in which it might itself do explanatory work. (c) Apply both models to the following case. A historian explains why a particular famine-relief policy was adopted by recounting a sequence: an early poor harvest, a delayed report reaching the capital, an official's misreading of that report, a public outcry, and then the policy. Say what each model must say about the case, which handles it better, and whether the two models are genuine rivals or might be combined.

Student's answer. (a) To explain an event on this model is to show that it was to be expected: we set out a deductive argument whose premises include at least one general law and statements of particular conditions, and the event follows as the conclusion. Because the argument is deductive, the same premises that explain an event would have served to predict it; explaining and predicting are two uses of one structure, differing only in whether the conclusion is known in advance. Where the historian has only probabilistic regularities, the argument becomes inductive rather than deductive: the event is shown to be highly probable given the laws and conditions, and that is sufficient for an explanation, since we cannot demand of history more than we demand of the statistical sciences.

(b) The strongest objection is not that historians happen to lack laws. It is that the temporal order of the events does explanatory work. What explains the policy is the sequence — harvest, report, misreading, outcry — and this sequence cannot be represented as a set of initial conditions feeding a law, because the significance of the earlier items is fixed only by the later ones: the poor harvest counts as a cause only because of how things turned out. A narrative, then, is not merely a way of presenting an explanation that could in principle have been given as an argument; the narrative form is part of what explains. One should distinguish the claim that histories are narrated, which is a fact about their form, from the claim that narration explains, which is a claim about what does the explanatory work.

(c) The covering-law model cannot handle this case. The historian explains the policy by tracing the sequence, and the explanation states no general law; and the historian could not have predicted the policy from the early harvest, since the report might have been read correctly. The fact that we can explain the policy without being able to predict it shows that the asserted symmetry between explanation and prediction is false, and with it the covering-law model. The narrativist account does better, because it takes the temporal structure of the case seriously. The two models cannot be combined, since the narrativist's claim is that the narrative form itself does explanatory work. The covering-law model is at its strongest where a regularity really is available — the spread of a disease through a population, or the effect of a price change on supply — and there the deductive form does capture what we want.
