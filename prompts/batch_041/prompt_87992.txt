I write about computing, physics, and the foundations of mathematics for a general-interest magazine, and alongside the long features I run a short fortnightly column that assesses research claims quickly; the pitches I get come in waves, and one family keeps coming back. A start-up with optical or analogue hardware says its device solves in seconds a combinatorial problem no digital computer can handle; a theorist sends a toy model of a spacetime in which one observer can complete infinitely many computational steps before another observer completes a single one, and asks whether that means undecidable questions are decidable after all; someone else revives the argument that human mathematical insight outruns any formal system, since we can recognise the truth of a sentence that the system itself cannot prove; and every so often it is a quantum annealer, framed as a machine that has stepped outside the limits of ordinary computation. I have about forty minutes per pitch, no literature to hand, and no appetite for another correction notice, so what I need is not a verdict on any one of these but a reusable procedure I can run a dozen times a year and defend to my editor, who signs off on the column and will start handing these pieces to someone else if I get one badly wrong. Ground the procedure in what the Church-Turing thesis, as Turing and Church framed it, actually asserts, and in what it conspicuously does not. Before you draft anything, spend a few lines working out for yourself which distinctions have to survive the translation into a usable checklist, because I suspect most of my past mistakes came from dropping one of them: the difference between reading the thesis as a claim about the pre-formal notion of an effective procedure and reading it as an empirical hypothesis about what physical systems can do; the difference between whether a system can compute a function at all and whether it can compute it quickly; and the difference between a machine that genuinely operates over exact real values or takes infinitely many steps and one that computes a Turing-computable function in an unfamiliar encoding. Show me that working, then give me the procedure.

The procedure should be a short ordered sequence of questions, and for each one say what counts as a sufficient answer, what a negative answer settles, and whether the question is really a matter of fact or a disguised value judgment about how much idealisation we are willing to count as physical; say clearly where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a this is not the interesting question, and where it hands the piece to a human being because the remaining issue is one of physics we do not have or of editorial judgment about what the story is. Assume I know the terms only loosely, so define what you need in a clause rather than a paragraph, and keep it runnable in ten minutes with a notebook. Then run it, in full, on four pitches: an analogue device claimed to outstrip digital computers on a hard combinatorial problem; a relativistic supertask construction offered as a way to decide an undecidable question; a Gödelian argument that minds exceed machines; and a quantum annealing announcement of the kind that arrives every few months. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not enjoy, and I expect the quantum case to be that one, so be blunt about what the procedure tells me to do with a story whose framing is wrong but whose underlying result may be real. Finish by telling me which step of your own procedure is most likely to be wrong, what would have to turn out to be true for one of its verdicts to flip, and which kind of case, if it appeared, would force me to revise the procedure rather than just apply it. End the response with a single JSON object and nothing after it, with these fields: procedure_steps, an array of objects each having step, question, sufficient_answer, verdict_if_no, and judgment_call (a boolean, true if the step cannot be made mechanical); handoff_cases, an array of strings naming the situations the procedure cannot settle; worked_cases, an array of objects each having case, verdict, deciding_step, confidence (one of high, medium, low), and what_would_change_it; most_likely_failure, a string; and revision_condition, a string.
