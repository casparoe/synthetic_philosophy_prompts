I am trying to make sense of a passage in John Stuart Mill's Essay on Nature, published posthumously in *Three Essays on Religion*, and I would like help working through it. The passage is this:

"It thus appears that we must recognise at least two principal meanings in the word "nature". In one sense, it means all the powers existing in either the outer or the inner world and everything which takes place by means of those powers. In another sense, it means, not everything which happens, but only what takes place without the agency, or without the voluntary and intentional agency, of man. This distinction is far from exhausting the ambiguities of the word; but it is the key to most of those on which important consequences depend."

The surrounding argument, as I read it, is that the word "nature" is systematically ambiguous, that "follow nature" is therefore an unreliable maxim, and that the actual course of nature — with its diseases, its predators, and its destruction of the innocent — gives no support for treating nature as a moral standard. Mill catalogues the ways nature kills and maims human beings without regard to desert, and concludes that nature is not a norm.

What I cannot see clearly is the hinge. The passage itself is about a verbal ambiguity; the conclusion is about the moral authority of natural processes. Those look like two different claims, and I do not know whether Mill is entitled to move from one to the other, or whether the move is really doing the work.

I also want to know what happens to the argument when its subject is not human beings but wild animals. Some writers today argue that we have reason to intervene in nature to reduce the suffering of wild animals; others hold that interfering with natural processes is presumptuous, or that we cannot know what is good for a wild animal. Mill's denial that nature is a standard seems relevant to that dispute, but I cannot tell whether it supports intervention, undercuts it, or is simply silent.
