I'm trying to write down my commitments in epistemic game theory for a workshop, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate them. Here is where I stand. (1) Solution concepts should earn their keep epistemically: Nash equilibrium is the right prediction exactly when rationality and the players' strategies are common knowledge, while rationalizability is what rationality together with common belief of rationality implies and nothing more. (2) Common knowledge of rationality is a legitimate idealization for small, transparent games among sophisticated agents, not a fiction to be waved away. (3) I accept the result — usually credited to Aumann — that in finite games of perfect information, common knowledge of rationality forces backward induction. (4) In the finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma and in the centipede game, I think a sophisticated player should cooperate or continue; backward induction gives the wrong advice there. (5) I think the surprise-exam argument is a fallacy — an exam can genuinely surprise students who have heard and believed the announcement. (6) I hold the decision-theoretic conception of rationality: a player is rational when she maximizes expected utility given her beliefs, and her beliefs need not be correct, so rationality does not require her to know what others would do at nodes that will never be reached.

Tell me which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the least costly revision would be for each real conflict. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and say which single revision would clean up the most. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on epistemic foundations, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
