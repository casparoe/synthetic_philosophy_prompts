Ok so I'm putting together a little reading-group thing on the Great Divergence and I need a dialogue for it. Two economic historians, both sharp, arguing over one specific thing: is the question "why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain rather than in China?" a well-posed question at all?

One of them thinks it's a perfectly fine question, definately a productive one — asking it is basically what got the whole field going. The other thinks it's badly formed, maybe even a pseudo-question, and that we should either dissolve it or replace it with something better.

Neither should be a strawman. The skeptic shouldn't just be a relativist who refuses all counterfactuals, and the defender shouldn't just wave at "big questions are useful." They need to actually take each other's points seriously and respond to them, not talk past each other.

I want them to get into the weeds, so here's a specific idea I'd like one of them (probably the skeptic) to throw in: a rough rule of thumb for when a "why X and not Y?" question is legitimate. The rule: Y has to be a live alternative — you should be able to reach a world where Y happens from the actual world by some small, statable nudge, not by rewriting everything. If there's no such nudge, the question is like asking why this particular lottery ticket won; it's smuggling in a reference class after the fact. Have the other historian push back hard on that rule — e.g. that "small nudge" is doing a ton of hidden work, and the real fight is over which Y's even count as alternatives in the first place.

Let them use concrete stuff — the Yangtze delta vs. England, coal, wages, empire, whatever — but keep the disagreement about the *form* of the question, not just about which single cause was decisive. If they start listing causes at each other, pull them back.

Finish with one of them giving ground on a specific point without giving up their overall position, and then tell me which of the two you think has the better of it and why.

Length: maybe 700-1000 words. Don't bother with citations or further reading, I just want the arguement itself.
