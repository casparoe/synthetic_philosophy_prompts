I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and my book club — sci-fi mostly, but someone insisted on a philosophy pick — is grinding through the chunk of Hegel's Philosophy of Right about states and war, and I keep getting into arguments I don't have the vocabulary to finish. Best I can make of it: war isn't just an evil to be minimized, it does some kind of ethical work, because a society that never has to put itself on the line goes soft — I'm fairly sure theres a bit about wind keeping the sea from stagnating, though I'm probably mangling it. And there's no court sitting above states, so when two of them disagree there's nothing left but force. Fine. Where I get stuck is the part everyone yells about, whether he's praising war or just describing a mechanism.

Here's what I actually want to ask. I've been reading public war-game writeups for work (I do distributed scheduling, so the autonomy stuff is adjacent to what I do), and a couple of us sketched our own scenario on a weekend. Two states, a disputed island chain, and the entire fight is machines: drone boats, loitering munitions, a couple of models picking targets, a scheduler deciding who goes where. Nobody's kid is within a thousand miles of it. It runs four days, one side's fleet is scrap, the other's is mostly scrap, they sign something, done. Zero human casualties either side, no conscription, no rationing, the public watches highlight reels on their phones like it's a cup final, both economies carry on like nothing happened, and the losing government stays in power because, honestly, who's angry? One friend says this is definately good — cheap wars, no funerals. Another says a war nobody can die in isn't a war, it's an expensive boat race, and the cheapness is exactly what makes it dangerous. A third said something I can't shake: that if you took Hegel seriously, the bloodless version might be the worse one, because the whole thing was supposed to be about people being willing to give their lives for the state, and if nobody's staking anything then the state has lost the thing that kept it honest. But that seems backwards, since the standard complaint about him is that he glorifies slaughter.

So: he died in 1831, and drones, nuclear standoff, and autonomous targeting are all a long way after that. If you showed him this four-day machine-on-machine war — and also the permanent standoff where the war everyone plans for is one nobody intends to fight — what would his position actually be? Is the bloodless war war in the full sense, war in name only, or something better than war? Does it vindicate him, in that the sacrifice got superseded without anybody dying, or does it blow up the whole argument? Where do the machines fit — can a state stake its existence through things that have no existence to stake? And is there any reading where cheap and casualty-free makes it worse rather than better? I want a committed answer: pick a reading and defend it, don't lay out the options and leave me to choose. Flag which bits are solidly his and which bits are you stretching him. And please don't say we can't know what a dead man would think — I know that, I want the reconstruction anyway. Also no reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up right now; if a passage matters, tell me what it says, not where it lives.
