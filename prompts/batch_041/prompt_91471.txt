I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about the lamp puzzle for weeks and I want to see it handled in verse. The setup: a lamp is switched on, then off after half a minute, on after a quarter, off after an eighth, and so on, so that infinitely many switches occur before one minute has passed. When the minute is up, is the lamp on or off? The usual answer is that there is no answer, and I do not want a poem that merely repeats that. I want the missing answer to feel like a structural feature of the situation, not a trick.

Please act as a philosopher-poet: someone with a firm grasp of the metaphysics of infinity and supertasks, and someone who knows how to make a fixed form carry an argument. First, brainstorm several ways a poem could embody the puzzle; then choose the strongest and write it. Think carefully about how the refrains can carry the argument before you draft. The poem should be a villanelle: five tercets followed by a quatrain, with the first and third lines of the first tercet returning as refrains. Let the two refrains be the two answers the sequence seems to force on us—that the lamp must be on and that it must be off—or your own equally short formulations, and let the final quatrain bring them together so that the contradiction stands rather than resolves. The form should do the philosophical work: the refrains should keep returning because each answer keeps being reasserted by the pattern, and their final pairing should leave the reader with the unresolved limit.

To keep the poem from being a familiar restatement, add one new element: suppose the switch is operated by a machine that is itself subject to a supertask—at each step it must first decide whether to flip, and that decision takes half the remaining time. Or, if that becomes unwieldy, contrast the toggle lamp with a dimmer lamp whose brightness after each step is halfway between its previous brightness and full brightness, so that its limit is well defined. Use whichever variant sharpens the point, but do not let the poem become a technical digression. It should be accurate enough that someone who has thought hard about supertasks would recognize the problem and not wince, and clear enough that someone who has never encountered it would still feel the stakes. No citations or names of philosophers. The poem should be suitable for reading aloud at the end of a discussion group and should leave the room unsettled rather than consoled.
