i want the full spec for a theory of punishment before i commit to either side of the retributivist vs consequentialist thing. don't sell me a side — give me the desiderata: what any adequate account of why we're allowed to punish has to deliver, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the findings it has to stay consistent with, and the consideration behind each requirement. lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.

Cases i specifically want on the list, including some i don't think the standard debate has earned the right to skip:

- the offender who, between the crime and sentencing, has the disposition and the memory removed (surgery, whatever). is there anyone left to punish, and if your answer flips the verdict, which requirement was doing the work?
- a sentence served somewhere demonstrably nicer than the offender's previous housing and healthcare. is that punishment at all, or has the theory lost its grip on what punishment is?
- harm the offender already suffered by pure accident before trial — bad car crash, serious illness. does it count against the sentence? if yes, why, and if no, what's the difference between that and state-inflicted suffering?
- preventive detention of someone who hasn't done anything yet but is predicted (say, with near-certainty) to. is that punishment, and does the theory owe a separate justification for it?
- the person who committed the offense in order to be punished.
- punishing the dead, or their estate.
- the innocent scapegoat whose punishment would prevent a riot — yes it's the oldest chestnut in the drawer, put it in anyway, because everyone acts like it's settled when it isn't.
- collective punishment of a group for one member's act.
- a fine calibrated purely as a price, with no censure attached. punishment, or a fee? does the theory have to draw that line, or can it leave it fuzzy?

Distinctions i don't want collapsed: punishment vs. penalty-as-price; punishment vs. quarantine or other pure prevention; punishing vs. compensating; justifying the practice vs. justifying a particular act; may punish vs. must punish; and who has standing — victim, state, vigilante, some international body. Also the gap between desert and suffering actually delivered.

Results it can't contradict: whatever criminology actually says about whether harsher sentences deter (hedge if you're unsure), the fact that wrongful convictions happen at scale, and that offenders aren't a natural kind — corporations and states offend too.

Then: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference, flag any pair or triple you think can't be jointly satisfied (i suspect there's at least one and it's the best part), and tell me which single requirement, kept alone, knocks out the most theories. a short scorecard for retributivism, consequentialism, and one hybrid at the end is fine, but keep it short — the list is the deliverable, not the grading.
