Ok, so I've been trying to work out what I actually think about Pascal-style arguments and I keep going in circles, so I want to just hand you my gut verdicts and see if there's a principle underneath them.

Here's the thing that bugs me. When a decision has an infinite payoff on one option, standard expected-value reasoning says that option wins no matter how small the probability is, as long as it isn't zero. In the abstract I believe that. In particular cases my gut doesn't. Roughly the cases I keep chewing on:

1. The original wager setup: infinite heaven, infinite hell, finite cost of belief. Verdict: the arguement has real force for me. If I took the setup at face value I don't see how I get to shrug, and I wouldn't call someone irrational for organizing their life around it.

2. The coin game where the pot doubles every time it comes up tails and you take the pot at the first heads, so the expected value is infinite. Verdict: I'd pay maybe twenty dollars to play. Not my car. Not my house.

3. The mugging one: a stranger says that unless I hand over my wallet he'll torture some gigantic number of people, like 10^100 of them, in a simulation he's running. Verdict: I keep the wallet, and I don't feel irrational about it.

4. Same mugging, except first he tells me something he couldn't plausibly know -- my childhood pet's name, the last four digits of a card I've never mentioned to anyone. Verdict: now I feel the pull, and I might actually hand it over. Which bothers me.

5. Two rival religions, each promising infinite heaven to its own adherents and infinite hell to everybody else's. Verdict: the wager just cancels out here, it gives me zero guidance, and I don't think that's me being lazy.

6. The god who sends believers to hell and atheists to heaven. Verdict: feels like a cheap trick. I refuse to take it as seriously as #1 even though structurally it looks the same.

7. The god who gives everyone infinite bliss regardless of what they do. Verdict: nothing to decide, and notice the wager has nothing to say about this case -- it only gets its grip because there's a punishment in the background.

8. The god who gives Christians infinite bliss and Muslims infinite bliss plus one extra unit. Verdict: I'm apparently supposed to care about the plus one, since infinite-plus-one beats plain infinite, and then you can keep adding and it goes somewhere absurd. My gut says don't care. I can't say why not.

9. Finite but astronomical: ten dollars buys me a one-in-a-trillion chance of saving a trillion lives. Verdict: I do it, no hesitation, and I'd think less of someone who didn't.

10. A guy who spends his whole life maximizing his odds on an infinite payoff -- no family, no friends, health wrecked -- for a 1-in-10^100 shot at eternal bliss. Verdict: I think his life is a tragedy and probably a mistake, even though I genuinely cannot see the difference between him and #1.

My hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than just accept: what seperates the cases I accept from the ones I don't is whether the infinite payoff is attached to a stable, repeatable kind of decision, versus a one-off claim from a single unreliable source. But #2 is about as repeatable as it gets and I still won't pay much, so that hunch looks broken, and maybe the real answer is that infinite values can't be compared to each other or to finite ones at all, so any decision with an infinite option is just a broken decision problem, and my intuitions are tracking the brokenness rather than anything about the payoffs.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets as many of my ten verdicts as possible. For each candidate, say which verdicts it captures and what case motivates each clause of it, what it predicts about cases I haven't listed -- especially one where the infinite payoff is conditional on doing something I'd otherwise find monstrous, and one where the probability is genuinely unknown rather than merely small -- and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. If no single principle gets them all and something has to go, say which one should go and why that one rather than the others. And if part of what's going on is that I'm just wrong about the math rather than having a conflicting value, tell me that too.
