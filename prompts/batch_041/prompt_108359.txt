I am a novelist completing a book about a woman who, after years as a field surgeon in a war zone, joins a small community that lives by a single doctrine: that the only thing of value is the absence of craving and suffering. The community calls this tranquilism. For the novel's climax I need her to deliver an argument, not a confession, and I need it to be rigorous enough that a philosopher would not dismiss it as a mood. Please make an argument for the following position: that tranquilism is true as an axiology but false as a practical doctrine, because any attempt to pursue the absence of craving is itself a craving, and therefore the doctrine can describe a valuable state but cannot prescribe a method for reaching it. In making the argument, distinguish between first-order craving (a desire for some object or state) and second-order craving (a desire to be free of first-order craving), and show what happens to tranquilism if that distinction is collapsed. Consider the strongest objection—that the second-order desire is not a craving but a commitment—and answer it. Say what the tranquilist can still do—whether she can act, love, or choose—and what the doctrine must give up. Do not cite sources, recommend readings, or appeal to authority; I want the argument itself. Open with the position in one sentence, then defend it. Maximum 800 words.
