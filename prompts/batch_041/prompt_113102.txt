I'm about two thirds through a historical novel -- 1790s, a woman who inherits a share in a plantation and spends the whole book not dealing with it -- and I've been having the same dinner argument for a year now, so I want to actually settle what I think. It's the man-of-his-time thing, the one that shows up whenever Churchill or Columbus comes up and everyone's uncle says you can't judge them by today's standards, and someone else says there were always dissenters so nobody gets off. My brother-in-law thinks it's a closed question. My friend Priya thinks it's a closed question the other way. I keep discovering I have a rule I can't state, and I suspect it's really two rules fighting, with one of them doing all the work. I don't want sources or reading lists, I just want to know what my gut is running.

Here's what my gut does, case by case. Halvard Renn, an 18th century naturalist, buys two enslaved servants as a young man, keeps them thirty years, and his notebooks say plainly that the trade is monstrous; he frees them in his will with a pension. My gut: guilty, and the notebooks make it worse rather than better -- knowing should count against him. The abbess, 13th century, has a girl's hand struck off for stealing from the altar, and nobody in her world would have blinked. My gut: not guilty, and I feel no pull to say otherwise, which bothers me. The anatomist, 1600s, buys corpses off the hangman and dissects them -- wrong then and now -- but people did object, loudly, on grounds of desecration rather than consent. My gut: not guilty, even though dissent existed. Seth Craddock, a Victorian mill owner, employs nine year olds, lobbies *against* the Factory Acts, and his diary admits the work is degrading. My gut: guilty twice over, for knowing and for fighting the remedy. Augustine Pell, a suffragist who campaigned for women's votes and also wrote that Black men shouldn't have the vote, and whose own co-campaigners broke with him over it. My gut: guilty -- and notice this looks exactly like the anatomist, dissent existed and here it counts. Nils Bohm, a slave-port merchant, whole city built on the trade, no abolitionist argument ever reached him, no sign he ever thought about it, no alternative he could have run. My gut: I want to excuse him and I dont; guilty, which seems to contradict the abbess and the anatomist both. Casimir Roth, who in 1902 signs off on a punitive expedition that kills a few thousand people, and who in 1911 very probably stops a European war. My gut: the ledger counts, I can't just call him a murderer -- but when I run the same ledger on Craddock (his mills clothed a nation cheaply) I hate the answer, so either the ledger works for both or neither. The abolitionist in 1740: I admire him enormously, but if being of your time excuses the slaveholder, it should excuse the man who wasn't -- he was just lucky in his birth and his reading. I want the excuse available to one and not the other and I can't see how. And then the one I keep going back and forth on, which is my own protagonist: Hester Aldworth puts a sympathetic enslaved character in her 1792 novel, so she clearly *had* the concept, and she lives on the money till she dies and frees nobody. Half the time she's the worst of the lot, half the time she's just me with worse lighting.

The principle I'd tentatively write down is this: someone is guilty of a wrong only if it was visible as wrong under some description available in their own time, and only if they had a live option to do otherwise -- but if they actually saw it, or had the evidence in front of them and looked away, the defence is void. Does that get all of the above, or do I have to drop one, and if so which one and why that one rather than the abbess? I'd like two or three candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could tell them apart on a case I haven't given you, so please also hand me one new case that splits them, and say what each does with pure profit-taking -- someone who never lifts a finger, just cashes the cheques.
