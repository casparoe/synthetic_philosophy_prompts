I'm a senior in high school and my civics teacher lets me run one 45-minute class a term on whatever I want, so next week it's Sen's liberal paradox — the one where everyone getting to decide their own business and the rule that if everybody prefers one option to another the group goes with it can't both hold. There's about 20 juniors and seniors and I definately don't want to open with a lecture.

Design the session for me. My rough plan: small groups, each group invents a fair rule for a tiny club (three people, three options, something trivial like who gets the good desk or which film gets shown), then I hand out the actual preference rankings on slips of paper and they run their rule and watch it break. Fill in the details — the scenario, the exact rankings to hand out, who plays which role, a timeline that actually fits 45 minutes, and a debrief.

The bit I care about most: the rule they agreed on has to spit out a winner that everybody in the room ranked below some other option that was on the table, and it has to come out of their own counting, not out of me announcing it. So make them agree the personal-freedom part and the everyone-agrees part are both fair before the rankings show up, otherwise the clash won't land.

Then a second round: let one person give up — waive — their say over their own thing, and see whether that unbreaks it. In the debrief I want a straight answer on whether that's a real fix or just a tidy cheat, and what it costs the person who gives it up.

Two more things: what will predictably go wrong, especially the kid who'll say "just don't let people have opinions about other people's business" — how do I handle that without lecturing? And what can't the activity show them? I'm guessing it can only show that this one pair of rules blew up on this one profile, not that there's no clever fix out there — is that right, and what do I say at the end about the bit we couldn't do?
