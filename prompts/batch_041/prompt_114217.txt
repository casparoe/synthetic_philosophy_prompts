I am a graduate student in philosophy working on the aggregation of small harms, and for the last two years I have kept a running tally of the verdicts of a colleague in my reading group — call her R, a careful and unusually consistent moral philosopher with strong anti-aggregation instincts — on the choice problems we argue about. In each problem we stipulate that we must bring about one of two outcomes, that both outcomes are pure harm with no compensating benefit to anyone, and that no one's consent, identity, or prior desert is at issue; R is not a utilitarian, has no settled allegiance to any named theory, and is scrupulous about giving the same kind of reason each time. Her record so far: (i) one person tortured for fifty years, versus a googol people each receiving a single speck of dust in the eye for one second — she prevents the torture, and says no number of specks, however large, could change that; (ii) one person with a severe migraine lasting a day, versus a billion people each receiving one sharp pinprick — she prevents the migraine; (iii) one person with a mild headache lasting an hour, versus a billion people each with a faint itch on the scalp lasting a minute — she prevents the mild headache, on the ground that a billion itches that faint do not add up to a headache; (iv) one person with a mild headache lasting an hour, versus a billion people each with a mild headache lasting an hour — here she prevents the billion headaches, on the ground that these do add up. Notice that every case we have ever argued about has involved harms lasting no more than a day, and that we have never once had to trade intensity against duration.

The new case, which I want to put to her before she reads it in my draft: option A is one person enduring a moderately bad migraine for a day; option B is a billion people each experiencing a faint, barely noticeable scalp itch — the sort you would never mention and would seldom even attend to — continuously for thirty years, waking hours only. My own hunch is that she is tracking the worst single experience that anyone has to undergo, but a friend insists she is really tracking something about what people would have standing to resent or complain about, and these two stories may not be the same story.

Act as a philosopher who works on the structure of moral principles and on how thought experiments constrain them, and treat this as a problem in rational reconstruction rather than exegesis: I want no survey of the literature, no citations, no reading list, and no appeal to what any named author has said. Begin by brainstorming the several genuinely distinct rules that fit verdicts (i) through (iv) equally well — say explicitly which candidate rules the record rules out and which it leaves standing — and then, for each surviving rule, say what it predicts about the new case. Give me your best guess at R's verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reconstruction over its rivals, and tell me what the choice between the rival rules really turns on: whether the operative quantity is momentary intensity or a person's burden over a lifetime, whether duration enters the comparison at all, whether she is counting experiences or counting complaints, or something else entirely. Finally, name the single further case that would best separate the surviving rules and specify it precisely enough that I could put it to her next week, and tell me where your own reasoning is weakest and what would move you.
