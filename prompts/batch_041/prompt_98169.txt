Here is an argument I've been trying to break, assembled from the reductionism/anti-reductionism dispute about testimony, and I can't decide whether it actually refutes the reductionist or just picks an easy target. (1) Reductionism says a hearer is justified in believing a speaker only if the hearer has positive reasons, independent of the speaker's say-so, for thinking the speaker reliable on that topic. (2) Take a headline result from a large collaboration — a particle detection, say — where thousands of people each ran one piece of the analysis and no one, insider or outsider, could have independently checked the whole chain. (3) So for the headline claim, nobody holds independent reasons running through every step. (4) By (1), the headline claim is then unjustified for everyone, including the people who produced it. (5) But it is justified — that is what makes it a scientific result rather than a rumor. (6) Therefore (1) is false, and testimony must be able to transmit warrant without the hearer holding independent reasons of his own — the anti-reductionist conclusion. A friend tells me I'm cheating, because a careful reductionist only demands reasons for the particular speaker's reliability, not an independent audit of every inferential step, and on that reading my collaboration case proves nothing. I've tried to steelman both sides and I'm stuck. Who is right? If the reductionist falls back to the local reading, does the case still have any force? And if I really do have to abandon the global version of (1), what exactly have I given up — does "testimony is a basic source of warrant" amount to anything more than "trust by default until something gives you a reason not to"?
