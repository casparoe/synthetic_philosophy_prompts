I edit an anthology of new short fiction on metaphysical themes, written for readers who have some philosophy in them but who come to the book for the stories. I would like you to draft one of the pieces.

Write a complete short story of roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words, with a title. Its subject is a disagreement about what it is for a person to exist in more than one way the world might have gone. On one side is the view that each person exists in exactly one world: whoever occupies the corresponding place in another world is a numerically different individual, linked to the first only by close resemblance, and that link is not guaranteed to run in both directions and not guaranteed to carry across a chain, so that someone may resemble a second person, and that second person a third, while the first and the third are linked by nothing at all. On the other side is the view that there is literally one person who exists in many worlds and merely has different properties in each, so that what happens elsewhere is something that happened to them.

Constraints for the piece:

1. No named philosophers, no schools, no citations, no epigraphs, and no narrator or character who stops to explain the theory. A reader who has never encountered either view should be able to reconstruct both from the events alone.

2. Do not use the technical vocabulary of the debate. The words possible world, modal, counterpart, and transworld identity should not appear in their philosophical senses. If the characters need a name for the phenomenon, invent one that belongs to the setting.

3. The mechanism by which other-worldly selves become practically relevant must be mundane and institutional rather than a laboratory: a probate court, an insurance office, a marriage registry, a religious tribunal, a debt collector, a school. Choose one, make it old and taken for granted, and let it do the work. No scene of first discovery, no inventor, no exposition.

4. The difference between the two views must be made to matter legally or morally, whether to a promise, a debt, a marriage, a crime, or a parent's claim on a child. Include at least one scene in which a character is told, in the story's own vocabulary, that a certain act was not theirs but the act of someone who closely resembles them, and must decide whether to treat that as an acquittal or as an evasion.

5. The failure of the relation to carry across a chain must be load-bearing rather than decorative. The plot should turn at some point on a sequence in which A is linked to B and B to C but A is not linked to C, and on what that does to a claim that has been travelling down the sequence.

6. The ending must be legible both ways. It should not disclose which view is correct, and it should not resolve the ambiguity with a revelation. The same final scene ought to read as a consolation under one view and as a loss under the other.

Give me the story itself, not an outline, and not a note on your intentions.
