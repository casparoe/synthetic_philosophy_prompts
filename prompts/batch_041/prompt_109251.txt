Ok, this is a wierd one and I'm probably overthinking it, but I have to have something written for Thursday so here goes.

I'm an oncology nurse and I've sat on our hospital's ethics committee for four years. Two things collided this spring.

One is mine. I got a kidney stone in March and in the ED they gave me something — I want to say ketamine, the night's a blur. The pain didn't go away. Same spot, same intensity, I could've drawn you a picture of it. But it stopped being a problem. It was still hurting and I genuinely did not care — not "too high to care," I mean I could look right at it and it had no weight at all. Strangest experience of my life, and it made me think I'd been wrong my whole career about something basic: I always assumed the whole point of pain is that it's bad, and that's why we treat it.

The other: a patient on 4 West, 60s, bad arthritis in both knees for years. Rates it 8/10 every time. Refuses everything — no opioids, no blocks. Not depressed, not confused, sharp as a tack. He keeps saying some version of "it hurts, I just don't care." He grimaces getting up, guards the knee. The neurologist says that in him the sensation and the awfulness have come apart, that it happens with certain brain injuries, and there's a word for it, asymbolia or something. Our policy is the fifth-vital-sign one: reassess, and a 4 or above triggers a treatment plan unless the patient refuses, and refusals get escalated and charted. So the nurses are stuck — by the book this man is in uncontrolled pain and we are failing him, and the man himself says he's fine. One nurse is adamant you cannot leave an 8/10 untreated, and that his not caring is part of the problem rather than a reason to do nothing. The resident wants to treat him "for his own good" because he thinks the indifference is itself a symptom.

Write me the opinion to circulate. I need a recommendation we can actually act on, with a time frame, and I need it to take that nurse seriously — there's a version of her position that scares me. But push on the real question: is the badness of pain built into the sensation, or is it something the pain does to you, the grip it has on you? Because if it's the second, this man is telling the truth about his own life and our whole scoring system is measuring the wrong number. And say what to do about a competent adult's refusal when the thing that supposedly makes pain bad seems to be missing — does the refusal carry less weight, or more? Also tell me what to do about the policy itself, not just this one patient. I don't want to be back here in six months.
