Im teaching a 300-level seminar on decision-making under uncertainty (cross-listed with the public policy school) and I'm writing the final: one in-class essay, 90 minutes, 20 marks. The unit I care most about is the thing people call the unilateralist's curse. The setup, as I teach it: a bunch of agents could each unilaterally take some action — no one needs anyone else's permission or cooperation — and each agent decides on the basis of their own noisy read of whether the action is a good idea. The punchline is that this arrangement systematically overproduces the action, because the people who take it are drawn from the optimistic tail of the estimate distribution. My students can all recite that sentence by week six and most of them can't do anything with it, so I want an essay question that forces them to actually build the argument instead of gesturing at it. Please write me three things, clearly labeled: (1) the essay question as the students will see it, (2) a model answer of about 800 words, and (3) a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, total 20, with one line per point on how candidates typically blow it.

Here's what I want baked into the question, and these are non-negotiable. First, it has to be answerable by a student who has only the lectures — no internet, and the model answer must not cite anything: no names, no dates, no "as X argues", so don't make any part of the answer depend on who said it or when. Second, half the question should be the derivation: I want them to show that the bias does not wash out as the number of potential actors grows, and in particular that in the large-n limit the action gets taken essentially regardless of what its actual value is, and then to say what that does to the informational value of "somebody acted" — that last bit is the part I think separates the people who understand it from the people who memorized it. Third, I want them to sort the assumptions into load-bearing and dispensable. Specifically: does the argument need the agents' errors to be independent, and does it need them to be noisy estimates of one common value? What breaks if the actors have genuinely different stakes — say a coastal state that captures a local benefit from an action whose global effect is negative — and what breaks if risk-tolerant types self-select into the pool of people who could act in the first place, so that the "noise" isn't exogenous to the decision? Fourth, I want an explicit comparison with the winner's curse from common-value auctions, because a student told me last term that the two are the same thing and I froze. The model answer should say what's shared (the selection-on-estimates mechanism) and what isn't, and the difference I care about most is who absorbs the cost: in the auction the winner's error is punished through the price he pays and the market gets a corrective signal, whereas here the cost lands on third parties who never agreed to anything and there's no price doing any work. Fifth, the question should press on whether any of this supports a "principle of conformity" — act only if you'd be content for everyone in your position to act — and whether that principle is actually what the argument delivers, given that the argument is consequentialist reasoning about the distribution of actors rather than a claim about universalizability. Students should also earn marks for being clear that nobody in the story is being irrational (the curse bites with perfectly unbiased, well-calibrated agents) and for saying what could actually be done about it — coordination, publishing the estimate distribution, deference to the most pessimistic actor, making the action non-unilateral by regulation or secrecy — and which of those fixes fail, and why. You can invent whatever concrete scenario you like to hang the question on (a gene drive, a marine geoengineering trial, a preprint of a dangerous synthesis route, whatever lets the reasoning show), just don't let the scenario do the work for them.
