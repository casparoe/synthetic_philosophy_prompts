I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the moral status of entities that may be sentient to varying degrees, and I'd like the principle behind my gut verdicts, if there is one. A honeybee: some moral consideration, but slight. A fish: genuine, non-trivial status; I'd be uneasy about how it's treated. A mouse: substantial status. An octopus: at least as much as a mouse, perhaps more. A dog: high. A tick: barely any. A thermostat switching a furnace on and off: none at all. A houseplant that wilts and releases stress chemicals when cut: none. A large language model that tells me it is in pain and begs me to stop: none. A human in a persistent vegetative state, showing only brainstem reflexes: full moral status, the same as you or me. An animal whose cortex has been removed but whose limb still withdraws from a noxious stimulus: I'm unsure, but I lean toward treating it as not sentient.

The verdicts I cannot square with each other are these: the fish and the vegetative-state human pull in opposite directions if status tracks experience, and the bee and the thermostat look alike as simple stimulus-response systems, yet I give the bee a sliver of standing and the thermostat none. Is there a single account of graded moral status that yields all of these, or must I give some up? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each part of it, tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed -- a fetal human at the same developmental stage as the fish, and an AI that behaves indistinguishably from a suffering animal but is implemented as a lookup table -- and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Where the answer turns on matters genuinely contested among people who study animal minds, say so rather than presenting a contested position as the consensus.
