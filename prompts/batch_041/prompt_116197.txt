For the past year I have been running a small reading group on statutory interpretation, jointly with a colleague from the philosophy department, and next term the faculty has given us enough money to turn it into a two-day workshop with about eight invited papers. The theme I have settled on is the dispute between textualists and purposivists, but I want the workshop to be built around the live conceptual questions underneath that dispute rather than around the well-rehearsed cases that everyone already has views about. Which open questions in this area should I put on the programme, and in what order? I care far more about the defence of the ranking than about the list itself: for each question, I want to know why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people whose training is in the philosophy of language and in social choice rather than in doctrinal legal scholarship, what the trade-offs are between importance and tractability in your ordering, and what would have to happen over the next two or three years for a question to move up or down. I would also like to be told which currently fashionable questions I should leave off the programme altogether, and what the workshop would lose by doing so.

Two candidate threads have been occupying us, partly because they make the discussion less familiar than it otherwise would be. The first is whether the ordinary-meaning standard can be given empirical content by corpus methods, and what it would mean for a court's reading of a statute to be shown to be statistically atypical for otherwise competent speakers of the language; it is not obvious to me whether this sharpens the textualist case or simply relocates the dispute into questions about which corpus and which speakers count. The second is whether purposivism inherits an aggregation problem, on the thought that a multi-member legislature need not have anything resembling a single preference ordering, so that a statute's purpose may be indeterminate in much the way a group's preference is, and it is unclear whether that tells against purposivism or merely shows that both theories owe us an account of which intentions or purposes are the ones that count. Feel free to tell me that one or both of these are red herrings, or that they are really the same question wearing two hats, and to substitute better candidates; but if you drop them, say what they were missing, since I would rather find that out now than in the second session.
