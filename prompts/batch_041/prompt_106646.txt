Suppose Aristotle were confronted with the modern problem of infinite ethics. Three claims are on the table: that if the future lasts forever and contains infinitely many lives worth living, its total value is infinite, so that any finite improvement to it is negligible; that a vanishingly small probability of an infinite good rationally swamps every finite consideration — the fanaticism worry; and that no way of ranking infinite streams of wellbeing can respect both Pareto and impartiality at once. Drawing on his account of the infinite as potential rather than actual, never a completed whole, and on his conception of the human good as a bounded, complete activity rather than an unbounded accumulation, say what he would make of each claim: which he would treat as a genuine problem, which as an artifact of assumptions he rejects, and where his own framework would force him to a conclusion he might not welcome. I want concrete verdicts on each, not a survey of his remarks on infinity.
