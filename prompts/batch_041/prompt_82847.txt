I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and the habit has left me with a fairly specific set of wants: I care about whether a view can be broken rather than about who held it, I try to state my positions in terms of what would change my mind, and I would rather be handed the shape of an argument than a reading list. So please argue with me rather than pointing me at literature. I am not looking for sources, and names will not impress me.

The position I want you to attack is one I have been defending about divine foreknowledge, which I will call the no-script reading of middle knowledge. The background, compressed and in my own words rather than in anyone's technical vocabulary: the classical Molinist picture assigns God three bodies of knowledge. First, knowledge of what is possible — which situations are compossible, what any possible creature could do. Third, knowledge of what God himself has decided to create and do. Sandwiched between them, middle knowledge: God's knowledge of what each possible creature would freely choose in each situation it might be placed in. The entire point of the middle sandwich is that it comes before God's creative decision, so that God can survey the counterfactual landscape, pick the world whose free choices he likes, and thereby guarantee the outcome of history rather than merely forecast it.

My reading is that the middle moment is not a third kind of knowledge and does not need to be. The standard objections — that nothing makes a counterfactual of freedom true before anyone has chosen anything, and that a choice known with certainty in advance is not free — both look to me like artefacts of picturing middle knowledge as a script that exists prior to God's decision. What God has, on my reading, is a perfect model of every possible agent: a function from situations to what that agent would choose, known with certainty even where the choice is genuinely undetermined. He is not reading a transcript; he is the perfect predictor. Providence is then not the selection of a pre-written story but the craft of arranging situations so that the agents he has modelled do what he wants without being made to. If this is right, the metaphysics of counterfactuals of freedom can be dropped and the practical content of Molinism kept.

The analogy I keep returning to is Newcomb's problem, which strikes me as the cleanest available test of how an agent should reason in the presence of a perfect predictor. My sense is that the Molinist God is doing what the predictor does, and that the quarrel between evidential and causal decision theory is the same quarrel in different dress — though I hold that loosely, and I would like to know whether it is a real isomorphism or only a suggestive rhyme.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure: what would have to be true, either in the world or in the logic of the concepts, for the no-script reading to be false, and which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or possible cases would count against it. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and separate honestly what would genuinely refute the view from what would only make it uncomfortable. I am especially interested in three things. First, the collapse worry: if a perfect predictor's certainty about an undetermined choice must be either lucky or grounded in something about the agent, does the script come back in through the back door, and if so does my view shrink to the claim that God is a very good Bayesian about free agents? Second, whether my view leaves God able to guarantee anything at all: on the no-script reading, does prophecy become a risky forecast, and is that a consequence I should accept or a reductio? Third, what the view predicts that its rivals do not — what should look different about the world, about divine action, or about the deliberations of agents who know they are being predicted, if the no-script reading is true rather than Open Theism or straightforward determinism?

I also want the defender's answer, not only the critic's: which of these defeaters would a committed Molinist concede as counting against me, and which would they dismiss as begging the question? And tell me plainly whether my formulation has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether there is any evidence or argument that could move me, or whether I have arranged things so that every apparent counterexample gets reabsorbed as a confusion about scripts.

Finally, since I would rather know what to do with my time than what to believe: the thing that would actually move me is a concrete case in which the no-script reading yields a determinate prediction about how a rational agent, divine or otherwise, should act, and that prediction turns out to be obviously wrong. If no such case exists, say so, and tell me what that says about the view.
