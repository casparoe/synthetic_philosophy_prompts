I've been trying to work out how much real work Adam Smiths impartial spectator is doing and how much of it is just a nice picture. So here's the comparison I want you to take seriously. Smith's spectator is supposed to be impartial, informed, and disinterested: you judge yourself by imagining how a bystander with no stake in your case would feel about it. Set against that a rival standpoint I'll call the invested judge -- fully informed and sincerely self-critical, but it keeps its attachments, and it only asks that you be able to defend those attachments without flinching when someone who doesn't share them pushes back. No pretense of a view from nowhere.

Which of the two actually explains how moral judgment works, and which is more defensible as a standard? Specifically: does impartiality definately earn its keep, or is the real work being done by sincerity plus consistency? And does Smith's picture have a problem the invested judge dodges -- since he also thinks we mostly want our feelings echoed back by the people around us, the spectator you internalize might just be local consensus in a nicer outfit, and he himself notices we're suckers for the rich and powerful. Does that make the impartial spectator a device for getting moral questions right, or just for getting everyone to agree?

Test both on one hard case (you'd have to let a stranger die to save your brother, say), then tell me which one you'd keep and where I've stacked the deck.
