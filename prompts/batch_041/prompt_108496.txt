A colleague with whom I have co-taught a seminar on the morality of harming for several years holds a set of verdicts that have never varied under questioning, and I would like to reconstruct the principle behind them before a student forces the issue next week. He is often described as a double-effect theorist, but I have never been sure that the label captures what he is actually doing.

His verdicts on the following cases are settled.

One. A runaway trolley is bearing down on five track workers. The operator can throw a switch that diverts it onto a siding, where it will kill a single worker standing there. He throws the switch. Verdict: permissible.

Two. The only way to stop the trolley short of the five is to push a large passerby from a footbridge into its path, where his body will arrest it. Verdict: impermissible.

Three. A transplant surgeon can save five patients who would otherwise die by killing one healthy patient and distributing his organs. Verdict: impermissible.

Four. A rescuer can save five people trapped in a building by opening a fire door; opening it will release a backdraft that kills one person in the adjoining room. She does not want that death and does not need it, since the door and not the death does the saving, but she foresees it clearly. Verdict: permissible.

Five. A commander can destroy an enemy supply bridge, which is necessary to relieve a surrounded battalion; the strike will certainly kill several civilians who live beside the bridge, though their deaths are not a means to destroying it and he would spare them if he could. Verdict: permissible.

Next week a student will present the following case, and I must predict my colleague before he speaks.

Six. The trolley is again bearing down on five workers. The only switch diverts it onto a track that curves back and rejoins the main line just short of the five. On the curve stands one worker. If the trolley strikes him, his body will slow it enough that it stops before reaching the five; if it does not strike him, nothing will stop it. The operator throws the switch, foreseeing that the worker will be killed. She wants only to stop the trolley, would save the worker if she could, and cannot stop the trolley without striking him.

Tell me what he will say, and, more importantly, why. Set out the distinct rules that are jointly consistent with his five earlier verdicts, state what each of them predicts for the sixth case, say which rule he is most likely running and on what grounds, and name the further case that would best separate the rules that survive. Do not simply pick a verdict: I care as much about the rival rules his record permits as about the one he is actually using, and about what each of them would cost him to give up.
