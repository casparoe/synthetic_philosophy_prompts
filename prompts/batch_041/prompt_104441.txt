I am a retired field ecologist. For some thirty years I worked on insect population dynamics, mostly the selection of pesticide resistance, and since retiring I have been sitting in on philosophy seminars, which has left me with more questions than I can settle alone. Next month I have been asked to run a single seventy-five-minute session for first-year psychiatry residents, with a whiteboard and no assigned reading, on Ian Hacking's notions of looping effects and interactive kinds. I do not want the idea explained to me; I want a plan for how to teach it. Please take the role of a philosopher of psychiatry who knows both Hacking's work and the clinical classification debates well enough to know how this idea is usually mangled. Think the matter through before you write, and begin by working out what the audience must already accept before looping effects can look like a phenomenon rather than a paradox; then give me the plan itself.

Be concrete about the following.

1. Order. What has to be introduced first, and what depends on what. I want the dependency structure, not a list of topics.

2. The carrying example. I have three candidates and I distrust all of them: dissociative identity disorder; prolonged grief disorder, which entered the DSM's text revision in 2022 and also appears in the ICD-11; and orthosomnia, a term clinicians coined a few years ago for people who grow anxious about the numbers their sleep trackers report, which is not a formal diagnosis at all. Choose the one that should carry the session, justify it, and say why the others are wrong for the job. If none of them will do, say so and name what would.

3. My own foothold, and where it gives way. I keep reaching for the loops I know from my old field, pesticide and herbicide resistance, antibiotic resistance, artificial selection, because they look like classifications feeding back on the kind. The plan should use this, but it must also locate precisely where the analogy breaks. My suspicion is that the difference lies in whether the kind can take up its own description. Place the analogy at the point in the sequence where it does the most work, and tell me how to show the residents where it fails.

4. Misconceptions, in the order they will arise, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses: that a looping effect means the disorder is not real or is a passing fad; that it is merely labeling theory or a self-fulfilling prophecy; that the loop runs only one way, from label to person; that interactive kinds are therefore purely social and have no biology; and that a kind which loops cannot be a valid diagnostic category. Add what I have missed, and tell me which of these is the most dangerous.

5. The check. I want to close by handing them a case they have not seen, something like the sleep-tracker term, and asking a question whose answer tells me whether they have the loop rather than the vocabulary. Design that question, and tell me what a good answer contains and what a plausible-sounding bad one contains.

6. What to leave out at this level, and how to say so out loud.

Keep the whole plan under about nine hundred words. No reading list and no citations: the residents will have read nothing.
