I'm putting together the final for my third-year "Evolution and Behaviour" course and I want one long essay question on the units of selection. The problem is that every version I've tried gets answered with the same two moves: "kin selection and group selection are mathematically equivalent," plus a recap of the Dawkins–Gould spat. I want something that actually makes them think about whether the equivalence claim is doing any work.

Here's the setup I want to use. Give them a species divided into local demes with limited migration, where a gene G makes its bearer help defend the deme against raiders — costly to the bearer inside the deme, good for the deme as a whole in between-deme competition. Then the actual question:

(a) Give the gene's-eye / Hamiltonian account of when G spreads, and the multilevel / Price-style account, and say precisely what "the two frameworks are equivalent" is supposed to mean.

(b) Now the bit I care about — I want them to say whether the equivalence is a claim about the same causal process or just the same arithmetic, and whether there's any experiment or observation that could tell the two frameworks apart. I'm thinking of something like: we crank up the migration rate, or we change how correlated group membership is with relatedness — does either framework predict something the other can't?

(c) And I want a section where they grapple with the fact that you can partition the same fitness data in more than one way — e.g. regressing offspring on the individual's phenotype with the group mean held fixed, versus on the group mean itself — and that the choice can change whether a "group selection" term even shows up. Does that make group selection partly a modelling choice rather than a fact of nature, and is that a problem for the whole debate or just for sloppy bookkeeping?

The exam gives them 75 minutes for this one, so aim the model answer at roughly 700–800 words. Please write the question, then the model answer, then a marking scheme out of 25 that separates a first from a 2:1 by naming the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — I especially want a line on how candidates typically get each one wrong.

One thing though: in the model answer, be careful to seperate what's actually settled from what's still genuinely fought over among people who work on this. I dont want the model answer parroting "the frameworks are equivalent, full stop" as if that ended the debate — I know people who'd say the equivalence is trivial and people who'd say it's substantive, and I want the answer to show it knows which parts are contested.
