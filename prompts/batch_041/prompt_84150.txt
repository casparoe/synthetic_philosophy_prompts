So I've been asked to give the keynote at a naturalization ceremony next month — sixty people taking the oath in a federal courthouse, their families packed into the back rows, two judges up on the bench, and a reporter from the local paper who is defintely hunting for one nice quotable line. I naturalized eleven years ago myself; they asked me because I own a shop downtown and apparently that counts as being a community leader, so I get about eight minutes at the podium.

The trouble is the standard version of this speech is boilerplate — the whole welcome, you now share in the rights and responsibilites of citizenship, go register to vote, congratulations thing. I can't give that speech, because I don't believe what it says about voting. What I actually think:

Voting isn't a prize you get handed for passing the test. It's a duty that comes from being bound by the law and having to live with what the law does. That's why I'm for compulsory voting — small fine, easy opt-out, and a none-of-the-above box so nobody's forced to endorse a candidate they despise. The argument isn't about turnout; it's that if a state is going to rule you, you ought to have a say in how, and blowing it off is like blowing off jury duty.

And if that's what grounds the vote, then the line this ceremony is drawing runs in the wrong place. My aunt has been here twenty-two years, pays taxes, employs four people, never naturalized — I think she should be voting. Same for the teenager in the third row who turns sixteen in the spring. I'd draw it at permanent residents who've been here five years: not tourists, not a guy on a two-year contract. People tell me to settle for letting non-citizens vote in school board and city elections and I think that's a cop-out — the same person who can be deported by federal law is bound by all of it. Where I get off the bus is the other, more generous principle, the one that says anybody whose interests are affected by a decision should get a say in it. Sounds lovely, and it hands votes to foreigners and to people who aren't born yet. Being bound is the narrower line and the better one. And no, I don't buy the version where parents cast an extra ballot for each minor child — that isn't giving the kid a vote, it's giving the parent two.

So here's my bind. I'm supposed to stand up and congratulate a room on crossing a threshold I think shouldn't be there, and a good chunk of the people in the back — the spouses, the parents, the older brother who's been here longer than the person taking the oath — are people I think are being denied something they're owed. I can't pretend I don't think that. I also can't hijack somebody's citizenship ceremony to argue about my hobbyhorse, and I can't say anything from that podium I'd have to walk back later.

What I want from you is the actual speech. One speech, the one you'd deliver — not three options, not a menu of tones. You make the calls: how hard to push the non-citizen part, and how to say something honest to the people in the back without making the new citizens feel like they're getting a scolding on their big day. Around 1,000-1,200 words. Keep the register warm — these are real people, some of them come from places where the ballot was a formality or a threat, and I don't want to romanticize voting at them. One of the judges up there thinks compulsory voting is a straight violation of liberty, so don't strawman that; it has to be a speech that survives her listening to it.

Then, after the speech, a short note, 200 words or so: where you softened or took liberties with what I actually believe, which parts of my position the speech is quietly resting on, and which bit of it is most likely to blow up if somebody in that room disagrees.
