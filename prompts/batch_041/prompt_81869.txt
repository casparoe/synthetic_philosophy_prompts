Me and a couple of friends have been running an informal reading group on the philosophy of consciousness, and for the last month we've been stuck on illusionism — the view that phenomenal consciousness, the thing we mean when we say there's "something it's like" to see red or taste coffee, over and above the brain just taking in and using information, doesn't really exist; what exists is the brain's habit of representing itself as having experiences like that, and that self-representation is where the illusion comes from. The part I can't stop chewing on is the reframing that goes with it: instead of asking why information processing supposedly gives rise to experience, you ask why we're so utterly convinced that it does, and you treat that conviction as just another thing for cognitive science to explain, no more mysterious than any other false belief. Im mostly convinced, but every time I try to say what actually follows from it, people either shrug it off as a harmless way of talking (fine, stop calling it "phenomenal" and move on) or treat it as an apocalypse that does away with everything worth caring about, and neither reaction feels earned.

So I'd like you to lay out for me the most important implications of taking illusionism seriously — and I mean the ones that actually bite, not just the tidy consequences for how philosophers word things. I care about the spots where it rubs up against real work: the whole research program of hunting for the neural correlates of consciousness when the thing you thought you were finding correlates of may not be there; how you'd do ethics and diagnosis for patients in disorders of consciousness if the ordinary notion of "they're still in there" gets undermined; what it does to the moral status we extend to animals and to the new push to worry about AI welfare; whether practices that are all about getting to know your own experience from the inside — meditation, certain kinds of therapy — are tracking anything real or just rehearsing the illusion. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, most important first, and skip any intro or wrap-up; I just want the five.
