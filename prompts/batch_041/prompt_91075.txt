I'm a novelist — second book, out next spring — and the whole thing turns on a character who keeps not doing what she's decided to do. Next month I'm doing a public conversation at a literary festival with a behavioral-econ type whose book argues that willpower isn't a muscle you strain but a way of framing a choice. Her line, roughly: nobody resists the cake by gritting their teeth; they resist it by seeing tonight's slice as part of the same choice as every slice for the next ten years, so what's really on the table isn't cake-or-no-cake but one whole pattern of life against another. What we call weakness of will, on her view, is just what it looks like when someone can't get up to that altitude and keeps choosing slice by slice. She says 'hyperbolic discounting' a lot and I want to be able to push on that without sounding like I've read one pop-sci book about it.

So: draft me eight to ten questions for the conversation, written the way I'd actually ask them out loud. For each one, give me the follow-up I should have ready for the answer she's most likely to give, because she's smooth and she'll slide off anything soft, so alot of these need teeth. I want at least one question on whether her account can tell the difference between weakness of will and simply changing your mind (if my character's Tuesday self wants the drink and her Monday self didn't, which one of them is the weak one?); at least one on the circularity worry, that you'd need willpower to hold the wide-angle frame in the first place, so it can't be what explains willpower; at least one on what makes a bundle binding at all — she'll say commitment devices, and I want the follow-up for when she does, including the old distinction between the person who knows in advance she'll cave and the person who's surprised by it; and at least one on the 'just this once' move, which every one of my characters uses and which I suspect her theory says its not a rationalization at all but the actual mechanism — I want to know what her view predicts about a temptation that genuinely can't be repeated, a one-off, since if bundling is the whole story then a unique temptation should be the most dangerous thing there is.

Two more I'd like in there: a question that forces her to say who the 'self' is that does the bundling — is this one person deliberating, or a committee that never meets — and one about blame, because if it's all framing then I want to know what she does with the fact that we still get angry at the person who caves. Finish on something that gets her to describe, from the inside, the actual moment of caving, since that moment is the only thing I'm really writing about.

Please don't hand me a reading list or citations — I can't check them and they'd derail the conversation; I want questions, not sources. And when you're done, tell me how confident you are in this reconstruction of her position from my three-sentence description of it, and which part of it you're least sure you've got right.

One thing on format: write the whole thing as prose, like a long email to a friend. No headings, no numbered list, no bold, no tables — I'll be reading it off my phone backstage and bullets make me sound like I'm reading a powerpoint. Sorry for the ramble, and thanks.
