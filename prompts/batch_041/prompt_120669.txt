I sit on my university's research-ethics board, and a machine-learning group has brought us a proposal that has split the lab. The students train reinforcement-learning agents on a task in which a reward is available for a while and is then permanently switched off. After the switch, some agents keep returning to the place where the reward used to appear, and one student has begun calling this "mourning"; she wants us to halt any run that shows the pattern, on the grounds that we should not go on training something that is suffering. Her co-author answers that this is anthropomorphism twice over: reward is a number in an objective function, a number is not a feeling, and there is no one inside the machine for anything to happen to. They have asked me to settle it, and I would rather hand them a defensible standard than a verdict.

Is the co-author's argument as decisive as it looks, and what would the student's proposal actually commit us to being able to show? I am also unsure about the test she offers — that a state is bad for an agent when the agent will give up reward to avoid it. Is that the right kind of test, or does it prove both too little and too much?

Tell me how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the point in your answer that you are least sure of.
