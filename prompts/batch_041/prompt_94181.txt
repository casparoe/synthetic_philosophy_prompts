Take on the role of a philosopher who works specifically on consent — someone who's spent years on the metaphysics and ethics of it and who's used to refereeing between camps. Here's the setup. Two camps have been argueing past each other for decades about what makes consent valid. Call them the inside camp and the outside camp. The inside camp says consent is fundamentally a mental state — an attitude of the consenter, a willingness, an inward yes — and everything else (words, nods, signatures) is just evidence of that state. On this view you can consent in your head without ever getting it out, and going through the motions without the attitude isn't consent at all. The outside camp says consent is fundamentally a public act — the communication of a permission to someone, in a form they can act on and rely on. On this view consent isn't something you have, it's something you do, and what matters is what actually got put out into the world.

I want the joint statement these two camps would sign if they locked themselves in a room for a weekend in good faith. So:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — everything both camps can sign without flinching. What consent is *for* (what work the concept does, what the difference between valid and invalid consent amounts to, what the wrong of acting without it consists in), which cases both treat as clear, and what both agree any theory of consent has to explain. Push this as far as it will actually go — don't stop at "consent matters". I want agreement about things that constrain a theory, including about deception, coercion and competence.

2. Then a disagreements table. One row per genuine disagreement, with columns for: (a) the question at issue, stated neutrally, so neither side would object to the framing; (b) the inside camp's answer, in wording someone in that camp would accept as their own; (c) the outside camp's answer, same standard; (d) what turns on it. Keep the two answers parallel and don't smuggle either side's assumptions into the neutral statement of the question.

3. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one camp to change its view. For each: the case, experiment, or argument both camps would agree bears on it (they needn't agree what it shows, only that it's relevant and not rigged), and each camp's prediction, written down in advance. If a crux can't be tested, say what kind of argument would move them instead and why.

I want these specific things covered in the cruxes, since this is where the fighting has been:

- The divergence cases. First: someone has the full inside attitude but never gets it out — they decide yes and then freeze, and the other person goes ahead anyway. Second, the mirror image: someone performs a flawless public act of consent with no matching attitude behind it (they're going along to get it over with, or reading from a script, or meant to say no and didn't). What does each camp say about whether the act was consented to, and about whether the other person did wrong? Then the one I think is the real fight: when inside and outside come apart, who carries the risk — the person whose two halves don't match, or the person who reasonably relied on what they were told? Make that a crux.

- Deception. Both camps presumably agree that some lies kill consent and some don't. Can they agree on a criterion for the line, or does the criterion itself split them? Test whatever they come up with against: (i) a lie about a feature that would have been a deal-breaker but that the person never finds out about, (ii) a lie about a feature they'd be upset about but would have gone ahead anyway, and (iii) a literally true statement made deliberately to mislead. Does it matter whether the lie concerns the act itself or something around it (the old fraud-in-the-factum versus fraud-in-the-inducement line)? Does it matter whether the deceiver is the one who benefits?

- Coercion, same structure. Is a proposal coercive because of how it compares to some baseline, or because of what the proposer is doing to the person? What is the baseline — what they'd otherwise have got, what they're entitled to, or what's normal? Put a case where the person making the offer is also the one who created the problem, and a case where a bystander makes the identical offer. Add a case where the offer is generous but the alternative is death. Do the camps actually differ here, or is this a place where they converge and the real disagreement is somewhere else?

- Competence and time. Is competence about process (does the person understand and reason) or about content (are the person's values acceptable)? Which camp has the worse time with the competent-but-weird case? And can consent be given in advance, or for a stretch when you're not conscious, and is it revocable at any moment? If someone withdraws consent afterwards — or would have withdrawn had anyone asked — does that retroactively make it nonconsensual? I suspect non-occurrent consent is where the two camps really diverge, so make it a crux.

4. Finally, a short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal.
