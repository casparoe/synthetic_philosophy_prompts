I'm partway through a novel in which a woman runs what she calls a standing tribunal: people come to her not with their grievances but with their right to air them, and she rules only on whether a would-be blamer has the standing to blame at all, never on whether the person blamed actually did anything wrong. She's a hypocrite herself, which is the joke. Trouble is, halfway through the draft I realised I don't know what I think the right answer is, so write me an essay settling it. When you blame someone for a wrong you're busily committing yourself, do you forfeit the standing to blame, or is your blame merely less apt or less forceful — is it losing a right to speak, or only a loss in the weight your speech carries? And is standing lost wholesale or piecemeal — only before certain audiences, only when the fault is the very same one, only once someone has actually called you out on it? In the novel the tribunal's rulings are tradeable, so tell me too whether standing is even the sort of thing that can be handed on to someone else, or whether second-hand blame is a category error. If you reckon the whole notion of standing is a lawyers' import doing no real work and that we should just talk about how much blame is warranted, argue that instead. Take a position and defend it — no fence-sitting, though a hedged verdict like "it depends whether the blamer is sincere" is fine so long as you say what it depends on. Also seperate for me what's genuinely settled in this area from what's contested, so I don't have a character confidently asserting something that half the people who know the debate would reject. British English please.
