I am a high school junior, and I would like help thinking through a situation that has quietly taken over my life and the lives of my friends, because I suspect that all the advice we give one another is missing the real problem.

Here is the situation. Six of us are applying to the same small set of selective summer programs and, eventually, to the same tier of universities. Over the past year, each of us has given up something we loved — a sport, an instrument, hours of sleep — and added activities we do not enjoy, not because we wanted them, but because we found out that someone else was doing them and felt we could not afford to fall behind. In absolute terms, none of us is better off. Our applications look much as they did relative to one another, we are all exhausted, and we all enjoy our lives less. We have even had several honest conversations in which every one of us agreed the whole thing is pointless and that we should scale back.

What confuses me is why we cannot stop. My friend Maya says this is simply a failure of cooperation, and that the solution is a binding agreement: we should all promise to cut back and appoint someone to hold us to it. But I have noticed that even those who sincerely want to stop keep going, and I think I see why. What we are competing for is not a fixed quantity of achievement but a ranking, and the programs admit a fixed number of students. So even if all six of us stopped, someone outside our group would take the spots, and whoever among us stopped would end up relatively worse off. A second friend, Dev, says that this makes it a plain conflict of interest, and that the only sensible response is to compete as hard as we possibly can. But Dev's answer feels wrong too, because the harder all of us push, the less any of it seems to matter.

I would like to know whether Maya is right that this is a problem of cooperation that a strong enough agreement could fix, or whether there is something in the structure of the situation that makes agreement insufficient no matter how sincere we are — and, if the latter, exactly what that something is. I would also like to know what follows for me as an individual. If opting out means being worse off relative to everyone else, is it irrational to be the one who quits? And is there any honest advice for a person caught in a situation where each individually sensible move makes the collective outcome worse, and where the two obvious fixes, cooperate or compete harder, both seem to fail?
