I am a philosophy graduate student working on a paper at the intersection of population ethics and the axiology of pain. I have drafted an essay defending a view I call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in normative ethics, decision theory, and the measurement of welfare, and give me detailed comments. Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I cannot check them, and I want conceptual feedback instead. Begin by brainstorming, in a short unnumbered list, the three or four most important questions you think my essay needs to answer. Then restate my central thesis in your own words so I can see whether it is clear. Then give me comments under four headings: first, accuracy and internal consistency; second, whether the view is genuinely distinct from more familiar positions such as prioritarianism, sufficiency views, and standard negative utilitarianism; third, the strongest objections and whether I have resources to answer them; and fourth, concrete suggestions for revision.

My essay has three parts. In the first, I defend a lexical priority claim: when a creature is below a certain threshold of well-being—what I call the suffering floor—no amount of positive experience elsewhere can justify adding a further negative experience to that creature, or failing to remove one. The lexical priority is not absolute, however; it applies only to states below the floor. In the second part, I try to specify the floor. I argue that it should not be a fixed hedonic level, because adaptation and contrast effects shift what counts as a baseline. Instead, the floor is a dynamic band defined by the difference between a creature’s current affective state and its recent adaptive baseline, weighted by the duration of the state and the degree to which it disrupts the creature’s characteristic functioning. I give a thought experiment: a laboratory animal whose pain is caused by an experiment that also produces a large benefit for many humans; I argue that the lexical priority blocks the benefit-based justification below the floor, but not above it. In the third part, I defend commensurability above the floor: once the floor is cleared, pleasures and pains can be traded off on a single cardinal scale, and I propose that the exchange rate be set by a two-stage procedure, first a hypothetical insurance choice behind a veil of ignorance, then a lexical constraint that prevents the procedure from licensing a small number of severe pains for a large number of mild pleasures. I call the resulting view lexical threshold negative utilitarianism.

I am especially worried about three things. First, is the suffering floor doing any independent work, or does it collapse into a version of prioritarianism or a sufficiency view? Second, is my dynamic-band definition of the floor coherent, given that adaptation can make people report themselves as satisfied in objectively deprived conditions? Third, does the two-stage procedure for the exchange rate above the floor smuggle in lexicality at the wrong place, and if so, where should the lexical constraint be located? I would also like you to suggest one or two additional thought experiments that would test the view, and to tell me which of my claims is most likely to be false. Please think carefully and write as a critical but constructive referee; do not soften your objections, but also do not invent empirical findings or cite literature. If you think the essay is confused at a fundamental level, say so directly and explain what the confusion is.
