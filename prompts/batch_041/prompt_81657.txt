Construct a thought experiment that isolates the structure of a commitment race, then use it to compare three decision-theoretic perspectives.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Two parties, A and B, with symmetric preferences and common knowledge of one another's rationality. No perfect predictors, no acausal influence, no exotic physics, no superintelligence. The setting should be mundane: two firms, two states, two laboratories, two departments, or similar.
2. Each party can make an irreversible commitment to one of two options: a cooperative option that is better for both if both choose it, or a unilateral option that secures a first-mover advantage if chosen before the other party commits. Committing early is costly because it forecloses deliberation and prevents the parties from designing a better joint option; committing late risks being preempted.
3. Both parties would prefer a coordinated delay that lets them design a superior joint commitment, but each has an incentive to commit first to the unilateral option. The race must arise from the payoff structure and the irreversibility of commitment, not from irrationality, bad faith, or a mistake.
4. The scenario must not be a relabeled prisoner's dilemma or Newcomb problem. The distinctive feature should be the timing and irreversibility of commitment, not the mere presence of a dominant strategy.

Then do the following:

A. Present the scenario in no more than two paragraphs. State the payoffs, the timing, and what each party knows.

B. Show that under a standard causal, forward-looking decision theory, each party's optimal move is to commit as early as possible and as irreversibly as possible, so the race is the unique equilibrium. Explain why delaying is not credible.

C. Now consider a decision theory that evaluates policies rather than individual acts: it asks what policy a party should commit to before knowing whether it is A or B, or before knowing the exact moment at which it will act. Show whether such a theory can recommend a conditional policy that dissolves the race, such as committing only if the other party also commits, or committing to a delayed commitment. If it can, explain exactly why the policy is available and what makes it credible. If it cannot, explain why not.

D. Consider a third perspective: an institutional or mechanism-design perspective that asks whether an external enforcement mechanism, a costly delay, or a public commitment device could implement the cooperative outcome. State what such a mechanism would have to do, and whether it is available in the scenario as described.

E. Identify the single feature of the case that does the most work in generating the race. Say what happens if that feature is removed or weakened. Then state how a defender of the perspective that comes out worst would attack the case: what would they say is unrealistic, question-begging, or a misapplication of their view?

The deliverable should be the scenario plus the analysis. Do not cite literature or name authors; argue from the structure of the case itself.
