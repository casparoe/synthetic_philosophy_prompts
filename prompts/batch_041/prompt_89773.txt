I'm a third-year political theory student living a strange double life, and the strangeness is why I'm writing. My dissertation is on the boundary problem: the suspicion that any democratic procedure for deciding who belongs to the demos must already presuppose a demos entitled to run that procedure, so the founding decision about membership cannot itself be democratic. Meanwhile I've drifted onto the advisory group for a small charter city — a new municipality with negotiated legal autonomy, perhaps ten thousand residents over its first decade, whose founding rules (including who counts as a founder, and who counts as a resident once the doors open) will be drafted before anyone lives there. The two halves keep contaminating each other, and I've lost the ability to tell which parts of the theoretical literature are load-bearing for the practical problem and which are just professional furniture.

So: given three years of one philosopher's time, and the constraint that whatever I work on should be able to change what actually gets written into a founding charter, which of the open questions in the boundary problem should I prioritize, and in what order? I want the ordering defended against explicit criteria — how many other questions in democratic theory and constitutional design depend on the answer; whether it can be settled by argument alone, without the empirical work I'm not trained for; and whether the answer would alter a founding document rather than only a footnote. Two distinctions I'd especially like you to take a position on, because I suspect one of them is where the real work lies. First, whether the founding case and the revision case are governed by the same principles at all: demos boundaries drift constantly through migration, expatriates, children aging in, residents of dependencies, and I genuinely don't know whether a principle that legitimates an initial boundary has anything to say about a boundary being quietly renegotiated for the fifth time. Second, whether the self-selection answer — whoever accepts the rules and shows up constitutes the demos — is a solution or merely a relocation of the problem into the conditions under which self-selection means anything: exit costs, information, whether anyone had an alternative.

Rank at most four items, most important first, and for each say what would move it up or down my list over the next two years. Close with the questions you'd tell me to deliberately ignore, and be willing to say outright if the honest answer is that the founding case is a philosopher's puzzle and the practical drafting should ignore it entirely. Cap the whole response at 700 words, in continuous prose with the ranking clearly marked — no reading list.
