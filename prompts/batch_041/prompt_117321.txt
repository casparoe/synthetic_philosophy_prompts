I am a metrologist by training. My working vocabulary is the vocabulary of measurement: standards and their realization, calibration against a reference, unbroken chains of comparison, uncertainty budgets (the itemised accounting of every contribution to the doubt attaching to a result), and the special standing we give to the standard at the top of a chain — the one whose value we accept without checking it against any other standard of the same quantity. I have no philosophical training beyond a little unsystematic reading.

I would like you to take a problem from epistemology that I have been trying to understand — the bootstrapping problem, together with the charge that certain inferences deliver 'easy knowledge' — and restate it in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Here is the problem in the form in which I have met it, so that you can see what has to be carried across. A reliabilist holds that a faculty — my colour vision, say — can be certified as reliable by using that very faculty: I look at things, notice that the colours I see match the colours I am told they have, and conclude that my colour vision is reliable. The critic objects that this is circular, and that the same pattern would license inferring, from the fact that a table looks red, that it is not being lit by a hidden red lamp — knowledge too cheap to be worth the name. A third position grants the circularity but denies that it is a defect: we are entitled to presuppose the reliability of our faculties without having earned it.

Restate the problem and each of the three positions in my vocabulary, and then give me the residue report. For each position, tell me (i) what carries across cleanly — which of its claims become ordinary, uncontroversial statements about measurement practice; (ii) what carries across only with distortion, and precisely what is distorted; and (iii) what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Two further things I want from the report. First, I am interested in where the analogy fails. My discipline ends its regress of comparisons in a definition — a value that is stipulated rather than measured — and we are untroubled by that; and we have lately gone further, defining our base units by fixing the numerical values of physical constants, so that the system no longer bottoms out in a material artefact. Does the epistemologist have any legitimate counterpart to a stipulated standard, or does the correspondence break exactly where the philosophical problem begins? Second, I want your view on which side of the residue the defect lies: is there something my vocabulary cannot express that the epistemologists are right about, or does the residue suggest that what they are describing is a measurement problem stated without the resources to solve it?

I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the translation and the residue.
