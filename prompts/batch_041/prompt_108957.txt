I work as a software engineer and have no training in philosophy or economics, so I would appreciate a careful, non-technical answer to a dispute I keep losing. On a committee at my company that decides which internal projects to fund, I argued for a project whose main benefits would arrive in roughly forty years — mostly maintenance and archival savings, plus a large reduction in downstream risk — and a colleague dismissed it with the remark that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. He took that to be decisive, and everyone nodded. I suspect the sentence is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them bear on the decision in front of us.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. I would like them as a menu rather than a single interpretation, ranging from the nearly trivial to the substantive: claims about what a dollar can earn if invested, about opportunity cost, about the productivity of capital, about sheer impatience with the future as such, about uncertainty over long horizons (including the chance that there is no one around to collect), and about the diminishing value of additional resources to people who will likely be richer than we are. For each reading, say whether it is true, false, or merely contentious, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it actually supports my colleague's conclusion that a benefit arriving in forty years may be treated as worth approximately nothing today.

A few things I would like you to take seriously rather than wave at. First, the arithmetic: a positive annual discount rate compounds, so a benefit even a few centuries out becomes a vanishing fraction of its nominal value, and I want to know whether that is a discovery about value or an artifact of a modeling choice. Second, whether the justification for discounting is the same when the setting is a private firm's capital budgeting, when it is a public project, and when it is the interests of people who do not yet exist and whose very existence may depend on what we do now. Third, whether the ethical objection to pure impatience — that caring less about a harm because it happens later is a moral error akin to caring less about a harm because it happens to someone unlike me — is a genuine argument or just a rhetorical comparison, and what a defender of discounting should say in reply. If the correct verdict depends on which of these frames we are in, say so explicitly, and tell me which frame my committee is actually operating in, whether or not we realize it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and please do not restate my question.
