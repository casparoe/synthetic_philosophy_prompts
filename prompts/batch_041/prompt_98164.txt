I have just come from a long argument with a friend about Simone Weil, and I would like to turn it into an exam question. My friend insisted that Weil’s hostility to political parties was merely a temperamental dislike of organization, and that any serious democratic politics must be party politics. I argued that this misses what is distinctive in her objection: she is not against organization as such but against a form of collective life that manufactures consent by monopolizing attention, converting judgment into loyalty, and making the group rather than the truth the object of devotion. We did not resolve it. I teach an upper-level seminar in modern political thought, and I want the final essay question to make students confront precisely this dispute rather than recite the usual summary that parties turn persons into things. Please draft the question, a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes by a student who knows Weil’s work but has no internet access, and it must not require citations, quotations, or bibliographies. To keep it out of the ruts of the secondary literature, build it around the following hypothetical: a municipal reform movement that calls itself a network, has no dues, membership cards, or central office, but whose participants reliably vote as a bloc, police a shared vocabulary, and treat defection as betrayal. Students must say whether Weil’s critique of parties applies to this movement, what in her account makes it apply or not, and what their answer implies for her view of whether democratic politics can survive without parties. They should also address one serious objection: that without parties, politics is left to notables, clients, and factions that are less accountable, not more. The model answer should not simply choose a side. It should reconstruct the strongest version of two readings—first, Weil as a radical anti-party thinker who would reject even the network if it exhibits party spirit; second, Weil as a critic of a specific pathology, for whom the network is acceptable only so long as it remains porous, reversible, and oriented toward attention rather than collective identity—and then adjudicate between them. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. It should clearly separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain.
