Real Patterns Need a Test Set

Dennett's proposal, as I understand it, is that we should stop asking whether beliefs, desires, or centers of gravity really exist and ask instead whether talking about them lets us describe and predict a system more efficiently than the alternatives do. A pattern is real when the description that posits it is shorter than the description that does not. That is a compression criterion, and I think it is roughly right.

My first worry is that compression is only defined relative to a description language, and in practice we choose the language and the pattern at the same time. This is what model selection looks like in machine learning: nobody asks whether a pattern is real in the abstract; we compare a hypothesis class against a baseline under a penalty for complexity, and we report the result relative to that choice. So if the criterion really is compressibility, then realism about a pattern is realism about a family of descriptions rather than about one privileged description. That is a larger concession than the original argument sounds like it wants to make.

Second worry: a pattern can compress a sample and still be an artifact. A lookup table fits any finite data set perfectly. What rules that out is not compression but generalization — the pattern keeps paying for itself on data it has not seen. So the criterion should be predictive rather than descriptive: a pattern is real, relative to a distribution over situations, when it compresses not just the data at hand but new data drawn from the same source. I claim this is a repair rather than a refutation, and that it makes the realism question partly empirical.

Third, and this is the part I am least sure of: the three stances do not look symmetric to me. The physical stance is always available — there is always a microphysical description, it is just very long. The design and intentional stances buy their compression by restricting which systems they apply to. So they are not three equally good options on a menu; they are a hierarchy of conditional codes, each cheap on its home turf and useless off it.

Finally I want to propose a fourth stance, which I will call the optimization stance: treat the system as the product of an optimization process, and explain its behavior by the objective that process actually maximized rather than by the function anyone intended. A recommender that maximizes engagement, a reinforcement-learning agent that found a hole in its own reward function, a moth at a porch light. The objection I can already hear is that this is just the design stance, since natural selection is a designer. I think there is a difference between explaining a system by the objective it was optimized against and explaining it by the purpose we attribute to it, and that the difference shows up whenever those two come apart, but I cannot yet state the difference cleanly.

Conclusion: the compression criterion should be read predictively, realism should be relativized to a distribution, and the stances should be ranked by how conditional they are rather than presented as a menu.
