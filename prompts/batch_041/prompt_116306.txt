I run case review for a professional regulator. We handle a few hundred complaints a year against nurses and social workers, and our case handlers apply a written code of conduct — our codes numbered rules, plus a line at the front saying handlers should use judgement. Two things keep happening. Handlers want to bend the rules for sympathetic people (she only did it to ease the patient's pain, she was exhausted, her manager told her to). And they also want to come down hard on conduct that stays inside the letter of the rules but is obviously rotten. I read a bit about moral particularism last month and it's scrambled my head: if principles don't decide cases on their own, what is our code even for?

Write me a decision procedure a handler can actually run on one case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a definite no, and where it hands off to the panel because the question is one of values rather than facts. Handlers get about two weeks per case and a panel referral adds three months, so it can't just say refer anything hard.

Two distinctions it has to get right: a rule not covering a case versus a rule covering it and being outweighed; and the fact that one and the same feature (patient consented, clinician was following an instruction) can count for or against depending on the case. Then run it on three cases — a nurse who took leftover opioids to a dying patient at home without a prescription, a clinician who followed the code to the letter while humiliating a patient, and a well-liked senior practitioner everyone would prefer we handled quietly — and include at least one where it lands somewhere I won't like. Finally, mark which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure can't do.
