I have been arguing with a colleague about how luck egalitarianism should draw the line between bad brute luck and bad option luck, and I want to pin your view down with a number. The proposal on the table, which I will call the scalar-control view, is this: the relevant line is not a dichotomy but a single scalar, effective control, defined for any given disadvantage as the product of two factors — how foreseeable that disadvantage was to the agent when she chose, and how much the outcome would have differed had she chosen otherwise — such that a disadvantage is unjust exactly to the extent that effective control is low. Give me your credence that the scalar-control view is correct, and decompose it. I want a rough probability for each of the following and an account of how they combine: whether foreseeability and counterfactual responsiveness really do multiply into one morally relevant quantity or are incommensurable; whether the foreseeability factor is itself so saturated with the luck it is supposed to sort out that the view is circular; whether the familiar objection that luck egalitarianism licenses abandoning the imprudent is decisive against any account that conditions entitlement on control, scalar or not; and whether the view ultimately collapses into a desert-based account that luck egalitarians are committed to rejecting. Then tell me which of these terms the final number is most sensitive to, and which single consideration, resolved one way rather than the other, would move your credence furthest. Please write this as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
