I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a module on chance, credence and self-locating belief; the Sleeping Beauty problem has occupied the second half of the term, and I have been asked to run next week's revision session for my study group. I would like to bring one practice question written in the style of our final paper, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that the six of us can sit it under timed conditions and then mark one another's scripts honestly rather than nodding along.

My complaint about every Sleeping Beauty question I have been able to find is that a candidate can do well on it by attaching the labels 'halfer' and 'thirder' to the two standard slogans and then padding; nothing in the question obliges anyone actually to reason. I have therefore tried to construct a case whose answer cannot simply be recalled. Please build the question on exactly the following setup, which I have adapted from a problem-class exercise:

On Sunday evening Beauty is put to sleep. The experimenter flips a fair coin, C. If C lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If C lands tails, the experimenter consults a randomiser which, with probability p, sends her down a double-awakening branch (woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, with an amnesia-inducing drug administered between the two awakenings) and, with probability 1 − p, down a single-awakening branch (woken once, on Monday). The value of p is fixed in advance, lies between 0 and 1 inclusive, and is known to Beauty. On each awakening she cannot tell which day it is and cannot tell whether she has been woken before; she is asked only for her credence that C landed heads. She knows the whole protocol.

I want the question to have three numbered parts and to be worth 20 marks, answerable in about 75 minutes. Between them the parts should require candidates to (a) derive the credence in heads at an awakening as a function of p, on each of the two views our course has distinguished — the one on which self-locating information is evidentially inert, and the one on which a credence should match the proportion of awakenings in the experiment that are heads-awakenings — and to check both answers at p = 0 and p = 1; (b) say what the p = 0 case shows about whether the disagreement between those views is a substantive one or a verbal one, and what a defender of the second view has to concede in the light of it; and (c) connect credence to action by asking what each view implies about a bet offered and settled at every awakening. The question should not presuppose which view is correct, and no part of it should be answerable by naming a position rather than deriving what follows from it.

Please then write (1) the question as it would appear on the paper; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words, doing the arithmetic explicitly and marking clearly where the two views diverge and why; and (3) a marking scheme totalling 20 marks, naming the specific moves a first-class answer must make, the marks attached to each, and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates each of those marks.

Two requests about the marking scheme in particular. I am fairly confident that one error worth penalising is the candidate who counts the distinguishable awakening-situations and divides by their number; I would like you to work out what that mistake yields in this setup and to identify the other mistakes you would expect. And I would rather the marks attach to the derivations and to the argument about what the parameter p does to the dispute than to the verdict a candidate reaches, since I am not confident that either side has the better of it.

Finally, please keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, no names of who argued for what, and nothing in the model answer that rests on a source I would have to look up. Everything needed should be stated in the question or derivable from it.
