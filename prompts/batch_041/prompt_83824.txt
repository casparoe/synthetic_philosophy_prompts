You are a philosopher of language with a specialist's grasp of the semantics and pragmatics of belief reports. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the full range of cases that fall under this heading; commit to the list only afterwards.

I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about Moore's paradox — about why it is absurd to say something like 'The meeting is at three, but I don't believe the meeting is at three', when the very same claim can be true. His line is that it is all a matter of what assertion commits you to and that there is no puzzle about the propositions themselves; my suspicion is that this cannot be the whole story. We talked past each other for hours because we never settled what a theory of the phenomenon is even supposed to deliver. So I want the specification before the theories: the desiderata that any adequate account of the absurdity of Moorean sentences has to satisfy.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or contrast that motivates it, and for each say what an account forfeits by failing it. At minimum I want these covered: the omissive form, in which one asserts that p while disavowing belief that p; the commissive form, in which one asserts that one believes that p and then asserts that not-p; the same content entertained as a thought or a belief rather than asserted aloud; the past-tensed variants, in which locating the disavowed belief in the past appears to drain the absurdity away; the third-person variants, which are not absurd at all; and the generalization to other attitudes — knowing, wanting, intending, remembering. Then separate the requirements that are genuine constraints on any theory from those that merely encode one camp's commitments, and say where a purely pragmatic account and a semantic account will each have to fight hardest. If you think some pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which and why — that is the part I most want. And tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the most popular accounts.

Two cases I have been unable to place, and I would rather not be told they are marginal. First, graded versions: 'It is very likely to rain, but my credence that it will rain is low.' Is the oddity there the same phenomenon, or a distinct one that a theory of Moore's paradox must not conflate with it? Second, the first-person plural, where a committee chair says 'The committee thinks the deadline is Friday, but I don't think the deadline is Friday', and the harder variant in which the subject is a single collective mind rather than an aggregate of members. Do these belong among the cases the theory must get right, and if so, what do they add to the requirements?

No survey of published positions and no reading list — I want the specification itself, defended.
