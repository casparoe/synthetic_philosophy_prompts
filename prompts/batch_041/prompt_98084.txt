I'm drafting an essay on interpersonal utility comparisons and I want a second pair of eyes before I commit to the argument. Here's the gist of what I've got so far.

The whole fight between the ordinalists and the people who insist welfare economics needs interpersonal comparisons is, I want to say, a fight about the wrong thing. Both sides ask *whether* two people's utilities are comparable, as though comparability were a feature of the utilities themselves, like two rods happening to be the same length. I want to argue that comparability is a feature of a practice. Two people's goods are comparable relative to some shared scale or project they jointly maintain — the way two lengths are comparable relative to a ruler that both parties are holding. There's no comparability in the abstract; there's only comparability-under-a-scale.

Three things I want to pull out of that:

1. The utility monster isn't a reductio of utilitarianism. It's a reductio of pretending the scale exists independent of any project. Ask "a scale for what?" and the monster stops being a problem.
2. The distinction between comparing how well off two people each are (levels) and comparing how much better off one is than the other (units) is real, but it's derivative — it just tracks two different jobs you might want a scale to do, and there's no reason a single practice has to do both.
3. So interpersonal comparisions are more like promises than like measurements. They can be kept or broken, wise or reckless, but not true or false.

Can you tell me where this goes wrong? I have a nagging feeling that 3 doesnt follow from 1 and 2 — that even if the scale is practice-relative, once you're inside a practice the comparison "A is worse off than B" is still just true or false, so I haven't earned the anti-realist ending. And maybe 1 is too quick: the monster might come back as a point about the *shape* a scale has to take rather than about whether it exists at all. Comments on accuracy, and on whether the pieces actually fit together, would be great. Don't worry about polish.
