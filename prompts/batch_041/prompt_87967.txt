Q3.

The asymmetry thesis says testimony functions differently in the moral case than in the non-moral case. In ordinary empirical matters, if a reliable person tells me it's raining in Oslo, I can believe them and that gives me knowledge; I don't have to go and check. But if someone tells me that eating meat is wrong, it seems I can't just take their word for it in the same way, even when I know they're reliable and even when they're right.

Argument for: moral beliefs have to be arrived at by the agent herself, because what matters morally isn't just getting the right answer but grasping why it's the right answer. If I believe eating meat is wrong only because you said so, then when the case shifts slightly — say the meat comes from an animal that died of natural causes, or the alternative is malnutrition — I won't be able to work out what to think, because I never did the reasoning in the first place. So moral testimony can give me knowledge but not understanding, and understanding is what we actually need. What I'm missing in that case is a piece of information, namely the reason why the act is wrong, so this is the informational kind of deference the handout was worried about.

Argument against: the asymmetry looks ad hoc. We think it's fine to rely on expertise in non-moral matters, and moral expertise doesn't seem different in kind — if I can know that a doctor is better at diagnosis than I am, I can know that a more thoughtful person is better at moral reasoning than I am. The thesis also has trouble locating the line it wants: testimony about what I ought to do all things considered and testimony about the reasons I have both involve deferring on a normative question, so it isn't clear how one could be fine and the other not without already assuming the conclusion.

What follows: if the thesis is right, I should never rely on moral testimony, and moral testimony can't transmit knowledge at all, since knowledge requires the understanding that testimony can't give. In practice I should form my own moral beliefs from scratch.

There's also a different argument in the background, that acting on moral testimony is wrong because the action has no moral worth — an action only has moral worth when it's done from duty, and if I act because someone told me to, I'm not acting from duty, I'm just following instructions. So that gives a second route to the asymmetry.

Overall the asymmetry is a real phenomenon, and the interesting question is how much of it survives once we get clear about which kind of deference we're talking about.
