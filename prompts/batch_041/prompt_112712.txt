I'm a trustee of a small charitable trust that runs a residential care home for about forty residents, and I sit on its finance and risk subcommittee — five of us, half an hour set aside for this item on Tuesday. We have to settle how we cover one low-probability, high-cost risk: failure of the building's heating and water plant in a cold snap, which would force us to evacuate the residents at short notice. Our adviser has modelled three options and put an annual expected cost against each.

Option A: continue the current regime of inspections and patch repairs, with a capital contingency held in reserve.
Option B: a full plant replacement this autumn under a long maintenance warranty, funded by a loan.
Option C: begin a managed wind-down — move the residents to a partner home over two years and sell the building.

On the adviser's numbers, A and B come out level, at roughly the same expected annual cost, and C a little worse. That is where my problem starts. The figure for A rests on our own maintenance records going back twenty years — patchy, but ours — plus the mutual insurer's loss data on comparable homes. The figure for B rests on an engineering report and on four cases elsewhere in the country where a similar plant was replaced under a similar warranty: four cases, none of them a building like ours, none of them followed past year three. The figure for C is, as far as I can make out, the adviser's judgement dressed up as an estimate; nobody of our size and in our position has done it, so there is nothing to extrapolate from.

My instinct is that A and B are not equally well founded even though the figures agree, and that B and C cannot really be compared at all — the second is not so much worse than the third as unbacked. The chair of the subcommittee says I am dressing up a hunch: a probability is a probability, if the numbers match the options are equally risky, and if I want to argue otherwise I must say what extra thing the figures are missing. I cannot yet say what that is, and we have to decide on Tuesday.

What I am groping for is a difference between how likely something is and how much we have to go on in saying so. I have started sketching a second column beside the adviser's expected costs, headed "how much this number is standing on", to be filled in with the number of comparable cases, how long they were followed, and whether any of them resembles us. The chair says that is a confidence interval under another name, and that we should simply ask the adviser for the interval if we want one.

So, please give me the following, in this order.

"The nub of it" (about 150 words): what my unease amounts to, and whether it is a real distinction or a hunch. If it has a name, give me the name in a single line — I don't want a reading list.

"The case for the chair" (about 200 words): the strongest version of the chair's position, including what is right about treating the figures as interchangeable.

"What the arithmetic can and cannot carry" (about 300 words): go through A, B and C and say what can and cannot be said about each, and about the comparisons between them, on the evidence we actually have.

"The second column" (about 200 words): whether my column is coherent, what belongs in it, and whether the chair is right that it collapses into a confidence interval. Be frank if the honest answer is that it cannot be scored in a way that would survive a committee.

"What I should say on Tuesday" (about 200 words): what to put to the subcommittee — the substance, plus a sentence or two I can actually say aloud.

"What you would decide" (about 150 words): what you would do about the decision itself — choose now, choose with a caveat, or buy time to gather evidence — given that the cold-snap risk is this winter's.

Write in British English, in continuous prose except where I have asked for something to say aloud. No references, please: I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
