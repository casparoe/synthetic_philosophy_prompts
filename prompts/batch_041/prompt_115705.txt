I'm writing the final exam for a graduate seminar I teach on the ethics and governance of AI -- mixed bunch, some CS students, some philosophy, a couple from the policy school. It's a single 90-minute in-class essay, closed book, and the one question is worth 30 points. I want it on process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI systems, but I'm sick of the canned version of that topic.

The canned version goes: process supervision rewards the reasoning steps, outcome supervision rewards the final answer, process is more sample-efficient, outcome is easier to verify, done. They've all seen that in a blog post. I want a question where that framing collapses under them.

The hook I want the question built around is a made-up proposal (so they cant just look it up) -- call it StepGrade. Under StepGrade, a separate verifier model scores each step of the policy model's written chain of thought, and the policy is fine-tuned to raise those step scores. The baseline it's compared against just rewards correct final answers, no step-level signal at all. The question should make them argue about which is the better way to supervise, and it should end up forcing two things into the open.

First: StepGrade only supervises a process if the written chain of thought is actually the process that produces the answer. If it isn't -- if the model's stated reasoning is a post-hoc story -- then StepGrade is training the model to write more convincing justifications, which is not the same as training it to reason better, and could end up worse than the dumb outcome-only baseline. A strong answer has to say why, and has to be clear about what would have to be true for the step scores to track the real computation.

Second, and this is the part I care about most: the whole sales pitch for process supervision is that it grades the system on what it does rather than on how the chips fell -- the outcome is noisy and luck-dependent, the process is the thing the system "controls." I want the question to make them notice that the process is downstream of stuff the system didn't control too (its training, its priors, its architecture), so the luck doesn't go away, it just moves. A top answer has to say whether that kills the control-based case for process supervision, and if not, what a version of that case looks like that isn't quietly circular or self-undermining.

So: give me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words (a sketch of the ideal answer, not a full timed essay), and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer has to make -- I'd like five must-hit points with point values totaling 30 -- and for each one, a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong (e.g., the usual mistake on the second part is treating "it's under the system's control" as just meaning "it's internal to the model," which dodges the whole problem). Put the whole thing in American English. And no citations needed anywhere -- I dont want the model answer leaning on who said what.
