Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words for an anthology that teaches philosophical ideas through narrative rather than exposition. The idea I want it to carry is Keynes's distinction between the probability of a proposition and what he called the weight of the argument for it: two judgments can assign a proposition the same probability while resting on evidence of very different depth and completeness, and Keynes treated weight as a second, independent dimension of rational belief rather than as just another probability. He also held that probabilities need not be numerically measurable, or even comparable with one another, so a rational agent may have to decide without being able to compute which option is more probable.

Give me a story in which a character must make an irreversible, one-off decision between two courses of action: one that is quite probable on thin, one-sided evidence, and one that is somewhat less probable but supported by evidence that is deep, varied, and mutually corroborating. No long-run frequencies are available, so no law of large numbers can settle the matter, and at the outset the two probabilities cannot be reduced to numbers that would let her simply pick the larger. Let the reader feel the pull of the thin high-probability option and the countervailing pull of the deep-evidence option, and let the resolution turn on how she weighs evidence she cannot fully itemize. At least one thing she learns along the way should raise the weight of her judgment without much changing its probability.

Constraints: don't mention Keynes or any other philosopher, and don't have a character explain the distinction in dialogue or narrate it in summary — it should be legible only through what she notices, discounts, and cannot bring herself to ignore. Choose a concrete setting with real constraints on action, and avoid the stock examples: no roulette wheels, coin flips, betting markets, or arguments about God. End with the decision made or refused, not with a moral spelled out.
