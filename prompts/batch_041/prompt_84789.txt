I am a journalist working on a feature about the gap between what people resolve to do and what they actually do. To get the concepts straight, I asked two people to walk me through the terrain: a philosopher who works on action and practical reason, and a behavioral economist who models how people weigh rewards over time. What was meant to be background turned into an argument, and I could not referee it. Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it, with the moves numbered so they can be addressed one by one.

(1) The economist said weakness of will is not a puzzle at all. It is present bias: we discount a reward more steeply the closer it gets, so a reward's ranking flips as it approaches. What we call akrasia is simply the agent's preferences reversing over time. There is no separate faculty of will, and so nothing for it to be weak.

(2) The philosopher said that cannot be the whole story, because a preference reversal is not yet weakness of will. If I want cake now but would want salad if I were ordering for next Tuesday, my preferences have merely changed. Akrasia requires that I act against my own judgment about what I should do — I judge that I should refuse the cake and take it anyway. A person can be perfectly time-consistent and still be akratic (breaking a promise out of spite), and perfectly time-inconsistent without being akratic (revising a plan for good reasons). Present bias may describe the terrain, but it leaves out the essential element: the conflict with one's own better judgment.

(3) The economist allowed that a judgment is in there somewhere, but said the judgment is just another preference — a preference about one's preferences, or about the long run. Model the agent as having a long-run preference for health and a short-run preference for cake, and the conflict with judgment becomes a conflict between two preference orderings, which the standard framework handles. He added that the fact that people pay to restrict their own future options — locking away the cigarettes, signing a savings commitment — shows they expect their future selves to defect, which is exactly what the model predicts for a sophisticated agent who knows she is present-biased.

(4) The philosopher raised two objections. First, if the better judgment is just one more preference, there is no way to say when someone has failed rather than merely preferred differently; on that picture no one is ever weak-willed, they simply have the preferences they have. Second, the commitment device cuts against the economist: if my future self is the same person as me, why is treating him as an adversary rational? Either Ulysses tied to the mast is a single agent managing his own future states — in which case there is an agent doing the managing, which is the will the economist said did not exist — or the present self is coercing a different person, which forces the question of which one is really me.

(5) The economist said the question of which one is really me is a red herring the model does not need. On the first objection, the model does not deny that weakness of will happens; it treats it as a pattern of choices rather than a distinct mental event. He added that some newer accounts suggest the declining discount rates usually attributed to present bias may instead be an artifact of how people subdivide an interval — so that hyperbolic discounting may be a description of the data rather than a mechanism, which he took as further reason to treat akrasia as a description of behavior and not a special faculty.

(6) The philosopher replied that if hyperbolic discounting is only a description, then it is not an explanation of akrasia either, and the economist has conceded the point: the description leaves out what makes the act a failure — that the agent herself, all things considered, holds that she should do otherwise. A taxonomy of choices, he said, is not yet an account of the defect in one of them.

I do not need to know who won. I need to know who is right about what. Please work through the six moves and rate each one, from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating. Then say which of the sub-disputes are substantive and which are merely verbal, identify the crux the disagreement really turns on, and finish with what, if anything, both of them are missing.
