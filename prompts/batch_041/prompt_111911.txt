The question is whether neural networks actually have representations, or whether that's just something we say because it makes the maths easier to talk about. Priya's line: there's no fact of the matter about what's inside the thing, only facts about what it does, and every "the model represents X" is us projecting. My line: that gives up too much, too fast.

Here's what I've got so far.

1. Two questions get run together. (a) Does an internal state carry information about some feature of the input, information that later layers actually use? (b) Is there a determinate fact about what that state is *about*? Most interpretability work nails (a) and then talks as if (b) came free. That slide is the whole disagreement in miniature, I think.

2. I want to distinguish the network's own vocabulary from ours. Call it in-house vs borrowed. The network has whatever distinctions its weights actually make and use; we describe those distinctions in our words. A representation claim is a translation between the two, and translations can be better or worse without there being exactly one right one. So I'm a pluralist — several incompatible glosses can all be legitimate — but not an anything-goes-ist, because the network's counterfactual structure constrains which translations survive. Priya says this is just relabelling the problem, which is fair enough as a heckle and I'm not sure I've got an answer.

3. Here's the constraint I want to put on a candidate representation claim. It has to earn its keep three ways: causal use (steer the state, get the predicted downstream change); counterfactual tracking (it varies with the thing it's supposed to be about, across inputs nobody tuned it on); and re-identification (the same state gets picked out again in a different context, rather than us naming a fresh thing per example). The third one is doing the real work and it's the one nobody states. Without it you can just name every neuron after whatever input maxes it out and call that a representation.

4. The sparse-autoencoder worry, which I think is the crux. If you train a dictionary on activations you get a description that compresses well. But compression isn't representation. Are those features re-identified across contexts, or is the dictionary just a convenient basis that the network itself never uses? Tentative line: a feature is real iff it's a causal handle — you can steer along it and get the predicted change — but I think that only buys you (a), not (b), and people slide between the two constantly. If that's right then the autoencoder work is silent on the interesting question, which is a big claim and I'd like you to attack it.

5. Why I don't buy the projection story. If Priya's right, then two interpreters with equally good predictive records but incompatible glosses are both right. Fine. But then the same has to go for a thermometer, or a retina, or a thermostat, and that seems to give up more than we should. My move: representation is interest-relative but not arbitrary — it's relative to a capacity we're trying to explain, and once you fix the capacity, the admissible glosses shrink a lot. Is that a real position or is it verificationism with a new coat on?

6. The bit I'm least sure of. Maybe "the network represents X" is just the wrong form of words, and the honest form is always "represents X for the purpose of capacity C, under description D". If so the realism debate mostly dissolves into a debate about which capacities we care about, which might be the true answer and also might be a cop-out. I genuinely can't tell which.
