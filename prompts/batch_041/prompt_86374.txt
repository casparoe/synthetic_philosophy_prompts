I'm a high school student preparing a poem to read at my school's philosophy club, and I've gotten stuck on a problem I'd rather have the poem embody than explain. The view is epiphenomenalism: conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but cause nothing in turn. If that's right, then a poem written by someone in pain, about the pain, cannot have been caused by the pain — the words, including the word "pain," have to come from whatever physical events are also producing the experience, so nothing the speaker says about the feeling is said because of it. Write that poem as a sonnet, in the voice of someone in pain who is trying to write it anyway. I'd like the form to do some of the work: keep the rhyme scheme strict enough that it visibly forces the words, and let the speaker notice partway through that the rhyme they thought they were reaching for was already waiting for them, the sense of having chosen arriving after the choice was made. The volta should be the moment they see that if they are right, this poem — which gets the feeling exactly right — was written by something that never felt it. Someone who knows the debate should find it accurate, and someone who has never heard of the view should still find it a good poem.
