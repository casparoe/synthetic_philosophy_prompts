I want your credence that the continuum hypothesis is true, and I want the number to come apart into the parts that produce it rather than arrive as a verdict. Take the proposition to be this: every uncountable set of real numbers has the same cardinality as the set of all real numbers, equivalently there is no cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the reals. Two ground rules for the exercise. First, treat the possibility that there is no determinate fact of the matter about CH as a genuine option: report separately your credence that the question has a truth value at all, then give your credence that CH is true conditional on there being a fact of the matter, so I can see how the two compose. Second, do not treat the independence results as settling anything by themselves; the interesting question is what positive considerations bear on truth, and how much weight each deserves.

Then decompose. I want a rough probability and a one- or two-line justification for each of the following, plus any term you think I have left out.

(a) The dart-throwing symmetry argument. Fix any rule that assigns to each real number a countable set of real numbers. Throw two idealized darts at the unit interval. After the first lands at x, the assigned set is countable, hence of measure zero, so the second dart lands in it with probability zero; by the symmetry of the order in which the darts were thrown, the same holds in the other direction. The intuition that both almost-sure exclusions can be simultaneously realized yields the statement that there exist two reals each of which lies outside the other's assigned set, and that statement turns out to be equivalent to the negation of CH (Freiling's axiom of symmetry; the underlying equivalence is due to Sierpiński). How much does this probabilistic intuition move you toward the negation of CH? Then how much does the standard objection deflate it, namely that the argument tacitly assumes a well-behaved probability for every set of reals while the axiom of choice delivers non-measurable sets, and that a close variant of the same reasoning contradicts the axiom of choice itself, which suggests the intuition is really about the impossibility of well-ordering the reals rather than about CH? Give me your net weight on this term, not just the two sides.

(b) Maximality and forcing axioms. Principles in the forcing-axiom family are often defended as capturing the idea that the universe of sets is as rich as possible, and the strongest of them push the continuum up to the second uncountable cardinal, hence against CH. But richness also seems to pull the other way: CH can be read as saying the power set of the naturals is as small as it can be, and one might think the richness of the universe is a matter of height and of which subsets exist, not of how many reals there are. Which reading of maximality should carry the day, and how much does your answer move your credence on CH? Say whether you think there is a single maximality intuition or whether the forcing-axiom intuition and the plenitude-of-reals intuition are in genuine conflict, and price that in.

(c) Inner models and canonicity. There is a long-running program of identifying a canonical inner model of set theory together with an axiom that would decide CH; the conjecture that the relevant axiom implies CH is a live one, though the model is unfinished and its status is contested. How much should the existence of a canonical, preferred model count as evidence for CH, given that the choice of model is driven by our current techniques rather than by an independent conception of the universe? If you think this term deserves near-zero weight because the program's success would reflect what we can build rather than what is true, say so and say why.

(d) Reflection and the iterative conception. How much do reflection principles bear on CH — the thought that the universe cannot be describable in a way that singles it out, so that any property of the whole already holds of some initial segment? Do they generate enough large-cardinal strength to reach the continuum, or do they run out before the size of the continuum is fixed? Give me your reading and a number.

(e) The base rate for independent statements. What is your prior that a statement independent of ZFC, and specifically one about the size of the continuum, is true, false, or indeterminate, and how much of your final number is just that prior rather than any of the specific considerations above? Be honest about whether (a) through (d) are doing real work or rationalizing a prior.

Then combine: give the credence that CH is true, the credence that there is no fact of the matter, and the conditional credence given a fact of the matter, showing the arithmetic. Tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most — in particular, whether any conceivable mathematical development, as opposed to a shift in philosophical taste, could move you by more than a little.

For reference, my own credence that CH is true is about 0.25, and my credence that there is no fact of the matter is about 0.3.
