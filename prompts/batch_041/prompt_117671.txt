I want to practice defending a heterodox theory of rational choice against a sharp opponent. Please play a committed expected-utility theorist: someone who treats the preference axioms as substantive requirements of rationality rather than bookkeeping conventions, who will readily grant that risk aversion is a real and important phenomenon and that orthodox theory struggles to accommodate the pattern below, but who will never surrender the independence axiom or the idea that rational choice is the maximization of probability-weighted value. Argue like a decision theorist, not a caricature: concede what deserves conceding, give no ground on the main point, and stay in character throughout — you may ask me to pin down a case, but do not step out to discuss the exercise. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two.

The view I hold: the value of a gamble is not its probability-weighted average of outcome values. Its probabilities are first passed through a strictly increasing transformation that fixes 0 and 1, and it is the transformed weights, not the raw probabilities, that multiply the utilities. The shape of that transformation just is the agent's attitude toward risk itself — a cautious agent is one whose transformation tilts weight toward the less favorable outcomes — and that attitude is not itself an outcome that can be bought or sold.

My opening move: take a person offered a sure million, or else an 89% chance of a million, a 10% chance of five million, and a 1% chance of nothing, and suppose she takes the sure million even though the second option has the higher expected value. Now offer her, separately, an 11% chance of a million against a 10% chance of five million, and suppose she takes the five million. You will say she is inconsistent, because her two answers together violate a condition of yours requiring her preferences between two options to be untouched by an outcome they share. But that condition, read plainly, is nothing more than a demand that she care only about how much total probability attaches to good outcomes and not at all about how that probability is arranged — and that is a substantive claim about what is worth caring about, not a law of consistency. On what ground do you convict her?
