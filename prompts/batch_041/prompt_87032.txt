I spent most of a long train ride last week arguing with a friend who works in evo-devo, and I still can't let the argument go. We were trying to say what we mean when we attribute a function to a structure — the function of a heart valve, the function of a kinase in a signalling cascade — and we kept sliding between two readings: what the thing was selected for, and what it does within a working system. She held that only the first is a genuine function claim, and that the second is a description of a mechanism dressed up in teleological clothing; I held that when we say a valve's function is to prevent backflow we are not making any claim about the valve's ancestry, and that if we were, most of physiology would be uninterpretable. What struck me afterwards was that we agreed about nearly every case and disagreed only about what a function claim commits us to: she took it to commit us to a counterfactual about what would have happened in an ancestral population under selection, I took it to commit us to a counterfactual about what the part would contribute in a system that is working. My half-formed idea is that the long-running standoff between selected-effects and causal-role accounts of function is not really a dispute about which items have functions — on the easy cases the extensions coincide — but about which counterfactuals and which norms function-talk is meant to underwrite, and that this is why each side keeps winning on its own examples and losing on the other's.
