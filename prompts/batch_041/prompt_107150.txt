I retired from a career in entomology a few years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since, mostly the ethics stuff. I have a request, and it's a build-a-thing request rather than a tell-me-about request.

Background so you know where I'm coming from: for about 35 years I did behaviour work on insects, and I was always uneasy about whether any of it was 'going on inside'. These days people in the animal welfare world keep asking me alot whether the things I studied can suffer, and I honestly don't have a good answer. What I want from you is a decision tree (or a taxonomy, whatever you think fits better) that starts from a handful of plain questions and ends up sorting a person into a named position about how moral status is spread across the whole spectrum -- mammals at one end, insects and worms in the middle, and plants, tissue cultures and thermostats down at the other.

A few things I need from it:

- The branches have to be the questions that actually seperate the positions, phrased so someone can answer without knowing which label it leads to. No branch that basically asks 'so, are you a utilitarian?'.
- I want these distinctions to do real work, because they're the ones I keep tripping over: (a) is sentience itself a matter of degree, or all-or-nothing with only its weight varying; (b) what's the mark of the state that matters -- just reacting to damage, or having experiences that are good or bad for the creature, or having interests at all, or wanting things, or being the kind of thing with a past and a future; (c) does the substrate matter (a real nervous system) or only the pattern of processing; (d) what you do when you can't tell whether something is sentient -- fold the doubt into a probability and act on the expected value, or apply some precautionary rule, or demand positive evidence before granting anything; (e) is moral status itself graded, or binary once you cross some line.
- Cases I want used as the diagnostic questions, at least most of them: an insect that limps and grooms a damaged leg but shows no other sign of caring; a creature that will pay a cost (skip food, take a shock) to stop a nasty stimulus; a fish or a crab that learns to avoid a spot where it got zapped; a plant that releases a chemical when it's chewed; a dish of neurons firing in spontaneous patterns; and a thermostat or a chatbot that produces the right words. Use the cases to split the views, don't just mention them.
- Where the tree ends, each leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'nobody's view' if the slot is empty) plus one line on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending.
- Point out any combinations of answers that no one seems to hold, and say whether you think that's because the view is incoherent, or merely unattractive, or just not explored yet.
- Flag any two positions your tree can't really tell apart, instead of pretending it can.
- Size: roughly 8 to 12 questions, maybe 8 to 12 leaves. Write it for someone like me -- no philosophy training but comfortable with biology.

And please, no reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up, I just want the instrument.
