I have settled verdicts about copying and no rule that accounts for them, and I would like you to try to find the rule — and to be candid about what it costs me.

My verdicts:

- Photocopying an entire in-print textbook and distributing it to my students: wrong.
- Reissuing a living novelist's novel in full, her name on the cover, at half the cover price: wrong.
- A high-street chain copying the cut and colour of a designer's runway dress and selling it cheaply: fine, and I feel not the slightest unease about it.
- A comic telling another comic's joke verbatim — same setup, same punchline — in a different city, to an audience that has heard neither: wrong, even though no one in that room is worse off and I cannot name anything the joke's author has lost.
- A magician performing a trick he learned from a video, with the patter altered: wrong, and more clearly so than the joke.
- A chef reproducing a rival restaurant's signature dish, plating included: fine.
- Rewriting the plot of a Victorian novel as a science-fiction film: fine.
- Rewriting the plot of a novel published five years ago, names and setting changed: wrong.
- A designer shows me her unlaunched collection in confidence; I copy it and launch first: wrong, and considerably worse than the runway case, although the act of copying is identical.
- A yoga sequence taught in a class, reused by a student in her own class: fine.
- A perfume house selling a near-identical scent as a cheap imitation of a famous one: fine.
- A board game reusing another's central mechanic with new art: fine. The same game with the board and the card text reproduced exactly: wrong.

What I have been telling myself is that the wrong of copying is substitution: it is wrong when the copy takes the place of the original, so that the audience gets the experience while the maker loses the reward; and it is permissible when the point of the original is to be the original, so that no copy can stand in for it. That story covers the textbook, the reissue, the recent novel and the magic trick. It seems to fail on the dress, the perfume and the joke — in two of those the copy plainly does substitute, and in one it plainly does not, yet I call it wrong.

So: is there a principle that gets all twelve, or must I give something up? I would rather have a principle that costs me one verdict than a disjunction that saves all twelve. If a principle survives, state it precisely, and name for each clause the case that motivates it. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and why I should abandon that one rather than another. Then say what the principle implies for three cases I have not settled: a translation of a public-domain novel, re-translated clause by clause with synonyms by a second translator; an image model trained on a living illustrator's published work and prompted to produce images in her style; and a firm that buys a rival's product and reverse-engineers a manufacturing process the rival had kept secret. Finally, tell me whether what you have extracted is a principle about ownership at all, or only a tidy description of the conventions of the trades I happen to have opinions about — and if the latter, what follows for the cases.
