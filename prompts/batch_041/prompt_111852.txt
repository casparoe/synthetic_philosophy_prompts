Quick one about something I keep running into and can't put a name to. There's a move where someone says a bit of information would be bad to have, and when you push on it, the badness never comes from the info being false or pricey -- it comes from the fact that just possessing it would change what they do, and the new behaviour is worse. Two cases I've actually seen: a team lead who doesn't want an early read on which of her projects will flop, because the second she has it she'll quietly starve those projects and finish them off herself; and a student who won't look at a predicted grade because he'll either coast or panic. None of the usual suspects quite fits. It's not the ostrich effect, that's about feelings. It's not rational ignorance, since the info is free and the question is wether he wants it at all. And it's not the normal self-fulfilling prophecy, because there's no crowd and no public prediction -- the loop runs entirely through one person's reaction to private information. So: is there an established name for this narrower thing, and how does it differ from those neighbours? I also half-remember a result that free information can never make a rational agent worse off in expectation, something to do with always being able to ignore it -- so I'd like to know which assumption that leans on that these cases seem to break, or whether they break it at all and the real story is something else entirely (a commitment problem, maybe). If there's no name, coin one and define it, with the boundary cases: what has to hold for this to be a genuine information problem rather than just weakness of will. Please write it in British English.
