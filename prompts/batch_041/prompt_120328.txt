ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past one ethics elective I barely passed, and my team keeps having the same dumb argument. We ship personalization stuff -- what order your feed goes in, what the notification copy says, which badge shows up, when we ask you for a rating. Half the team thinks we're just helping people find what they want. The other half thinks we're manipulating them. Nobody can say where the line actually is, so nothing gets resolved, we just ship.

I want to make the case in the format our team actually reads, which is the changelog. So: write a satirical changelog plus FAQ for the "Engagement Platform" at a made-up company, going through six or seven releases (v1.0 up to like v4.3), deadpan the whole way through, no winking, no "and that's why this is bad folks." The joke should be that you can watch a product slide from ordinary persuasion into manipulation one completely innocuous-sounding release note at a time, and a reader who has never read a word of philosophy should still be able to tell what's being made fun of by the end.

Its only funny if its accurate, so a few things it has to get right:

- the real distinction people draw between persuading someone (giving them reasons they can actually weigh) and manipulating them (getting the same result by going around their reasoning instead of through it)
- that "we're only showing them true things" and "we're only giving them what they already wanted" are not defenses, because you can manipulate perfectly well with true information by picking the frame, the order, the default, the moment
- that manipulation generally prefers to be invisible, plus the awkward fact that it can still work on someone who knows it's happening
- coercion vs manipulation: we are not holding anyone down, and the piece should be honest that this isn't the same thing as being fine
- the "it's for their own good" move. Some philosophers think manipulation is sometimes ok -- little kids, therapy, someone about to do something they'll regret -- and I dont want the satire to be smug "manipulation always bad." I want it to actually feel the pull of that defense and then keep going anyway
- consent: the user did technically agree, in a toggle that nobody has ever opened
- the same technique being called "good onboarding" in our standup and a "dark pattern" the day a regulator emails us

Include a Known Issues section and an FAQ with 4 or 5 entries. Somewhere in there put one quote from a made-up staff philosopher or ethics consultant who is clearly doing their honest best with a bad brief. 700-900 words. Don't invent citations and don't name real researchers or companies, just make up the company and the people.
