I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology on heuristic arguments. The topic is the presumption of independence: the default rule that licenses treating variables or events as independent unless there is a positive reason to think they are dependent. I want a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts the positions one might take on how this presumption should be formalized. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or references to particular papers; I want the logical space, not the literature. The reader will know basic probability and some Bayesian epistemology, but not the specialist literature, so keep every node answerable from the concepts themselves.

The tree should begin from three initial commitments: first, the locus of the presumption, that is, whether independence is taken to be a feature of the world, of our evidence, or of our credences; second, the force of the presumption, that is, whether it is a defeasible default, a prior probability, or a constitutive constraint; and third, the scope of the presumption, that is, whether it is global or local, synchronic or diachronic. Each branch node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf should name a position, or be marked unnamed if no one defends it, and carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Include leaves for a maximum-entropy formalization, a graphical-model formalization that treats missing edges as independence assumptions, a causal-structure formalization, a defeasible-default-logic formalization, a randomness-based formalization, and a reference-class skeptical position. Keep the tree to at most twelve leaves.

Where the tree yields combinations that no one occupies, say so and diagnose whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, flag them rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them.

The specific puzzle I want the tree to illuminate is this: the presumption of independence is supposed to be a heuristic, but any formalization of it seems to make a substantive claim, either about the world, or about the right prior, or about the right reference class, and that claim can be defeated by evidence in a way that the heuristic itself cannot anticipate. The tree should make clear where each position locates that substantive claim, and which positions can absorb the defeat without giving up the presumption. End with a short note on which branch, if any, can treat the presumption as a genuine heuristic rather than a disguised substantive commitment.
