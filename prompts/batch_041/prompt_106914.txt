I am writing a history essay on why industrialisation began where and when it did, and I keep meeting a pattern of explanation that I cannot name, so I am hoping that philosophy of social science has a term for it. The pattern is this: an outcome is explained by a value supposedly held by the people who produced it, while the only evidence offered for the value is the very outcome it is meant to explain. One account attributes a region's early factories to its culture of disciplined work and thrift, and the proof that such a culture existed is that the factories appeared. Another attributes a country's present prosperity to an entrepreneurial spirit, with the prosperity serving as the whole of the evidence for the spirit. Read more widely, the values seem to be selected after the fact to fit whatever happened: where growth occurred, thrift is praised; where it did not, the culture is called fatalistic; and elsewhere the same competitive restlessness is credited with success in one place and blamed for stagnation in another. Nothing appears able to count against the claim. I have also read the classic argument that a Protestant emphasis on disciplined labour and deferred reward helped early capitalism, and I cannot tell whether what I am noticing is that thesis or a criticism of it.

I suspect this is more than ordinary circular reasoning, but I cannot say how it differs from the idea that interpretation moves back and forth between a part and a whole, from the mistake of treating a summary of people's behaviour as a thing that causes that behaviour, or from the statistical worry that the direction of cause and effect has been reversed. Does the pattern have an established name? If it does, I would like to know what the nearest concepts are, how the described thing differs from each of them, and which fits best. If it does not, I would rather you coin a term and define it than leave me with a bare label. Either way, tell me where the edges are: what would count as a legitimate explanation of an outcome by a group's values, so that I can distinguish the two when I read.
