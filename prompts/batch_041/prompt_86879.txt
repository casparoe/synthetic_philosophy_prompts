I am a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through the second draft of a book set partly in a coastal town whose faith, work and speech are not mine, and for the past month I have been rereading Rorty in the evenings in the hope that his antirepresentationalism would tell me what I owe the people I am writing about. So far it has told me the opposite of what I wanted: that descriptions are not copies but tools, that truth is settled among describers rather than by correspondence to how things stand, and that the vocabulary at which a person's justifications simply run out — his final vocabulary — is contingent, held rather than compelled. This is bracing at four in the morning and useless at nine, when I have a scene to revise and a reader from that town to answer. What I need from you is the translation I cannot do for myself: turn Rorty's antirepresentationalism, together with his claim that solidarity is made rather than found, into a procedure I can actually run on a passage of my own prose, deciding when a passage is claiming to have got something right and when it is offering a redescription I merely hope will be useful, and what follows from the difference in each case.

To be concrete about the conditions: I revise for perhaps two hours most evenings, so I have about twenty minutes per passage and no philosophical training beyond having read a fair amount of it for pleasure. The decisions recur several times a sitting — every scene that depicts a practice, a place or a kind of person from outside my own experience raises them — and the stakes are not small: publication, the response of readers who live in that town, and my own sense of whether I have been honest. My editor has the final say on the text, and I pay a sensitivity reader to go through the whole manuscript once; the procedure should therefore tell me which questions I can settle alone, which are worth putting to the reader, and which are mine alone to decide because they are questions of value rather than of fact. Give me the procedure as an ordered set of questions, each with a note on what a sufficient answer looks like and what an evasive one looks like, marking the points where it stops with a no and the points where it must hand the decision to a person. Then run it on three passages from my book: the invented ritual in the town's chapel, which a reader from the town has told me is wrong but which I researched carefully and still believe is defensible; a third-person chapter that enters the mind of a man who has done something monstrous, which I think of as a redescription of cruelty but which I suspect no reader can avoid taking as a claim about how such men actually are; and a comic scene whose humour depends on the dialect I have given a minor character, where I genuinely cannot tell whether the joke is at his expense. I would rather you said plainly where the procedure misfires, and which of its steps are judgments dressed up as procedure, than that you smoothed the joins.

Begin with the procedure itself — the ordered questions in their finished form — in your first sentence or two, then give the reasoning behind it, then the three worked passages, then the caveats. No preamble, and no restating of what I have asked. Please write in British English.
