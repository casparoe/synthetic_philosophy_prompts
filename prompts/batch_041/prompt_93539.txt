I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, chiefly philosophy of probability and metaphysics. I have drafted the short essay below on the dispute between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. I would be grateful for your comments. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want to know whether the argument is sound, whether the central distinction is drawn correctly, whether the essay would be of any interest to someone who already knows this literature, and which parts are weakest.

Essay: Indexical Slack and the Presumptuous Philosopher

The self-sampling assumption (SSA) says that you should reason as if you are a random observer drawn from the set of all observers in your world. The self-indication assumption (SIA) says that you should weight a world by the number of observers it contains, so that the bare fact that you exist is evidence for worlds with more observers. The presumptuous philosopher is the character who uses SIA to infer, from his own existence, that a hypothesis predicting vastly many observers is vastly more likely than a hypothesis predicting few. The doomsday argument uses SSA to infer that we are likely near the end of human history. The two assumptions are usually presented as rival answers to a single question: how should indexical evidence enter credence?

I want to suggest that this framing is a mistake, and that the mistake generates the paradoxes. SSA and SIA are not rival answers to one question. They are answers to two different questions. SSA is a rule for distributing credence over positions within a world, given the world. SIA is a rule for distributing credence over worlds, given that you are an observer at all. The presumptuous philosopher does not make an error of indexical reasoning; he makes an error of prior reasoning. He treats the existence of an observer as a datum that can swamp any prior over world-types. But if one has a prior over world-types that is not indifferent to the number of observers, SIA can be tamed. The real dispute is not SSA versus SIA; it is about the prior over world-types.

To make this concrete, consider the two-stage model. Stage one: a prior over world-types, described without reference to any particular observer. Stage two: for each world-type, a conditional distribution over observer-moments within that world. SSA is the natural rule for stage two. SIA is not a rule for stage two at all; it is a rule for stage one that has been misdescribed. Once the two stages are separated, the presumptuous philosopher's inference is seen to depend on an assumption that the stage-one prior is uniform over observer-counts, or at least that it does not penalize large worlds. Nothing in the concept of an observer forces that assumption. A simplicity prior, or a prior that treats observer-count as a parameter to be estimated rather than a quantity to be maximized, can block the inference without denying any indexical datum.

But this two-stage model is not yet a solution. It relocates the problem to the stage-one prior. What could justify a prior over world-types? If we say simplicity, we need an account of simplicity that is not just a disguise for our preferences. If we say indifference, we are back to the presumptuous philosopher. The reference class problem returns at stage two: which observer-moments count as relevantly similar to mine? I want to propose that the missing ingredient is what I call the causal fingerprint. Your indexical evidence is not merely that you exist, or that some observer exists. It is that you exist with a particular set of observations, memories, and causal connections to the rest of the world. The presumptuous philosopher ignores his causal fingerprint. He treats himself as a bare observer, when in fact he is an observer with a specific history. Once the causal fingerprint is included, the reference class is narrowed, and the inference to a vast world loses much of its force. The doomsday argument may be similarly weakened: our causal fingerprint includes a particular position in a particular technological trajectory, not just the bare fact of being a human observer.

I am not certain this is right. It may be that the causal fingerprint is just the reference class problem under a new name, and that any attempt to specify it will either be arbitrary or will smuggle in the conclusion. It may also be that the two-stage model is incoherent, because there is no way to specify a world-type without already presupposing an observer perspective. I would like to know whether these worries are fatal, and whether the distinction between sampling and indication is as clean as I have made it.

3. Is the causal fingerprint a genuine constraint on indexical reasoning, or is it just the reference class problem?

5. Is there a better way to frame the central point that would make it more interesting to philosophers?
