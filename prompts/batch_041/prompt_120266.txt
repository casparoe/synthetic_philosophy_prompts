I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar that sits between the philosophy of biology and the philosophy of mind, and what I need is a specification rather than a survey. The subject is the idea of a free-floating rationale: the practice of explaining what a system does by citing the reason for it — the problem it solves, the end it serves — in cases where the system contains no representation of that reason and no designer put it there. I want the criteria that any adequate theory of this practice has to satisfy, not a list of the views that have been held about it.

Begin with a short section titled The target (~150 words) that fixes what is being theorised: state plainly what kind of claim an ascription of a free-floating rationale is, and what it would take for such a claim to be false. Treat the following as the cases the theory must accommodate, and use these rather than substituting cases you prefer:

1. A river, over decades, cuts a channel whose profile comes close to minimising the energy it dissipates. Nothing in the river represents a gradient, and nothing selected it for this.
2. A network trained by gradient descent converges on a policy that exploits a shortcut in its reward signal. The strategy has a rationale relative to the proxy, and none relative to the goal its trainer had in mind.
3. A colony's foraging traffic follows a pattern close to optimal for the distribution of food in its territory, though no individual ant represents either the distribution or the optimum.
4. A male's courtship display admits two incompatible rationales — one as an honest signal of quality, one as an exploitation of a pre-existing sensory bias — and the evidence does not settle between them.
5. A moth steers by keeping a distant light at a fixed angle to its body. This fails catastrophically near a flame: the rationale that governs its ordinary flight is present, and is also the reason it dies.
6. A trait's current utility differs from whatever it was selected for, and the two rationales cannot both be the reason the trait persists.

Then a section titled The desiderata (~700–900 words) giving the requirements, numbered, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and a sentence on what a theory that failed it would get wrong. I expect the list to cover: what makes an ascription true or false; whether ascription is a fact about the system or a stance the theorist takes; whether the theory must distinguish selection for a trait from the mere presence of a trait that happens to help; how it handles cases with no selection history at all (1 and 2); how it handles rival rationales that fit the same behaviour equally well (4); whether a rationale must be capable of being right or wrong for the system, and what failure looks like (5); whether reasons-talk must do work that plain causal description cannot, and if so what work; whether ascriptions come in degrees; and whether the theory must sit consistently alongside the rest of biology and physics, which contains no purposes. Add any requirement I have missed, and say why it belongs.

Then a section titled Genuine requirements, camp preferences (~200 words) separating the criteria that any theory must meet from the ones that only look compulsory because a particular tradition takes them for granted. Be specific about which tradition, and about what it is protecting.

Then a section titled Where the list cannot be satisfied together (~300 words). Identify any subsets of the desiderata that cannot be jointly met, explain the conflict in each case, and say which requirement you would give up and at what cost. This is the section I care most about; a clean list with no tensions in it is a failure.

Then a section titled The contenders, scored (~200 words) taking the two or three most natural positions one might hold here — for instance that these ascriptions are literal causal claims about selection, that they are a stance we adopt for prediction, that they are claims about the counterfactual structure of the system's responses, or that they are not claims at all but instruments — and saying briefly how each fares against the list. Keep this short; the list is the deliverable.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, and no appeals to what some author has argued. I want the criteria defended on the strength of the cases. If a requirement needs an authority behind it in order to stick, that is a sign it is not yet doing its work.
