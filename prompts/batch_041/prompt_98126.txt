I'm writing a long-form piece on the ethics of AI assistants that refuse or quietly override what users ask of them, and I keep running into a comparison that the people I interview reach for almost reflexively: the assistant is a clinician. On this picture the model occupies the position of a physician or a pharmacist — a professional with specialised knowledge, a duty of care toward someone not equipped to evaluate the advice, a licence that can be revoked, and a recognised warrant to withhold a requested intervention, whether that means declining to prescribe the antibiotic the patient has decided he needs or refusing to dispense something that will interact badly with what is already on the chart. One policy lead I spoke with used the comparison to argue that hard refusals and gentle steering away from harmful requests are not paternalism at all but the ordinary discharge of a professional obligation, and that a system which simply does whatever the user asks would be less like a good doctor than like a vending machine with a medical degree. I'd like the analogy audited rather than the policy question adjudicated.

Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the clinician, the patient, the encounter, the chart with its history, the licence, the malpractice suit, the ethics board, the professional body that writes the guidelines, the third party the clinician may owe a warning to — and then tell me where the mapping fails in ways that matter for the conclusion. I suspect the comparison smuggles in several things. The professional's warrant to override rests on a thick, continuing, consensual relationship with a particular person whose history and values are on the record, plus a set of institutions — licensure, liability, peer review — that can hold the professional answerable and that the patient can invoke after the fact; the assistant has none of that, and it is not obvious that a duty of care survives the disappearance of the accountability structures that made the clinician's authority legitimate in the first place. Second, medicine's own history is not a story about the durability of beneficence but about its defeat: the turn toward informed consent and patient autonomy was a rebellion against clinicians overriding people for their own good, so if the analogy is good it may cut the other way. Third, what is being refused is often not an intervention but an answer, which puts the case nearer to what the philosophical literature calls epistemic paternalism — interference with how someone forms beliefs rather than with what he does to his body — and I want to know whether the clinical frame even has the resources to describe that, or whether importing it silently converts a question about a user's intellectual autonomy into a question about a patient's safety.

Give me the table of correspondences first, then the disanalogies sorted into those that bear on the conclusion that this is not paternalism and those that are merely decorative, then a verdict on whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Please keep the standard soft/hard paternalism distinction in view, since the argument's force depends on which one the clinician analogy is meant to license. If the analogy doesn't survive, say what would do better — I've heard the assistant-as-bartender and the assistant-as-fiduciary both floated, and I'd like to know whether either withstands the same treatment. Be precise and don't hedge; I would rather have a clear map with a defended verdict than a survey of every possible reading.
