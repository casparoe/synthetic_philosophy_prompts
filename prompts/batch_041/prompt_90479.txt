Two of my friends got into it at a neighborhood meeting last week and I've been chewing on it since. The city wants to close a four-block stretch of street to cars and turn it into a park. It'd mildly annoy a few thousand drivers -- a couple of minutes each on the detour -- and hugely improve daily life for the few hundred people who live on the stretch. The council's own report puts a 'wellbeing score' on both sides, adds them up, and declares the park a net win.

Mo thinks the report is basically fine. His line: you can put a number on what a thing is worth to someone, either what they'd pay for it or how much happier it makes them, add it all up, and if the total comes out positive you do it. Thats all 'wellbeing' means, he says, a way of adding up.

Rina says there's no fact of the matter about whether his mild annoyance and my big delight sit on the same ruler. And willingness to pay isn't a measure of how much you care, she says, it's a measure of how much you can afford -- if the drivers are richer than the residents, the report is just reporting bank balances.

Mo says fine, drop money: ask everyone to rate their happiness out of 10 and average it, that's a ruler.

Rina: someone who's had a rotten life can still report a 7, because they've stopped expecting better and the scale quietly recalibrates. And even taking the numbers at face value, her 7 and his 7 aren't the same thing -- your assuming a common zero and a common unit that nobody's shown exists. You can compare one person to herself over time (she's better off than she was) without being able to compare her to him (she's better off than he is), and the park plan needs the second kind.

Mo: then we can't make any policy at all. Every road, every tax, every hospital bed is an interpersonal comparison. Saying we can't compare doesn't stop the city acting, it just hands the decision to whoever shouts loudest, and the people who like the status quo never have to defend it. Refusing to compare is itself a comparison -- it says the current distribution wins.

Rina: I don't deny we have to decide. I deny that deciding makes the comparison true. When we decide anyway we're applying a convention, a political choice about whose pain counts and how much, and dressing it up as 'wellbeing' hides the choice. The council isn't measuring, its voting with arithmetic.

I couldn't referee it and I still can't. Go through it move by move and rate each one from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who's right about what, and tell me what both of them are missing.
