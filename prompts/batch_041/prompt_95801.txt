I'm giving a shiur at my shul in three weeks: adult ed, about thirty people, mostly Modern Orthodox professionals in their forties and fifties who take it for granted that the Torah is divine and eternal, plus our rabbi -- yeshiva trained, knows the Guide of the Perplexed backwards, but suspicious of the whole rationalist project and thinks it flattens the mitzvos into utility -- plus one retired professor of Bible in the back row who knows the Ancient Near Eastern cultic material cold and has no theological dog in the fight, and a young couple who are both vegetarians and keep asking why God wanted animals killed in the first place.

My thesis is about Maimonides' account of the sacrificial laws, the one where he says they were given because the Israelites had grown up among idolaters and were used to worshipping that way. That reading usually gets treated as the embarrassing bit of the Guide. I want to argue it's the strongest bit, but only under one of two ways of taking it, and that Maimonides' own text wobbles between them. On the weakness reading, the sacrifices are a crutch God handed to a primitive people because they couldn't take anything better, something He'd have prefered to skip -- and then the eternity of the commandments really is in trouble. On the pedagogy reading, the sacrificial cult is the fully adequate form of service for a people at that stage of understanding, no less rational than prayer, and every detail of it has a point. I'll argue that the second is the only reading Maimonides can consistently hold, and that the sheer specificity of the cult -- which animals, which parts, which days -- is evidence for it, since a crutch doesn't need to be specified down to the last detail.
