I am a machine-learning engineer who has spent the last decade building systems that manipulate symbols, and I have recently begun sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the university in my city. Last month, during an internal demo, a colleague asked me in front of the whole team whether the model I had just presented knew what it was talking about. I gave a flippant answer, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. I want to work out where I actually stand on the symbol grounding problem — and I want to do that by discovering which position my own commitments commit me to, rather than by picking whichever label sounds most flattering.

Please act as a philosopher of cognitive science with a specialist's command of the symbol grounding problem and of the various attempts to solve, relocate, or dissolve it. Think carefully before you write. Begin by working out for yourself the logical space of possible answers: what the axes of disagreement actually are, and which combinations of answers are even coherent. Only then place named positions in that space. Do not write a survey essay; I am not interested in a literature review, and I want no citations, reading lists, or attributions to particular authors. State each position in its own right, as a claim someone could actually hold.

The deliverable is a decision tree: a classification instrument that takes a reader from a handful of commitments they can state about concrete cases to a single named position on what it would take for the internal states of a symbol-manipulating system to mean anything at all. It should be usable as a handout for a mixed group of philosophers and engineers, so wherever a term of art is unavoidable, define it in a clause the first time it appears. Keep it to at most ten branch nodes and at most ten leaves.

Requirements:

- Every branch node must be a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Phrase each one as a concrete case or a flat yes/no claim about one, never as "do you accept the embodiment thesis?" The cases must include at least the following: the internal variable in a numerical weather model that reliably tracks sea-surface temperature; a chess engine's representation of a queen; a dictionary that defines its words only in terms of other words in the same dictionary; a system trained only on text written by people who could see, which then uses colour words correctly; a blind person's competent use of colour words; and a robot whose symbols are wired to transducers and effectors but which has never been rewarded, punished, or selected for anything.
- Each leaf must carry three things: the name of the position, one sentence stating the commitment that lands you there, and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.
- The following positions must appear somewhere as leaves, though you may add others: that grounding requires sensorimotor coupling to the world; that grounding requires some causal or informational connection to the world, but not specifically a sensorimotor one; that grounding is unnecessary because meaning is inferential role; that grounding is a matter of degree and a property of whole systems rather than of individual symbols; that a system can be grounded derivatively, by its place in a community of already-grounded speakers; that grounding depends on a symbol's history — its selection, training, or function — rather than on its present connections; that the symbol/non-symbol distinction is the real error, so the problem dissolves once we stop drawing it; and that no arrangement of form alone can ever yield meaning.
- Tell me which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and why. Do not paper over the gap by adding a question that only works if the reader already knows the answer.
- Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, say so, and say briefly whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to explore it.
- Finally, tell me which single question in the tree does the most sorting work, and why.
