I am a philosopher of the social sciences, and next month I am giving a colloquium paper to the archaeology department at my own university. I want you to prepare my question-and-answer sheet for the discussion period. Do not write the paper, do not defend the thesis on my behalf, and do not give me citations or reading lists — I know the literature in my own field and I have read theirs. What I need is a prediction of the room.

Here is the thesis, so you can see what the objections will be aiming at. I argue that "collapse" is not a descriptive category at all but an observer-relative judgment wearing descriptive clothing: it names a disappointment, not a process. What the canonical cases actually exhibit is the loss of one specific capacity — the ability to sustain institutions that outlive the individuals who staff them — and that loss is neither abrupt nor total. I therefore propose replacing the noun "collapse" with a two-place relation: a society contracts relative to a baseline of institutional scope, where the baseline is chosen by the observer rather than found in the ground. Once the relation is made explicit, the live questions become historiographic and normative rather than empirical — who picks the baseline, and whose interests the collapse framing serves. I work this through three canonical cases, each illustrating a different failure mode of the narrative: one where the "collapse" is an artifact of textualist bias, so that we mourn the palaces that wrote and not the villages that did not; one where it is an artifact of treating monumentality as a proxy for complexity; and one where it is a colonial morality tale projected onto an island.

The audience matters more than the thesis here, so let me describe it exactly. The department is mostly Mesoamericanists who work on the Terminal Classic and its aftermath, and several of them have spent entire careers arguing against the word "collapse" in exactly this register. There are two Aegean prehistorians who work on the Late Bronze Age and who, by contrast, are likely to think collapse is a real and well-evidenced phenomenon that my reframing softens. There is one archaeologist who does quantitative demography and is openly impatient with theory of any kind. And there are a handful of graduate students who will ask the questions everyone else is too senior to ask. The culture of this department is that outsiders — and a philosopher is an outsider — are tolerated if they are useful and resented if they seem to be reinventing a wheel that was already turning before they arrived. They care a great deal about descendant communities and about continuity, and they are suspicious of anyone whose clever reframing might, as they would put it, aestheticize other people's catastrophes.

Give me the prep sheet as a table with exactly these columns, one row per objection, ordered by how dangerous each is to me overall — likelihood multiplied by damage, not either alone:

- Rank (integer, 1 = most dangerous)
- Objection (in the precise words this audience would use, not a neutral paraphrase)
- Who raises it (which subgroup, and why it is they and not someone else who says it)
- Likelihood (High / Medium / Low)
- Damage (High / Medium / Low, plus one clause naming what it threatens — my thesis, my method, or merely my standing in the room)
- Concede (what is genuinely right in the objection, stated without hedging)
- Hold (what I must not give up, and why giving it up would cost me the paper)
- Reply (the two-minute spoken answer I would actually give, in the first person, in the vocabulary of the person who asked)
- Soft spot (the weakest point in that reply, stated plainly — I would rather know now than discover it in the room)

Aim for the eight to ten objections I will really get, not the ones I would get from philosophers. After the table, add one short section: the question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is the one that would actually hurt, and a sketch of what I would say if it comes anyway — including, if the honest answer is that I have no good answer, that I have no good answer.
