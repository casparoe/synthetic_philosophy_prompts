I'm in this awkward spot where I've read enough philosophy of biology to have opinions about the units of selection debate but not enough to know whether my opinions hang together, so I want to dump my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and have you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. Here's what I think. A gene that makes its bearer have more kids: that's selection, sure, and I genuinely don't care whether we call the gene or the organism the unit, nothing seems to ride on it. A gene that spreads through a population while making its bearers worse off — the classic meiotic drive / segregation distorter sort of case — I want to say that's selection acting on the gene, full stop, and the organism is basically a vehicle it's driving around. Sterile workers in a social insect colony: individual fitness is zero, the colony thrives, and I'm fine saying selection is acting at the level of the colony here, or at least at the level of the family, not just on the genes inside each worker. Somatic cells in a tumour, dividing when they shouldn't, killing the body that hosts them: I want to say that's not really selection, or if it is it's a degenerate case, and I get suspicious when people call it selection at the level of the cell. A heritable trait that's good for the species but lowers the fitness of every individual that has it: I think that's almost always a confused way of talking and I'd rather deny the thing exists. And then the one that actually keeps me up — human groups with norms that punish free-riders, which seem to outcompete groups that lack them: I find myself wanting to say yes, that's group selection, cultural group selection, it's real. And I notice I have no principled reason for allowing it there and not in the tumour case except that I like the conclusion.

So: is there a principle that gets all six, or close? I suspect the honest answer is that my gene verdict and my group verdict pull in the same direction, and my tumour verdict and my good-of-the-species verdict pull against them, so I can't have it both ways — I either admit cell-level and group-level selection as the same kind of thing as gene-level selection, or I demote the gene one too and say it's all bookkeeping about fitness effects with no fact of the matter about levels. Tell me which, and dont be gentle about which of my verdicts has to go.

Once you've given me the principle, run it on a couple of cases I haven't listed — a transposable element that copies itself around the genome at a cost to its host, say, and a behaviour that spreads because people imitate it rather than because it helps anyone survive. And flag anywhere I'm quietly smuggling in a criterion I haven't noticed I'm using, like replicator vs. beneficiary vs. real causal level, since I have a feeling that's where the inconsistency is actually coming from. I'd rather have one clean principle with a verdict I have to swallow than a complicated one that saves everything I said.
