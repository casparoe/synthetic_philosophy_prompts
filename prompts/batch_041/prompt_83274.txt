I'm a TA on a joint medical-ethics/philosophy seminar — final-year undergrads plus a few MA students sitting in — and I've been handed the take-home final to write. The module is nominally about autonomy and informed consent, but the part I actually teach is the messier question of whether a person's earlier, competent self has any authority over her later, incompetent one: advance directives, Ulysses contracts, all of that. I have to produce one essay question with a model answer and a marking scheme, and I'm thoroughly sick of questions that can be answered by writing out the standard precedent-autonomy-versus-present-welfare pair of moves and then shrugging. My students can do that in their sleep, and it tells me nothing. I want a question where that pair of moves is the *opening* of an answer rather than the whole thing. Level is a two-hour in-class final, so assume they can write maybe 900 words under pressure; the model answer should be about 700.

Here's the case I want the question built around, and please keep all of it inside the question, since they obviously can't look anything up. Margaret is 62, a former nurse, and five years ago, shortly after a diagnosis of early-onset Alzheimer's, she signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics for life-threatening infection, refusing artificial nutrition and hydration, and declining resuscitation, naming her younger brother as proxy. It was properly witnessed and by all accounts she was competent and deliberate when she wrote it. She also insisted on adding a clause of her own composition, which the solicitor kept: in substance, that if she ever seemed content and cheerful in the care home, that contentment was not to be trusted, because by then the disease would have made her someone who no longer knew what she was losing — in her own words, 'if I'm smiling, don't believe me'. Five years on she is severely demented, doesn't recognize the document or her brother, scores near the floor on cognitive testing, and by every observable measure is happy: sings at the piano, has favourites among the staff, says she wants to live. She now has a chest infection. The brother says the directive governs, discounting clause included. The daughter says the clause is grotesque and self-defeating — her mother is *right there*, content, and the document is being used to overwrite the only person still able to speak. I also want a deliberate asymmetry in there so they can't run one template: have the directive *also* request that she be kept alive as long as she isn't in pain, in case she changed her mind about the antibiotics — so she forbade treatment and requested it in the same document. Let them work out what to do with that.

Two further things I want the question to force, and for the second I'm asking you to introduce a distinction that I don't believe exists under these names in the literature, so they have to actually work with it rather than recall it: the question should distinguish a directive's *authorship authority* (it binds because she made it — the maker's hand) from its *fidelity authority* (it binds because it accurately represents what the person she now is would want, or would have wanted had she known). I want them to say whether those two come apart here, which one the brother is really invoking, and whether the discounting clause is an exercise of authorship authority or an attempt to gut fidelity authority from the inside. Second, I want them to say whether 'who is the patient?' is answerable in general or only case by case, and whether the daughter's line — that the document is the place where her mother survives — is a claim about identity or a claim about grief wearing identity's clothes. So: the question itself, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 with five named must-hit moves, each with a one-line note on how a B answer typically blows it. Please make the top-band/B-band split turn on whether they handle the discounting clause at all, because that's the bit I think separates them.
