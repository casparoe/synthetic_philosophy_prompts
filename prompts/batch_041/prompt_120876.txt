I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political philosophy, and my dissertation defends a version of luck egalitarianism that I've been trying to state precisely enough that it can actually lose. Here is the version. What egalitarian justice is sensitive to is not the brute/option luck distinction as it is ordinarily drawn, and not the choice between resources and welfare as the currency of equality, but a comparison between a person's actual condition and the condition she would have occupied under a counterfactual in which her exercises of choice are held fixed while her unchosen circumstances are varied — call it the choice-fixed baseline. A disadvantage is unjust exactly to the extent that the person falls below her own baseline, and the size of the required correction is the size of that gap. I add one refinement: avoidability comes in degrees, so the correction should be proportional to how difficult the choice was to avoid rather than switched on or off by whether it was voluntary at all.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this — what would have to be true for it to be false. Not the familiar objections in the abstract; I know the shape of the abandonment objection and the shameful-revelation objection, and I can recite them without being moved by them. What I need is the exposure: which cases, which structural or formal results, and which facts about how preferences, capacities, and choices are actually formed would tell against the choice-fixed baseline in particular, sorted by how decisive they would be, with the merely awkward kept separate from the potentially fatal. Press especially on two things. First, whether the baseline is well-defined at all when a person's capacity to choose, her preferences, and her circumstances are not independent of one another — if her choices are partly constituted by the very circumstances I am supposed to hold fixed or vary, does the counterfactual survive, and if it does not, is the view refuted or merely under-described? Second, whether the scalar avoidability clause makes the position unfalsifiable by allowing any counterexample to be absorbed as a matter of degree, and if so, what it would take to stop that absorption without giving up the scalar.

Close with an honest assessment of how exposed the view is: what a committed defender of it would herself concede counts against her, which escape routes she would take and at what cost, and whether the qualifications I have built in have insulated the position from evidence rather than exposed it to more of it. Then, as the last line, give me the deliverable in fixed form: a line beginning Verdict: followed by no more than twenty words naming the single defeater I should treat as decisive, or the words none: insulated if you conclude that nothing on your list should move someone who already holds the view.
