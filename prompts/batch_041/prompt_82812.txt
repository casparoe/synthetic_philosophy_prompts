I want your credence in a claim about rational preference — call it fanaticism — together with the full derivation of that number. Here is the claim, stated as precisely as I can manage.

Fix a preference relation over risky prospects. Fanaticism is the thesis that this relation is not Archimedean: there exist outcomes L, M and W with L ≺ M ≺ W such that for every ε > 0, however small, the prospect yielding W with probability ε and L otherwise is rationally preferred to the sure prospect M. Read colloquially: no guaranteed middling good, however large, can outweigh any positive chance, however small, at a good that is sufficiently greater. Call this thesis F.

I want the question posed over a deliberately awkward case, because the clean version lets everyone retreat to their favourite axiom. Take W to be the flourishing of an enormous number of future people, and take the chance of securing it to be not merely small but model-dependent: three defensible ways of estimating it yield roughly 10⁻¹², roughly 10⁻⁶⁰, and no determinate value at all, on the grounds that the relevant measure over possible futures fails to be normalizable. Part of what I am asking, then, is whether F is even well formed when ε is not a determinate real number, or whether it silently presupposes a precision our estimates cannot supply.

What I want from you is not a survey of what has been said about this — I have no way to check references, and neither do you, so argue it out from the inside rather than gesturing at positions. I want a number, not a shrug about axioms. Cover at least the following sub-questions, and add any term you think belongs:

1. Whether the Archimedean or continuity axiom is a genuine requirement of rationality, or a mathematical convenience we are free to drop once we see what it costs.
2. Whether F can be given a coherent, decision-guiding representation at all — lexicographic orderings, infinitesimal-valued utilities, or whatever else — or whether it collapses into a formalism that cannot rank anything.
3. Whether arbitrarily small positive credences are rationally available in the first place: whether regularity is required, whether infinitesimals are legitimate credences, and whether a credence of 10⁻⁶⁰ is a credence at all or merely a placeholder for ignorance.
4. Whether the value scale is unbounded above in the way F needs, and whether unboundedness survives once we admit outcomes of infinite or undefined value.
5. Whether F, if true, has any practical bite — whether it obliges anyone to act, or whether it is a formal truth that leaves actual deliberation untouched.
