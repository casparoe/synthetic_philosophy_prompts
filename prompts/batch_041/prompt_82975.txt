I want a credence here, not a verdict, and I want the reasoning laid out rather than summarized.

Start from the strategy-stealing argument in combinatorial game theory. In any symmetric two-player game with no draws and no zugzwang — that is, one in which an extra move can never be a disadvantage to the player who has it — the second player cannot have a strategy that guarantees a win: the first player can make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter play the second player's supposed winning strategy, treating the opening move as a discard. The argument is non-constructive; it establishes that a winning strategy exists without identifying it. It fails precisely where the extra-move premise fails, as in chess, where zugzwang is possible; give chess a legal pass and the same reasoning shows that the first player can at least draw.

Now transplant the schema to argumentation. Consider the class of two-party adversarial argumentative formats — a formal debate, an appellate oral argument, a two-party negotiation over a fixed list of issues — and take the following as the target claim. In any format in that class whose rules are symmetric between the parties (identical repertoire of moves, payoffs invariant under swapping the parties) and in which asserting an additional proposition is never strategically worse for the assertor than saying nothing, the party that speaks second cannot have a strategy that guarantees victory; and consequently, wherever an opener appears to be structurally disadvantaged, the appearance is attributable to an asymmetry in the rules rather than to the order of speaking.

What is your credence in that claim? Give me the number and the decomposition: the probability that any format we actually have is symmetric in the relevant sense, given that debate roles typically differ in burden, the affirmative having to defend while the negative need only obstruct; the probability that additional assertion is ever costless, given the commitment it creates, the burdens it imposes on the assertor, the target it offers the other side, and doctrines of waiver and estoppel — the failure mode I would call argumentative zugzwang; the probability that the conclusion follows from the two premises, which I expect to be near one but want priced separately; the probability that, where a symmetric format does exist, an apparent first-mover disadvantage is explained by rule asymmetry rather than by the guaranteed-win notion being nearly empty once the players are bounded and the argument tree unbounded; and any further term you think belongs, each with one line of justification. Then give me the combination, name the term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what you would have to see — an example, an argument, a formal result — to move the number most.
