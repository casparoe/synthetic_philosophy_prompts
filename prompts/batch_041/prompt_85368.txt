I teach a 400-level seminar on pleasure and pain, and every single year some student asks me why the question "what do all pleasures have in common?" isn't just answered by "they all feel good," and every year I do a bad job of showing them why that answer is in trouble. I want a short story that makes the trouble vivid instead of settling it.

Use this setup, roughly. A mid-career researcher, call her Dana, has spent nine years building what she calls a hedonic integration device — a headset that outputs one number for how pleasant the wearer's current experience is, from -10 to +10. The number has to be comparable across people and across kinds of pleasure, because the money depends on a clinical use: dosing decisions for patients who can't self-report. So in calibration she runs subjects through a battery. A long bath. A sugar hit. The relief when a headache finally lifts. An orgasm. Finally finishing a proof that took three weeks. A sad song the subject says she'd never skip. A mother watching her kid sleep. Schadenfreude at a rival's failure. Scratching a mosquito bite until it bleeds. The anticipatory buzz before a first date.

Everything goes wrong in an instructive way. The numbers are stable within a kind and refuse to line up across kinds. Subjects who give the bath a 6 and the proof a 6 insist the two are nothing alike and that the scale is doing something violent to them. The headache subject rates the relief a 9 and says there's no positive experience there at all, only the absence of one. The schadenfreude subject reports high pleasure and also reports not wanting to have it. And the one subject on opioids reports pure pleasure with no object, no content, nothing it's a pleasure *in*, which breaks the device's model completely.

So at the level of plot the story is about a scientist deciding what to put in her final report. At the level of ideas it's about the heterogeneity problem. Let Dana actually try the two textbook moves and show where each one strands her. The sensation view: pleasure is a distinctive felt quality, a quale, shared by every pleasant experience. The attitudinal view: what unifies pleasures isn't a felt quality at all but a pro-attitude, a liking, directed at the experience. Show what each one buys her with the data above and where each one fails. The relief case and the opioid case should be doing real work here, and so should the sad-song case, which looks like a pleasure taken in something the subject would describe as painful. If a hybrid view, or a deflationary "there's no one thing and that's fine" answer, starts looking better to Dana by the end, let her get there honestly, out of the data, not by announcement.

I'd rather you not have her solve it. What I want is the scene where she realizes the device works — it predicts behavior, it tracks the reports, the numbers are definately real — and still can't answer the question the project was named after. End somewhere in that vicinity, with her writing the sentence that says the instrument measures something and that "pleasant" may not be one thing.

Under about 2,500 words, one narrator, and no lectures from the narrator: the philosophy should come out of the subjects and the readings, not out of an essay dropped into the middle. I don't care whether the neuroscience is accurate. I do care that the pleasures feel seperate and specific and unlike each other, because that's the entire point.
