Hey — fourth-year PhD student here (political theory, though half my committee are intellectual historians, so my life is a mess). I'm running the session on 'Western values' in our grad workshop next month and I want to hand out a decision tree instead of a reading list, because the discussion always collapses the same way: one person says obviously the category is constructed, next question, another says obviously there are Western values, they're called the rule of law and free speech, and then everyone talks past each other for ninety minutes. I want an instrument that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which slogan they're loyal to.

So: build me a tree from the logical space up, not from a list of -isms. Every node has to be a question someone could answer without already knowing which leaf it drops them on — so no node that just asks 'do you think Western values are real?' Leaves get a name where there's a recognizable one and 'unnamed' where there isn't, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Say 10-12 leaves; I'd rather have four dense branches than twenty hairline ones.

Nodes I'm fairly sure I want — add what I'm missing and tell me if any two are really the same question:

- Where does the phrase get its content: canonical texts, actual historical practice, present-day institutions, or current public opinion — and which one wins when those come apart?
- Does 'Western' mean originated here, uniquely held here, or best realized here? I think people slide between these three constantly.
- When two societies use the same value word for incompatible practices (freedom as non-interference vs. freedom as collective self-rule), is one of them wrong about the value, or is the value just the word?
- Is the extension fixed by exemplars — point at a few societies and generalize — or by a definition you could state without pointing at anything?
- Is a purely contrastive definition acceptable, i.e. the West is whatever the current Other isn't?
- Who has the authority to settle what counts? Is that question empirical, stipulative, or essentially contested?
- Is the phrase doing descriptive work or argumentative work in the sentence where it appears?

Cases that have to appear somewhere in the tree, since these are the ones that actually split the room:

- A liberal state running an empire while proclaiming universal rights: are the values absent, aspirational, or betrayed?
- A society that adopts a Western technique (constitutional review, say) but justifies it in local terms: has it adopted a Western value?
- A society that loudly rejects 'Western values' — is the rejection itself an instance of one? (A colleague keeps claiming this and I can't tell if it's deep or just unfalsifiable, so the tree needs to place him.)
- A global rights document drafted with heavy non-Western participation: Western or not, and does that turn on the idiom or on the authors?
- A constitution guaranteeing religious freedom, read two ways: one citizen calls that a Western value, another calls it a Christian-secular compromise. What would settle it, and what does someone's answer to that reveal?

Leaves that must appear (collapse any that turn out identical, but tell me when you do):

- substantive core view (there's an enumerable content)
- family-resemblance view (real, but no essence)
- genealogical view (unity is historical lineage, not a list)
- paradigm-case / institutional view (extension fixed by exemplars)
- purely contrastive view
- performative view (the phrase doesn't denote, it does a job in an argument)
- aspirational view (the values are a commitment about what the West ought to be, not a description)
- agonistic view (the West names a site of conflict among incompatible values, not a consensus)
- proprietary-fiction view (values real, label is a claim of ownership)
- abolitionist view (drop the phrase)

Finally: tell me which answer-combinations the tree leaves empty, and for each one whether you think it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree can't actually seperate rather than fudging it, and if one of my required leaves turns out to be an answer to a different question than the rest, don't force it onto the same tree — say so and tell me where it hangs instead. And no reading list please, I have plenty of those, I need the structure.
