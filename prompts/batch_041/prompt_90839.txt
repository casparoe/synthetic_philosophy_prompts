EXAMINATION QUESTION

(a) State John Stuart Mill's harm principle as he sets it out in On Liberty, and explain the work that the word harm does within it. In particular, explain how Mill's notion of harm relates to, and differs from, mere offense.

(b) Mill's example of the corn-dealer is often read as an early statement of the idea that the circumstances of an utterance, and not only its content, determine whether it may be restricted. Explain the example and say what Mill takes it to establish.

(c) Consider a case Mill did not discuss. A private individual publishes a message urging violence against a named public official; in all likelihood the message would have been seen by almost no one. A social-media platform's recommendation system then distributes it to several million users, and the platform's own internal research indicates that this has made a serious risk of violence substantially more likely. During the term we drew a distinction between a speech act and the distribution of that speech act. Does Mill's harm principle permit restricting the platform's amplification of the message, as distinct from the message? Defend your answer.

CANDIDATE'S SCRIPT

(a) Mill's harm principle holds that the only end for which society may rightfully exercise power over an individual, against that individual's will, is to prevent harm to others; the individual's own good, whether physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant. This is the ground of Mill's opposition to paternalism: the state may not restrain my conduct merely because it would be better for me. The word harm does two jobs at once. First, it sets a threshold. To be harmed is to have one's interests set back — one's security, one's property, one's ability to carry out one's plans — and that is more than being offended, disliked, or disapproved of. Mill is emphatic that the mere fact that others find an opinion or a practice distasteful does not license interference; if it did, the principle would protect nothing, since every dissenting view offends somebody. Second, harm to others is the only permissible ground of interference, so the principle states a necessary condition on legitimate coercion rather than a sufficient one; Mill does not hold that every act which harms someone may be prohibited. The principle is grounded in a natural right against interference — the right of each person to think and act for himself — and it is this right, rather than any balance of consequences, that does the real work in Mill's argument.

(b) The example concerns the claim that corn-dealers are starving the poor. Circulated in the press, such an opinion must be tolerated, however inflammatory it is. But if the same words are delivered orally to an excited mob assembled in front of a corn-dealer's house, they may justly be punished. The point is that one and the same proposition can be an exercise of opinion in one setting and an incitement to violence in another; what determines whether restriction is permissible is the circumstances of the utterance, not the content of the thought. Mill's own formulation of the criterion is the clear and present danger test: expression may be restricted when it creates a clear and present danger of the harm it advocates. This anticipates the modern law of incitement and shows that Mill is no absolutist about speech.

(c) The platform's amplification is a different act from the original publication, and Mill's principle bears on it directly. The amplification does not merely offend; it materially raises the probability of violence against a named person, and that is a setback to his interests of just the kind Mill calls harm. Nothing in the principle turns on whether the harmful act is done by the state or by a private party. On the contrary, Mill's central worry in On Liberty is social tyranny, the pressure of prevailing opinion, and he treats informal social power as no less capable of violating the principle than law is. I conclude that the harm principle permits restricting the amplification. It does not follow that restriction is required, or that it should take the form of suppressing the message; the least restrictive remedy consistent with preventing the harm is what Mill's larger utilitarian framework would demand.
