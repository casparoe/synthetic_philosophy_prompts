Bit of an odd request, but here's what I'm after. I'm a data engineer — my days are lineage and provenance on a pretty grubby data lake: schemas, ETL, deduping, entity resolution on records that are supposed to be about the same thing, deciding which of two conflicting rows to trust, tracking who copied what from whom and how many hops back, and occasionally dealing with an upstream team that has an incentive to make the numbers look good. Stats is decent (Bayes, correlated errors, missingness not at random). History is basically zero.

So I've gotten weirdly into how anyone knows anything about the distant past — the Herodotus and Thucydides thing, and then the whole discipline of source criticism that built up over the centuries after them. What I want from you is a translation job: take the historian's method and restate it in the vocabulary I actually use. How they establish that a source is what it claims to be. How they establish whether it's any good on a particular question. How they weigh two sources that flatly disagree. What they do with the fact that everything we have is just whatever happened to get copied and kept. And the trick where they squeeze a fact out of an author who had no idea he was being used as evidence. Put all of that into my terms: sensors and their biases, channels and their error rates, provenance graphs, chain of custody, copying as a lossy transformation with its own characteristic failure modes, independent corroboration vs correlated failure, selection and survivorship in what made it into the archive at all, priors and base rates, adversarial vs merely noisy sources. Plus whatever they have names for that I don't have a slot for yet.

A few of their rules of thumb I've heard and don't know what to do with, so work these in explicitly: that the weirder of two disagreeing readings is usually the older one, because copiers smooth things out; that a detail which embarrasses the author's own side probably wasn't invented by them; and that being early doesn't beat being independent, since an early author who's just parroting an even earlier one is worth less than a late author who's genuinely separate. I don't know if those are method or folklore, so tell me which in my terms.

Two constraints. First, a friend of mine who does early modern stuff keeps insisting that reliability isn't a property of a source, it's a property of a source-question pair — a guy can be a flawless witness to the price of grain and a total fantasist about who slept with whom, and you have to score him separately on each question. Build that in, because I suspect it's the thing my vocabulary most naturally gets wrong. Second, don't be nice to my vocabulary. If something goes through clean, fine, but I want the residue report: which parts translate exactly, which only translate if you distort them (and say what the distortion is), and which parts have no counterpart at all in how I think about data.

Then the part I actually care about. For the stuff that doesn't translate — is that a defect in my vocabulary, something I should be tracking and currently aren't? Or is it a sign the historians are doing something vaguer than they let on, dressing up judgement calls as method? My hunch is it's a mix and I want to know which is which. And don't cheat by doing the translation backwards — i.e. don't assume that because I'd model a scribe as a noisy channel, a noisy channel is what they think he is. If they mean something else, say so and say what.

No citations or reading lists please, I'm not going to go track down the books, and anyway I'd rather have your reconstruction than a set of pointers. Long is fine.
