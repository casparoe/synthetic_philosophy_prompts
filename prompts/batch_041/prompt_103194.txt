I am a high school senior who has taken AP Statistics, spent two years being scored, ranked, and re-scaled by standardized tests, and absorbed more than is healthy about how the SAT and ACT are equated and normed. My working vocabulary is scaled scores, percentile ranks, z-scores, raw-to-scale conversions, norming samples, and grading on a curve. I understand the difference between a bare ordinal ranking and a score whose differences carry meaning, and I understand that a scaled score is only meaningful relative to the population and the conversion rule that produced it. I have no formal philosophy training beyond a unit on utilitarianism, but I can follow a careful argument.

I would like you to restate the philosophical debate over interpersonal comparisons of utility in this vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report what did not survive the translation.

For the restatement: take the main positions on whether the well-being or utility of different people can be meaningfully compared — the view that it cannot, because each person's utility is defined only up to a positive affine transformation on their own scale, so that no fact of the matter fixes the relation between one person's scale and another's; the view that some comparability is both possible and necessary for social choice and distributive justice; and the intermediate views that grant comparability of levels but not of differences, or of differences but not of levels. Render each in terms of what I know: rankings versus scores, the arbitrariness of a scale's origin and unit, what equating across different test forms or norming groups would have to accomplish, and when a difference between two scores is meaningful rather than an artifact of the conversion. Then handle the special moves — the attempt to manufacture comparability from behind a veil of ignorance, or from an ideal observer's extended sympathy — and the compensation tests that call a change an improvement when the gainers could in principle compensate the losers, and say what each of those looks like as a scoring procedure.

For the residue report, be precise and honest about three things: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only by being distorted and exactly what gets distorted, and which have no counterpart at all in the psychometric vocabulary. Do not smuggle in that vocabulary's assumptions — that the trait being measured is unidimensional, that the underlying distribution is normal, or that a score measures a capacity rather than a state of well-being — and then present the result as a translation. I want to know where the disanalogy genuinely lies.

Finally, my suspicion is that this philosophical dispute is a measurement dispute that my framework simply dissolves: once you construct a common scale, the comparisons are as meaningful as the scale is well built. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, or what the residue reveals that the scoring vocabulary cannot express — and whether that residue is a defect of the scoring vocabulary or evidence that the philosophers were arguing about less than they believed.
