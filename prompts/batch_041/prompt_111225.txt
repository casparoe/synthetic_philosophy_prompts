I am presenting a paper at a small philosophy of religion conference next month, and I need a preparation document for the question period. My thesis is a version of Molinism I call humble Molinism. I argue that God’s middle knowledge can be defended without treating the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom as grounded either in God’s creative decree or in the creature’s essence or dispositions. On my view, such counterfactuals are brute but semantically determinate truths about what a possible free creature would do in a complete set of circumstances; God knows them and actualises a world on the basis of them, but does not make them true. I claim this preserves a robust libertarian freedom, blocks the standard grounding objection, and still gives God providential control. The argument proceeds by distinguishing the truth-conditions of a counterfactual from its truth-makers, by treating the counterfactuals as members of a restricted class of contingent truths about possibilia, and by showing that the openness of the future is compatible with the determinate truth of counterfactuals about non-actual circumstances.

The audience is mixed and I know roughly what each faction will say. There will be three or four open theists, who will press that if the counterfactuals are true and God knows them, the future is not open in the way their view requires; a couple of Reformed Thomists, who will say that my brute counterfactuals are an ungrounded posit and that I have saved human freedom only by compromising divine simplicity and aseity; and several analytic philosophers who work on counterfactuals and causation but not on theology, who will ask why these counterfactuals are not either vacuous, arbitrary, or reducible to ordinary causal claims. There may also be one or two pastoral theologians who care about what this does to prayer, assurance, and evangelism. I need the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people, in their vocabulary, not a generic defence of Molinism and not the objections I would get from a Calvinist or a process theologian. Please order them by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrase each as the person would phrase it, and give me a two-minute reply to each, with a candid note on where the reply is weakest and what I should concede rather than defend. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session. Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I know the literature. I need a briefing sheet I can rehearse from, with the hard cases marked, and a short opening concession I can make at the start of the question period to lower the temperature.
