Once a fortnight I sit in on a work-in-progress seminar run by a colleague whose whole research programme is the is–ought gap. Each session she takes a single argument of the kind that turns up in the wild — in a policy paper, a science column, a first-year essay — and rules on it, using exactly three verdicts: no gap, meaning the inference stands as it is; gap, but only enthymematic, meaning it goes through solely because a normative premise is being suppressed; and the naturalistic fallacy properly so called, meaning the argument treats the normative conclusion as a consequence of what the descriptive words mean. I have been keeping the record, and it now reads like this. (1) "This solution will kill you if you drink it, so you ought not to drink it": gap, but only enthymematic — she says the suppressed premise is that one ought not to do what will kill one. (2) "You want to be in Edinburgh by noon and the only train that will get you there leaves at seven, so you ought to catch the seven o'clock": no gap; the end does all the work, she says, and nothing further is being smuggled in. (3) "Jones uttered the words 'I promise to pay you on Tuesday' in the appropriate circumstances, so Jones ought to pay you on Tuesday": no gap; her line is that once you describe what he did as promising rather than as uttering, the obligation is already inside the description. (4) "Everyone in the village has always given way to elders and I was raised to, so I ought to give way to elders": gap, but only enthymematic; a custom is a fact about what people do, and becomes a reason only once you add a premise. (5) "The heart's function is to pump blood, so a heart that cannot pump is defective": no gap; on her view 'function' is not a purely descriptive word. (6) "The only explanation of why we cannot shake the judgment that X is wrong is that X is wrong, so X is wrong": no gap; the gap, she says, was always about entailment, and this is not entailment. (7) "Cooperation increases inclusive fitness and 'morally good' means nothing more than 'what increases inclusive fitness', so cooperation is morally good": the naturalistic fallacy properly so called; here the conclusion is being sold as a matter of what the words mean.

Next week she is putting up an argument of her own construction, and I am the discussant, which is why I am asking you rather than her. A screening laboratory has a validated test; the prevalence of the condition in the population it screens is 0.4 per cent; the test's sensitivity is 97 per cent and its specificity 92 per cent; the sample in front of the technician returns a positive result, which by Bayes' theorem yields a posterior of a little over 4 per cent; and the argument concludes that the technician ought to believe that the donor does not have the condition. Her draft then adds a second half — that because the laboratory's stated purpose is to report what the evidence supports, the report ought to read 'not detected' — and she cannot decide whether to include it; neither can I.

What I want is not a summary of Hume, nor a ruling on whether 'naturalistic fallacy' is a good name for the thing Moore was worried about; I want the rules her record actually commits her to, stated precisely enough that they can disagree with one another, at least two of which fit everything above; what each of them predicts about next week's case; your own best guess at what she will say and why; and, if her rules genuinely underdetermine it, the case she has not yet considered that would best separate them. Bear in mind that every ruling so far has concerned a practical ought and next week's is doxastic, so tell me whether her rules extend to it as they stand or need an extra clause, and if they need one, what it would have to say.
