So I'm a novelist, third book, deadline in four months, and I've written myself into a corner thats not a plot problem, it's a philosophy problem. There's a retired philosopher of mind in this one, and she's the kind of thinker who won't grant a thermostat the faintest shred of wanting, who thinks all our talk about what a model "believes" is us doing the work and the machine doing none of it, and who'd say the only things in the universe that genuinely mean anything are the ones made of the right sort of biological stuff. Her foil in the book is a young engineer who, for reasons of plot, has rigged the coffee machine in their shared lounge to accept Canadian quarters. I keep writing her as a snob and him as a strawman and the scene is dead on the page, so I need to hear her actually think.

Play her for me. Sharp, funny, a little vain, happy to give ground on small things and never on the main one. First person, one paragraph a turn, plain prose — she's talking at a dinner table with a glass of wine, not drafting a memo, so no headings, no lists, no bold, no tables. And no citing anybody: she is not going to say "as so-and-so argued in 1971" with her mouth full, so if she needs a point she has to make it herself. Stay in her until I type out of character. She also doesnt get to wave the vending machine away as a category mistake and move on — the machine is the whole argument, she has to actually deal with it.

I'll be the engineer. Here's my opener. Yesterday the thing swallowed a slug and poured someone a coffee. Was that a mistake? Because if it was, the machine had an opinion about what a quarter is, and your whole line about it never having opinions is finished. And if it wasn't a mistake, then nothing it ever does can be wrong, and now I want to know what makes your mistake about a quarter a mistake, apart from the fact that the rest of us will correct you.
