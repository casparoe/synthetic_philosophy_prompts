I run the forecasting desk at a small research shop -- geopolitical and tech stuff, mostly for policy clients -- and I've inheritied an internal process that I never really questioned until recently. Here's how it works. Everyone submits a probability. Then we do a "share and update" round where people lay out their reasoning and the evidence behind it, and anyone who wants can revise. Then we take the median of the revised numbers as our official estimate, and we quote that median to clients as "the probability" that the thing happens. People who stay far from the median after the update round get flagged in their annual review as poorly calibrated or as "not updating on the evidence." And we sink real money into debiasing workshops whose entire point is to get people to move toward each other.

What's bugging me is this: the forecasters who hold out the longest against the group tend to be the ones who've thought hardest about the question. And some of them say things like "I just don't weight base rates the way she does, and no amount of her evidence is going to move me, because the disagreement is upstream of the evidence." Our process treats that as basically a confession of irrationality -- like, if you're rational and you've heard my argument, you should move. But I'm not sure that's right, and I don't want to be running a shop that punishes the best people on the team for a technicality I don't understand.

So: what does this whole apparatus actually presuppose about disagreement? I have a vague sense there's some old result in decision theory about rational agents not being able to "agree to disagree" once their beliefs are common knowledge, and that it's supposed to follow from something about shared priors -- but I don't know what it actually says or how strong it is. Walk me through it. Does our process require us to assume everyone starts from the same prior, or does it only need something weaker? Which rival pictures of disagreement does it quietly rule out -- for instance, that people can rationally hold different priors, that priors can themselves encode evidence, that some disagreements are about values or about how the question is even framed rather than about facts? And is our process internally consistent, given that we also advertise to clients that we hire diverse teams precisely because we want a spread of viewpoints? Finally, if we dropped the assumption, what would concretely have to change -- in how we aggregate, how we score people, and in what we're entitled to tell clients that median number means?
