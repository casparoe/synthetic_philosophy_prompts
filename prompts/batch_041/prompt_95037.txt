I am a law and philosophy student, and I have been puzzling over an asymmetry in the way courts treat claims arising from the birth of a child with a severe disability. In a wrongful birth action, parents may recover damages on the ground that, had they been properly advised, they would have avoided conceiving or carrying the child to term; the law treats the child's existence as a loss to the parents. In a wrongful life action, by contrast, the child herself may claim that she should never have been born, and courts almost universally reject the claim, often saying that the alternative of nonexistence is not a condition that can be compared with existence, or that life is a benefit however impaired. I find this pattern philosophically striking: the same birth that is a legally cognisable harm when viewed from the parents' perspective is not a legally cognisable harm when viewed from the child's.

What theory of the value of coming into existence does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose the procreation asymmetry—that it is bad to bring into existence a person whose life is not worth living, but not good to bring into existence a person whose life is worth living—or does it presuppose a different view, such as that existence is always a benefit to the child, that nonexistence cannot be a state of the person, or that the child's claim is barred by a duty not to harm that does not extend to bringing into existence? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it. Finally, if the procreation asymmetry were adopted as an explicit legal principle, what would have to change in the way these two kinds of claim are handled? Please be explicit about how confident you are in your reconstruction, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure about. Write your answer in British English.
