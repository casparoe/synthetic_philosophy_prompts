I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and as part of a departmental arrangement I spend one day a week as the methodology consultant for a lab in the psychology department and, through them, for an editor at a mid-tier journal who forwards me the cases her reviewers cannot agree on. The recurring case is this: a paper reports that a hypothesis is confirmed by data, and a reviewer objects that the data were already in hand before the hypothesis was formulated — that the effect was found first and a story fitted to it afterward — and concludes that the data therefore cannot confirm the hypothesis at all. The authors reply that the data still raise the probability of the hypothesis, and that it is absurd to say a hypothesis can never be supported by evidence that already existed. Both sides are invoking something real, and I keep having to improvise a verdict in under twenty minutes, for an audience with no formal training, with the editor's name on the decision and mine nowhere on it. I have been trying to turn the Bayesian literature on confirmation, and specifically the cluster of puzzles that goes under the heading of old evidence, into something I can actually run. My own working distinction is between temporal novelty — when the data came into existence — and epistemic novelty — whether the data were in the reasoner's evidence set at the moment the hypothesis was fixed — and I suspect the whole dispute lives in the gap between the two; I also want to make room for the counterfactual question of how surprised we would have been by the data had we not already had them. But I do not trust my improvisation, and I would rather have a written procedure than keep reinventing one.

Please write me that procedure: a checklist or decision tree in prose that a smart non-philosopher can run on a case, stating the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a verdict of "this evidence should not count toward the hypothesis," the point at which it returns "it counts, and roughly this much," and the point at which it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of norms rather than of probability. It should keep whatever distinctions from the theory genuinely do the work — the difference between evidence that is old to the community and evidence that is old only to the individual, the difference between a hypothesis that was merely formulated late and one that could not have been formulated earlier, the difference between updating on evidence and merely redescribing it — and drop the ones that matter only to specialists. Be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three cases and show your work. First, a researcher who collected a large dataset for one purpose, noticed an unanticipated interaction in it, and only then wrote down the hypothesis that the interaction exists. Second, a case in which the relevant result had been published and was common knowledge in the field, but this particular researcher genuinely did not know it when she formed her hypothesis. Third, a case you construct in which the procedure returns an answer I will not like — ideally one where the intuitively right verdict and the procedure's verdict come apart — and tell me what that divergence shows about the limits of the procedure. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot decide, and what a philosopher, as opposed to a reviewer, would still want to argue about.
