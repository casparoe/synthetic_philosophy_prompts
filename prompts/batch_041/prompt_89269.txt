Ok so its been bugging me for a while and I want to figure out what the practice is actually assuming.

Insider trading is illegal basically everywhere, and the standard justification is that it lets people with inside info fleece people without it, and it erodes confidence in markets. Fine, sure. But here's the bit I can't square. There's an old result in decision theory that goes roughly like this: if traders share the same prior beliefs, and the only reason to trade is that you think you know something the other guy doesn't, then in equilibrium basically nobody trades -- because anyone offering to sell to you is, by that very fact, telling you something, so you update and back off. Real markets obviously look nothing like that. So either people don't share priors, or they trade for reasons that have nothing to do with information -- they need the cash, they're hedging, they're just not rational.

So what does insider-trading enforcement actually presuppose about traders' beliefs? It seems to need it to be true that some traders know more than others and that trading on that edge is a genuine harm. But the same regulators, and alot of finance theory, turn around and assume prices already reflect all available information -- that's the whole basis for light-touch regulation and for the idea that you can't beat the market. Those can't both be the underlying picture, can they? And mandatory disclosure sits in the middle: it seems to presuppose that prices ought to reflect information and that getting info into prices is a good thing, which is a strange thing to believe if trading on information is what you're sending people to prison for.

So: reconstruct the view of belief, information and price that this whole cluster of practices needs in order to hang together. Which parts are strictly presupposed and which are just convenient? Do the enforcement practice and the efficient-markets practice actually contradict each other, or can they be reconciled? And if the whole thing only works because people don't share priors, does that mean the law is quietly committed to the idea that disagreement is fundamental rather than just a matter of missing evidence -- and how deep does that commitment go? Tell me what would have to give, in the law or in the theory, if we dropped the shared-prior assumption.
