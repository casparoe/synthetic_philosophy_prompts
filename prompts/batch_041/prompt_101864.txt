I am putting together a paper on the ethics and aesthetics of forgery, and before I begin assessing the accounts on offer I want the specification settled. What must a theory of the value of originals — a theory that tells us why, if at all, an authentic work is worth more than a copy of it, and why, if at all, forgery is wrong — actually be able to deliver? Please write me the desiderata: the full list of what any adequate account has to get right. I mean the cases it must handle, the distinctions it must not collapse, the intuitions it must either vindicate or explain away, and any findings it has to remain consistent with. Behind each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, so that I can see which requirements are doing genuine work and which are merely the prejudices of one camp — the collector's, the connoisseur's, or the moralist's. Where you think some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so plainly; that, to me, is the most interesting part of the exercise.

To keep this concrete, and to stop it collapsing into the same two or three examples everyone reaches for, I would like the list built around a set of cases I have been collecting. First, invented provenance rather than copied content: a forger who paints an entirely new picture and attributes it to a plausible but wholly fictitious dead artist, so that there is no original for it to be a copy of. Second, a workshop picture: a canvas designed by a master but executed throughout by an assistant, then sold for years as the master's own hand. Third, a self-forgery: an artist who, late in life, makes copies of his own early work and passes them off as the early originals. Fourth, a perfect physical replica, molecule for molecule, produced by a hypothetical replicator, of a painting whose original still exists. Fifth, a forged banknote, where what is counterfeited is not an aesthetic object but a token whose worth is entirely institutional. Sixth, an object made by a later hand but with the original's own tools, materials and workshop, so that it is neither a copy nor the original. Tie each requirement to whichever of these it is needed to handle, and mark which of them you take to be genuine constraints on any account and which are really just the commitments of one tradition. You may close with a brief scorecard of the main families of view — the one that locates value wholly in the aesthetic experience the object affords, the one that locates it in the object's history of production, and the one that treats authenticity as a social or symbolic construction — but keep that short; it is the criteria I am after, not the verdicts.
