I am the chair of a small philosophy department, and next month we are holding a retirement dinner for my colleague, Professor [Name], who has spent thirty-five years working on Nyāya epistemology—specifically on the status of śabda-pramāṇa, testimony, as an irreducible source of knowledge. The room will be split roughly in half: on one side, a dozen Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā specialists who have heard her argue this a hundred times and will notice immediately if I soften or misstate her position; on the other, her family, former students from other fields, and a few colleagues from the sciences who know almost nothing about Indian philosophy. I have been asked to give the toast. I want it to be genuinely warm, and I want it to take her ideas seriously rather than treating them as exotic decoration.

The complication is that I am not entirely persuaded by her central claim. I think there is a real question whether testimony is a basic pramāṇa or whether it can be reduced to inference plus perception—whether, when we learn from someone else, we are really making an inference from their reliability, or whether the knowledge is somehow immediate. She has always said I am wrong about this, and she has always said it kindly. I do not want to pretend at her retirement dinner that I have come around, and I do not want to turn the toast into a seminar. I want the disagreement to be present in the room in a way that honors her, not in a way that scores a point.

Please write the toast. It should run about five to seven minutes when spoken. It should use the structure of the four pramāṇas—perception, inference, analogy or comparison, and testimony—not as a list to be explained but as a way of moving through the speech, so that each one gives a different angle on her work and on what she has meant to the people in the room. It should be intelligible to the non-specialists without being condescending to them, and it should be precise enough that the specialists do not wince. You may mention the Mīmāṃsā and Advaita additions of postulation and non-perception if it helps, but do not turn the toast into a survey. Do not use quotations from any text, and do not cite sources. The toast should end in a way that leaves the disagreement intact but makes clear that it has been part of a long and affectionate conversation.
