I'm in the second half of a graduate seminar on epistemic utility theory, and I've come to realize that I don't have a clear picture of what the framework is supposed to be a theory of. The standard pitch runs roughly like this: credences are estimates of truth value; a credence is rational to the extent that it does well by a scoring rule that rewards accuracy and is proper, since under a proper rule you maximize expected accuracy by reporting your actual credence, which is why the Brier and logarithmic rules and their relatives are the ones worth using; and dominance reasoning with such a rule gets you probabilism, while expected-accuracy reasoning gets you conditionalization and, in a more delicate form, something in the vicinity of the Principal Principle. What I want from you is the specification underneath all of that: the desiderata that any theory of epistemic utility has to meet before I will accept it as an account of what makes a credal state rational, rather than as a convenient bookkeeping device for scoring forecasters. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that generates it, and tell me whether it is non-negotiable or merely a commitment of one camp. I want the list to cover at least three kinds of requirement. First, the cases the theory has to get right: the incoherent agent who is vulnerable to a sure loss; the agent whose credences are guaranteed to be accurate because a demon arranges the world to match them; the agent whose credences happen to be accurate but not because of anything she did or had; the agent whose evidence bears only on what her evidence is; and the agent whose evidence suggests that credences formed the way hers were formed tend to be inaccurate. Second, the distinctions it must not collapse: accuracy versus justification; the accuracy of an individual credence versus the accuracy of the whole credal state; first-order versus higher-order accuracy; precision versus imprecision. Third, the formal constraints it must live with: the existence of proper scoring rules and whatever propriety forces on their form, and what becomes of the dominance argument once the partition is infinite rather than finite.

Two things in particular I want the list to settle. A friend in the seminar has proposed that we score an imprecise credal state by the worst accuracy of any precise credence it permits, and then treat an imprecise state as justified when no other state does better on that score; I cannot tell whether that is a proposal our desiderata should be expected to adjudicate or a sign that the desiderata are incomplete, so I want to know what a requirement would have to say in order to have a verdict here. The second is more troubling to me. The program is standardly sold as the claim that accuracy is the only thing that makes a credence rational, and I have never seen a requirement on the list that would explain why an agent who simply does not care about being accurate is doing anything wrong — the norms seem to come out conditional on a desire for truth — and the demon case makes me suspect that accuracy cannot be sufficient for justification either. So I want a requirement that pins down what the theory owes on that score, along with an honest verdict on whether it can be met without abandoning the accuracy-only claim. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be satisfied together, say so explicitly; I suspect the accuracy-only claim and the demand for a genuine justification norm are the pair to watch, and that is the part of the answer I care about most.

Set the bar for the purpose I actually have in mind: a theory good enough to be the complete account of credal rationality, not merely a consistency check on degrees of belief. Please do not send me a reading list or citations — I know roughly who is who, and I want the specification stated in your own terms, with the cases spelled out. Close with a short paragraph on which single requirement does the most to rule out the views currently on the table, and no more than a few sentences each on how the main packages — the dominance-based argument for probabilism, the expected-accuracy defense of conditionalization, and the imprecise-credence alternative — fare against the list; keep the weight on the requirements themselves.
