Hey — I'm a second-year PhD student, currently stuck in a speech-act seminar, and I've hit a wall. Three weeks on the norm of assertion and every paper I pick up is basically the same move: here's knowledge, here's truth, here's belief, here's a couple of cases, scorecard at the end. I don't want another scorecard. Before I read anything else I want the spec — what does any adequate theory of the norm governing assertion actually have to deliver?

So give me the desiderata list. I mean the cases it has to get right (selfless assertions, where someone asserts something true that they don't believe and couldn't know; lottery-style cases where you're right but clearly shouldn't be asserting; lying vs bullshitting vs merely misleading; the absurdity of asserting something while disclaiming belief in it; the fact that we retract assertions rather than just deny them; assertions under genuine uncertainty, like a doctor hedging), the distinctions it can't collapse (the norm of assertion vs the norm of belief vs norms for acting on a claim; being appropriate vs being warranted vs being excused; the illocutionary act vs its perlocutionary effects; maybe a norm indexed to an audience vs one norm for everyone), and the background considerations it can't contradict (we're fallible; assertion has something to do with passing on knowledge or entitlement to believe; hearers are entitled to rely on what they're told; we can assess assertions along some kind of evolving conversational score). For each requirement, say why it's a requirement and what happens to a theory that drops it. Two things I especially want. First, mark which of these are genuine constraints and which are just prejudices of whichever camp the author happens to be in — I suspect half the standard list is imported from a prior commitment to the knowledge norm rather than being independently motivated. Second, tell me whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable; if you can't get lottery cases and selfless assertions and fallibility all at once, or if demanding all three forces you to give up the idea of a single norm, that's the result I most want.

Oh — and can you test the list against a couple of cases I've been kicking arround that I haven't seen anyone discuss? One is a sealed letter you write to your future self, asserting something you'll need later; there's no other audience, and the 'you' who reads it won't remember writing it. The other is an anonymous warning sign, no speaker you can point to, that gets read as an assertion. I don't know if these even count as assertions, but if the theory of the norm can't say whether they fall in scope, I'd count that against it. And honestly, if the whole 'there is a single norm' framing is a mistake, or the desiderata can't be cleanly seperated from which camp you're already in, just say that straight instead of handing me a tidy list — I'd rather be told the question is malformed than get a neat answer that papers over it.
