You are a philosopher of mind and language with a working specialism in the semantics and pragmatics of belief ascription, and you have spent real time on the family of sentences usually gathered under the heading of Moore's paradox. I want careful conceptual spadework, not a survey; there is no need to cite anything or point me to further reading.

Last week in a seminar I watched a colleague shut down a discussion by saying, of "It's raining, but I don't believe it", that belief aims at truth, and then, when someone asked whether the oddity of such a sentence is a semantic defect or a pragmatic one, that the aim is simply what beliefs are for — a fact about the mechanism, not a rule anyone is under. I came away convinced that at least three separate claims were being run together, and that the second remark was being made to do work the first cannot support.

Before you write anything, brainstorm the space of things that "belief aims at truth" could mean. Then lay the readings out as a menu, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical. For each reading, state exactly what claim it makes and what kind of claim that is — a contingent generalization about how beliefs happen to be formed, a claim about the function or standard of correctness that makes a state a belief at all, a normative claim about how one ought to believe, or a claim about the standards implicit in our practice of evaluating beliefs — and then say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivially true or genuinely substantive, and what it does and does not explain about the absurdity of the Moorean sentence. Be explicit about the difference between a regularity and a norm, and about where the slogan is doing real explanatory work and where it is a metaphor standing in for one.

Three test cases should separate the readings, so use them throughout. First, the pair "It's raining, but I don't believe it" and "I believe it's raining, but it isn't": is the absurdity the same in the two cases, and does each reading predict that? Second, the first-person/third-person asymmetry — the sentence sounds absurd in the first person but perfectly fine when said of someone else; which readings account for the asymmetry, and which treat it as an accident? Third, the knowledge version, "It's raining, but I don't know that it is", which many people find just as absurd: does that tell in favour of a norm of knowledge rather than a norm of truth, and can any reading of the original slogan accommodate it?

Then, and only then, adjudicate: say which single reading, if any, would actually license my colleague's dismissal, and whether his teleological gloss — what beliefs are for — can be combined with the normative reading or stands in tension with it. Finish with a short verdict on which reading is most defensible and which one people most likely have in mind when they repeat the slogan.
