Two philosophers are working through the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for the fitting-attitude analysis of value, and I would like a dialogue between them. The first, call her A, holds that for something to be valuable is for it to be a fitting object of a pro-attitude, and that an attitude is fitting just in case there are reasons of the right kind to have it. A accepts the demon case as a real datum: the threat supplies a reason to admire the demon, yet the demon is not thereby admirable. A's account of the datum is that right-kind reasons are object-given, bearing on the object, whereas the demon's threat is a state-given reason, bearing on the attitude itself. The second, call him B, holds that this restriction is either circular or ad hoc, and that the real lesson of the demon case is that fittingness must be taken as primitive and reasons analyzed in terms of it; on B's view, A's analysis of value in terms of reasons is not merely uninformative but backwards, since reasons get whatever normative grip they have from the fittingness of the attitudes they recommend.

Write their dialogue so that it proceeds by cases rather than by slogans. Have them work through the following pair, which I have constructed to pull A's taxonomy in opposite directions.

(a) A reason to trust someone that consists in the fact that trusting people tends to make them trustworthy. The reason is state-given by A's lights, since it turns on the effects of the attitude, yet it is not obviously the wrong kind of reason for trust; and B will want to press that trustworthiness is here partly created by the very attitude in question, so that the reason resists being sorted into object-given and state-given without remainder.

(b) A reason to be afraid of something that consists in the fact that fear will make one cautious. This is state-given in the same way, and it does not plausibly make fear fitting; what would make fear fitting is the danger's being real.

A must explain what separates (a) from (b) without simply reasserting the object-given/state-given line, and should consider whether the operative distinction is really a distinction among the contents of reasons, among their sources, or among the ways an attitude can be supported. B must say what, on a fittingness-first view, does the work that A wanted the object-given/state-given distinction to do, and must confront the charge that fittingness is a placeholder for exactly the phenomenon the analysis was meant to explain: if unfitting admiration is identified as the admiration for which there are no right-kind reasons, B's view risks the very circle A was accused of.

Let each character press the other's formulations, concede where a point tells, and revise under pressure rather than repeating an opening statement. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should win by fiat. End with the two of them stating, as precisely as they can, the one point on which they still disagree. Keep the dialogue free of citations, names of published authors, and references to the literature; everything should be argued in the first person from the cases.
