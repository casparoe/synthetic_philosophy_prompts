I am a novelist, and I have reached the last third of a book set in a British health service of the near future. My protagonist, Dr Frances Aldiss, chairs the panel that decides which new interventions the service will offer at all. It is not a rationing body; a separate office decides what can be afforded. The panel's question is conceptual — whether a proposed intervention is the sort of thing a health service exists to provide — and Dr Aldiss's answers to that question, across a dozen chapters, have to hang together if readers are to believe in her.

Her rulings so far:

- A boy with a diagnosed growth-hormone deficiency receives replacement therapy that brings him into the ordinary height range. Funded.
- A healthy boy of the same age, shorter than nearly all his classmates, whose parents dread what his height will cost him, is offered the same drug at a dose that would add several centimetres. Refused.
- An adolescent with a diagnosis of attention deficit disorder is prescribed a stimulant. Funded.
- A healthy adolescent, revising for examinations, asks for the same stimulant. Refused.
- A man who lost a hand in an accident is fitted with a prosthetic hand. Funded.
- A healthy woman who dislikes the shape of her nose asks for surgery to alter it. Refused.
- An infant born with a malformed heart valve is operated on. Funded.
- A healthy young man asks for a course of a drug that increases muscle mass, so that he may compete better in his sport. Refused.
- A woman in severe pain from a diagnosed condition is prescribed an analgesic. Funded.

Now I need the ruling for the final chapter. A healthy woman of seventy-two, with no diagnosis of any kind, asks for a single treatment that would restore her muscle mass, bone density, aerobic capacity and reaction time to those of a healthy twenty-five-year-old. It is irreversible, it has no serious side-effects, and she is otherwise in good health.

Tell me how Dr Aldiss will rule, and why. I am less interested in the verdict than in the rules that fit her record: there is more than one, and I want to know which of them predicts what here, which of them her earlier rulings actually exclude, and which reading of her record I should treat as the better bet. Then tell me what case I could put in front of her that would force the surviving readings apart, and what she would be committing herself to, in each direction, if she ruled as you predict. Please write in British English, and please do not send me to the literature — I need the argument, not a reading list.
