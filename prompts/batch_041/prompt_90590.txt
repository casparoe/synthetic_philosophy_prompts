I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on suffering-focused ethics, and I want the treatment to be diagnostic rather than doxographic. Please construct a decision tree, or a taxonomy with the same structure, of the positions on whether the reduction of suffering should be the organizing aim of ethics. Every branch node must be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so nodes should be concrete cases or substantive commitments rather than invitations to endorse a label; every leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I need roughly nine to ten leaves, and the tree must be able to reach at least the following: the strong view on which the only intrinsic disvalue is suffering, and which therefore makes the painless annihilation of all sentient life obligatory if that would minimize future suffering; a weaker view on which suffering is disproportionately weighty but not lexically prior to every other value; a person-affecting version that grants the badness of suffering while denying that it generates any duty not to create new sufferers; an impersonal version on which creating a sufferer is itself the harm; antinatalism reached on grounds independent of negative utilitarianism, via the asymmetry between the presence of pain and the absence of pleasure; a far-future variant that directs the whole enterprise at preventing suffering that does not yet exist rather than at alleviating present suffering; the deontological variant on which we may not inflict suffering even in order to prevent more of it; an interventionist view that extends these obligations to wild animals; and the view that suffering is bad only insofar as it exceeds the sufferer's capacity to bear it.

Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the named positions in it second. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Three cases should do work somewhere in the structure: a single life containing both great suffering and great joy; the choice between alleviating wild animal suffering and leaving nature undisturbed; and whether to bring into existence a being whose life will be net positive but not free of suffering.
