Hey, so I write about science and ideas for a general audience and I'm working on a piece where Frank Ramsey keeps coming up — the Cambridge guy, died in 1930 at 26, did the maths on probability and truth, translated Wittgenstein as a teenager. What I actually care about is the short thing he wrote near the end of his life about the meaning of life, the Epilogue that got tacked onto the collection of his essays that came out after he died. A friend who's read alot more of him than I have sent me this gloss, and I want to know how much of it I can put in print:

"Ramsey's Epilogue is an early version of the absurdist position. He's an atheist, and his main claim is that the universe has no meaning — there's no purpose out there, and the craving we have for one is a leftover from religious thinking. His famous image is a picture drawn in perspective: things near the viewer look bigger than things far away, and in the same way our own lives and interests loom large to us even though they're objectively microscopic in a universe that size. His point is that we should correct for that distortion — see our own concerns as the tiny things they really are — and then, having accepted that nothing matters cosmically, find satisfaction in our work and in the people around us. He says something like, the stars may be enormous but they can't think or love, which I take to be his version of the consolation: size isn't what counts. So: no cosmic meaning, no despair, just get on with the small stuff."

That's the gist of what he sent. I'm inclined to believe the general shape of it, but two things nag at me. First, the perspective metaphor seems to be doing something more interesting than "here's a distortion, now fix it" — if a picture in perspective were distorted, every picture would be, and the analogy would evaporate. Second, I'm not sure Ramsey is a nihilist about anything except maybe the cosmic question, and I have a feeling he'd say that question is confused rather than answered in the negative. But I've only read him in fragments and I don't want to make an idiot of myself in a magazine.

Can you go through the gloss sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on Ramsey would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And where the gloss draws a conclusion — that we should deflate our own concerns, that this amounts to a consolation — say whether that follows from anything Ramsey actually holds or whether it's the glossator's own spin. Also tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you're least sure about, since I might end up leaning on this in print.
