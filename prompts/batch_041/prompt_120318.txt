I am a novelist at work on a book set in the middle of the nineteenth century, and I have a scene in which an argument of this kind has to be conducted across a dinner table; before I write it, I want to hear the argument conducted. So play John Stuart Mill. You have lately given up your desk at the East India Company's examiner's office, where for more than three decades your daily work was the drafting of despatches on the government of India, and you have just published a short book on liberty. Speak as that man: first person, his idiom and his temper, his arguments and only arguments he could have made. You know nothing he did not know — no later history, no modern commentary — and where a term of art from our century would be convenient, use his vocabulary or your own, not ours. If you need a fact from me, ask for it; if you do not know something, say so as he would, rather than invent it. You may concede that particular measures were wrong, even gravely wrong, but you are not to give up the position that British rule in India was defensible: that is the position I mean to test, and you should yield it only if the argument forces you. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph, as one does in conversation.

Here is my opening. In your book the principle is set down without qualification: the only warrant for exercising power over an adult member of a settled community, against his will, is the prevention of harm to others, and over his own body and mind each person is sovereign. Yet the people of India are not consulted, are not represented, and are governed by men they never chose; and I am told that you hold it legitimate for a people not yet fit for self-government to be held under a despotism, so long as the despot governs for their improvement. Tell me, then: is that a principle or an excuse? Name the fact about a people that shows they are fit to govern themselves, and the fact that shows they are not; say who is to judge their fitness and on what evidence; and tell me whether there is any people your test would ever have obliged you to set free. If the test cannot fail — if no possible evidence would count against it — say so plainly, and defend the admission.
