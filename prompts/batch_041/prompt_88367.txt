I run a grad seminar on social kinds (90 minutes, about 14 students, mix of philosophy and sociology) and next week's topic is generations -- are Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z etc. real kinds, or just a convenient label for 'people born around the same time'? I dont want to just stand up and lecture them the answer. I want them to walk into the hard part themselves and only get the vocabulary afterwards. Design the session for me.

The setup I'm imagining, sharpen it as you see fit: groups of 3-4. I hand every group the same fictional dataset -- the 'Harbury Social Attitudes Panel', five waves of a survey in an invented country, mean scores on a handful of attitude items (trust in institutions, confidence in the press, etc.) broken down by age band. Headline pattern: younger bands score lower, and that's been true in every wave. Each group has to write me the causal story -- why are the young less trusting?

Then the turn. One group will say it's an aging effect (people get more trusting as they get older). Another will say it's a period effect (everyone dropped after some event, we're just looking at who happens to be young right now). A third will say it's a cohort effect (this batch got formed a certain way and carries it forever). I want the materials arranged so all three stories fit the same table equally well -- please actually check that the numbers do that, don't just assert it. Then let them try to design the study that separates the stories and watch the naive fixes fail: another wave doesn't help, adding controls doesn't help, and following the same people over time doesn't either, because within a single person you still can't split age from period. I want the punch to land as an identification problem, not a 'we need more data' problem.

I'd also like a role or a planted prompt that pushes the reference-class question -- someone asking why the bands are five-year bands and not 'everyone who was job-hunting during the crash', i.e. is the cohort the birth year or the shared event?

The debrief matters alot here. It should map what actually happened back onto: cohort vs age vs period as kinds; whether a generation needs a shared formative experience to count as real at all or whether that's a story told after the fact; and the difference between a cohort label being useful and being a cause. Roles, rules, timeline, materials, please. And tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time -- I've had activities like this die when one student already knows the statistics term and blurts it out. Also be honest about which part of the 'are generations real kinds' question this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.
