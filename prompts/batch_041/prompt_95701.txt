ok so I'm a second-year PhD student in decision theory — econ department, but my advisor is technically in philosophy half the time, it's a whole thing — and last week our reading group did Aumann's agreement theorem and it broke everyone's brain in the fun way. What got me is how badly the thing gets mangled the second it escapes the seminar room: you see it cited in blog posts as if it proves that rational people can't disagree, which is basically backwards, since the whole bite of the result is that it only kicks in when the posteriors are common knowledge, the rationality is common knowledge, and there's a shared prior — and those conditions are the interesting part, not the footnote. Anyway, another student joked that Aumann is really just a management technique, and now I can't stop picturing what a company run on the agreement theorem would actually look like, so I want to get the parody down before someone else does.

Here's what I'm after: a deadpan internal employee-handbook section (or an FAQ — numbered items, flat corporate-newspeak voice, no winking) from a fictional company that has adopted the agreement theorem as its official conflict-resolution policy. The joke is that they've taken a result about idealized Bayesian agents who share a prior and turned it into HR. Something like 700–900 words, plus a short sidebar. The thing being satirized is the move of treating every human disagreement as a pure information-aggregation problem — plus the broader habit of pressing a formal result into service as life advice — but it has to be *fair*: a decision theorist reading it should laugh because the details are right, not wince because they're wrong. So the humor needs to come out of the actual machinery: the gap between mutual knowledge and common knowledge and the infinite regress that opens up with the latter; the fact that the theorem needs a shared prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors and rationality; the fact that it says nothing about how anyone should update, or how agents would ever *reach* common knowledge in practice; the point that merely learning that a colleague disagrees is itself information; and the fact that the real punchline is how strong the conditions are, not how people should behave at standup. Someone who's never heard of the theorem should still be able to read the whole thing as a workplace comedy.

A few bits I'd love in there, since they'd signal the author actually knows the literature: an onboarding ritual where new hires' priors are published, with a clause for what happens when somebody's prior is uncomputable or improper; a policy for the case where the two of you have exchanged everything you know and *still* disagree, which the handbook resolves in the most bureaucratic way imaginable rather than the correct one; a stretch where an employee asks how the company could ever make anything common knowledge given the regress, and the handbook answers with something like a meeting about the meeting; and a short pull-quote testimonial from a "concerned" colleague who isn't a Bayesian (or is one and objects to the whole enterprise anyway), whose complaint should be technically correct. Invent the company and everyone in it — no real people, and please don't name-drop any actual papers or authors, the piece should stand on its own. Write it in American English.
