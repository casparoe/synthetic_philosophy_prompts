I serve on a small departmental appointments committee, and I recently witnessed a prolonged argument between two of my colleagues, Alex and Blair, after a vote. I am not taking sides; I would like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

The committee had to decide whether to make an offer to a candidate. Before voting, we all accepted a rule: the offer (H) should be made if and only if the candidate is adequate in teaching (T) and adequate in research (R). We then voted separately on T, R, and H. The three members voted as follows:
- Member 1: T, R, H
- Member 2: T, not-R, not-H
- Member 3: not-T, R, not-H

Each member's set of votes is internally consistent. But the majorities are: T (2–1), R (2–1), not-H (2–1). The set of majority judgements is therefore inconsistent with the rule we all accepted.

Our standing orders contain a consistency clause: if the majority judgement on each issue is inconsistent, the committee must adopt a consistent set of judgements that agrees with the majority on the greatest number of issues. The chair, however, simply announced: "The majority voted against H, so we do not hire."

Alex objected: "The consistency clause applies here. The consistent set {T, R, H} agrees with the majority on T and R—two issues. The set {T, not-R, not-H} agrees on T and not-H—also two. The set {not-T, R, not-H} agrees on R and not-H—also two. So the clause leaves a tie. But the premises are logically prior to the conclusion. We should break the tie in favour of the premises, which gives {T, R, H}. The committee's judgement is to hire."

Blair replied: "The clause says 'a consistent set', not 'the consistent set that prioritises premises'. It does not resolve the tie, and it certainly does not authorise a premise-priority rule that we never adopted. The chair's declaration stands: the majority voted against H, so we do not hire."

Alex came back: "Then the T and R votes are decorative. The point of voting on them was to make the decision track the reasons. If the H vote can contradict them, why vote on the premises at all?"

Blair answered: "They inform the H vote, but the committee is not a single rational agent. Collective consistency is not a requirement. The individuals are consistent; the aggregate need not be."

Alex said: "If consistency is not a requirement, the committee can endorse a contradiction—teaching is adequate, research is adequate, and we should not hire. That leaves us with no coherent position to report, and we must act."

Blair responded: "The contradiction is an artefact of treating the majority set as a single belief set. If you want the premises to trump the conclusion, you need to adopt a rule that says so. We did not adopt one. You are smuggling it in after the fact. And in any case, no procedure satisfies all the obvious democratic desiderata: a premise-based rule can produce outcomes that a majority voted against, while a conclusion-based rule can ignore the premises. The choice is political, not logical."

Alex replied: "I am not claiming it is uniquely rational. Given the votes, the only consistent reading is to hire. The H vote was based on the same information, so it is not as if it is a different question."

Blair said: "It is a separate question. Some members might accept T and R but still not want to hire for departmental reasons. The H vote is a separate expression of judgement. You cannot assume it is a mistake."

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move—(1) the chair's declaration, (2) Alex's consistency-clause argument, (3) Blair's reply about the clause, (4) Alex's 'decorative' objection, (5) Blair's response about collective consistency, (6) Alex's contradiction objection, (7) Blair's response about smuggling and democratic desiderata, (8) Alex's 'same information' reply, and (9) Blair's 'separate question' reply—say whether it is clearly mistaken, questionable, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say what each party is missing and who is more right overall. Do not simply pick a side; distinguish the points on which each is correct. The answer should be in British English. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence overall verdict.
