And here's what Marta sent in:

"Popper's target is what he calls historicism, which he defines as the idea that the main job of the social sciences is to predict the future course of history, and that this is possible because there are laws of how societies develop. He splits historicists into two camps — the pro-naturalistic ones, who want to copy the methods of physics, and the anti-naturalistic ones, who say society has to be understood from the inside and can't be studied that way at all — and he thinks both camps are wrong, for different reasons. Interestingly he doesn't side with the anti-naturalists either: he wants one unified method of conjecture and testing for both the natural and the social sciences.

His main argument is the difference between laws and trends. A trend is just a statement that something has been happening in a certain way in a certain time and place — the population of Europe rising, empires growing and then falling apart. That's not a law, it's a singular statement about one stretch of history, and it could stop tomorrow without contradicting any science. To turn a trend into a prediction you'd need a law saying the trend must continue, and there is no such law. So the famous laws of history are really just trends dressed up as laws.

Then there's the deeper argument, about knowledge. If history is driven partly by what people know and believe, and knowledge keeps growing, then to predict the future you'd have to predict what we're going to discover — but if we could predict it we'd already know it, so it wouldn't be a discovery. So a full science of historical development isn't just hard, it's logically impossible. It's not that we aren't clever enough.

He also has this idea he calls the Oedipus effect: predicting an event can change the event, because people hear the prediction and act differently. So you get a feedback loop where the prophecy brings about or prevents what it foretold. This is basically the observer effect from physics, where measuring something changes it, and I think this is the heart of his case — it's what makes social prediction different in kind from prediction in the natural sciences.

The upshot, as I read it, is that there can't be laws about human societies at all, so the social sciences can only describe and interpret, they can't explain or predict, and history can never be a theoretical science.

On the modern data question: I think Popper would say it's hopeless in principle. You can have all the data and all the computing power in the world, but if the prediction gets out and people react to it, the prediction undermines itself before it starts, and if the future depends on discoveries nobody's made yet, there's nothing to compute from. Maybe you can get short-term statistical forecasts of small things, but not the big structural stuff, and certainly not a science of revolutions.

That said, he isn't gloomy about social science in general. He thinks you can do piecemeal social engineering — fix specific problems and watch what happens, instead of planning the whole society at once — and that you can study the logic of the situation people find themselves in. So it's not that we can't know anything, only that we can't know where history is going."
