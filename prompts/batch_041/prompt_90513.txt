I work on philosophy of biology and I run the fortnightly reading group in our department, and for about three months now we've been completely stuck on Dennett's free-floating rationales — the idea that there can be reasons in nature that nobody entertains, so the rationale floats free of any reasoner. The maddening part is that we all agree about the cases. We agree that the lancet fluke drives its ant up a blade of grass to clamp its jaws on the tip, that this gets the ant eaten by a grazing sheep and so completes the fluke's life cycle, that it wrecks the ant, that the ant has nothing you'd want to call a belief about any of it, and that selection assembled the whole arrangement over deep time. Same with the ant mill: the trail-following rule that works beautifully almost always turns into a death spiral once the pheromone loop closes, and no ant intends a thing. Facts: agreed. Interpretations: not remotely.

So we have (a) the Dennettian, for whom the fluke-ant system just has a rationale — climbing is done in order to get the fluke into a sheep — and nobody needs to represent it, because reasons are cheap and selection makes them; (b) the deflationist, for whom that's simply the trait's function restated, and calling it a reason smuggles agency and normativity back in through the word; (c) the mentalist, for whom a reason is something that can be weighed against other considerations, so no weighing, no reason, and free-floating rationales are therefore either secretly represented (in which case they aren't free-floating) or not reasons (in which case they aren't rationales); and (d) the one who insists reasons justify whereas selection only explains, so a free-floating rationale can never be more than a dressed-up causal story.

My own hunch is that most of this is verbal — one side means 'consideration that explains', the other means 'consideration that counts in favour' — but a colleague beleives the real crux is revisability: a state is a reason only if the system could in principle revise it in the light of new information, and free-floating rationales fail that test by construction, which would make the disagreement substantive and about the conditions on mindedness. I honestly can't tell which of us is right, and the four of us are now largely talking past eachother.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who wins: is this verbal, is there a hidden empirical question about what these systems actually represent, is it a disagreement about what counts as an explanation of behaviour, or is it bedrock about normativity and where it can come from? Tell me what each side is actually committed to as opposed to what it says, whether the sides are even answering the same question under one heading, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, an empirical result, or your honest view that none is coming and why. Please don't just survey the literature at me; I've read it. And write it in British English, since I'm lifting chunks of this into a lecture handout and the rest of my material is in British English.
