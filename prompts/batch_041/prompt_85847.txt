Three weeks ago, during a stretch of weeks I had been calling normal, I signed a Ulysses clause with my psychiatrist: if I stop sleeping, start spending, stop being able to hear how I sound, and refuse treatment, then the document on my sister's shelf speaks for me and I do not, and she is authorized to act on what I signed rather than on what I say at the time. The paper is in an envelope. I would like a poem to go in with it — for her to read on the day she has to decide whether to use it, and for me to read later, as the person the clause was written against.

Write it as a pantoum of four or five quatrains, in which the lines that come back are the clauses themselves. I want the form to carry the argument. The pantoum is a device for making each stanza inherit the one before it (a stanza's first and third lines are the previous stanza's second and fourth), and its closing stanza is obliged to return to the opening lines, so the poem cannot get out of its own first stanza any more than the person in the episode can get out of the signature. Concretely:

- The recurring lines should be the terms — the instruction to disregard my later instructions, the naming of who holds the envelope, the statement of what I want done — and they should recur verbatim, spoken first by the one who signed and then by the one the clause is aimed at, so that a reader can hear these are not the same speech act even though the sentences are identical. That difference is the whole poem.
- Open in the signing voice: unhurried, unheroic, clear, and aware that it is writing to someone who will find its composure insulting.
- Close in the other voice, and land the final line back on the first, so that the opening sentence has to be read twice and cannot mean the same thing both times.
- Give the enforcer her due: she is bound by this too, and she has to be deaf in a way I have asked her to be. The old story — the man lashed to the mast, the crew with wax in their ears, the standing order to ignore the orders he will give — is the actual structure of what I have done, and I would like it present as a fact about the arrangement rather than as an allusion. If the Sirens appear, they should be working.
- It is not a promise, and the poem should know the difference: a promise would require the cooperation of the very person it is meant to constrain. At least one line should be addressed to my sister rather than to me, since I am not the audience that matters here.
- One image of the constitutional version — the clause a later majority cannot repeal — is welcome, but as an echo only; do not turn the analogy into a thesis.
- Do not resolve it. No verdict on which of us is the real one, no assurance that it will hold, and no contempt for the one in the episode, who will read this too. Leave the two of them in the same envelope.

Title it with the date of the signature. Do not name the diagnosis.
