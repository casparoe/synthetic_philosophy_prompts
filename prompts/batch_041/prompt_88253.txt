I write the "how this happened" section of short policy briefs — our shop turns out a two- or three-page lessons-from-history memo for incoming officials every couple of weeks, 800 words of actual text, no footnotes, and a section at the end that is supposed to explain why the episode in question went the way it did. I've been doing this about a year and I keep getting the same two complaints back from the academic historians we send drafts to. If I pick a single cause and build the paragraph around it — it was the drought, it was the bankers, it was one minister's decision — they say I've flattened an episode with half a dozen moving parts, and that the cause I named was on its own neither necessary nor sufficient. If I do the opposite and write "a confluence of factors" followed by eight bullet points, they say a list isn't an explanation and I've said nothing. A collegue of mine who did a philosophy degree told me the word for my problem is overdetermination, and that what I want to be able to write is something called an INUS condition, but when I looked both up I couldn't get from the definitions to a single sentence I'd actually put in a brief. I have about a day and a half per brief, I'm working from a stack of secondary sources and two or three interviews, and I hand the draft to our research director, who will ask me "so what actually caused it?" and will not accept a shrug.

What I'd like is a procedure I can run in that day and a half — the steps in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, the point where the procedure stops and I have to make a call myself, and an honest note on which of the steps are really just me exercising judgment with a flowchart drawn around them. I'd also like a rule for that last paragraph: how many causes a reader can hold, whether to lead with the remote ones or the immediate one, how to tell a cause from a mere background condition, and what to do when two or three causes were each by itself enough that removing one of them wouldn't have changed the outcome — because that seems to be exactly the case the historians are complaining about, and it's the case I can't write. Please don't send me a reading list; I won't read it, and I need the thing itself. Tell me plainly what the procedure can't do. Then run it on the two cases below and flag anywhere it gives me an answer I won't like.

Case one is our own mess. A pilot program we ran with a partner organization to get low-income households signed up for a benefit went badly: enrollment came in at a third of what we projected, the households who did sign up were mostly ones already connected to services, and the partner pulled out eight months in. The candidate causes in my notes are: we picked a city where the benefit was already heavily advertised; the partner's director left four months in; our sign-up form ran to eleven pages; we launched in late August; the funding formula paid the partner per enrollment, which made it rational for them to enroll the easiest households first; we changed the evaluation design halfway through; and a rival organization launched a similar program in the same city two months after us. Case two is a historical one we reuse constantly, the 2008 financial crisis, where my candidate list is subprime and predatory lending, the shift to originating loans in order to sell them on, the ratings agencies, leverage at the big banks, low interest rates and the global savings glut, the housing bubble itself, the incentive structure of Wall Street pay, and fragmented regulators. On the first case I suspect the honest answer is that our own design did it and the partner was behaving rationally, which is not something my director will enjoy reading, so if the procedure can reach that conclusion it should be able to say so out loud.
