I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I keep running into an idea I would like to get straight. The idea is that there can be a good reason for a system to behave as it does — a reason that makes the behaviour correct or appropriate — even when nothing in the system, and nothing anywhere else, entertains that reason. The examples I've seen are evolutionary: a bird's broken-wing display is a good way of drawing a predator away from the nest, and that is why the display persists, although the bird has no notion of drawing anyone anywhere. I've seen the label free-floating rationale attached to this. What I don't know is how far the idea stretches, so I've assembled a list and I'd like you to rank it.

Rank the following from the clearest case of a free-floating rationale to the least clear, give each a score between 0 and 1 for how comfortably it fits, and add one or two sentences of justification per item:

1. A river that, over geological time, comes to run along the steepest available descent to the sea.
2. The bimetallic strip in a domestic thermostat, which bends by different amounts in its two metals and so opens the circuit as the room warms.
3. A bacterium that swims up a sugar gradient by suppressing its tumbles whenever the concentration is rising.
4. The ventilation of a termite mound, which draws fresh air through the nest by a chimney effect.
5. A compiler's register allocator, which spills whichever variable has the longest live range.
6. The price of a commodity in a competitive market, which moves towards the cost of producing one more unit.
7. A classifier's decision boundary, which minimises expected error over its training distribution.
8. The antibody population of an immune system, which comes to bind the pathogen currently multiplying in the body.
9. A language model's next-token distribution, which assigns high probability to the token that in fact tends to follow.
10. A spreadsheet's goal-seek function, which iterates a cell's value until a formula reaches a target.

Then, for the list as a whole, I want three things. First, tell me which of your placements are substantive and which are largely verbal — that is, where two items differ only because of how we have chosen to talk about them, and not because anything about the systems themselves differs. Second, name the single item where you think the notion is doing the most explanatory work and the single item where it is doing the least, and say what distinguishes them. Third, and this matters most to me, say what would change your ranking: which argument or consideration would move an item up or down, and where your reasoning is most likely to be unreliable — including any point at which you suspect you are pattern-matching on familiar examples rather than thinking the case through. I'd rather have your own reasoning than pointers to literature, since I have no way to check the latter and no background to place it in.

Format: the ranked list with scores and justifications, then the three discussion sections.
