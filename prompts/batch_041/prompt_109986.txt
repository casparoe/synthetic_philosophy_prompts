A friend of mine dropped an argument on me over dinner and I've been chewing on it for a week, which is a problem because I write novels for a living and this is definately my problem. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) Whenever some things compose a bigger thing, they do it because they stand in some special relation R — being stuck together, being caught up in one living body, whatever.
(2) For any R you name, you can lay out a smooth series from a clear case of R to a clear case of not-R, with nothing anywhere marking the exact point where R stops holding.
(3) So any rule that says composition happens here and not there draws a line the world doesn't mark, which is to say the rule is arbitrary.
(4) There are no arbitrary facts about what exists.
(5) So either any things whatsoever compose a further thing, or nothing ever composes.
(6) First horn: there's an object made of my left boot, the moon and the number seven. Second horn: no chairs, no bodies, no novels.

He says I already believe (3), since I'll cheerfully slide a chapter break a sentence either way and insist the break was mine and not the story's. I want to keep (4), because I don't believe the world has arbitrary seams, and I also want to keep my chair, and my novels. Where does this go wrong? I don't need names or a reading list, just the options and their price tags. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate my argument back to me.
