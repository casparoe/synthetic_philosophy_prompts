I cover politics and ideas, and I've written about citizens' assemblies and lottocracy enough times now that I can feel the arguement going in circles. The advocates say random selection plus real deliberation beats elections on both quality and fairness; the critics say a lottery just launders a group with no accountability, and that the people who show up to an assembly aren't the public anyway. Nobody ever concedes anything. My hunch is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about whether sortition works — one is asking whether it produces better decisions, the other is asking whether it can make a decision that binds me, and both get filed under "legitimacy." What pushed me there: an assembly picked by lot that lands on a policy I'd have voted for, but that I had no part in, versus one that lands on something I think is wrong, but that I could have been in the room for. I'd feel I owed the second one something I don't owe the first, which seems backwards if this is all about accuracy. And half the time nobody's even clear whether we mean assemblies that recommend or assemblies that decide. A friend who works on deliberative stuff says the real question is who carries the burden of deciding, not who holds the power — I can't tell if that's profound or just a restatement. So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — a hidden empirical dispute about how assemblies actually perform, a fight about what counts as a political reason, or two sides answering different questions under one word? And is there anything that would move it — a distinction, a study, a redesigned institution — or should I stop waiting?
