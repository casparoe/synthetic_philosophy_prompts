I don't have any formal training in philosophy — I write software — so excuse me if I mislabel things, but there's a pattern I keep running into and can't resolve.

The pattern is this. Individual-level climate practices — personal carbon-footprint calculators, the offset marketplaces where you buy credits to cancel your own emissions, "flight shame," the general exhortation to fly less and eat less beef — all treat one person's consumption as the morally relevant unit. Every one of these tools and campaigns assumes the point is to change what you, personally, do. But a single person's emissions are a rounding error in the global total; my skipping a flight changes no climate outcome and wrongs no identifiable person. And meanwhile the same organizations and governments, whenever they actually set policy, treat only aggregate emissions as the thing to be controlled, and no individual is ever held to account for their own negligible slice. We act as if the individual is the responsible unit in the consumer-facing practices and as if only collectives are in the policy-facing ones.

I want a reconstruction of the theory of individual moral responsibility that the individual-level practices presuppose. Specifically:

1. Which view of a person's contribution to a collective harm do these practices require — that a person is answerable for a causally detectable share, that they are complicit in a collective act regardless of their marginal contribution, that their act carries expressive or symbolic meaning, or that what matters is the rule it would be best for everyone to follow? Which rival views do the practices quietly rule out?

2. There's a tension I can't sort out. Within the same broad movement, some people buy offsets and treat their footprint as a running balance to be cancelled out; others refuse to buy offsets and simply abstain, on the grounds that paying to cancel the harm does not undo the wrong. The offsetters seem to assume the harm is fungible and commensurable — the wrong lives in the net total, so it can be zeroed out. The abstainers seem to assume the wrong lives in the act itself, so nothing bought afterward touches it. Are these two commitments consistent, and can a single theory of individual contribution underwrite both, or do the practices presuppose incompatible views? I would put the difference as net-harm bookkeeping versus act-character, though those are my own labels, not anyone's technical terms.

3. The obvious objection — my act makes no difference, so it cannot be required — is supposed to show that individual-level practices must be resting on some non-causal notion of responsibility. Is that right, or could a purely forward-looking or expressive account license the same practices with no notion of causal contribution at all?

4. Separate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, and tell me where they are underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same behavior, so the practice does not decide between them.

5. Finally, if we took the aggregate-only view seriously — only states and firms bear responsibility, individuals as such bear none — what would have to change about the individual-level practices? Would they have to go, or could they survive in a reinterpreted form?

I'd rather you commit to a reading than survey the literature. Where your reconstruction is a judgment call, say so, and end by telling me what argument or consideration would most likely push you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Finish with a last line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a few words, the view you judge the individual-level practices most likely to presuppose, or the word "underdetermined" if you think no single view is.
