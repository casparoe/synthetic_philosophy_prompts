Next month I'm giving a seminar paper to our archaeology department argueing that the shift to agriculture was on net a bad deal for the people who lived through it and after it, and that it's the deep root of durable inequality. The room: mostly Neolithic and Near Eastern archaeologists, a couple of people who work on the Americas, two osteoarchaeologists from the bone lab, and one economic historian who comes to everything. They're an empirically minded bunch, they've all excavated with their own hands, and they roll their eyes at anything that sounds like Rousseau in a lab coat. My talk is roughly: foragers worked less, ate better, were healthier and more equal than early farmers and most of what came after them; farming bought us more people, not better lives; and once you've got stored surplus and fixed fields you get hierarchy and there's no way back. I'm not claiming anyone sat down and chose it. I do call it a mistake, meaning it in a strictly welfare sense — tell me if that framing is going to get me killed, and why.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion afterwards. I want the objections I'll actually get from these people specifically — the ones a bone person or a dirt archaeologist will really raise, in their vocabulary — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each does, with a reply I can give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front rather than defend; I'd rather hand over the things I can't win and fight on the ground I can hold. And give me the one question I'm secretly hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask.

For aiming: my strongest material is the work-hours stuff and the skeletal record (caries, enamel defects, stature, the anemia indicators), plus the demographic point that early farmers were sicker but more numerous, so 'success' is doing a lot of quiet work in the standard story. The part I'm least sure of is the violence claim — I say forager violence wasn't obviously worse than what came later — and I want to know how exposed that leaves me. Don't hand me a literature review; I need to know which specific claims in my talk get hit and how.

Format: numbered objections ordered by damage, then a short concede-up-front paragraph, then the unasked question on its own line, and finish with a last line reading exactly Verdict: <the single objection most likely to sink the talk, five words or less>.
