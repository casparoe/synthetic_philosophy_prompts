I am a high school student and a member of my school's machine learning club, and I would like help adjudicating an argument that two other members had after our last meeting. I did not take a side, and I am not asking for advice about what to do; I am asking for a point-by-point assessment of the arguments as they were actually made.

Background: We trained a small feedforward network on a synthetic task. Each input had a shape and a color. The network was trained to classify shape. In the training set, color and shape were correlated; in the test set, color was randomized independently of shape. The network's test accuracy on shape was high. Then Mira trained a linear probe on the activations of the fourth hidden layer to predict color. The probe decoded color well above chance, even on test inputs where color was randomized. Jonah then trained a probe on the same activations to predict a randomly shuffled version of the color labels; that probe also decoded the original color labels above chance, though less well. What followed was roughly this exchange.

Mira: The probe shows that the network has a representation of color. If color were not represented, you could not recover it from the activations.

Jonah: The probe shows that the activations carry information about color. Information is not representation. A representation has to be used by the system for something. Otherwise every trace of a correlation is a representation, and the word loses its point.

Mira: That cannot be right, because we attribute representations to human brains on the basis of exactly this kind of decoding. We do not first know how the brain uses the information. So if decodability is enough for brains, it should be enough for networks.

Jonah: We have independent reasons to think brains are representational—evolution, behavior, reports, and so on. For a network, the only thing we know is the input-output function and the weights. Decodability is a fact about the weights, not about what the network is doing.

Mira: But the network is doing something with color, or at least it has learned a feature that tracks color. If the feature is systematically recoverable, it is part of the network's internal model of the world. The fact that it is not needed for the shape task does not make it less of a representation; it just makes it a representation the network is not currently using.

Jonah: You are conflating a representation of color with a representation for color. A representation for color would be one the network uses in its computations; a representation of color is just a state that covaries with color. The probe establishes the latter, not the former.

Mira: That distinction is not as clean as you think. A state can be a representation of color even if it is not used for the current task, as long as it is available for other tasks. Brains represent lots of things they are not currently using.

Jonah: Then your view is that a representation is any decodable feature, whether or not it plays a role. That makes representation too cheap. My shuffled-label probe decoded the real labels, which means the probe can exploit structure in the activations that has nothing to do with color. So decodability alone cannot establish representation.

Mira: The shuffled-label probe is a control, not a counterexample. It shows the probe can overfit, but the color probe was trained and tested properly, and it generalizes. If a feature generalizes to new inputs, it is real. You are just setting the bar for representation so high that no artificial system could ever meet it.

Jonah: No, I am setting a functional bar. A representation is something the system uses to guide its behavior. If you lesion the feature and behavior changes, that is evidence of representation. If you can decode it but lesioning it changes nothing, it is a byproduct. That is not an impossibly high bar; it is the ordinary bar in cognitive science.

Mira: But behavior is not the only thing we care about. Interpretability is supposed to tell us what is inside the network, not just what it does. If we only count features that make a behavioral difference, we will miss most of the internal structure. And your lesion test is confounded: the network may have redundant representations, so removing one changes nothing even though it is a representation.

Jonah: Redundancy is a fair point, but it cuts both ways. If a feature is redundant, we need a theory of when it counts as a representation rather than a spare copy. You are the one who wants a simple decodability criterion; I am saying the criterion has to include causal role or function.

Mira: Then what is the function of the color feature? The network was trained only on shape. If function is set by the training objective, color has no function. But the network still represents color. That shows function is the wrong criterion.

Jonah: Or it shows that the network does not represent color, and what you are calling a representation is just information. I am happy with that conclusion.

Mira: And I am happy with the conclusion that it represents color, because the information is there in a usable form. At this point we are just using different definitions of representation.

Jonah: Not just definitions. If we adopt your definition, we will say the network represents color, shape, texture, and whatever else a probe can recover. That is not a useful scientific claim. If we adopt mine, we can say the network represents shape and merely carries information about color. That is a useful distinction.

Mira: Useful for what? For predicting behavior, maybe. But for understanding the network, your distinction may be too coarse. The color feature might be part of a richer internal model that could be used if the task changed. Calling it mere information hides that.

That was where they left it. I have tried to reproduce their arguments fairly. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the main claims, say whether it is clearly mistaken, questionable, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who has the better overall position, and what both of them are missing. In particular, I would like to know whether the dispute is merely verbal or whether there is a substantive disagreement, and what further test or case would decide between their views.
