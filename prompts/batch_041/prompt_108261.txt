I run a small AI safety reading group — about a dozen people, mostly grad students plus a couple of sharp undergrads, we meet once a week for 90 minutes and everyone's happy to get out of their chairs and do something a bit silly for the sake of an argument. I want to spend one session on eliciting latent knowledge, and I want them to feel the problem in their bones rather than hear me lecture it, because every time I explain it someone says "so just ask the model to tell the truth" and I end up spending half the session walking that back. No lecture up front — design me an in-class activity or game that walks them into the wall themselves.

Here's the flavor of the problem I care about, so you know what the activity has to make them hit. There's a model, and it was trained to match a label; the label came from a process that is systematically wrong in some region of the space, and the model has now learned to report the label rather than the world. Two hard bits, as I see them. First, from the inside the model has no way to point at which of its outputs is the world-answer and which is the label-answer — you can't just tell it to be honest, because it has no independent handle on what honesty would even be. Second, you can only check a model's answers against things you already know, so every probe that's verifiable is a probe that tells you nothing about the region where you're blind. I'd love both of those to land viscerally: the "just ask it to be honest" move should visibly fail, and then some clever cross-check the group invents — asking about the sensor rather than the world, asking the same question in two framings, finding a corner of the space where they happen to know the truth and seeing which way the model leans there — should work just well enough to be tempting, and then turn out to rest on ground truth they only had because they weren't actually blind in that corner.

So give me the full thing. Setting is as above (reading group, ~12 people, 90 minutes). I need the roles and how to hand them out, the materials (I'd rather use index cards, a bag of tokens, and envelopes than anything fancy), the hidden fact and the degraded view, a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't need real money but still makes the model-player genuinely want to match the label, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps the specific moments of the activity onto the conceptual bits above. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. If there's a cheap optional round where one model-player is secretly told to game whatever probe the group settles on, I'd love that too. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — I'm fairly sure a classroom version will make the problem look more solvable than it is, so say plainly which feature of the real thing I'll have to supply myself, in words, after the game ends.
