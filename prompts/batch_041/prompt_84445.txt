I'm a philosopher who works on the ethics and governance of AI, and my first training was in economics — contract theory and mechanism design are still the vocabularies I think in. I'd like you to translate a debate I keep having with machine learning colleagues into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what does not survive the trip.

The debate is about tool AI versus agent AI. One side holds that the systems worth building are tools: oracles, question-answerers, solvers we invoke and dismiss, systems whose behaviour is a function of our requests and which have no ends of their own, and which are therefore safe in the way a hammer is safe. The other side holds that this is either unattainable at scale or a distinction without a difference, because any system that reliably attains an objective across novel situations must plan, pursue sub-goals, and preserve its own capacity to act — which is to say it must be an agent, whatever we call it. The safety anxiety attached to the second camp is that such a system's objectives can come apart from ours in ways nobody specified and nobody foresaw.

(i) Restate the dispute in the terms of principal–agent theory and the economics of delegation: principals and agents, discretion and delegation, objective specification, moral hazard and adverse selection, monitoring and observability, incentive compatibility, complete versus incomplete contracts, residual control rights, and mechanism design when the principal cannot describe the relevant state space in advance.

(ii) Then give me the residue report, element by element: what translates cleanly, what translates only under distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I am especially interested in four things. (a) Does 'having a goal' survive translation, or is it quietly replaced by 'having a utility function over a fixed and commonly known outcome space'? (b) Is the tool/agent line a distinction between kinds of systems, or only between kinds of contracts we could write, or only between stances an observer can take? (c) We build the agent; its objectives are ours to specify. Economics takes the agent's preferences as given and treats conflict of interest as exogenous. Is that assumption doing illegitimate work when it is carried over, and if so, what would a framework look like in which the agent's objective is endogenous to the principal's design problem? (d) I suspect residual control rights are the closest analogue to the worry about a system's behaviour in states its designer never contemplated — tell me whether that is a translation or an analogy that flatters the economics.

(iii) Do not let the target framework's assumptions in as though they were translations. Where you must assume that preferences range over a shared and antecedently known outcome space, that the principal can commit, or that the agent has an outside option, flag the assumption rather than leaning on it. Then give me your verdict: does the residue mark a genuine gap in the economic vocabulary, or does it show that the tool/agent distinction was less substantive than the debate assumes?
