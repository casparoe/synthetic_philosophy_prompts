I'm in my second year of a philosophy degree, and this term I'm taking a course on Aristotle's logic and its later reception. Last week's seminar was on the ninth chapter of De Interpretatione, where Aristotle worries about whether a sea battle must happen tomorrow if it is already true today that it will; I followed the argument well enough in the room, but I came away unsure what it is supposed to establish about the words people actually use. The occasion for my confusion is small: a flatmate of mine follows football, and after a match whose result he had been dreading he shrugged and said it was always going to happen; when I asked what he meant by that, he offered, as though it settled the matter, that whatever will be, will be. I recognised the shape of the thought at once as the one the seminar had been circling, but I could not tell whether his slogan was the conclusion of the sea-battle argument, one of its premises, or something much weaker that needs none of it.

What I would like from you is not a defence of any of this but a disambiguation. Take the slogan and lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical: the reading on which it says nothing at all; the reading on which it says that no one could ever have acted otherwise; the reading on which it says only that statements about the future already have a determinate truth value; the reading on which the future is fixed but our deliberations nonetheless make a difference to it; the reading on which it is a claim about providence rather than about truth; and any further readings the phrase naturally carries that I have missed. For each, spell out what it commits one to, say which is most likely what my flatmate meant, and say which of them would actually do the consoling work he wanted it to do. My suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that the consolation needs far less machinery than the metaphysics that usually trails behind it.

Then, since the point of the seminar was that this puzzle bears on exactly such claims, tell me which premise each reading depends on, and what becomes of the reading when the premise is dropped. The candidates on my handout are: bivalence for future-tense statements; the idea that a statement's truth consists in things being as it says they are; truth being timeless rather than indexed to a time of utterance; the further step from the necessity of the consequence to the necessity of the consequent — that is, from the conditional's being necessarily true to the consequent's being necessary in itself; and the thought that no one has any power over the past. I should like to know which readings survive the loss of each of these, which collapse, and which of the surviving readings the fatalist in my seminar would actually need in order to get his conclusion. I would be grateful if you would write your answer in British English.
