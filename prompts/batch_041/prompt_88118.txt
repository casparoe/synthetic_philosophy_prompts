Long-running arguement in my epistemology seminar and I need an outside read on it. One of the guys keeps saying the whole foundations-vs-coherence fight is just the gold standard debate over again: foundationalists want every belief redeemable in something valuable in itself, the way a note used to be redeemable in gold, while coherentists are fine with fiat money, where a belief is worth something because the other beliefs will take it and the whole system holds up as long as everyone keeps honouring it. His punchline is that this shows foundationalism is nostalgia. Nobody's on the gold standard anymore, fiat money works, so the craving for a basic belief that justifies itself without any help is a relic, and a well-run circle of mutually supporting beliefs is all we ever needed or could have had. I'm fairly sure the analogy is carrying more weight than he's admitted, but when I try to say where, I just wave my hands.

So map it out for me. What plays the role of the gold, of convertibility, of the peg, of the central bank, of the speculator who attacks the peg, of the shop where you finally swap the note for bread, and of the closed loop that a currency union makes? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for what he's using it for -- I suspect the fiat-money story has an anchor he's quietly ignoring, and I want to know whether that anchor is a foundation in the epistemologist's sense or something else entirely. He also waves off the third option in that debate, the one where the chain of reasons just runs on forever with no first term and no circle at all; on his picture that should be a currency pegged to a currency pegged to a currency with nothing at the end, so tell me whether that makes it look stable, absurd, or merely underdetermined, and whether that verdict carries back over to the regress.

And the part I actually care about: does his conclusion survive? If fiat money needs the tax office and the supermarket, then maybe the real moral isn't 'no foundations' but 'the anchor isn't a belief', and I want to know whether that's a genuine third position or just foundationalism wearing a costume. If you think a better analogy is available for the same job, give me that instead.
