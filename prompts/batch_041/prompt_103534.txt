Write me a short story set about forty years out, in a world where a cheap biotech fix has mostly abolished suffering. Not a painkiller -- an actual re-engineering, given prenatally and available as a patch later, that keeps the brain's alarm system from ever tipping into real agony. Everyone's on it. The story follows one of the holdouts: a woman in her fifties who can still suffer, and who gets paid to sit on legal and ethics panels as a kind of witness, because the courts have ruled you only have standing to claim you were wronged if you're capable of the feeling. The thing I want the story to live inside: the abolitionists' own research found that the circuitry they damped is tangled up with grief, moral outrage, and the sharp edge of love, so she's the last person in the room who can be genuinely wounded -- and nobody can agree whether that makes her sacred or just the last place cruelty can land. Work in the consent problem too: the recalibrated generation never agreed to being made unable to suffer, and now can't feel the grievance either, so she's the only one who can even put words to what may have been done to them. No twist ending, no tidy moral. I want the texture of a world where suffering is optional and nearly gone, and the unease of a reader who can't tell whether its a paradise or a loss.
