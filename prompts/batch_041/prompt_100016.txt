I am a software engineer by profession and have no formal training in philosophy, though I have been sitting in on a reading group that has been working through Paul Grice's writings on meaning and conversation. I keep running into a puzzle at work that I suspect is really a philosophical puzzle in disguise, and I would like you to write a dialogue that brings out the two sides of it. The puzzle is this: in code review and in incident retrospectives, people often say things that seem to carry a criticism or a warning without ever asserting it. A staff engineer writes that she has noticed the migration has been taking a while, and everyone in the thread understands that she is saying the deadline is at risk, or that the author has been distracted. When the author asks her directly whether she meant that, she can say, truthfully, that she only said the migration has been taking a while. I have started calling this conversational debt: the speaker gets the communicative benefit of an implicature while keeping the deniability of a literal assertion. I want a dialogue between two engineers who have each developed a theory of what is going on, and who disagree about whether conversational debt is a real phenomenon or a confusion.

The first character, whom you can call the Intentionalist, holds something like the Gricean line: an implicature is present when the speaker intends to convey something beyond what she says, and intends the hearer to recognize that intention. On this view, the staff engineer did implicate that the deadline is at risk, and her later denial is either a lie about her own intention or a failure of self-knowledge. The second character, whom you can call the Normativist, holds that implicature is not a private mental fact but a public, norm-governed one: an utterance implicates what a reasonable hearer, given the standing norms of the conversation and the shared purposes of the participants, would be warranted in inferring. On this view, the staff engineer may have implicated nothing at all if the norms of the thread did not license the inference, or she may have implicated it even if she sincerely did not intend it. I want the two of them to go back and forth about the staff engineer case, about whether Grice's cooperative principle and his maxims can handle speakers who are not fully cooperative, about whether the case is a particularized or a generalized conversational implicature, and about whether the Intentionalist can make sense of deniability without collapsing into the Normativist's view. Neither should be a strawman; each should press the other on the strongest version of their position. The dialogue should be roughly 1200 to 1800 words, with at least six substantive exchanges from each character, and it should end with each saying what the other has forced them to give up or modify, and with a clear indication of which view, if either, the exchange has made more plausible. Please do not worry about citing sources or mentioning secondary literature; I care about the argument, not the bibliography.
