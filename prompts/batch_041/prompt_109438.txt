I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I fumble the terminology. My new book has a character in it — a woman who loses her faith not over suffering or science or anything dramatic, but because she spends a year sincerely, non-defensively trying to get some kind of answer and gets what feels like silence. She isn't angry, she isn't resisting anything, she's just open, and nothing comes. Her brother, who's a bit of a smug guy, tells her that if God existed He'd obviously make Himself known to anyone honestly looking. She doesn't have a good comeback and neither do I, which is why I'm asking you.

What I want is an essay on the whole divine hiddenness question — whether the fact that God (if there is one) isn't obviously available to everyone counts as genuine evidence against God's existence. Weigh the main moves on both sides: the thought that a real relationship has to be freely entered and can't be coerced, the thought that some kind of distance is what makes room for trust or moral growth, and the pushback that those answers only land for people who already believe something like that. But here's the thing I actually care about, and please don't skip it: I keep noticing that as a novelist I'm on every single page of my book, and yet you could read the whole thing cover to cover and never meet me in it. I'm not hidden because I'm absent — I'm hidden because the kind of thing I am can't show up as a character without wrecking the book. Is that a real analogy or am I flattering myself? Does it actually help the theist, or does it just relocate the problem — why would God build a world where meeting Him would wreck it? Steelman it properly before you tell me whether it survives.

Don't end on some wishy-washy both-sides-have-a-point note. I need a verdict I can put in her mouth at the end of chapter nineteen — either the hiddenness really is evidence against God, or it isn't and here's why, or something in between but stated precisely enough that she could say it out loud without sounding like she's dodging. I've thought about this alot and read enough theology-adjacent twitter to know the usual greatest hits, so please don't just list them; actually think about whether the author analogy does any work, and about the case of someone who isn't resisting anything at all, because that's the case my character is and the one the standard answers seem built to avoid.
