I am the governance architect for a federation of about forty member organizations, and I have to hand the board something by the end of the month that I can defend in the room.

The charter we adopted this year is meant to be genuinely self-amending: any provision in it, including the provisions that govern how amendments are made, can be changed by the amendment procedure the charter itself lays down. The members insisted on this; they did not want a document that only the founders could revise. We are now learning what the commitment costs. We have had an amendment fight in which two provisions each appeared to claim supremacy over the other. We have had a proposal to change the amendment procedure itself in a way that would make every later amendment easier for whoever holds the current majority. The board has asked me to entrench a short list of provisions so that they can never be altered, and I suspect that is not something a self-amending document can coherently promise. And no one can tell me who decides whether an amendment was validly adopted when the provision assigning that authority is the provision being amended.

I keep returning to the print of two hands, each drawing the other, and to the thought that in any such tangle there has to be a level that is not itself caught in the loop — a level at which the rules are simply fixed, and from which everything in the loop draws its meaning. I gather the idea comes out of formal systems, where the rules of inference and the interpretation of the symbols sit on a level that the system's own theorems cannot reach; our charter has no such level written into it anywhere, and I suspect that is the whole problem. I have been calling it the inviolate level. I want that thought turned into a procedure.

Please write the procedure.

Requirements. It should be a sequence of questions, run in order on a proposed provision or a proposed amendment, whose output is a placement verdict: inviolate — not subject to the charter's own amendment machinery; tangled — freely amendable by that machinery; or, where neither fits, a third verdict that names what would have to happen outside the charter for the provision to change. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, what to do when the answer is unclear or the facts are unavailable, and whether the stop is because the facts are missing or because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be runnable by a competent non-lawyer in about an hour, with no research into other organizations' charters or legal systems, and it has to yield a written verdict in a small fixed vocabulary — no more than five possible verdicts — so that the board can audit why a provision was placed where it was.

The procedure must handle at least these cases, and should say which question is doing the work in each:

1. A provision that says how the charter may be amended.
2. An amendment adopted by the correct procedure that changes that procedure.
3. Two provisions that each say they prevail over the other.
4. A provision the founders want to be unalterable forever.
5. The question of who decides whether an amendment was validly adopted, when the provision assigning that authority is the one in dispute.
6. A provision whose meaning depends on something outside the charter — a court, a registrar, a statute, or ordinary usage.
7. An amendment that changes what counts as a valid amendment for acts already done, so that past amendments may or may not survive.

Then apply the procedure in full to three proposals: the board's plan to entrench a list of provisions permanently; a member proposal to lower the amendment threshold from two-thirds to a simple majority, with a clause making the change effective immediately; and a proposal to submit the interpretation of the charter to an outside arbitrator whose rulings bind the federation. At least one of the three must come out in a way I will not like. Say plainly why I will not like it, and whether I should accept it anyway.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot decide. And the thing I most need: where the inviolate level in our case actually is. It may be nowhere in the document. If it is an unstated practice, a body whose authority comes from outside the federation, or simply the fact that the members could always walk away and constitute themselves again, I want that said in plain terms — and I want to know what it would look like from inside the system if the inviolate level itself were to shift.
