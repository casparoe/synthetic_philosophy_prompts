I have been compiling a colleague's verdicts on cases of material constitution, in the hope of predicting what she will say about a case of my own. Her record so far is this.

1. A bronze statue and the lump of bronze that occupies exactly its region are two numerically distinct objects, not one. They share all their matter; the difference is that the lump would survive being flattened into a sheet and the statue would not.
2. A cat and the mass of feline tissue that exactly coincides with it are one object, not two. She says the mass of tissue does not name a thing over and above the cat; only the cat is there.
3. If the statue is melted down, the statue ceases to exist while the bronze does not; if that same bronze is then recast into the very same shape, the result is a numerically different statue.
4. In the ship of Theseus, the ship kept afloat by the gradual replacement of its planks is the original ship, and the ship later reassembled from the discarded original planks is not.

The case I want to put to her is a beaver dam. It is a standing structure of felled branches and packed mud, raised across a stream by beavers, and it is patched and extended season after season, so that over several years every stick in it has been replaced while the dam itself is never rebuilt from scratch. The sticks are branches the beavers cut from living trees on the bank. Suppose that a flood later carries those sticks downstream and a different colony of beavers assembles them into a dam at a new site.

What will she say about the first dam — is it one object (branches and mud, arranged) or two (the dam, together with the material that constitutes it)? And what will she say about the second — is it the same dam again, or a new dam built out of the old dam's sticks?

Please set out the rules that fit her four earlier verdicts, say which of them her record leaves standing and which it rules out, and give me your best guess at the rule she is actually running, with your reasons. What I am really after is the principle that makes the bronze a thing in its own right alongside the statue while leaving the tissue with no such standing alongside the cat. Then name the further case that would best separate the surviving rules: I have wondered whether a spider's web, which is spun in place out of silk the spider produces as it goes rather than assembled from material that was already an object in its own right, would do that work, but I would rather you tell me which case you think is decisive.
