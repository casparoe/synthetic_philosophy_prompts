I teach a graduate seminar on information in biology, and every year I get students who arrive from computer science and formal semantics and want to know whether the slogan that DNA is a code is literally true. I have enough of a programming background to follow the analogies—syntax versus semantics, source code versus machine code, code versus data, compilation versus interpretation, and the difference between Shannon information and semantic content—but I am not a biologist, and I want to give them a translation exercise rather than a verdict. Please restate the main positions in the debate over genetic information and the DNA-as-code metaphor in that vocabulary. Distinguish at least three things that the slogan can mean: the codon table as a conventional mapping from triplets to amino acids; the gene as an instruction or program that the cell executes; and DNA as a representational vehicle whose information is about something, in the way that a sentence or a data structure is about something. For each, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in programming or formal semantics at all.

Then give me the residue report. I am particularly interested in a colleague's objection from developmental systems theory—the parity thesis, as she calls it: that DNA is only one resource among many in development, and that calling it the code privileges one cause among many. Can my vocabulary express that objection, or does it simply lack the resources to see the point? And what about the normative or teleological flavor of information talk—the sense in which a codon can be said to be correct or incorrect, or a gene to carry misinformation? Does that survive translation, or is it exactly the residue that shows the metaphor is doing more work than the vocabulary can cash out? I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the systematic translation and an honest account of what does not survive it.
