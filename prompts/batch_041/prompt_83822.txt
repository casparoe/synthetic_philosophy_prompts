I'm a third-year philosophy student, currently taking a seminar on weakness of will and rational choice, and I've also been hired to mark the midterms for the second-year course that feeds into it. Before I hand the scripts back, I'd like to check my reading of one answer. The exam question was:

Does hyperbolic discounting explain weakness of will? Discuss, making clear both what you take a case of weakness of will to involve and what the discounting model actually claims.

The answer I have to grade runs as follows:

Most of the classical discussion treats weakness of will as a puzzle about knowledge. An akratic agent judges, all things considered, that she ought to do A, and yet voluntarily does B; her enkratic counterpart does A despite a strong desire to do B. The puzzle is how the first case is possible at all, since the agent seems to have to judge A best and yet be motivated against A at the very same moment.

Hyperbolic discounting supplies the missing mechanism. On the standard exponential model, the value of a future reward decays at a constant rate, so the ranking of two rewards stays fixed as both draw nearer and preferences are time-consistent. If discounting is hyperbolic, by contrast, the discount rate declines with delay, so that a smaller-sooner reward which was dispreferred while both options were distant comes to be preferred once it is imminent. This is precisely the pattern of the akratic case: the dieter who prefers health to cake while the cake is a week away, and reverses that ranking as the cake arrives. What the model shows is therefore that weakness of will is a preference reversal rather than a failure of knowledge, which is why it is more accurate to describe the phenomenon as a conflict between successive selves than as a single agent contradicting herself.

The naive/sophisticated distinction sharpens the point. A naive discounter is one who sees the reversal coming and therefore arranges in advance to be constrained, buying a commitment device the way Odysseus has himself tied to the mast; the sophisticated discounter, by contrast, does not anticipate her own reversal and so repeatedly forms plans that she will not keep. It follows that the interesting question about the weak-willed agent is not whether she knows the good, but whether she can arrange her circumstances so that she need not rely on her future self's willpower.
