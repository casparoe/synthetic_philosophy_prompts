In my high school's philosophy club we have spent this term on the philosophy of time, and last week's meeting produced an argument I have not been able to settle in my own head. The basic division is familiar enough: the presentist holds that only the present moment is real, so that the past and the future are not "out there" anywhere, while the eternalist holds that past, present, and future moments all exist on an equal footing, with the present being merely the moment we happen to occupy. What made the meeting unusual was a thought experiment our faculty adviser invented on the spot, which she called the unrecorded afternoon. Suppose that on some ordinary afternoon a person briefly thinks one particular thought and then forgets it entirely: nothing he says or does afterward reflects it, no one else ever learns of it, and it leaves no physical trace in his brain, in any notebook, or anywhere else in the world. The adviser then asked whether, on a strictly presentist view, it can be true that the thought was ever thought at all, given that the past thought itself does not exist and no present fact seems to point back to it.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent, well-informed characters — a presentist and an eternalist — who are trying to work out together what the unrecorded afternoon shows and how each of their views can accommodate it. I want the characters to respond to each other's actual points rather than delivering speeches past one another: the presentist should get to offer the strongest reply available to her (for instance, concerning how truths about the past might be grounded, or what it even means to demand a truthmaker for them), and the eternalist should then have to answer that reply rather than merely restating his opening claim. Neither side should be a strawman, and neither should prevail simply by being handed the better lines.

As a final touch, please close with a last line beginning "Verdict:" in which you name which position, if either, your dialogue leaves better supported, followed by no more than a dozen words of justification.
