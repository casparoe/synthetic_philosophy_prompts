I've spent the better part of three years reporting on the mind-uploading beat — the conferences, the preprint threads, the very earnest people who want to know whether they'll survive the trip — and the debate over gradual replacement has gone strangely frozen. Everyone I interview agrees about the operational facts. Replace a neuron with a functionally identical silicon unit, one at a time, and the subject's memory, temperament, reports, and behavior are indistinguishable from before; do it all at once and you have a corpse; do it slowly enough and the subject walks out the far end insisting nothing happened. The camps have stopped arguing about those facts and started reciting themselves. One side holds that the person survives, because a person just is a pattern, and the new substrate carries the pattern exactly as well as the old one did; the other holds that the person dies somewhere along the way, because the continuity that matters is the continuity of the original living tissue, and a seamless copy is still a copy. Nobody has moved in years, and nobody will concede the obvious counterexamples — the slow version that everyone intuits survives, the instant version that everyone intuits doesn't.

What I want is not another round of adjudication but a diagnosis of the impasse itself. A colleague who studies classification disputes keeps telling me this is a familiar pattern wearing new clothes: one side treats "the same person" as a determinate natural fact to be discovered, the other treats it as a status we confer by decision or convention, so the two sides aren't so much contradicting each other as inhabiting different pictures of what kind of question is even being asked. I find that plausible and too quick. So tell me what the disagreement actually is, using what each side says rather than a survey of positions. Separate at least three things that get run together: whether the same person persists; whether the successor's future should matter to me now the way my own future does; and whether there is any fact about the first that could come apart from the second. Say which apparent disputes are really disputes about which of those questions, and whether the rival formulations — the lights staying on versus a new light coming on in the same socket — name anything beyond two ways of describing one physical process. Consider, too, whether the camps are quietly divided by a background commitment they've never surfaced, for instance about whether persisting things are extended-through-time worms or three-dimensional objects wholly present at each moment.

Then press on a wrinkle that I think neither camp has absorbed. Suppose the replacement is done as a relay: each silicon unit is later swapped back out for a fresh biological neuron, so that the endpoint is a wholly biological brain again, with the same pattern and no experiential seam. If the biological-continuity side still says the original died, it looks as though its criterion was never really about the material at all; if it now says the original lived, it looks as though it was tracking the pattern all along and merely misdescribing what it cared about. Does that symmetry-breaker work, and if it does, which side does it damage? End with the honest bottom line: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a thought experiment that genuinely breaks the tie, a formal result about continuity, a change of question — or is the correct answer that no progress should be expected, because this dispute is doing work that argument cannot do?
