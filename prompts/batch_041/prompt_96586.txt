I write a weekly column on philosophy and the human sciences for an educated general readership, and I am trying to build a piece about the everyday business of deciding whom to believe — not the courtroom drama or the laboratory scandal, but the ordinary case in which someone tells you something you have no way to check and you either take it or leave it. The two positions I keep running into are the ones usually labelled reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony: on one side, the thought that a hearer needs positive grounds, some track record or inductive basis, before another person's word can give her a reason to believe anything; on the other, the thought that a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told by default and needs a specific defeater before doubt is even in order. The standard exchanges have gone stale on me — the child learning her first words, the impossibility of auditing every link in a chain of transmission, the threatened regress of reasons, the sheer ubiquity of testimony — so I want a dialogue that works a case those exchanges never touch.

The case is this. A hearer has no independent access to some subject: she cannot go and check, and there is no one else to ask. She puts her question to the only person who plausibly knows, a source who appears to her both sincere and competent. He answers in two strokes. He tells her what is believed in his field — call it q. And he tells her that his field is a poor place to get beliefs from: that claims in it are routinely overstated, that the people who make them are mostly guessing, and that she should give anything she hears about the subject, his own present testimony included, very little weight. Both statements are made sincerely, in one breath, and both parties know that the hearer has no other route to q. I would like a reductionist and an anti-reductionist to take that case apart together: what the hearer's epistemic situation actually is, whether she may believe q, whether she may believe the warning, and whether the two verdicts can be held at once.

A few constraints, since I intend to run the result past people who will hold me to them. The two speakers must be equally competent and equally well informed; neither is a foil, and neither may win by caricature — the reductionist may not simply declare that unchecked testimony is worthless, and the anti-reductionist may not simply declare acceptance the default and stop there. They should answer each other's actual points rather than talk past one another, concede where a point lands, and introduce a fresh distinction only while flagging that they are doing so. Neither may quietly amend the case; if one of them wants a variant, he has to say what work the amendment does and whether it changes the verdict. I would also like them to identify which feature of the case is doing the real work — the content of what is said, the hearer's lack of any alternative route, or the source's volunteering the warning — and to say what becomes of their verdicts if that feature is stripped out. Let each of them, somewhere in the exchange, name the consideration that would move him toward his opponent's side. Then close in your own voice rather than either character's, in a short paragraph, saying which of the two you think has the better of the case as specified, what would change your mind, and where your own reasoning is likeliest to have gone wrong.
