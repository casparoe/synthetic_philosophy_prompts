Question (12 pts). Two players, A and B, alternate moves, with A moving first. There are at most four moves. At each move the player whose turn it is chooses either Take, which ends the game immediately, or Pass, which hands the move to the other player; if all four moves are Passes, the game also ends. Payoffs are listed as (A, B):

- Move 1 Take: (1, 0)
- Move 2 Take: (0, 2)
- Move 3 Take: (3, 1)
- Move 4 Take: (2, 4)
- All four Pass: (5, 3)

(i) Solve the game by backward induction and state the equilibrium outcome. (4 pts)
(ii) Is the equilibrium outcome Pareto efficient? Explain. (2 pts)
(iii) This is a version of the "centipede" game. Explain what it is supposed to show about backward induction, and say how the experimental evidence relates to it. (6 pts)

And here's the student's answer:

---
(i) Backward induction works from the last decision node. At move 4, B is choosing between Take, which gives him 4, and Pass, which ends the game and gives him 3. Since 4 > 3, B takes. At move 3, A knows that if she passes, B will take at move 4 and A will end up with 2; if she takes, she gets 3. So A takes. At move 2, B knows that if he passes, A will take at move 3 and B will get 1; if he takes, he gets 2. So B takes. At move 1, A knows that if she passes, B will take at move 2 and A will get 0; if she takes, she gets 1. So A takes. The game ends on the first move with outcome (A=1, B=0). This is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

(ii) No, it is not efficient. If both players passed at every opportunity the outcome would be (5, 3), and both players prefer 5 to 1 and 3 to 0. So the equilibrium is Pareto-dominated by an outcome that is right there in the game.

(iii) The point of the centipede game, which I think was introduced by Rosenthal, is that backward induction is a completely airtight argument and yet it leads to a bad outcome — everyone can see they'd all be better off passing, but the logic forces the first player to take. The natural reading is that the players in the game are being irrational, and the experimental literature backs this up: when the centipede game is run in the lab, subjects almost always take on the first move, which is exactly what the theory predicts. The philosophically interesting bit is that taking is the dominant strategy at every single node, so a player who passes is leaving money on the table no matter what the other player does, which is why people say rationality and cooperation come apart here.
