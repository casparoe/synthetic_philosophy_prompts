I'm a novelist and I've somehow agreed to run a 90-minute session at a small writers' festival — about 14 people, mixed bag: three or four who can actually draw, one retired graphic designer, everyone else types for a living. What I want them to walk out with is the feeling that there's no shape you can point at and say *that's* what an 'a' is, and yet all of them read that last clause without blinking. An 'a' isn't a shape, it's a job — and whether some squiggle is doing that job depends on what all the other letters in the room are doing.

So design me an activity that makes them hit that themselves, before I say one word about it. Here's my rough sketch, scrap it if you've got something better: everyone gets a secret style card — one line, like "everything leans 15 degrees right," "no curves anywhere," "every stroke the same weight," "the letters look slightly melted" — and hand-letters the same short word in that style. We cut out just the a's and the group sorts them into definitely-an-a / not-an-a piles, then I reveal the full words, and some of the rejects turn out to be perfectly good a's in their own typeface while some of the confident accepts turn out to be the d or the g. Then a round where one table hands a half-finished alphabet to another, they have to supply the missing letters, and the first table judges whether the new ones belong.

I need materials, a running order with timings, some way to keep the people who "can't draw" from checking out, the small stakes or prize, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the actual point without me turning into a lecturer. Also — what will definately go wrong the first time I run it, and what part of this idea does the activity simply not touch, so I know what I have to say out loud myself? And is it worth name-checking the Hofstadter letter-spirit thing at the end, or does that just let everyone feel they've understood something? I'd rather they left a bit puzzled.
