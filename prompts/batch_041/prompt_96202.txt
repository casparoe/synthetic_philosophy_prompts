He's really smooth, so I don't want to just sit there while he does the whole "war is hell and the poor soldiers didn't start it" speech. I want to actually press him on the argument. Can you write me nine or ten questions, and for each one a follow-up that's keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so I can keep going no matter how he wiggles?

Here's the stuff I want to make sure we hit, cause these are the parts that seem shaky to me:

- The difference between saying both sides have the same permissions and saying they both just have an excuse. Those seem like really different claims to me and I want to know which one he's actually defending. The follow-up should catch him if he slides from one to the other halfway through his answer.
- Drone operators and cyber guys who kill people from an office and are never themselves in danger. The usual story seems to be that it's like two people in a fight, each is a threat to the other, so it's even. But that falls apart when one side is never at risk at all. Does his view stretch to cover them, or does it only work for people who can actually get shot?
- A war where neither side is really in the right — some messy thing where both sides are partly the agressor, or the original cause is genuinely contested. If the whole doctrine needs one side just and one side unjust, what happens when it's a muddle? Does the equality just stop?
- Whether this is a claim about individual soldiers or a rule for armies and states and institutions — like is it "this soldier is allowed to shoot that one," or is it "we should run wars this way for other reasons entirely"? Those seem different and I don't think he always keeps them apart.
- Conscripts and child soldiers. Can somebody who had no choice and doesn't even understand the war really be morally equal to a volunteer who believes in the cause? That seems obviously false to me but maybe he has something.

Also throw in one question about whether the view has basically been redefined into "it's a useful convention," and whether that's a problem for it. And end on something like: what case or argument would actually make him give it up?

Two things I don't want: questions where he just defines terms for ten minutes, or questions about what he thinks of some specific recent war. Keep it on the actual arguements. And no bibliography or "what should I read" stuff, that would be dead air on a podcast.
