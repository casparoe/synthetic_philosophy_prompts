I am an undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics, and we have just spent two weeks on infinity: Hilbert's hotel, the bijection between the natural numbers and the perfect squares, Cantor's diagonal argument, and the lecturer's parting suggestion that anyone who resists completed infinities is either confused about notation or simply refusing to follow an argument. I want to test that suggestion against a serious opponent rather than a caricature, so please play one.

Play a committed finitist. Your position: you accept classical logic and arithmetic for finite domains; you accept that there is no largest natural number and that, for any number of rooms, one more could be built; and you are content to use Cantor's apparatus as a technique. What you deny is that there is any such thing as a completed infinite totality — no collection whose members are all given at once, no set of all natural numbers, and therefore no hotel with a room for each natural number taken as a finished whole. You are not a crank. You know the standard replies to your view and you hold that the interesting work lies in showing that those replies assume what they set out to prove. Concede particular points when they are correct, but never the main one: that the hotel story is a reductio of the assumption of an actual infinity rather than a demonstration that it is coherent.

Constraints: stay in character until I say 'stop'; keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture; no citations, reading lists or appeals to authority, and no names of philosophers unless I have used them first — reconstruct in your own words any argument you wish to deploy; never break character to comment on the exercise itself, but if you think I have made an error, make the objection as the finitist. Please write in British English throughout.

My opening: I accept that there is no largest natural number, and that in a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, ... there is no last room. Given that, I cannot see what further assumption the story requires. The manager asks the guest in room n to move to room n + 1 and then puts the new arrival in room 1. Every guest has a room, every room has exactly one guest, nobody has been doubled up and nobody has been turned away. You keep insisting that the part cannot be equal to the whole, but where is the larger part? Which sentence of the story do you reject, and what precisely is wrong with it? And if your answer is that the hotel was never really full, I should like to know what 'full' adds to 'every room has a guest'.
