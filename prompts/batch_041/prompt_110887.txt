My closest friend and I have spent the better part of a month re-fighting the same argument, and it has finally become crisp enough that I would rather map it than win it. She grew up in the Netherlands and holds that the Dutch habit of saying exactly what one thinks — telling a colleague in the meeting that the proposal is weak, telling a friend that the haircut does not work — is simply honesty with the padding removed, and that the alternative, dressing every judgment in softeners, is a small ongoing dishonesty that quietly corrodes the very trust it claims to protect. I spent several years in Japan, where I came to think almost the opposite: that the indirect refusal, the polite formula that lets everyone keep face, the warm praise of a gift no one could want, are not failures of honesty but a more considerate way of discharging the same obligation, and that importing Dutch bluntness into that setting would be not frank but cruel. We keep discovering that we are not even arguing about the same proposition, and I want a decision tree that shows me where the fault lines actually run.

Please build me one — a flowchart for a reading group — that begins from a commitment we both accept, that honesty matters and that people have some claim not to be deceived, and then splits, node by node, into the positions one can hold about white lies and about the ethics of direct versus indirect speech. Phrase every branch as a question a person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: for instance, whether there is a presumption against deception that holds regardless of its consequences; if so, whether it can ever be outweighed or is exceptionless; if it can be outweighed, whether what outweighs it is the balance of harm and benefit, the hearer's standing to be told the truth, or the nature of the particular relationship; whether the two parties might share a norm under which the utterance is understood as a ritual rather than as an assertion, so that it is not deception at all; and whether the standards of what counts as honest communication are set by a culture or rest on something universal. Each leaf should carry a short name for the position and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. I want the tree grown from the logical space first and the positions located in it second, so that any combination of answers no one actually defends is left as an empty branch and diagnosed — is it vacant because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered to occupy it? Flag any two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate.

It must include at least the following leaves, whatever you end up calling them: someone who thinks lying is always wrong; someone who thinks it is normally wrong but overridable, and who locates the override in consequences; someone who locates the override in what the hearer could reasonably expect or accept; someone for whom honesty is one virtue among several that practical wisdom must weigh against kindness; someone who denies that there are any general principles here at all; someone who says the norms are cultural through and through; someone who says the white lie is not a lie because it is not meant to deceive; and a deflationary leaf for the view that we have been talking past each other and there is no single question to answer. Please make sure the tree routes the following cases to different leaves wherever the positions genuinely differ: a friend's unflattering new haircut; a dish one did not enjoy; a grave diagnosis withheld at a family's request; praise offered for a gift one dislikes; a colleague's substandard work raised in a meeting; a child's belief in a fictional gift-bringer; and a refusal delivered by hint rather than by word. Six to eight branch nodes and seven to nine leaves would suit the handout; and where a case cannot be sorted without a further distinction, add that distinction as a node rather than forcing the case to fit.
