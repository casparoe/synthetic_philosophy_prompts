I do a tiny podcast out of my apartment — mostly me and a friend talking to anyone who'll come on — and next month the guest is a decision theorist whose whole thing is the absent-minded driver problem. Not the friendly version of it: she thinks the problem is a real crack in decision theory rather than a puzzle people just get confused by, and she gets annoyed when it's waved away as a mistake about conditional probabilities. I've read enough to follow the set up (driver on a highway, two exits, one pays nothing, one pays a lot, overshooting pays a little, and he can't tell the intersections apart or remember whether he's already passed one), but I can never keep the payoffs strait and I get lost the second anyone starts doing the math. I'm not a philosopher, I did two years of econ and dropped it, so I need questions I can actually ask out loud without bluffing.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, in the order I'd ask them, with a follow-up attached to at least four of them that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? Things I want covered: (1) an opening request that she walk me through one concrete version of the problem with actual numbers, since I want to hear her pin down exactly where the contradiction is supposed to live and whether it survives once the payoffs are written out carefully; (2) the difference between choosing a whole policy before you start driving and choosing at the intersection with only what you know right then — which of those two gets to be called the rational one, and whether a theory that says different things about them is genuinely in trouble; (3) whether the driver is one agent or two (or a small team), given that the guy at the first intersection and the guy at the second share a body but no memory, and whether thinking of him as a team changes anything; (4) whether the memory failure is doing all the work — what happens if he can leave himself a note, tie the wheel, or use some trick to tell the intersections apart, and whether that shows the problem is really about memory or about something else entirely; (5) whether anything practical hangs on it, since I keep thinking of cases where a person's memory genuinely fades between two decision points, or of software that can't keep track of which step it's on; and (6) whether the whole thing leans on a technical assumption that a decision-maker has to be able to list in advance all the situations they might be in, and if so what becomes of decision theory if you drop that. I don't want anything where she just restates the puzzle or says it's a fun paradox — I want the questions where she has to commit to something and can't wriggle out. And please don't ask her what we should read on it; that's no use to us.
