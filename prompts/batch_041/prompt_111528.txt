I'm in a grad seminar on formal epistemology and we just did Aumann's agreement theorem last week. The martingale step in the proof I can just about follow, and I believe the result, but I've come out the other side more confussed than when I went in, and not in the productive way. Here's the thing that's nagging me: my roommate and I have what I would have sworn was the same prior about how reliable our mutual friend Dave is — same upbringing, same general view of him, we've compared notes — and last night we both read the exact same email from him, and we both know the other one read it, and yet I'm convinced it was a passive-aggressive snub while she thinks it was just him being terse and busy. Neither of us is being dumb about it, neither of us is budging, and the disagreement feels pretty stable, not like something that's going to dissolve with more conversation. Someone in the seminar said this just shows the common prior assumption is false, which felt like a cheap way out, but I honestly can't tell if that's the right diagnosis or if I'm misunderstanding what the theorem is even claiming.

So what I want is an explanation of a couple of things, and please don't just rehash the proof at me. First: what is the theorem actually a claim about? Everyone talks about it as if it were a procedure — you tell me your credence, I tell you mine, we each update, eventually we converge — but that seems like a misreading, since the result looks like an impossibility claim about a certain kind of informational state and appears to say nothing whatsoever about how an agent should revise upon learning that someone smart disagrees with them. If it's really just a static impossibility result, what licenses the leap to claims about actual disagreement, rather than it just being a consistency check on an idealization? Second: is the common prior assumption a norm of rationality or an empirical claim about people? If it's a norm, it looks like you're forced to say my roommate and I are both irrational, which seems way too strong; if it's empirical, the theorem seems to lose all its bite as a claim about what rational agents must do. And a third thing, which might be where I'm actually going wrong: is "having the same evidence" even well-defined here? Maybe the right thing to say is that the email isn't the same evidence for the two of us, because it's sitting on top of different background knowledge — but then I don't understand how the theorem's notion of evidence (whatever partitions are supposed to represent) relates to the everyday notion, and whether the "same evidence" case I keep worrying about is even expressible in the formalism. Also, just so you don't waste time on it: I have no trouble at all with the idea that agents can be uncertain about each other's evidence, so please don't spend the answer on that.
