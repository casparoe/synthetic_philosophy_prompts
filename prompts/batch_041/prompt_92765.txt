For the past term a small reading group I sit in on has been working through the literature on diachronic rationality, and we keep circling back to van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the thought, roughly, that if I now assign credence x to the proposition that at some later time I will assign credence y to A, then my present credence in A conditional on my later credence being y should itself be y. We have all the stock cases in front of us and we can all see why they are supposed to embarrass the principle: the case where I am told that at midnight I will be given a drug that will leave me 0.9 confident that a fair coin landed heads no matter how it actually landed, so that my present conditional credence in heads given my midnight credence of 0.9 ought to be 0.9 by the principle but is obviously only 0.5; the cases where I know I will be distracted, or will simply forget evidence I now hold, or will have been lobotomised into complacency. What frustrates me is that after a term nobody has moved an inch. One faction insists Reflection is simply a coherence requirement on a rational agent's credences, on a par with probabilism, and that the counterexamples show only that an agent in that situation is epistemically unlucky, not that the requirement is false; the other insists the counterexamples show the principle is false as a requirement and survives at best as a descriptive remark about agents whose future selves happen to be trustworthy. Neither side seems to be making a locatable mistake, and the exchange has the feel of two people who agree on every case and disagree about what the cases are cases of.

My own suspicion — and I would like you to treat it as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a position to be defended — is that the stall comes from running together two quite different things one might mean by treating a future credence as a reason. On the first reading, my future credence is a measurement of my own reliability, and the principle is really a claim about a reliability function: for each possible future credence value y, the objective chance that A is true given that my future self will be at y. Reflection then holds exactly when that function is the identity, and the drug case is simply a case where it is not, which makes the principle look contingent and empirical and makes the disagreement look like a dispute about which reliability profiles are possible. On the second reading, my future credence is the verdict of a process of inquiry that I endorse, and the principle is a claim about what it is to take one's own future reasoning seriously, which makes it look necessary but only for agents who already satisfy some further condition. I cannot tell whether this distinction is doing real work, whether both sides already have it in hand and the actual disagreement lies elsewhere, or whether my reliability-function reconstruction captures what either side means at all.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on Reflection. Is this disagreement verbal — two groups attaching different senses to rational requirement or to one's future self? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, about when a reliability function can be the identity and under what description? Is it two different questions being asked under one heading — a question about which credences are internally coherent and a question about which credences are justified or reliable — such that each side is right about its own question and neither has noticed? Or is it competing bedrock commitments about what epistemic norms are for, which no further case-work will settle? Say what each side is actually committed to, whether the reliability-function reconstruction of mine captures either of them or distorts both, and what would count as progress here: a distinction, a theorem, a counterexample of a kind nobody has produced, or a reason to think the question should simply be dissolved rather than answered. I am not after a survey or a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
