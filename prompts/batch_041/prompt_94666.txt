I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this term, and we've just finished the unit on the causal exclusion argument. The instructor has asked each of us to write up our own position in a page or so and circulate it before the next meeting, so that the seminar can press us on our own views rather than on the textbook versions of the debate. Here is my statement, and I'd like you to treat it as the thing to be examined. I am a non-reductive physicalist, and these are the commitments I would put my name to. (1) Mental properties — being in pain, believing that it will rain — are real properties of persons. They are not identical with any physical property, and they are realized by physical properties and supervene on them in this sense: no difference in a person's mental properties without a difference in that same person's physical properties. (2) The physical domain is causally closed: every physical effect that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause. (3) I accept a causal exclusion principle: an effect that already has a sufficient physical cause does not also have a distinct mental cause, since that would amount to systematic overdetermination of the physical by the mental. (4) All the same, I take mental causation to be real, and to be real in virtue of content. My taking an umbrella is caused by my belief that it will rain, and it is caused by that belief as the belief it is — alter what the belief represents and you alter the action. (5) I take the correct account of causation to be a difference-making one: to say that X caused Y is to say that an intervention on X would have made a difference to Y. (6) I am an externalist about content: what a belief represents depends on the believer's environment and history, and not only on what is inside her body. (7) I hold a locality constraint on causation as well: the transaction that produces a bodily movement is local and intrinsic, so the difference-makers for what my body does are all in my body, and not in the relations my body bears to its surroundings. Finally, here is the verdict I want to defend. Take the familiar pair of subjects with molecule-for-molecule identical bodies whose beliefs differ only in content because their environments differ. I want to say that the two of them act for different reasons, that each acts because of what she believes, and that this difference is not merely a difference in how we happen to describe them.

What I need from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these commitments pairwise and sort them: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be in tension once you notice what one of the commitments actually amounts to, and which of them are idle — doing no work that the others don't already do. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say precisely which commitment would have to move, and say what moving it would cost me elsewhere in the package; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most with the least collateral damage, and whether that revision leaves my verdict about the two subjects intact. I don't want to be told to be less metaphysically ambitious, and I don't want to be sent off to read a debate; I want the specific commitment that has to give and the specific consequence of giving it.
