I am an undergraduate in a seminar on eighteenth-century political thought, and our final assignment permits a creative piece in place of a conventional essay. I have chosen to write a piece of sustained institutional satire, and I would like you to draft it.

The scenario is this: the cultural affairs office of a mid-sized European city has produced a staff report recommending that the municipality wind down its subsidy of the professional playhouse and redirect the funds to a programme of free open-air civic festivals. The office's reasoning is taken, without acknowledgement and apparently without awareness, from Rousseau's case against d'Alembert's proposal to bring a theatre to Geneva. Treat the following as the report's settled findings, and let them do the argumentative work: that the actor's craft consists in displaying sentiments he does not possess and in being paid to be someone other than himself; that this is tolerable in a large and already corrupted capital, where people wish to remain hidden from one another, but ruinous in a small republic whose citizens are meant to be legible to each other; that tragedy's pity is a passive and sterile emotion which costs the spectator nothing and issues in no action; that comedy is worse still, since it recruits the audience's sympathy against the honest man and rewards the wit who exposes him; that the stage's treatment of love renders an entire audience effeminate and hands the government of taste to the women in the boxes; and that the proper alternative is a festival in which the distinction between performers and spectators collapses, because the citizens are themselves the spectacle.

Please write the report at roughly 750 words, in the flat, self-assured register of a genuine municipal document: a heading, several numbered sections, and a short appendix giving the proposed festival calendar. The piece must never wink, never signal that it is satire, and never name Rousseau or any other author; it should read as the office's own independent findings. It must concede, at some point, that a republic owes its citizens amusement, and must then explain why the festivals are not, properly speaking, entertainment — this concession is essential and should be handled without embarrassment. The humour should arise entirely from the accuracy of the reasoning and the deadpan of the administrative prose, so that a reader who knows the argument recognises it being taken with perfect seriousness, while a reader who does not still finds the document plausible. Do not state any of the obvious replies to this line of argument; leave them to the reader to supply unaided, and make sure they are felt rather than announced. Do not quote or cite anything, and avoid naming real institutions or individuals.
