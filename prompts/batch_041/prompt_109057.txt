I would like you to analyze a real episode in the history of decision theory through the lens of the theory it helped to destabilize. The episode is the choice problem Maurice Allais put to an audience of economists and decision theorists in the early 1950s, together with Leonard Savage's response to it. The problem comes in two pairs. In the first pair, the agent chooses between a sure million and a lottery that gives an eighty-nine percent chance of a million, a one percent chance of nothing, and a ten percent chance of five million. In the second pair, the agent chooses between a lottery that gives an eighty-nine percent chance of nothing and an eleven percent chance of a million, and a lottery that gives a ninety percent chance of nothing and a ten percent chance of five million. Most people choose the sure million in the first pair and the second lottery in the second pair. Allais's point was that this pattern violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory, and Savage, when the problem was put to him, is reported to have made exactly that pair of choices. He treated the episode as revealing an error in his own preferences rather than as a refutation of the axiom.

I want a careful case analysis that runs in both directions. First, map the episode onto the theory. State exactly what the independence axiom requires here, and why the common pair of choices violates it. Then distinguish the independence axiom from the sure-thing principle, which is the principle Savage himself formulated as a condition on acts rather than on lotteries, and say whether the Allais problem is best understood as a violation of one, the other, or both. Consider the further distinction between the common consequence effect, which is the form the Allais problem usually takes, and the common ratio effect, which is a different violation of the same family, and say which one this episode actually instantiates. Consider also whether the sure-thing principle requires an action-independence condition, and whether the Allais problem satisfies it. Say where the mapping is strained and where the theory is simply silent.

Then turn to Savage's response. He called his own choice an error. Is that the right diagnosis, or does it beg the question against Allais? What would it take for the pattern of choices to count as a genuine counterexample to the axiom rather than as evidence of a defect in the chooser? Allais himself thought the axiom was not normatively valid, and he offered an alternative account of rational choice under risk. Reconstruct the strongest version of his position and the strongest version of Savage's, and say which you find more defensible. Consider whether the disagreement is really about the axiom or about the underlying conception of rationality, and whether one side can be convicted of smuggling in a substantive value judgment under the guise of a logical requirement.

Then say where the expected-utility framework breaks. What features of the episode does it fail to capture? Consider the roles of regret, disappointment, the certainty effect, and the possibility that the chooser cares about the resolution of uncertainty rather than only about the final distribution of outcomes. Does the violation require a non-consequentialist or non-additive framework, or can it be accommodated within a more general expected-utility theory? Consider the resolute-choice and sophisticated-choice responses, and whether they make the violation rational. Say which of these moves you think actually helps and which merely redescribe the phenomenon.

Finally, say what the episode shows about the epistemic status of the independence axiom. Is it a normative requirement of rationality, a descriptive generalization about how people happen to choose, or a substantive and controversial value judgment? Does the Allais episode confirm the theory, expose an anomaly, or present a case the theory never anticipated? Give a verdict on whether expected utility theory remains the correct theory of rational choice under risk, and say what would have to be true for the axiom to be abandoned rather than merely qualified.
