I am a novelist at work on a book that treats temporal order as optional, and I have been rereading Slaughterhouse-Five alongside Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations—the solution in which the universe rotates, closed timelike curves exist, and, as Gödel argued, there is no global time order, so time is ideal rather than real. I have been calling the novel's central maneuver the Dresden escape hatch: the way Billy Pilgrim's unstuckness and the Tralfamadorian view that all moments are simultaneous suspend sequence and, with it, the ordinary claims of memory and guilt. Please read the novel through Gödel's views on time and rotating universes. Does Vonnegut's Tralfamadorian eternalism genuinely resemble Gödel's conclusion, or does it conflate a block universe, in which all times equally exist, with Gödel's more radical claim that there is no global time at all? What does the novel's reversed film of Dresden—bombs returning to the planes, the dead rising—do to Gödel's claim that time is ideal? And where does the philosophical idea resist the reading, so that the honest critic must say the analogy breaks down? I want the criticism to be faithful to both the novel and the physics, and to say what the novel adds to the debate that argument alone does not.
