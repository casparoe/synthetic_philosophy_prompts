I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last week I ended up as the unwilling referee in a fight at a board-game convention dinner that I'm still chewing on. Two friends of mine — Theo, a game designer, and Mira, a mathematician — went at each other for about an hour over the strategy-stealing argument, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. I had to have the thing explained to me twice, so let me set it up for you the way I now understand it.

Strategy-stealing is the trick where you prove the first player wins a game without ever finding the winning move. You suppose the second player has a winning strategy. Then you note that the first player can make some throwaway first move — provided that having an extra move can never hurt you — and afterwards just play the second player's supposed strategy. So both players would be guaranteed to win, which is absurd, so the second player never had a winning strategy to begin with, so (if there are no draws) the first player wins. The trick is usually credited to Nash, for the game Hex; it also works on Chomp, where you can show the first player wins on any rectangle, even though for a general board nobody knows a general formula for the winning first bite. It's a proof by contradiction that hands you no strategy at all.

Theo had just designed a new game — call it Lattice — that's symmetric, has no draws, and where, he says, adding one of your own pieces never takes away an option you already had. He ran the argument and announced the first player has a forced win. Then his playtesters kept losing as the first player, and he still couldn't name the winning opening. That's when Mira weighed in. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Theo: It's a proof, period. The second player can never have a winning strategy in Lattice, so the first player wins. I didn't have to play a single game to know that.
2. Mira: It's a proof that gives you nothing to play. It's a reductio — it never exhibits the strategy. For all the argument tells you, the winning move could be some specific square on move 40 that no human will ever find.
3. Theo: Existence proofs are real knowledge. It killed a whole class of second-player openings before I'd even built the board, and it told me my game wasn't a drawfest. In math you constantly know a thing exists without being able to produce it.
4. Mira: Except the only interesting premise isn't the stealing step, it's your lemma that an extra piece never hurts you. The stealing step is trivial — "if they had a winning strategy, I'd copy it." All the real content is in "extra move never hurts," and that's not a logical truth, it's a property of particular games. Chess fails it: zugzwang, where being forced to move is what loses you the game. So you can't wave this at any symmetric no-draw game; you have to check the lemma, and if the lemma is doing all the work, then your grand-sounding theorem is just a lemma in a costume.
5. Theo: Fine — but I did check the lemma for Lattice, it's monotone by construction, and the "trivial" step is what turns a fact about monotonicity into a fact about who wins. That's the point of a general argument: same trivial step, dozens of different games, reusable in games nobody's invented yet.
6. Mira: But in any game small enough to actually solve, the argument is redundant — you'd just look up the answer. And in games too big to solve, it hands you a fact no one can use. Where does it ever earn its keep?
7. Theo: It earns its keep by telling you to stop looking for a second-player strategy, and sometimes it's the only thing known. It's how we know the first player wins Chomp on every rectangle, including the ones where nobody can name the winning first move. And it's how Hex was known to be a first-player win before anyone had a strategy.
8. Mira: Which is exactly the strange part. "The first player wins, but no one knows how" is a weird kind of knowledge for a game — like knowing a treasure is buried somewhere in the country. And it can mislead: you can prove the first player wins, watch every first player lose, and start wondering whether your proof leaned on something that isn't actually true.
9. Theo: It even survives when you allow passing. In chess with a free pass, the first player can guarantee at least a draw: if you've got a winning move, play it, otherwise pass. So it's not as fragile as you're making it.
10. Mira: That version only gets you "at least a draw," and only because the pass is free. Make the pass cost something, or let it be used once, and the trick collapses. And in Go, where passing is legal and free, the game long ago added compensation points to the second player precisely to kill the first player's edge — and the moment you do that the position isn't symmetric anymore and strategy-stealing stops working altogether. So the argument dies exactly when the game gets balanced, which is suspicious.
11. Theo: No, that's the argument doing real design work. Komi exists because someone understood that a symmetric board with free passing gives the first player a draw at worst. The theorem is what tells you a patch is needed.
12. Mira: Then you can't have it both ways for a commercial game: either it's symmetric and the first player has a forced win — unfair to the second — or you add compensation and the proof is gone. And "the first player wins" that no first player can execute doesn't tell you which side of that you're on.
13. Theo: Right, and that's a diagnostic. If your symmetric no-draw game can't be strategy-stolen, then there's some zugzwang or non-monotonicity hiding in it, and knowing that is worth something.
14. Mira: And notice it never explains why the first player wins. It shows they must, by contradiction. It never tells you where the advantage lives.
15. Theo: It does explain: the reason is the combination of symmetry and monotonicity. The first player's spare tempo has to be worth something, so the game can't be a draw, so it's a win. That's a structural explanation, and structural explanations are explanations.
