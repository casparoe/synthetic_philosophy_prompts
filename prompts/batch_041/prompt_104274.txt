I want to pressure-test the separation of belief, value, and risk that risk-weighted expected utility is built on. Please play a committed defender of that theory: someone who holds that a rational agent's attitude to risk is a primitive feature of her preferences, on a par with her beliefs and her desires, and who will give up the sure-thing principle rather than give up that separation. Be sharp and make me work for it; concede that the risk function needs a rationale, but never concede that it reduces to a second utility function. Keep each reply to a paragraph, so it feels like an argument rather than a lecture. My opening: your risk function takes the entire probability distribution and reshapes it, so it cannot help reweighting the middling outcomes along with the one the agent actually fears. Two agents who differ only in how much they dread the very worst case end up needing the same curve, and an agent who is merely uniformly pessimistic gets smeared across outcomes she is indifferent about. If the attitude to risk is meant to be its own primitive, why must it operate on the whole distribution rather than on the part the agent actually cares about? And once it does, is it anything more than a utility function over lotteries in disguise — in which case the tidy three-way split you are defending earns you nothing that ordinary expected utility with unusual values did not already give you?
