I have been collecting cases in which something appears to count in favour of an attitude, and I want to know what principle, if any, separates the ones that genuinely do from the ones that do not. A stranger offers me a large sum to believe that the Atlantic is the largest ocean; I take the offer to give me no reason whatever for the belief, and to make the belief, if I could induce it, a defect rather than an achievement. A doctor tells me that patients who expect to recover recover faster; I take that as a reason to want the belief, or to take a pill that would produce it, but not as a reason for the belief itself. By contrast, that the dog is snarling is a reason to fear it, and that a joke is funny is a reason to be amused; those feel like reasons of the right sort. Yet that my employer pays a bonus to those who fear a market crash is no reason to fear the crash, and that a friend will be sad if I do not laugh is no reason to find his joke funny, at most a reason to laugh. The case I cannot place is this: my mother will be hurt if I am not grateful for a gift I did not want, and I do think her hurt gives me some reason to be grateful, not merely to say thank you, though this looks just like the joke case, where someone's distress at my lacking the attitude seemed to count for nothing. The best principle I have is that a reason must bear on the object of the attitude, on whether the ocean really is the largest, whether the dog really is dangerous, rather than on the attitude or its effects, but that seems to misfire on gratitude, where what moves me is precisely a fact about someone's feelings rather than about the gift. Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that yields all of these verdicts, that tells me what to say about a reason to intend something as opposed to a reason to do it, and that is honest about which of my verdicts it must give up? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, lists, or tables.
