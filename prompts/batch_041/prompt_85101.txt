I'm TAing an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory and formal epistemology this term — the syllabus is half boring (Savage, Jeffrey, the usual) and half the kind of stuff you'd find linked on a rationality blog, so Pascal's mugging gets a whole week to itself. I have to write the take-home final and I want one big essay question on the mugging. Can you draft the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme I can actually mark against?

Take the role of someone who works on this stuff for a living — the person you'd want refereeing a paper on fanaticism and expected utility, not someone who's read one summary. Think carefully, and brainstorm first: show me your scratchpad before the deliverable. I'd much rather you spend a while working out where the actual crux is than hand me the first plausible-sounding question.

Here's my main constraint, and it's the thing I care about most. This question has to be unanswerable by reciting the standard menu of replies. Every year half the class writes "well, you could reject unbounded utility, or reject tiny probabilities, or point out that there are infinitely many mutually exclusive muggers so no action is determined, so it's unclear" and then stops. Honestly I can't fully blame them, becuase that is basically what the secondary literature does. I want a question where that move earns a C at best.

So I want the question to force a diagnosis. Something in the neighbourhood of: is Pascal's mugging really a problem about (i) unbounded or infinite value, (ii) the legitimacy of astronomically small credences, or (iii) something structural about expected-utility maximization itself — namely that its verdicts are hostage to the tails of the prior, so a hypothesis with arbitrarily tiny prior and arbitrarily large payoff can flip any conclusion? Make them commit to one diagnosis and defend it, and make them say what happens to the other two: are those symptoms of the diagnosis they picked, or genuinely independent fixes?

Then add a second layer that I've basically never seen done well, because it's the part I think actually seperates people. A student in office hours insisted the whole thing is "just" the observation that any decision rule which accepts tiny-probability/huge-payoff offers is exploitable — an adversary who knows your rule can always construct a bet you'll take and lose. Ask whether exploitability is a genuinely fourth reply or one of the three above in a different hat, and make them say whether it matters that the mugger is an adversary who knows your decision procedure, as opposed to a natural hypothesis that merely happens to be astronomically unlikely and astronomically valuable.

Constraints:
- Model answer ~700 words, written the way a strong student would write it — committed, no fence-sitting, no "both sides have a point" ending.
- Marking scheme: five must-hit points, marks adding to 20, and for each point a single line on how students typically miss it.
- The question should be doable in about 60 minutes with no notes.
- No reading list and no citations of any kind — I'll add those myself, and I don't want students thinking they need them to answer.
- Don't hedge in the model answer. Take a side and defend it.
