I am a sixth-form student who has been reading about population ethics, and I have arrived at a position I should like you to stress-test rather than endorse. The position is this: because the universe is very probably spatially infinite, and so contains — or could contain — infinitely many people, the only comparisons of value that are well defined are local and finite ones. An action is better or worse solely in virtue of the finite sum of welfare it adds to or subtracts from the particular lives it actually touches; the infinite remainder is to be set aside entirely, since there is no coherent way in which it can be made greater or smaller. Let me call this local comparativism. I want to know what would count against it.

Please tell me which intuitions, which imagined cases and which formal results ought to move me, ranked from those that would be decisive against the view to those that are merely awkward, and please distinguish evidence that would refute it from evidence that a defender could absorb. I am especially keen to have the following addressed on their merits:

(a) Two infinite universes, U and V, containing exactly the same people. In U every life is worth living to degree one; in V every life is worth living to degree two. As I have stated it, local comparativism seems bound to call them equally good, since no finite, local change separates them. Is that a reductio, or can the localist bite the bullet?

(b) I am told that when an infinite sum is not absolutely summable — when its positive and negative terms only cancel rather than die away — the total can depend on the order of summation, and that by a suitable rearrangement it can be made to equal any number one pleases. If that is right, does it tell against local comparativism, or only against the totalist alternative that local comparativism is meant to replace?

(c) A friend proposes that instead of ignoring the infinite background we should compare welfare densities — within any finite window, the proportion of lives worth living to lives not worth living, together with how that proportion behaves as the window grows. Would density comparison rescue what local comparativism gives up, and what in turn would count against density comparison?

I should also like to know what a defender of local comparativism would concede, of their own accord, as counting against it, rather than only what its critics allege; and whether the view risks anything at all — that is, whether I have quietly qualified it into something unfalsifiable, and if so whether that is a serious fault in a view of this kind. Of the objections you raise, the one I should personally find hardest to survive is the thought that two universes differing in every life's welfare might yet be equally good; but I would sooner keep the view than lose that intuition, so I want to know what I should be paying for it. Please answer in British English.
