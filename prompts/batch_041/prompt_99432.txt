**Doing things together without meaning to**

1. Two flavors. Accounts of joint action tend to come in two flavors. On the *aggregative* view (Bratman is the cleanest version), a joint action is a structure of individual intentions: I intend to do my part, I intend that we do it by way of our doing our parts, I have a matching intention about you, you about me, the whole thing is common knowledge, and the intentions are mutually responsive. On the *unitary* view (Gilbert, and Searle in a different way), the group is a genuine subject: the intention is literally ours, not a bundle of mine and yours, and it doesn't decompose into individual attitudes without loss.

2. What's wrong with both. Aggregative views struggle with cases where the participants never represent the whole. A rower in an eight doesn't need a representation of the boat's trajectory, and if you insist she does, you've made the account intellectualist in a way that excludes young children, animals, and frankly most of adult life. Unitary views struggle with the fact that the group subject seems *made of* the participants in a way ordinary subjects aren't, and nobody has a good story about how the "we" gets anchored without smuggling the individuals back in.

3. A third option: role-dependence. Here's the idea. In a lot of joint action, what each participant intends is fixed not by a shared representation but by the *role* she occupies. The rower's intention is something like keep the rate and pull through the finish -- its content is given by her seat, her rigger, the cox's call. The roles, in turn, are constituted by the expectations the others have of whoever fills them. So the content of my intention is fixed by your expectations and yours by mine, but neither of us has to represent the whole. Call this role-dependence. It's a real pattern: crews, surgical teams, jazz rhythm sections, a lot of skilled manual labour.

4. Why that isn't just aggregation. Aggregation says the group's action is the sum of individual actions plus mutual responsiveness. Role-dependence says something stronger: the individual actions aren't fully specified until you know the role, and the role isn't specified until you know the others' expectations. So there's a constitutive circularity between my action and yours that a sum can't capture. This is supposed to be why a rower's pull is *the boat's* pull in a way that two strangers both yanking on the same rope isn't.

5. Why that isn't a plural subject. But it also isn't a group mind. Nothing here needs an intention that isn't anyone's. All the intentions are mine and yours; they're just role-indexed. So role-dependence promises a middle path -- genuine togetherness without a super-subject.

6. The overgeneration problem. Here's where I get worried. If all it takes is that individual intentions are fixed by roles constituted by mutual expectations, then *any* stable equilibrium counts. A traffic jam is role-dependent: I keep my distance because I expect you to keep yours. Everyone pouring out of a burning theatre is role-dependent. A market is role-dependent. And I don't want to say a traffic jam is a joint action. So role-dependence looks necessary but not sufficient.

7. The fix: accountability. What the traffic jam lacks is accountability. In a genuine joint action each participant is entitled to demand that the others play their roles, and is answerable to them for playing hers. The rower who stops pulling doesn't merely fail to contribute -- she gets called out, and she owes an answer. The driver who tailgates owes nobody an answer; no one is entitled to demand otherwise of him. So the mark of joint action is a mutual structure of entitlement-to-demand and answerability, not a shared mental state.

8. The worry. Accountability looks like it presupposes exactly what it's meant to explain. To be entitled to demand that you play your role, there has to already be some understanding between us -- which is the joint commitment I was trying to derive. So either accountability is primitive (and I've just renamed Gilbert's joint commitment and lost the middle path), or it's grounded in something else (and I need to know what). I suspect the way out is that entitlement-to-demand is grounded in *reliance* rather than agreement: you've arranged your conduct around my role, so I owe you something. But I can't tell whether that gets me out or just moves the circle.
