I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have spent the past few months arguing with friends about what people call expected-value fanaticism. The shape of the dispute I keep running into is this: under ordinary expected-value reasoning, a minuscule probability of an enormous payoff — a vast good that is very unlikely, or the avoidance of a vast bad — seems to swamp every ordinary consideration, so that almost any action with a nonzero chance of some astronomical consequence dominates everything else, and the reasoning stops being usable. People then propose fixes (bounding the utility scale, discounting tiny probabilities, lexicographic rules, ignoring probabilities below some threshold), and each fix appears to break something else. Before I read any more of these proposals, I want the specification: what must any adequate treatment of the problem actually deliver?

Write me the desiderata. Please structure the answer as follows.

**The problem, stated precisely** (roughly 150–200 words). What exactly is the fanaticism result — a theorem, an intuition, or a family of cases? Separate the versions that I suspect are being run together: the pure expected-value argument; the epistemic variant, where the probability is not merely tiny but unknown; and the framing in terms of the value of the far future. Say which version(s) a solution has to answer, and distinguish a theory that is itself fanatical from one that merely permits fanatical conclusions.

**The desiderata** (the bulk of the answer, roughly 700–900 words). The numbered list of requirements any adequate treatment must meet — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must stay consistent with — with, for each, the consideration or case that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. Candidate items to include, keep, or drop as you judge: it must still let expected-value reasoning govern ordinary decisions; it must not render the vast-but-unlikely consequences literally irrelevant; it must be action-guiding, telling me what to do and not merely what to believe; it must not depend on an arbitrary numerical cutoff; it must handle St. Petersburg-style games, where the expectation diverges rather than merely being large; and it must not license the very paralysis it was meant to cure. Add whatever else belongs.

**Which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of a camp** (roughly 200–250 words). For each requirement, say whether it is constitutive of the problem or merely something one proposed solution happens to want.

**Pairs that look jointly unsatisfiable** (roughly 250–300 words). This is the part I most want. Where do two requirements on the list pull against each other such that nothing satisfies both — for instance, a requirement to respect expected value across the ordinary range versus a requirement to be immune to the astronomically unlikely? State each tension cleanly and say what a solution would have to give up.

**How the standard fixes fare** (brief, roughly 200 words). Against the list only, not a survey: which of the familiar moves survives the most desiderata, and which single requirement does the most to rule candidates out.

Refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by name, and skip citations and reading lists entirely — I will fill in the references myself. Reason from cases and structural considerations rather than from particular texts.
