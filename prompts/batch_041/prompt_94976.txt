Two summers ago I came across a short piece by Simone Weil — I believe it was written during the war, not long before her death — in which she argued that political parties ought to be abolished outright, and the strangest part of her argument was that the trouble with a party has almost nothing to do with what it stands for. On her account a party is not chiefly an instrument for winning arguments or getting things done; it is a machine for making a crowd of people feel something together, and a machine of that kind cannot run without an opponent, a boundary, and some story about who belongs on the wrong side of it. That, she thought, is why party loyalty and truthfulness end up colliding: the member who contradicts the group is not treated as mistaken but as disloyal, and the group's need to keep the feeling hot will always outlast its need to be right. What stayed with me is that she did not reserve the word for organisations with party in the name — any group whose main business is manufacturing shared feeling, a congregation, a faction in a village, the readers of a newspaper, struck her as a party in the relevant sense, whatever it calls itself.

I have been turning this over for months and I cannot argue my way to a verdict, so I would rather see it. Would you write me a short story that makes her claim visible without ever stating it? Put it somewhere small and unremarkable — a village volunteer fire brigade, a tenants' committee, a church choir, a parents' association, a campaign to save a bus route — and let the group's declared cause be something nobody would call politics. I want to watch an ordinary set of decent people become a party in her sense: the point at which their togetherness begins to require someone to be against, at which a member who is factually right about some small thing is quietly reclassified as a traitor, and at which the others notice this happening and decide it is a price worth paying. It should be impossible for a reader to say when it happened — let the change be made of nothing but small, individually defensible concessions, so that no single moment can be pointed to. Please do not give it a villain, and do not end with a lesson or a wise outsider who arrives to explain everyone to themselves; the most disturbing figure in the story should be a perfectly reasonable person doing what the group needs. Give it a title.
