Nadia, my old tech lead, left to run a small company a few years ago, and ever since she has sorted competitive situations into "Molochian traps" and "not Molochian traps" — she sometimes says "multipolar trap" instead, and I have never worked out whether she means the same thing by it. The vocabulary comes from some blog post she read years ago, but she applies it with real conviction, and I have started keeping score, because I want to know whether she is running a consistent rule or just going on vibes. Her record so far, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) two delivery platforms in a mid-sized city, each burning money on delivery subsidies, both saying privately that they would love a truce, neither willing to be the first to stop, since whichever stops first loses its riders and dies — Molochian. (2) two long-time rivals in a small regional market who have kept prices high for years with no agreement and almost no contact, each retaliating within days whenever the other cuts — not Molochian; "the trap has an exit and they found it." (3) a factory dumping waste on a farm whose owner can sue and will win — not Molochian; "she has a lever, so she doesn't need the factory's cooperation." (4) a dozen maintainers of widely used open-source packages, each of whom says they would prefer a world where nobody ships features at this pace, but who lose their users within a quarter whenever they pause — Molochian.

Here is the case I actually care about, because my team is deciding this quarter whether to enter the market. A dozen companies are racing to ship a new class of developer tool. Nobody in the race seems to be unhappy about it: the founders I have met all say they would pick the same market again, the winners are getting rich, the losers get acqui-hired and their engineers end up working on the winner's roadmap, and a couple of the norms the early entrants followed — a full security review before every release, say — have quietly disappeared, because the two companies that kept doing them ran out of runway. We would be entering with our eyes open, and honestly we would probably enjoy the ride.

What I want is not so much her verdict as the machinery behind it. Tell me what Nadia will say about my case, but spend most of your effort on the general rules that fit her four earlier verdicts — I suspect more than one does — what each of those rules predicts here, which one you would bet on and why, and what further case would best separate the surviving rules. I am a software engineer with no philosophy training, so keep it concrete; and if her verdicts genuinely underdetermine my case, say that plainly rather than picking whichever rule produces a tidy answer.
