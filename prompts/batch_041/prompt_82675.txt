I have been reading Wittgenstein's Über Gewißheit in German alongside the English versions, and there is a short run of remarks — §§ 15–18 — that I cannot fit into any of the readings of the book I know. Here is the passage as it stands:

15. Daß kein Irrtum möglich war, muß erwiesen werden. Die Versicherung »Ich weiß es« genügt nicht. Denn sie ist doch nur die Versicherung, daß ich mich (da) nicht irren kann, und daß ich mich darin nicht irre, muß objektiv feststellbar sein.

16. »Wenn ich etwas weiß, so weiß ich auch, daß ich’s weiß, etc.«, kommt darauf hinaus, »Ich weiß das« heiße »Ich bin darin unfehlbar«. Ob ich aber das bin, muß sich objektiv feststellen lassen.

17. Angenommen nun, ich sage »Ich bin darin unfehlbar, daß das ein Buch ist« – ich zeige dabei auf einen Gegenstand. Wie sähe hier ein Irrtum aus? Und habe ich davon eine klare Vorstellung?

18. »Ich weiß es« heißt oft: Ich habe die richtigen Gründe für meine Aussage. Wenn also der Andre das Sprachspiel kennt, so würde er zugeben, daß ich das weiß. Der Andre muß sich, wenn er das Sprachspiel kennt, vorstellen können, wie man so etwas wissen kann.

What I want is an interpretation of this cluster taken as a unit, not a summary of the book's famous hinge passages. Specifically:

(1) How should §§ 15–16 be rendered, and where does the German resist a clean English rendering? I am interested in "erwiesen," "Versicherung," "unfehlbar," and above all "objektiv feststellbar" and "sich objektiv feststellen lassen": what range do these words have, what does the requirement they state actually demand, and of whom is it demanded?

(2) The final clause of § 15 is grammatically compressed. What is the subject of "muß objektiv feststellbar sein," and what does the sentence commit Wittgenstein to once that is settled? Does the requirement attach to the proposition known, to the speaker's state, or to the claim that no mistake was possible?

(3) Whose requirement is this? Set out the live options: that § 15 states Wittgenstein's own considered condition on "I know"; that he is ventriloquizing Moore; that he is voicing a foundationalist or verificationist demand in order to test it; that the requirement belongs to an interlocutor and is being led toward absurdity. Say which reading the surrounding remarks, especially §§ 13–14 and 17, support, and which they tell against. § 8 already narrowed the difference between "wissen" and "sicher sein" to the case where "I know" is to mean "I cannot be mistaken"; I want to know whether §§ 15–16 are pursuing exactly that residual case or quietly changing the subject.

(4) What is § 17 doing? "Wie sähe hier ein Irrtum aus?" can be read as a demand that the § 15 requirement be met by exhibiting a possible error, or as a demonstration that the requirement has no application here. Which is it, and does § 17 answer § 15, dissolve it, or leave it standing?

(5) § 18 turns from objective establishability to the other person and the language-game. Is this a replacement of the objectivity requirement by a public or communal one, a separate point about when "I know" is warranted, or a way of saying that the § 15 requirement was misdirected from the start? What does "Der Andre muß sich, wenn er das Sprachspiel kennt, vorstellen können, wie man so etwas wissen kann" add that § 15 did not already have?

(6) Does this cluster sit consistently with the book's later treatment of hinge certainties? If certain propositions are exempt from doubt, it is not obvious what becomes of a demand that infallibility be objectively established — either the demand lapses, or the hinges are narrower than the standard reading suggests, or Wittgenstein changes his mind between these remarks and the later ones. Argue for one of these, and say what would have to be true of the later text for your answer to be wrong.

Please work from the passage and its immediate context rather than from secondary literature, and do not give me citations or reading lists. Where the text genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so and lay out the competing readings with the textual details that favor each, rather than settling on one for the sake of a tidy result.
