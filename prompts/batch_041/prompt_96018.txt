I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, economics, or the social sciences, and I write satire on the side. I would like a piece that a researcher who works on this material would recognise as fair rather than embarrassing, and that a general reader who has never encountered it can still follow. Treat me as someone comfortable with specifications and entirely reliant on you for the substance, so please do not build the comedy on a technical error.

What I want: a leaked internal strategy memo of roughly 800 to 900 words, in the deadpan register of a genuine government planning document. Numbered objectives, named initiatives, quantified targets, a risk register, an appendix listing what was deferred to the next cycle. No winking, no comic asides, no narrator who knows the memo is absurd.

Who is writing it: the Directorate of Wellbeing Measurement of a small fictional country you invent, which has decided that its standing in the World Happiness Report is now a national priority. Invent the country and its institutions; do not use a real country, and do not attribute words or views to any real official, agency, or researcher.

What the memo's authors take for granted. Please treat all of this as the instrument they are managing, not as material to be explained or corrected:

- The published score rests on a single question in which a respondent places their current life on a ladder from 0, the worst possible life, to 10, the best.
- Each year's published figure averages three years of national samples of roughly a thousand respondents, so any change to a single year of data moves the score by about a third of its own size.
- The report decomposes each country's score into six factors — income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — measured against a hypothetical worst-case country, plus an unexplained residual.
- The report states that these factors are correlates of the score rather than inputs to it, and that the question itself never uses the words happiness, wellbeing, or satisfaction.
- Variables such as unemployment and inequality are absent from the model, largely because comparable data are not available everywhere.

The comedy should come from a specification problem: the staff reason correctly about the instrument they have been handed, optimise against it competently and in good faith, and never notice the gap between raising the number and making anyone's life better. Include, in the body of the memo:

- A dissent from an in-house economist raising the two objections that actually bite — that national income stops buying life evaluation past some level, and that people are poor at predicting what will improve their own evaluations — filed under a heading such as not actionable this cycle.
- The unexplained residual treated as the most attractive target precisely because nothing accounts for it.
- At least one initiative that would raise the score while leaving the underlying situation exactly as it was: sampling, timing, framing, or who ends up answering.
- At least one initiative that would raise the score while making life in the country worse in ways the model does not capture, defended in the memo's own terms using the relative weights of the factors.
- A closing paragraph whose logic is that, since the index measures what people say about their lives, improving the answers is improving the measured quantity, and therefore the distinction the earlier sections depend on does not exist. The memo must not notice what this does to its own argument.

Constraints: no quotations, no citations, no references to studies or authors, no real countries or their ranks, no footnotes, and no real-world statistics or data beyond what I have supplied above. The target is the practice of treating a ranking as a policy target, not an economic system, a political faction, or a named person. A reader who has never heard of the report should follow every step; a reader who works on it should find the result uncomfortably accurate rather than merely mean.
