I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of art seminar — fourteen of us, one eighty-minute meeting a week — and each student is responsible for leading a single session. Mine falls in three weeks, on the cluster of problems around authenticity: the fact that two objects can be perceptually indistinguishable, or related to each other in every way a casual observer could check, and yet we are prepared to pay a hundredfold for one of them; and the further fact that when we are asked to say why, the answer keeps sliding between the experience the object affords, the history it carries, and the bare fact that it is this one. I would rather not open with a lecture, because the moment the distinction is named the group will agree with it in the abstract and I will have taught them nothing. What I want is for them to commit to a valuation in public, discover that they have been valuing something they did not think they were valuing, and only then hear the vocabulary.

My working idea, which I would like you to rebuild rather than merely decorate, is a mock auction. Everyone begins with an identical purse of tokens (no real money, and the prize is trivial — a decent bar of chocolate, say — so that no one is tempted to play for the stakes rather than for the question). I put up six lots, describe each in a short catalogue entry, and run a straightforward ascending auction; a friend who reads law has already pointed out that the reserve prices and the auctioneer's patter are themselves creating value, and I would like the design to lean into that rather than hide it. Only after the hammer falls do I disclose the provenance of each lot, which the rules should be arranged so that I can do honestly: some are things I made, printed, or assembled myself in the past fortnight, some are genuinely old, and at least one is a lot for which the question is-this-a-forgery is not obviously well-formed — a fair copy of a poem whose text is already printed in a book they all own, or a recording of a performance where nothing in the sound would change if it had been produced by a machine. Each winner then has a short window to keep the lot and pay, or to hand it back at no cost and take a small token penalty. The design should force the same person, twice in one session, to give different answers to what they were buying, and the catalogue entries should be written so that the participants, not I, supply the missing distinctions.

Please give me the whole thing: catalogue entries for the lots, the auction rules and the token payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes, the wording of the reveal, and a debrief that maps each moment of the session onto the philosophical issue — the difference between the object, the experience, and the causal history; the contrast usually drawn between works where only this very instance will do and works where any correct instance simply is the work (what I have met under the label autographic versus allographic, which I would like the debrief to reach without my having to pre-assign it); and the point that our premium for originals is often a premium for a relation we cannot perceive. Tell me candidly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where participants will see through the setup, where the token economy will produce absurd bids, and where the allographic lot will confuse rather than illuminate — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about what an auction cannot reproduce: I suspect it cannot give them the slow acquaintance with a work that makes anyone care about the original in the first place, and that it cannot tell them whether the difference in price is an aesthetic difference or merely an associative one, so tell me what I will have to supply from the front of the room and what I should concede the activity leaves untouched.
