I teach a master's-level seminar on punishment and the ethics of killing, and I am writing the final examination for it. The exam is closed-book, ninety minutes, one essay question worth twenty-five marks. The students have read Camus's case against the death penalty and Beccaria's, and nothing else on the subject. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar two-step — the guillotine is cruel, and the guillotine does not deter — and I want the difficulty to sit on a crux the students will not have met in the secondary literature.

Here is the frame I have been teaching, which the question may assume as given. Camus's case, as I reconstruct it for the seminar, has three planks. The Premeditation Plank: the wrong of an execution lies not in the physical pain of the method but in the structure of the killing — the condemned is told the date, and then lives at the mercy of the executioner for months, which no murderer in private life does, so the standard equivalence between crime and punishment collapses. The Standing Plank: to sentence a person to death is to pronounce an absolute judgment from a position of absolute innocence, and in an unbelieving world no human institution occupies that position. Camus's contrast is with a pious age, in which the penalty had a coherent grammar, since the condemned was handed over to a divine tribunal and retained the possibility of atonement; a secular state that kills has inherited the power without the grammar, and can only dispose of a person it has declared irremediable. The Fallibility Plank: human justice errs, death is irreversible, so the harshest permissible penalty is the harshest reversible one, which for Camus is life labor — a penalty he took to be harsher than death, and one that leaves the condemned the option of choosing death.

Two hypotheticals, both mine, are to be stated in the question. First, the Sealed Verdict regime: a state retains capital punishment but strips out everything on Camus's list. Guilt requires a confession corroborated by physical evidence that cannot be reconciled with innocence; the method is stipulated to be painless; the condemned is not told the date, and the execution is carried out without warning while the condemned sleeps; nothing is published except that a sentence was carried out. Assume, for the sake of the exercise, that the stipulated conditions are actually satisfied. Second, the Token Execution: a state executes exactly one person per decade, selected by lottery from among those convicted of the gravest crimes, in a fully public ceremony, with the lottery announced in advance, so that the whole population of the guilty lives under the risk.

The question I want should require the students to do the following, and the marking scheme should be built so that each move is separately visible. First, show that the familiar two-step description mislocates Camus's objection, using the fact that he was willing to accept a self-administered lethal injection as an interim step and that his proposed maximum penalty is not a comfortable one. Second, say which plank carries the argument once the other two are neutralized by the Sealed Verdict regime, and whether that regime refutes Camus's case or illustrates it — I am looking for the move that the regime's own design, with its certainty, its missing interval and its unwarned killing, is a fantasy of divine judgment, and for a defence of whether the residual objection is a genuine moral objection or a dressed-up revulsion; the strongest answers should also face the retributivist reply, that if the murderer deserves death then the state's standing is beside the point, rather than ignoring it. Third, say whether the Fallibility Plank is contingent or conceptual — whether the regime fails because courts in fact err or because no empirical procedure can deliver the certainty the Standing Plank demands — and what follows for Camus's preferred maximum penalty. Fourth, decide whether the Token Execution fares better or worse against his case than the Sealed Verdict does, and say what the lottery makes visible about the expressive function of the penalty.

Please write the essay question itself, in the register of an examination paper and addressed to the students; a model answer of about eight hundred words that would earn full marks without a single citation; and a marking scheme out of twenty-five that names the must-hit moves, states what separates an A answer from a B, and lists the specific errors that should cost marks — including the error of treating Camus as an abolitionist about cruelty, and the error of treating the Standing Plank as a claim about deterrence or about fallibility.
