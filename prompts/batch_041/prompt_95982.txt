My film club is doing The Princess Bride next month and somehow I'm the one kicking off the discussion. I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class, so apologies in advance. The thing thats stuck in my head is the frame story, the grandpa reading to his sick grandson, cutting in to promise the kid she doesn't get eaten by the eels, skipping ahead when it drags. The kid obviously knows it's a bedtime story and still gets genuinely worked up about Westley and Buttercup, and by the end he wants it again tomorrow. I think this is the paradox of fiction — we're moved by people we know aren't real, and the usual answers seem to be that the feelings are just pretend feelings (a game of make believe), or that a thought can move you with no belief attached, or that we sort of half believe it while it lasts.

What I can't get past: the kid is himself a made up character in a made up movie. So if his fear for Westley is a pretend feeling, it's a pretend feeling inside a pretend world — pretend twice over. Does that actually break the make believe answer, since somewhere there has to be a floor, or does it show there's no floor and our tears are the same kind of thing, one level up? And the grandpa keeps editing the story to manage the kid's feelings, which seems to argue for the game side, except the affection between the two of them is the most real thing in the movie.

So: read the film through this. Where does the frame actually illuminate the paradox, where does the movie dodge it or have it both ways, and if you had to pick a side, which side does it land on? What scenes should I put in front of the group, and what does the film show that the arguments alone don't? No citations needed, I just want the reading.
