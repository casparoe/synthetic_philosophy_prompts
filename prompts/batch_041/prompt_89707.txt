I spent twelve years as a public defender before moving into philosophy, and next month I'm giving the keynote at a law-and-neuroscience workshop — my first time back in front of that crowd since I left. My thesis is that the free will worth wanting comes in two varieties that the literature habitually runs together: a thin capacity for responsiveness to reasons, which is what actually underwrites blame, desert, and legal responsibility, and a thicker capacity for self-authorship, which is what underwrites authenticity, regret, and the sense that one's life is one's own. I use cases of addiction and of sudden, unexplained character change to pull the two apart, and I argue that the thin variety is untouched by anything the neuroscientists are likely to produce, that the thick variety probably does not survive, and that most shouting matches in this area dissolve once the disputants say which variety they mean.

The audience will be mostly criminal-law scholars who are tacit retributivists and think the neuroscience is overhyped, plus two philosophers of mind — one a committed hard determinist — and one neuroscientist who studies volition and resents being used as a prop in philosophical arguments.
