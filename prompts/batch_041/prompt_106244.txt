My friend and I have spent the last month arguing about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and we have reached the point where the argument needs a case rather than more intuitions. Her position, which I have been calling cancellation, is that when a person appears bound by two requirements that cannot both be met, one of them was never a real requirement at all: it is void from the start. Her argument is the agglomeration principle — if you ought to do each of two things, then you ought to do both of them — so a genuine conflict would saddle someone with an impossible ought, and there are no impossible oughts. I think agglomeration is simply false, and that a person can really be required to do each of two things that cannot both be done.

Build me a case designed to put pressure on her view rather than to illustrate mine. Specifications. The conflict has to arise inside a single moral outlook, not from two outlooks colliding, so neither of us can deflect it by saying the other has the wrong list of requirements. Neither requirement may be weightier than the other, and no further consideration may break the tie, so that the cancellation theorist cannot void one of them by ranking. At least one requirement must attach to the agent without any act of hers — not something she agreed to or took on — and there must be no culpable route into the situation and no trap she should have foreseen. The stakes must be ordinary: no life-or-death choices, no children in peril, no trolleys, no triage, no medical or battlefield settings. Keep the scenario to a single paragraph, contemporary and realistic, and make the verdicts fall out of the requirements rather than being stipulated by the setup.

Then do the work. State exactly what the cancellation theorist is forced to say — which requirement she must void, when it goes void, and whether she can save agglomeration at all — and what my view says about whether the agent acted wrongly, whether she is to blame, and what is left over afterward. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the dilemma if I remove that feature, and give me her strongest reply: the case for saying the scenario is rigged by my stipulations rather than generated by the moral facts. Write in American English.
