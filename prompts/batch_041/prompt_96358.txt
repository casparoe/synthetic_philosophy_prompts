You are an expert in early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought, with a gift for deadpan satire. Your task is to write a satirical piece in the form of a central bank's official FAQ page, published by a fictional Irish National Bank that has decided to implement a Berkeleyan monetary system. Before you begin writing, think carefully about the philosophical views you are satirizing, and brainstorm a list of at least five genuine tensions or absurdities that follow from them. Then write the FAQ.

Context you should use: The philosopher George Berkeley, in The Querist (a work composed as a numbered list of questions on Irish economic conditions), argued that money is not a commodity with intrinsic value but a ticket or counter that stands for the power to command goods and labour; that true wealth consists in that power to command labour rather than in gold or silver; and that a national bank issuing paper credit could serve Ireland better than a metallic currency. He also held the more general metaphysical view that material substance does not exist independently of being perceived. You do not need to cite any sources, and you should not quote Berkeley directly; paraphrase his ideas as needed.

The satire should target the view that money is a mere convention or ticket, and the practice of monetary institutions that treat it as such, by following that logic to its uncomfortable conclusions. The jokes should come from getting the philosophy and the economics right: the difference between money as a commodity and money as a token; the role of trust and collective belief; the regress problem of what backs the backing; the relationship between money and labour; the idealist suggestion that if money is an idea, some mind must be holding it; and the political question of who gets to issue the tickets. The piece should be funny to someone who knows Berkeley's economic writings and the history of monetary theory, but still readable to someone who does not. Do not invent facts about Berkeley's biography or the actual historical fate of his proposals; the fictional bank is your own invention.

Format: A heading with the fictional bank's name, a short introductory paragraph in the voice of a bank spokesperson, and then 10-14 numbered questions and answers. The answers should be written in the bland, reassuring register of a real central bank FAQ, while the content quietly reveals the metaphysical and political problems. The humor should be dry, not wacky; the bank should never acknowledge that anything is amiss. Aim for about 700 words. Do not use quotation marks around any phrase attributed to Berkeley; if you want to gesture at his wording, paraphrase.
