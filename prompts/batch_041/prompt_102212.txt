I'm putting together a fake in-house legal document for a bit of fun and I'd like you to write it. It's the author agreement / warranty terms for a made-up scholarly press — call it Carcanet & Sons, I dont mind the name. The joke is that the press is trying to run a publishing house on the logic of the preface paradox, with a late clause quietly borrowed from the lottery paradox, and the document never once admits that's what it's up to.

Numbered clauses, deadpan legal register, roughly 700 words. The press requires every author to warrant, sentence by sentence, that each individual claim in their book is true — and in the same breath requires them, as a condition of being a serious scholar, to acknowledge that at least one of those claims is probably wrong. The document has to treat those two requirements as obviously compatible, and it has to get the actual epistemology right so that someone who's thought hard about this laughs instead of wincing. So let the clauses bump into the real pressure points: the closure step (if you believe each sentence, what's stopping you from believing all of them at once), the move of separating 'acceptance' from outright belief, the suggestion that a rational person can simply hold inconsistent beliefs, and the difference between 'somewhere in here there's an error' and 'I know which sentence it's in.' I want at least one clause that reclassifies conjunction-introduction as a restricted operation requiring separate authorization, and a penalty schedule for an author who ever asserts the whole conjunction out loud.

Then a later section — the press's own staff lottery — that reproduces the lottery version of the same structure without naming it: every employee rationally expects their own ticket to lose, the house rationally expects all of them to lose, and yet somebody wins every year. Same deadpan handling, same refusal to resolve anything.

Don't resolve the paradox for me. The funny part is a legal department that has papered over it and gone to lunch. No citations, no bibliography, no real people, no real press. If you want a quote, put it in the mouth of an invented officer of the house — a Chief Consistency Officer or something — and keep it consistent with the position the document is taking.

Voice flat and bureaucratic the whole way through: no winking, no 'of course this is absurd' asides, and a reader should be able to tell whats being sent up without ever being told. Start the reply straight with the document itself, clause 1 as the first line, no preamble or commentary before it.
