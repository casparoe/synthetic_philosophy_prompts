I am a novelist at work on a book whose two central characters — a research physician who came to philosophy late in life, and a philosopher of medicine of a decidedly normative temperament — have been circling the same argument for thirty years. I have reached the scene in which they finally sit down together and, instead of talking past each other, produce the document their dispute has always been missing. I would like you to draft that document.

The two positions are the naturalist account of disease, of which the biostatistical theory associated with Christopher Boorse is the canonical form, and the normativist account, on which a condition counts as a disease only against some evaluative standard — harm to the person, diminished well-being, or departure from a valued norm of human functioning. Treat these as the parties to a good-faith adversarial collaboration, and write what they could both sign.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims, each of which should cost at least one side something; if a claim is one that nobody would deny, cut it. I am particularly interested in what they can agree about the division of labor between the concept of disease and the concepts of illness and sickness, about the ways in which clinical practice is value-laden even if the theoretical concept is not, and about which of the classic hard cases are genuinely hard. Second, a table of their remaining disagreements, with a column for the point at issue, a column giving the formulation each side would accept as the statement of its own view, and a column for the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. Third, for each crux, the case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how it will come out, recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes include the reference-class problem (which population, age, sex, and environment fix what counts as species-typical, and whether that choice can be made without evaluation), the status of the notion of function the naturalist needs, and whether functional ability can be compared across losses without a weighting that is itself evaluative. Add, remove, or split these as you see fit. Fourth, a short closing section on which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and why.

Cases I would like considered, at least as candidates: aging and menopause, pregnancy, the declassification of homosexuality as a mental disorder, near-universal conditions such as tooth decay, and the thresholds for ordinary vision, which seem to track what people are expected to be able to do rather than any fact about the species. I do not need citations, and I would rather have a flag on a contested detail than a confident footnote; if the accuracy of a case matters to the argument, say so instead of settling it.

Two requests about how to answer. Be willing to tell me the framing is wrong: if these are not two camps at all, if the residual disagreement turns out to be entirely verbal, or if the naturalist's position cannot be stated without borrowing the normativist's machinery, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a symmetry, and mark anywhere you are guessing at what one side believes. And write in American English.
