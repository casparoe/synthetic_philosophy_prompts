I've spent years in the rationalist-adjacent commentariat watching arguments about repugnant markets—kidney sales, price gouging after a disaster, vote selling, paid surrogacy, paying students for grades—and they almost always proceed by leaning hard on one vivid case, or by treating a gut reaction as either decisive evidence or mere disgust. Before I commit to a side on any particular market, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata a theory of repugnant markets must meet: the cases it has to get right, with the case behind each requirement; the distinctions it must not collapse (for instance, between the good being traded and the terms of the trade; between wrongness sourced in coercive background conditions and wrongness sourced in the commodification itself; between what an exchange does to the parties and what it expresses or teaches to third parties); and the findings from outside philosophy it cannot contradict, whether from economics or from the psychology of disgust. Include among the cases a few that I think strain the standard theories—paying someone to surrender their place in a transplant queue, an employer buying out an employee's right to discuss wages, a market in posthumous apologies—so the list isn't just the familiar greatest hits. Flag any subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular contenders.
