Suppose a cognitive scientist argues that because Bayesian inference over rich hypothesis spaces is NP-hard in the worst case, human beings cannot be rational Bayesians. A philosopher replies that this inference is invalid: computational complexity is not an intrinsic property of a problem, but a relation among a problem, an agent's representational format, a distribution over inputs, and a resource bound. Call this the relational view of complexity. Argue for the relational view and against the cognitive scientist's inference. In doing so, (i) explain why worst-case asymptotic NP-hardness cannot by itself ground a claim about what a finite reasoner can rationally do; (ii) state and then defuse the strongest objection—that the relational view makes hardness so agent-relative that it cannot support any substantive normative epistemology; and (iii) show how the relational view can still explain why some inferential strategies are rationally mandatory, permissible, or forbidden for a given finite agent. Use at least one concrete example, such as satisfiability, shortest-path computation, or Bayesian updating, and keep the argument self-contained.
