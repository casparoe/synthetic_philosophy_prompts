Exam question: In decision theory, Good's theorem says that the expected value of information is non-negative. Carefully state what the theorem claims, identify the assumptions it relies on, and explain one important thing it does not claim. Then illustrate with a simple example.

Student answer: Good's theorem states that for any rational decision maker, obtaining free information before choosing an action cannot make them worse off in expectation. Formally, let U be utility, A the set of actions, and X the information. The theorem says that E[max_a E[U(a)|X]] >= max_a E[U(a)]. This holds as long as the decision maker updates her beliefs by conditionalization and the information is costless. It also requires that the information is true, since misleading information could otherwise reduce expected utility, and it requires that the decision maker is risk-neutral, because a risk-averse agent might prefer not to receive information that could force a difficult choice. The theorem does not claim that information always has positive value; if the information is irrelevant, the expected gain is zero. For example, suppose you are deciding whether to bring an umbrella. Without a forecast, you pick the action with the highest expected utility. With a perfect forecast, you can choose differently depending on whether it will rain, so your expected utility cannot decrease. Even if the forecast says it will not rain, you might still bring an umbrella if you are very risk-averse, but that does not violate the theorem because the expectation is taken before the forecast is received. A common misunderstanding is that the theorem says a Bayesian can never regret receiving information. That is false: after seeing the information, you might wish you had not seen it, because the state of the world might be worse than expected. But ex ante, the possibility of such regret is already averaged into the expected value.
