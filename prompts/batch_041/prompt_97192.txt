I'm a science journalist at work on a long-form piece about how the replication crisis has been litigated between frequentists and Bayesians, and I want the argument to earn its keep by running hard against one concrete episode rather than floating above the whole field. The episode I want to use is the precognition research published by the social psychologist Daryl Bem: a single paper reporting nine experiments, nearly all of them landing "significant" in the predicted direction — future events retroactively shaping present performance on memory and priming tasks — several of them only barely significant, and one of them framed as a conceptual replication of familiar priming effects run backwards in time. What followed is the part I care about most: the effect did not survive the wave of replications, and the paper became Exhibit A in the ensuing reckoning, whose headline number was that only about a third of the published effects reexamined in the large multi-lab replication effort held up at conventional significance. I will supply the salient facts in the piece itself, so I am not asking you to reconstruct them from memory; what I want from you is the mapping. Treat frequentism and Bayesianism not as tribal labels but as two rival accounts of what a piece of statistical evidence licenses — one built on the long-run behavior of procedures, one built on how a rational agent's credence should move. I want the analysis to run in both directions: each framework illuminating what the actors faced and should have concluded, and the episode in turn testing each framework, including where it is simply silent rather than vindicated.

I have a working proposal of my own that I want you to take seriously and then either defend or break. Call it the two-ledger account. On this view, the frequentist ledger governs the design and conduct of research — the stopping rule, the nominal error rate, the multiplicity of tests, whether the study was preregistered, how the sampling plan was fixed — while the Bayesian ledger governs the interpretation of evidence once it is in hand, telling a rational agent how far to move given a stated prior. The frequentist books answer a question about procedures; the Bayesian books answer a question about evidence; and the central pathology of the episode, on this account, is that the field kept reading one ledger's entries off the other's books. I want you to say whether that division of labor actually holds up under this case or collapses into a truce that flatters both sides.
