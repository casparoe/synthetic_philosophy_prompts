Construct a thought experiment in which the Stoic dichotomy of control delivers the verdict that is hardest to extract from it: the agent's judgment is not up to her, while the outcome of her decision plainly is. Constraints: one paragraph; a realistic setting with ordinary stakes (a workplace, a clinic, a family); a single agent making a single decision; the reversal must not come from drugs, injury, hypnosis, manipulation, or any science-fictional device, nor from the agent's ignorance or factual mistake — it should fall out of ordinary facts about how a professional habit, exhaustion, or an upbringing shapes what a person can bring herself to assent to; and the case must not depend on the truth of determinism or on any general thesis about free will, so that it reads the same way to a compatibilist and to an incompatibilist. Then do the check: for each of the two items at issue, state what counts as being up to the agent on a volitional reading and on a counterfactual-dependence reading, and show which reading each Stoic verdict requires; say what the Stoic concludes about whether the agent acted well; and identify the single feature that does the work, what becomes of the case when that feature is removed, and how a Stoic would argue that the case begs the question by redescribing the judgment as a mere appearance the agent could still refuse to follow. The case is meant to test whether the dichotomy of control does independent work in Stoicism's modern revival or merely restates the claim that virtue is up to us, so a case that stipulates the reversal rather than generating it from the theory fails.
