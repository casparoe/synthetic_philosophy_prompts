For the past year I have been writing a program that designs typefaces. It draws letters on a coarse grid and is meant to generate a whole alphabet from a small number of stylistic decisions, so that every 'a' it produces is both unmistakably an 'a' and unmistakably of the same family as the 'b' and the 'c' beside it. The program treats letterforms as points in a space of shapes ordered by similarity, and I have been defending that picture with the following argument, which I now suspect proves too much.

(1) A letterform is a shape, and nothing more than a pattern of marks.
(2) Shapes can be ordered by similarity, so the letter a shape is is fixed by its region in shape-space: shapes in the same region are the same letter.
(3) Between any two shapes there is a continuous path of steps, each of which alters the shape so little that no one could tell the shapes at the two ends of that step apart.
(4) If no one could tell two shapes apart, they are not two different letters.
(5) So there is a path from any 'a' to any 'e' on which no single step leaves the letter 'a', and hence 'e' is 'a'.
(6) Since that is absurd, the 'a'-ness of an 'a' was never there to be discovered: category boundaries are imposed rather than found, and there is no essence of the letterform for a program to recover.

I cannot accept (6) — if it holds, my program has nothing to discover and its output is arbitrary — but I also do not want to give up (1), which is the whole basis of the similarity picture, or (3), which looks like a plain fact about continuous variation. My supervisor blames (2), saying that shapes do not come with a metric attached; a colleague says the whole thing is just the sorites and that vague predicates are harmless. Neither has told me what I would be paying.

Tell me which step is the weak one, why it looked innocent, and what it costs to give it up, including whether there is a way to keep (1) and still avoid (6).

Format the reply in four titled sections: 'The weak step' (about 150 words, naming the premise or step you reject and stating briefly why); 'Why it looked innocent' (about 200 words); 'What each repair costs' (about 250 words, considering the two or three other steps one might have blamed instead and what giving up each would cost); and 'Verdict' (about 100 words, saying whether any essence of the letterform survives, and if so what kind of thing it is).
