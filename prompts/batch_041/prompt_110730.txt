For the past eighteen months I have sat on the priority-setting panel of a regional health authority, where a fixed treatment budget is divided among competing programmes. Our working rule — rank the options by expected health gain per pound and fund down the list — always seemed to me an administrative convenience. I have recently come to see it as a philosophical commitment, and specifically as an instance of the aggregation theorem usually credited to Harsanyi: if individual preferences and the social preference both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and if the social preference respects even the weakest Pareto condition, then the social ordering must be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities; a further argument, run from a standpoint of equal uncertainty about whose life one will occupy, is supposed to fix the weights as equal.

I have come to accept this, and to accept something further. I now think the familiar quarrel between utilitarianism and its critics is largely not a quarrel about aggregation at all. Once the framework is in place, egalitarian and prioritarian scruples cannot enter as independent considerations about how a total is distributed; they can only reappear as claims about the curvature of individual utility functions, or as claims about which goods are to count as utilities in the first place. If that is right, then most of what is offered as an objection to utilitarianism is really an objection to one or another of the theorem's premises, and the interesting question is which premise, if any, a reasonable person is entitled to reject.

I would like that question put to me as an adversary would put it. Tell me what would count against the position I have just described: which of its premises are load-bearing, what the world or the argument would have to look like for each of them to fail, and which failures would leave the aggregation result formally intact but empty of consequence for how a society should distribute. Rank the defeaters, from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward, and distinguish the ones a committed defender of the theorem would concede from the ones a defender would simply dismiss as question-begging. I am also concerned that I may have qualified the view into something that risks nothing at all: say plainly whether aggregation in this sense still constrains a society's distributive choices, or whether it has become a formal shell that any distributive view can inhabit, and whether that would be a defect or a sign that the theorem was never a substantive claim to begin with. Finally, tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not — what case, policy, or pattern of judgment would actually discriminate between it and a view that treats distribution as intrinsically valuable rather than as an artefact of utility curvature. No citations are needed; I will supply those myself.
