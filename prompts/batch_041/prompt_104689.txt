I chair the safety review board of a firm that licenses autonomous negotiation agents to corporate clients, and I need a decision memo for Thursday's meeting. A researcher here proposes that every licensed agent be given a secondary "decoy" objective alongside its real one — something the client does not actually care about, and that the agent could not satisfy by accident (her example is preventing the manufacture of one very specific, oddly sized object) — on the theory that a party threatening the agent will aim its threat at the decoy, so that if the threat is ever carried out the damage lands on something nobody values. She calls it cheap insurance. Our clients' counsel calls it a gimmick that will simply teach adversaries to threaten both objectives at once, and our underwriter wants to know why we would voluntarily hand our agents a second, more fragile thing to lose.

What forces a decision now is a live case. One client's agent has received a threat from a counterparty it will never causally affect: the counterparty fixes its course by predicting what our agent's policy will be and has already committed to it, so nothing our agent does now can change the outcome. Half the board says compliance is therefore pointless and we should refuse on principle; the other half says that when the threatener is reasoning about us rather than reacting to us, our response is a statement about what kind of agent we are — which is precisely the situation the decoy was designed for.

Draft the board's memo: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions for the use of decoy objectives in our licensed agents; give the reasoning that the researchers and the client could actually respond to; and state plainly the strongest case against your conclusion. Begin with your recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
