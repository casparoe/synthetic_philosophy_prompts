I just got appointed to my city's Heritage and Memory Review Board — we're the ones who decide what goes on the historical plaques and what the museum labels say — and it turns out one of the cases on the docket is my own family's, which is how I got roped into this in the first place.

Here's the track record I'm trying to read. Three years ago: a local legend said the town's founder freed the people he enslaved in his will, the probate file says otherwise, and we went with the file and fixed the plaque. Two years ago: the elders on the Eastside have always said the 1923 fire that took their church was set by white neighbors, there's basically no paper on it either way, and we put their account on the plaque as the account, full stop. Last year: a family said their great-uncle's farm got swindled off them in the 40s, the county books show a clean sale, and we split it — both versions, marked disputed. And in one case the community's story turned out to have been fed to them by a county agent back in the 60s, and we went with the archive and nobody really fought us on it.

Now mine. My great-grandmother's people say a group of them were held in a schoolhouse for four days in 1918 and run out of the county, and that two men died there. No official record of any of it. But a local history guy just digitized a 1918 sheriff's ledger with a line about transporting eleven persons of color on a date that lines up. So it corroberates the roundup and says nothing about anybody dying — and a 1920 census page lists both of the men my family says died.

What do you think this board does with it? Give me your best guess at the ruling, the rule the board actually seems to be running (I bet more then one fits the four cases above), and which of those earlier cases would separate the rules if it ever came back around.
