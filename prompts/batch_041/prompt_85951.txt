I'm three weeks into a seminar on cooperation and I've written myself into a corner I can't get out of by re-reading my notes. The unit we just finished was on how cooperative behaviour could have evolved in the first place, and the takeaway I copied down was that it takes hold only where something makes cooperation pay for the individual — repetition, reputation, assortment, punishment, whatever the mechanism turns out to be. When I tried to turn that into a clean argument for my response paper, I got this:

(1) A behavioural disposition can persist in a population only if, on average, the individuals who have it do at least as well as those who lack it. (2) Wherever cooperation has persisted, it is because some mechanism makes cooperating locally pay — repeated play, observability, positive assortment, or the credible threat of punishment. (3) In a one-shot anonymous interaction, none of those mechanisms is in play. (4) So in a one-shot anonymous interaction the cooperator ends up worse off than a defector would have. (5) An act is rational for an agent exactly when it maximises that agent's expected payoff. (6) So cooperating in a one-shot anonymous interaction is never rational. (7) So when a person quietly returns a stranger's wallet, at some cost and inconvenience, in a city they will never visit again, with nobody watching and no prospect of a return, they are behaving irrationally, and any admiration we feel for them is sentiment.

I cannot accept (7). I have returned a wallet, and I don't think I was being a fool, and I don't think the person who says so has simply got a better grip on my psychology than I do. I also half-remember from a lecture that in anonymous one-shot laboratory games a substantial fraction of people will pay real money out of their own pocket to punish a stranger who behaved unfairly, knowing perfectly well they will never see them again — the same embarrassment in a different key. My friend's reply is that this only shows people are frequently irrational, which he says is not news. I would rather keep (1) through (3) than keep (7), but I want to know what that preference costs me.

So: which step or steps do I give up, why did each look fine when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost? I'm not after reassurance that (7) is false — I already know I want it to be false — I want the joint identified. If there is more than one defensible exit, rank the exits by price and say what each one still lets me say about the wallet. I'm especially unsure how much work the evolutionary premises in (1) through (3) are actually doing, given that (4) and (5) alone seem to get me to (6). Keep the whole answer under 700 words.
