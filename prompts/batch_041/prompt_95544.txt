I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar meeting on the cognitive penetration of perception, and I want the handout to open with a poem rather than a case study, because the poem is to be the case study: the students will argue about which side it supports, and I do not want the poem to tell them.

The dispute I have in mind is whether what a perceiver believes or expects can alter the content of what she literally sees, as opposed to altering only her attention, her memory, or her judgment about what she sees. The penetration view, at its most demanding, requires that the penetrating state be about the same subject matter as the experience, that the resulting change be semantically coherent with the content of that state, and that the effect not be carried by attention, eye movements, or memory. The encapsulation view replies that the stimulus fixes the experience, that one and the same experience can sit beneath two different reports, and that whatever looks like penetration is really a change in attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment. I want a poem that either side can press into service and that nothing in the poem can resolve.

Write it as five unrhymed quatrains in loose iambic pentameter, built on a single refrain: one line reporting what is seen of one visible feature of one ordinary scene, repeated verbatim as the opening line of every quatrain, with the lines around it supplying the perceiver's changing beliefs about that scene. Conditions:

1. The scene must be one in which there is a fact of the matter, something a photograph or a tape measure could settle, and it must not be an optical illusion; nothing in the scene may be designed to deceive.
2. The feature reported must be one nobody could care about — the tilt of a hung picture, the spacing of floor tiles, the number of chairs at a table — so that the poem cannot trade on wishful seeing or on emotion. The changing lines must give beliefs about the scene, not feelings about it.
3. The refrain must report the scene and nothing about the perceiver: no verb of believing, seeming, or appearing, and no first-person pronoun. Every other line may be first-person.
4. The refrain must never be explained, paraphrased, or glossed, and no other line may say what it means or whether its meaning has shifted.
5. The refrain must appear, at its first occurrence, to report one thing and, at its last, to report something else, with no word of it changed; and a reader must not be able to determine from the poem whether what changed was the seeing or the frame around it.
6. The poem must not use the words perception, cognition, penetrate, top-down, module, bias, interpretation, schema, gestalt, or construct.
7. No exotic apparatus: no drugs, no goggles, no inverting lenses, no brains in vats.
8. It must work as a poem for a reader who knows nothing of the debate — someone looking at something and no longer able to trust her eyes — and it must be recognizable to a reader who knows the debate as a case of the disputed kind rather than a case of attention, memory, or emotion.
9. No title, no epigraph, no notes, and no commentary before or after the poem other than the final line specified below.

After the poem, on its own final line, begin with the word Verdict, then a colon, then one of the three labels penetration, encapsulation, or neither, followed by a single sentence of no more than twenty-five words saying why the poem's form commits it that way. I need that line so that I know which side I am handing out.
