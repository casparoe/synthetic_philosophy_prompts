I write novels, and the paradox of fiction keeps surfacing for me in a practical form: readers tell me they wept over a man I made up, and in the same breath that they know he never existed. I want a thought experiment built to order on this, and I want it to isolate a single variable. Construct a case in which one reader meets the same narrative twice — first taking it for a true first-person account, then learning that it is a novel — and in which her emotional response is stipulated to be equally intense and equally action-guiding on both readings. Hold everything else fixed: the same sentences, the same imagery, the same desires and attention. No trickery beyond that one revelation, no children, no self-deception, no pathology. Then do the work. Lay out the three standard positions — that she merely make-believes the man exists and so has a make-believe emotion; that the emotion is directed at the thought rather than at any existent object, so belief in existence is beside the point; and that what she has is not a genuine emotion at all but a quasi- or as-if emotion — and state what each predicts about this case and why. Close by telling me which feature of the case is carrying the argument, what happens if I soften it, so that the novel-reading proves even slightly less intense or less motivating than the memoir-reading, and how a defender of the make-believe account would try to deny that the two responses are really the same state.
