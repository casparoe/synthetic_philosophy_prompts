Quick context: I run a tiny type studio — me, one junior designer, and a freelancer who does the italics — and most of our work is custom faces for institutions. Right now we're five months into a signage face for a regional transit authority, so every glyph has to survive being read at 40 metres in glare by someone with low vision, and there's an accessibility consultant on the client side who can veto anything on legibility grounds. On top of that we do a lot of revivals and family extensions, where the question is never "draw an a" but "draw the a this face would have." So the same descision comes up maybe a dozen times a week: is this shape still the letter it's supposed to be, and is it still *this* face?

Everyone I ask says "if it reads as an a, it's an a," which is circular and useless to me at 11pm when I'm staring at a single-story a with a stubby little bowl that I'm honestly in love with and can't tell whether it's a bold choice or just a mistake. Years ago I read about a research group that tried to build a program to design a whole alphabet in one coherent style, and what stuck with me wasn't the program, it was that they couldn't write down what makes an a an a — you can't say "bowl plus stem," because a comes in a double-story form, a single-story form, an italic form, a form with a curled tail, and none of them literally share a shape, yet you look at any of them and it's obviously the same letter. Same story with g. And style is worse: twenty-six shapes have to feel like one family when they share almost no literal features, and I can't tell my junior "make it consistent," because she'll ask consistent in what, exactly.

So what I want from you is a procedure, not a pep talk. It has to be something my junior can run on her own on a Tuesday afternoon, and that I can run in twenty minutes before a client call, and it should go in stages: first a pass that decides whether a shape still belongs to its letter category at all, then a pass that decides whether it belongs to *this* face's style, then a step that tells us when the answer isn't ours to make (the consultant's veto, the client's brand, my call as the one who signs off). It should tell her what to do when she's genuinely unsure rather than pretending there's always a fact of the matter, and it has to handle the cases that keep biting us: the single-story a in a face that's otherwise all double-story; a g with the ear filed off; a dot on the i that isn't a dot; an l that's perfectly fine in English but would read as a different letter in another language we might get asked to support; and the tight letter-spacing pairs where two letters fuse into a third shape. Run it on three glyphs from the current project — pick ones that are actually hard, and at least one where the procedure says to cut a glyph we're attached to, because I'd rather hear that from you than from the consultant. Be honest about which steps are real tests and which are just taste wearing a lab coat, and say what the procedure can't do — I don't want to find out the limits in a client review. And please don't send me a reading list, I won't read it; I want the thing itself.

Format, in this order:
- "The procedure in one breath" — about 100 words, the whole thing compressed into something a junior could keep in her head.
- "The steps, in order" — the real deliverable, 700-900 words, numbered, and for each step: what she does, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, what to do on a fail, and roughly how long it should take.
- "Three glyphs, run through" — 400-500 words, each one carried all the way to a verdict, including the one we won't like.
- "Where a person has to decide" — 200 words, naming whose call it is in each case.
- "What this can't do" — 150 words, the failure modes, including the ones that are going to show up on this project.
