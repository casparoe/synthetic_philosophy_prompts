Been lurking on LW and the alignment blogs for like a decade now, and IDA is the one proposal that keeps dragging me back — the version where you train a model to imitate a human overseer who answers a hard question by breaking it into easier subquestions and then stitching the subanswers back together, then distill that whole amplified procedure back down into a single model, then run it again with the bigger model as the new overseer. Each round is supposed to stay safe because every step was something a human (or something already checked by a human) signed off on, and it's supposed to get more capable because the decomposition lets you go past what one person can hold in their head at once.

I'm going to actually work on this for the next year or so. No compute, no training runs — just me, a whiteboard, and two friends who'll tell me when I'm being an idiot. So what I want from you is a take on which open questions in this area matter most, and in what order I should go after them.

Some worries I already have, partly to show the flavor of what I'm after and partly because I want to know whether they're as central as they feel to me:

1. Distillation laundering. The alignment story is about the training process, but what gets deployed is the distilled student. If the student is a compressed approximation of the amplified thing, do the safety properties come along, or do they evaporate exactly at the point where the student gets most capable?

2. The overseer is the weak link. If the human can only check answers it can understand, then amplification can't get you past what the overseer can verify — and if the model does go past that, the human sign-off is theater. Fatal, or is there a version where the checking is indirect but still load-bearing?

3. The decomposition assumption. The whole scheme needs hard tasks to factor into subtasks whose correctness composes. Is that a claim about the world or a hope? Where does it break first?

4. The base-case regress. "Aligned by construction" bottoms out in a human labeling things correctly, which is the thing we don't know how to do in the first place. Does that make the whole thing circular, or does the amplification step actually buy something here?

5. Competence and alignment might be entangled. Distillation is supposed to preserve capability. But if the capability comes from exactly the cognition that's hardest to supervise, maybe the compression that keeps competence is the same compression that drops whatever made it safe.

For each of these — and anything big I'm missing — I want to know why it's upstream of the other questions rather than downstream of them, how tractable it is for someone doing pure conceptual work instead of running experiments, how neglected it looks, and what I'd have to learn for it to move up or down the list. Where a question is really empirical and I can't settle it from an armchair, say so, and say what the experiment would have to show.

Then rank them. I'd rather have three questions I can actually make a dent in then ten that all collapse into "train a bigger model and see". Don't bother pointing me at specific papers, I mostly want the reasoning. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind about the ordering — which argument or result would reshuffle it, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
