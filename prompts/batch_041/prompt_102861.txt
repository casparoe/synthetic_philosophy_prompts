I teach an upper-level seminar on Hume's moral and political essays, and I am writing the final examination. I need one essay question on Hume's Of Suicide that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar three-part summary — that suicide violates no duty to God, no duty to our neighbour, and no duty to ourselves. The question should turn on the crux described below.

Hume's argument that suicide violates no duty to God rests on his account of providence. The deity governs by general and immutable laws, and because all events proceed from powers that the deity has endowed in creatures, "All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty"; a house destroyed by human hands is, on this view, no more an interruption of providence than one that collapses under its own weight. The difficulty I want my students to confront is that this argument appears to prove too much: if no event lies outside the general order, then murder and theft disturb providence no more than suicide does, and the first prong threatens to license everything or nothing.

The question should therefore force students to distinguish a conception of providence as a general order that no event can interrupt from a conception of providence as a government that issues particular directives and reserves particular prerogatives, so that some acts can be said to usurp what belongs to God alone. Students should have to say which of these conceptions Hume's argument attacks, which one the traditional prohibition of suicide actually requires, and whether the over-generalization objection refutes Hume or is simply the point of his argument. They should also have to reach a verdict on whether the essay's conclusion that suicide is permissible can stand on the neighbour and self prongs alone if the first prong does no positive work.

The examination is a 90-minute in-class essay. Please give me the question itself, with whatever sub-parts the wording needs; a model answer of roughly 800 words; and a marking scheme that distributes 25 marks across the specific moves a top answer must make, with a one-line note for each move on how candidates typically get it wrong. The question must be answerable without reference to secondary literature, and neither the question nor the model answer should depend on citations.
