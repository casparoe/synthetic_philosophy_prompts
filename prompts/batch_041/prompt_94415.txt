I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and like a good many people in my field I have come to rely on the Stoic distinction between what is up to us and what is not. It is the most practically useful idea I have ever taken from a book, and I have read Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius more than once. A colleague who studied classics recently told me that the doctrine as it circulates today, my version included, is largely a modern construction: that the ancient distinction only does its work inside a cosmology of universal causation and providence that hardly anyone now accepts, and that without that setting it collapses into a coping technique, a way of feeling calmer rather than a philosophy. I cannot adjudicate this on my own, so I would like you to write a dialogue in which two equally well-informed and equally competent interlocutors work the disagreement through. One defends the modern reading and its practical use; the other presses the historical and philosophical objections. Neither should be a strawman, and each should concede the strongest point the other makes rather than talking past it.

The dialogue should engage these questions concretely rather than gesturing at them:

- Which claim the distinction actually is: that only my own judgements and choices are causally within my power; that only they are properly mine, and so the only things I can be answerable for; or that I can influence outcomes without controlling them. Contemporary practice slides among the three, and the dialogue should establish which reading can be defended and what goes wrong on the others.

- Whether the distinction survives the loss of its original setting. If, for the Stoics, everything except assent was fixed by fate, then the second category was doing real work. If we reject that, is the line between the categories arbitrary, or can some other argument fix it?

- Whether the doctrine is quietist. The charge is that telling people to attend to their own judgements breeds resignation before injustice; the reply is that Stoic virtue demands action on behalf of others and that the doctrine governs one's attachment to outcomes, not one's willingness to act. Test whether that reply is substantive or merely verbal, and whether it leaves the doctrine unfalsifiable.

- Whether the modern therapeutic use of Stoicism, including the techniques that are often traced back from cognitive therapy to Epictetus, is a legitimate continuation of the ancient practice or a selective borrowing that keeps the exercises and discards the metaphysics and the demanding ethics.

- Where the boundary actually falls if my judgements are themselves shaped by temperament, upbringing, illness and circumstance, and how robust a conception of agency the doctrine needs in order to bear that weight.

Close with the sharpest disagreement still standing rather than a tidy synthesis. Aim for roughly 2,000 words, and keep stage directions to a minimum.
