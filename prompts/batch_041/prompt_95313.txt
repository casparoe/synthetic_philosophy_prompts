I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and I'm writing the labeling guide for an internal classifier that sorts user content into categories nobody on the team can define precisely. We keep adding rules for the edge cases and each rule produces new edge cases, and it has occurred to me that my labelers may not be disagreeing about the data at all but about what it means for a thing to belong to a category — a colleague tells me this is the old point about family resemblance, and that a word's meaning is its use in some local practice rather than something it carries around with it.

Please build me a decision tree of the positions someone could take on what makes something a member of a category. Treat these six as the leaves, and describe each in plain terms rather than by any term of art: (a) there is a definition we simply haven't found; (b) there is no definition, only a network of overlapping similarities, so no single feature runs through every member; (c) membership is a matter of degree, measured against the clearest examples rather than against a line; (d) the category is real and held together by a cluster of properties that tend to travel together for a reason, so its boundaries can move without becoming arbitrary; (e) membership is settled by what the relevant community actually does with the word; (f) the question of what the category really is should be dissolved, leaving only the question of which classification serves our purposes.

Build the cuts out of concrete cases rather than abstractions, and use at least these: whether a virus is alive, whether a hot dog is a sandwich, whether a chatbot's "I'm sorry" is an apology, and whether a tomato is a vegetable. Phrase every cut as a question a labeler could answer by pointing at a case, so that no one needs the vocabulary to answer it.

Format: exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no closing summary. Each item is one cut, in order from the root to the leaves, stating the question, the two or three answers available at that node, and which position or which later cut each answer leads to. Give each position a short descriptive label of your own invention rather than a borrowed name. If a branch is empty because nobody holds that combination, say so in a clause and say whether you think it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two of the six positions cannot be separated by your cuts, name the pair where they coincide and say why the questions cannot prise them apart. No citations, no reading list, and no names of theories: I want to see what each position commits you to, not what it is called.
