In a seminar on constitutional law, a debate about whether a head of state may refuse assent to a bill, or dismiss a minister who still commands the confidence of the legislature, was shut down with the remark that the monarch "reigns but does not rule." I suspect that sentence is being made to carry more weight than it can bear, but I cannot say precisely what it asserts. Lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing — among them: that the monarch holds no legal power whatever; that legal powers exist but are conclusively governed by convention; that a residue of discretionary or reserve power survives for exceptional cases; that the monarch's real influence runs through being consulted, encouraged, and warned rather than through command; and that the phrase is doing something else altogether — and for each, say whether it is a description of how the office actually works, a normative recommendation about how it ought to work, or a definitional claim about what a constitutional monarchy is. Then say which of these readings would genuinely license the conclusion my interlocutor drew, which are compatible with one another, which exclude one another, and whether a modern constitutional monarchy — whether its powers are written into a codified constitution or left entirely to convention — can avoid committing to one of them. I am not after a single correct reading; I want the menu, with verdicts on each entry and on how the entries relate to one another.
