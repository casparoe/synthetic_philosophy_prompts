I am a graduate student in a clinical ethics seminar, and I keep noticing a pattern in the way institutions decide which creatures can suffer, and I would like help working out what view of consciousness those decisions must be assuming. For much of the twentieth century, surgeons in many hospitals operated on newborn infants with little or no analgesia, on the assumption that a nervous system that immature could not yet support the experience of pain; the practice shifted only once that assumption was challenged. Something similar is underway with animals: several jurisdictions have recently begun to extend welfare protections to octopuses and to crabs and lobsters, while the status of fish and of insects remains contested, and laboratory mice are protected for reasons that seem to have more to do with our sympathy for them than with any principled line. In the clinic, the decision to withdraw life-sustaining treatment from a patient who shows no behavioral response to commands is treated as a decision about whether anyone is still there to be harmed, even though a patient with locked-in syndrome also shows no behavioral response to commands, and everyone agrees that she is fully conscious and fully present.

What I want to know is what these practices presuppose about consciousness, and whether the presupposition holds together. Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Does the pattern presuppose a view on which a state counts as an experience only if the creature in some way represents that state to itself, a higher-order or self-representational condition that sets the threshold for a morally relevant inner life fairly high? Or does it presuppose something much weaker: a first-order view on which the relevant machinery is whatever makes information globally available, or simply whatever neural architecture resembles ours closely enough that we cannot help projecting? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it leave untouched? Someone in the seminar said the dispute here is between higher-order and first-order theories, but I could not tell whether that label was doing any real work or merely redescribing the disagreement.

Then I want you to press on three points where I suspect the practice is not of one piece. First, the criterion: if the practice is at bottom a practice of deferring to report, so that we believe the patient who says she is in pain and disbelieve the one who says nothing, then locked-in patients and the animals we protect should both fall on the wrong side of the line, and they do not; so is the operative criterion report, similarity, or the presence of some particular kind of representational architecture, and does the practice need a single criterion at all? Second, anesthesia: the whole institution of general anesthesia assumes both that a person can be conscious and leave no trace of it in memory, and that suppressing certain brain activity suppresses experience itself rather than merely the recording of it. A colleague proposes that we should attribute experience only where it makes sense to say that the creature could be mistaken about what it is undergoing, that is, only where there is a perspective on the state that could get it wrong; she claims this test explains why we extend the benefit of the doubt to mammals and not to a thermostat. I want to know whether the institutional practice actually embodies a test of that shape, and what such a test would exclude among the creatures we already protect. Third, the counterfactual: what would have to change in law, clinical protocol, and research regulation if the implicit view were replaced by its main rival? Be concrete about which current practices would become indefensible and which would become merely optional. Throughout, distinguish as sharply as you can between what these practices strictly presuppose and what they merely happen to fit, and tell me where they are philosophically underdetermined, that is, where several incompatible theories of consciousness would license exactly the same set of decisions. If the honest answer is that the practices presuppose no theory of consciousness at all, only a rough and reversible policy of extending the benefit of the doubt to whatever can protest, say so and defend it.
