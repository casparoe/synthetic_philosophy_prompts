I am a professor of philosophy at work on a paper concerning Marx, and I have been trying to formulate the principle that underlies my own judgments about exploitation and alienation. My verdicts on the cases are as follows. A factory owner who pays the going wage while appropriating the surplus exploits the workers, even if they freely contracted and could quit. A worker-owned cooperative that retains its surplus does not exploit its members, though it may still be alienated in its relation to the market. A freelance programmer who loves her work and owns her tools but must sell the product to a platform that takes a third is exploited by the platform, yet not alienated in the classic sense. A tenured professor who sets her own research agenda and identifies with her work is alienated in a way that has little to do with surplus extraction, because the university monetizes her output. A disabled person on an unconditional basic income who does no wage labor is neither exploited nor alienated, though dependent. A gig worker paid per task, unable to see the assigning algorithm, and forced to compete for ratings is both exploited and alienated, and the alienation seems to me prior to the exploitation. A wealthy artist who enjoys painting but despises the market that prices her canvases is not exploited but is alienated from her product. A member of a collectively owned factory that must extract surplus from itself to survive market competition: I want to say the members do not exploit one another, yet the market exploits them; but I also want to say exploitation requires an exploiter. An unpaid parent doing socially necessary care work that capital benefits from: I resist calling it exploited because there is no wage relation, but I call it alienated when the labor is invisible and unacknowledged.

My tentative principle is that exploitation is unilateral appropriation of surplus labor, while alienation is estrangement from activity, product, or species-being. That saves most verdicts but makes the professor, the parent, and the collective factory hard, and it leaves the priority of alienation in the gig case unexplained. Is there a single account, or a small set of candidates, that captures the pattern? State it precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, say what it predicts for cases I have not listed (a cooperative that hires temporary non-member labor; an AI system that allocates work without any human owner; a community that shares care work without wages), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the best account is plural, say what, if anything, unifies the parts.
