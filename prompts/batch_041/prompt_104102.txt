I'm a third-year philosophy student, and I'm taking a module on distributive justice this term while also sitting in on a graduate seminar on the philosophy of measurement, which is where the idea below first surfaced. I have to submit a two-page research proposal for a dissertation prospectus — one full-time term of work, roughly 8,000 words — and I'd like you to turn a half-formed thought of mine into something that would actually stand up to a supervisor's scrutiny. The thought is this: the literature on interpersonal utility comparisons is almost always framed as a question about whether such comparisons are metaphysically or epistemically *possible* — whether there is a common unit, whether we can reach into other minds, and so on. But it strikes me that the comparisons which do the real work for justice are not measurements of quantities at all. They are comparative judgments of the form 'X is worse off than Y', and these are made constantly, and without obvious embarrassment, by institutions — benefit assessments, clinical triage, awards of damages — none of which claim to have located a shared unit. So I want to prise the notion apart into two: a *metric* comparison (how much of some good each person has, on a common scale) and a *relational* or *justice* comparison (whether one person's being worse off than another stands in need of justification). The question I'd like the proposal to pose is whether a theory of distributive justice can do everything it needs with the second alone. If it can, then a great deal of the standard debate about the metric — and the levelling-down objection, which has always seemed to me to be attacking a position nobody is obliged to hold — may turn out to be beside the point.

There is a second wrinkle I keep circling and would like the proposal to take seriously. Comparisons are always *performed*, by some comparer, and the comparer's position — whether they are one of the parties, a disinterested third party, or an institution with a stake in the outcome — seems to shape what counts as a legitimate comparison, and indeed whether the comparison is a comparison or a decision in disguise. Yet the standard framing says almost nothing about who is entitled to make the comparison, or from what standpoint. I suspect this is where the genuine philosophical work lies, and that it bears directly on whether 'worse off' is a descriptive predicate or an evaluative one, and on whether the two can be separated at all.

Please write the proposal. I need: a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions — the standard metric debates, the levelling-down debate, and the relational-egalitarian move away from metrics — leave it open; the method, that is, what I would actually do, laid out month by month across the term, together with a defence of why conceptual analysis is the right approach rather than, say, a survey of how institutions in fact make these comparisons; a description of what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of scope, including what would make it too big for a single term. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations — refer to positions by description and I shall add the references myself — and write the whole thing in British English.
