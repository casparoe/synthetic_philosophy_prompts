I'm a backend engineer by trade — no formal philosophy training, though I've read a fair amount on the side — and next month I have the opening five-minute slot at a local philosophy-of-science reading group, ahead of a friend's talk on Bayesian confirmation theory. I want to open with a joke, and I want it to be a good one, so let me set out the puzzle I'd like it to turn on, stated as plainly as I can. On the standard Bayesian picture, an agent's evidence can raise the probability of a hypothesis only to the extent that the evidence was not already certain for her: if she had already assigned the evidence probability one, then conditionalizing on it leaves her credences exactly where they were, and on a likelihood-ratio measure of confirmation the ratio comes out to one as well. But in real scientific and engineering practice the strongest support we cite for a theory is often a fact we have known for decades — the theory earns its keep precisely by explaining something long taken for granted — and, closer to home, we treat a metric we already eyeballed before forming the hypothesis as though it could not really confirm anything, which is the intuition behind pre-registration and against hypothesizing after the results are known. Philosophers seem to call this the problem of old evidence, and as far as I can tell the honest escape routes are all unattractive: insist the agent was never really at probability one, let her run the calculation as if she had only just learned the fact, or concede that the confirmation is not a matter of conditionalizing at all.

Write me that joke.
