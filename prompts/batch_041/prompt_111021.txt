Two years ago I was asked to sit as the lay member on the Automated Decision Review Committee of our regional health authority. I am a retired geophysicist; forty years of measuring things indirectly left me with a taste for rules that make predictions, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with more appetite than method. That is the background to the request below.

Our committee rules on proposals to let a machine make decisions about patients with no clinician in the loop, and our rulings bind the authority. Six proposals have come to us. I set them out with the features I take to be relevant; the summaries are my own and fuller than the minutes.

1. Closed-loop insulin delivery in the diabetes clinic. The controller adjusts basal rates every five minutes and no clinician is in the loop. Patients are enrolled only with written agreement and can suspend the pump themselves. The objective is the patient's own time-in-range; a ceiling on total daily dose is coded as a separate veto that the controller cannot override; every adjustment is logged. Approved unanimously.

2. Autonomous antibiotic ordering from a sepsis model. The model orders without asking, but the intensivist must countersign within the hour and may cancel. Patients agree on admission to protocol-driven automated care. The objective is the patient's own survival. The ethical rules here are folded into the objective as penalties rather than kept as a separate layer, and the weights were learned from the unit's own historical clinician decisions. Approved.

3. A care robot in the long-term care home, handling transfers and repositioning. Residents, or their guardians, agree. Any member of staff, or the resident, can halt the machine with a physical button. The objective is the resident's own safety and comfort, and the machine hands control back to staff when its confidence is low. Everything is logged. Approved.

4. Referral de-prioritisation. The model quietly re-orders the specialist referral queue so that the clinics stay inside their annual budget. Patients are not told that their referrals are being scored; no clinician sees the ranking; the objective is spending against the budget; nothing is recorded in a form anyone reads. Rejected.

5. Attendance modelling. The model predicts which patients will not attend, shortens their reminder sequence without telling them, and re-offers their slots to others in order to raise clinic utilisation. No clinician reviews the reallocation before it takes effect. There is an appeal route in writing — a patient may write to the service manager — but the letter is read weeks after the slot has gone. Rejected.

6. Ward assignment at night. Each new admission is assigned to a general or a specialist ward by a model that minimises staff overtime. The patient is not told, no clinician sees the assignment before the move, and the record is kept but not reviewed. Rejected.

Next month's item is of a different kind, and I cannot get our own logic to yield an answer. The authority funds a fixed number of home-care nursing hours. There are at present some three hundred patients on the waiting list and enough hours for about half of them. The proposal is to let a system decide who receives hours and how many, reviewed monthly. Nobody on the waiting list will be asked to agree to being scored by it. The proposers' answer to that objection is procedural: the constraints the system must respect were written over four workshops with a standing panel of patients, carers and district nurses, and those constraints are implemented as a separate veto layer that the optimiser cannot override. The optimiser's objective is the expected benefit to the person receiving the hours — mobility, admissions avoided, strain on the carer — and not the authority's budget, which is fixed by the commissioners and does not appear in the objective at all. Allocations are published to the clinical team, remain provisional for forty-eight hours, and the duty clinician may reverse any of them. The system carries several candidate value models rather than one; when the leading models disagree about the ranking by more than a set margin, it declines to decide and escalates to a human panel. Everything is logged.

What I want is this. First, the verdict the committee is most likely to reach — or, if you think the better question is which verdict would cohere with our own record, then that, with the reasoning rather than a general discussion of how scarce resources ought to be allocated. Second, the several rules that our six rulings are jointly consistent with, and for each of them what it predicts about the home-care proposal; I can see that every approval had certain features and every rejection had none of them, but I cannot tell which part of that pattern is doing the work, and I suspect that more than one rule fits the record equally well. Third, which of the earlier six is the closest analogue to the new case, and what further proposal would separate the rules that survive — I would rather put that case to the committee deliberately than meet it by accident. And if the record genuinely underdetermines the home-care case, say what principle we would be adopting by approving it and what principle by rejecting it.
