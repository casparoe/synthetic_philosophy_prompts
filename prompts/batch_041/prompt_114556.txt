Hi — second-year PhD student in political theory, trying to write a section of my dissertation on sortition, and I keep triping over something about how citizens' assemblies actually get run. I can't tell whether it's a real tension in the practice or just me being slow.

Here's the practice, roughly as it goes everywhere. You've got a contentious question — abortion, assisted dying, constitutional reform — and instead of electing a body you sortition it: names drawn at random from the electoral register. Except almost nobody runs a clean lottery. In practice it's stratified, so that the group matches the population on age, gender, region, education, sometimes even on whether people turned out at the last election. Then once everyone's in the room you feed them a curated set of expert briefings and "balanced" witnesses, because obviously you don't want them deliberating off whatever they saw on Facebook. Members usually get paid a stipend, and the recommendations get taken seriously — Ireland ran an actual referendum off the back of one of these.

Now the other half. Jury service is the sortition we all actually live under, and it's the same institution turned inside out. Same lottery off the same kind of list, but there the lawyers get to strike people — voir dire, peremptory challenges — nobody's paid, nobody gets expert briefings, they just get the evidence and the judge's instructions. And yet we give juries far more deference than assemblies get. We'll overturn a conviction based on what twelve randomly drawn strangers concluded after a few days, but an assembly's recommendation is treated as advisory input into a process the politicians still control.

So what does the assembly practice actually presuppose? My first instinct was that sortition commits you to a view about ordinary people being competent to decide hard questions — that's the whole pitch against election, that you don't need to be a specialist to have political judgment. But then why stratify? If the lottery is fair because everyone's judgment counts equally, drawing to match the population only makes sense if you think the verdict has to be descriptively representative, which is a different and much stronger claim, and one a plain lottery is supposed to make unnecessary. And why brief them? If the body is a microcosm, curating its information looks like you don't trust the microcosm. I keep feeling like the practice is quietly commited to something like "people are competent enough to be handed a verdict but not competent enough to be handed the inputs."

Then there's the variant that really breaks my brain. Someone in my department wants to exclude from the pool anyone who's already taken a public position on the question — signed the petition, donated, posted about it, got into it at Christmas dinner. His reasoning is that the whole value of sortition is getting people without a dog in the fight. But that looks like it presupposes that having a stake disqualifies you, which is precisely what sortition is supposed to avoid presupposing — the lottery doesn't care what you think, that's the entire point of it.

Could you untangle this for me? Specifically: (1) what view of legitimacy, competence and representation the assembly practice presupposes, and which rival views it quietly rules out; (2) whether the pieces are consistent or whether it's two theories stapled together; (3) which parts are strictly presupposed and which are just convention, i.e. where the practice is underdetermined and several theories would license the same design; (4) what the jury contrast actually shows, since juries seem to run on a different picture of the same lottery; (5) what happens to the practice if you swap in the exclusion rule — does it turn into something else? And please don't hand me a reading list, I can find the usual names.
