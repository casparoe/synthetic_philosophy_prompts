I am a doctoral student in history, and my supervisor runs a weekly methods seminar in which a member presents a work and she then delivers a verdict in one of three forms: 'objective history', 'history but not objective', or 'not history at all'. Her verdicts so far:

- A reconstruction of a seventeenth-century treaty negotiation built entirely from diplomatic correspondence, which draws no lesson for the present and keeps the author's voice out of the text: 'objective history'.
- A microhistory pieced together from a single inquisitorial dossier, in which the author sets out her inferences one by one and marks each as an inference rather than a finding: 'objective history'.
- A quantitative study of a nineteenth-century famine, reconstructing wages, prices and mortality from parish registers, whose introduction states plainly that it is written to inform present-day famine relief: 'objective history'.
- A study of a colonial massacre that rests largely on the descendants' oral testimony, which the author treats as beyond corroboration, argues that the killings were genocidal, and closes by demanding reparations: 'history but not objective'.
- A study of a revolution told as a heroic romance; its documented facts are right, but it minimizes the counter-revolutionary testimony and arranges what remains so that the narrative culminates in the liberation: 'history but not objective'.
- A counterfactual exercise asking what would have followed had a particular battle gone otherwise, worked out with formal models rather than sources: 'not history at all'.

She has one phrase she uses about some of these: a work can keep the archive's floor and build its own ceiling, meaning that the evidence base is sound while the interpretive superstructure is the author's own.

Next week's book is a study of another nineteenth-century famine, built from the same parish registers and administrative correspondence as the earlier one and analytically disciplined throughout, with no evidence suppressed and no claim insulated from correction. It is written as a tragedy, and its preface says the tragic form is a choice the author is making for the present; but it pointedly declines to assign blame to anyone.

What will she say: 'objective history', 'history but not objective', or 'not history at all'? I care less about the label than about the machinery behind it. Set out the candidate rules that fit her record, say what each predicts about the new book, identify any candidate rule her record already rules out and on the strength of which verdict, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best separate the rules that remain.
