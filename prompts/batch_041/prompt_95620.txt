Write a sonnet in the voice of Mary, the colour scientist who has spent her whole life in a room without colour and has just come out. Let the octave be pure third-person description — the optics, the retinal machinery, the opponent channels, the reflectance curves — such that those eight lines alone contain everything she could have been taught in advance, and let them contain no colour word at all. The volta is the seeing itself: the sestet turns to the first person, and the poem must end with exactly one colour word, as its final word.

Two conditions. First, keep out the technical vocabulary of the debate — not qualia, not phenomenal character, not physical facts; the argument should be enacted in the poem's shape rather than named. Second, do not tip your hand on the question the debate turns on: a reader who knows the literature should be able to take that last word as a newly learned fact, as a newly acquired ability, or as neither, and the poem should sit equally well with all three.

Please write in British English. Give me nothing but the poem — no title, no epigraph, no notes — in no more than fourteen lines.
