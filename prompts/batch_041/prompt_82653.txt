I spent thirty-odd years running numerical weather models and retired a couple years back, and I've been teaching myself philosophy since, so I know the physics cold and the philosophy not at all — which I hope is a useful place to ask from. Here's what I want. Take the old for-want-of-a-nail story about 1914 — Franz Ferdinand's driver takes a wrong turn in Sarajevo on the 28th of June, the car stops, and Princip just happens to be standing on that corner — and run it through deterministic chaos, the thing I actually know. My suspicion is that the historians are reaching for the wrong model. Sensitive dependence, the butterfly business, does say small differences blow up, but it also says the outcome isn't traceable to the small difference: you can say the trajectories seperate, you can't read the war off the wrong turn. What the nail story actually needs is the other picture, a system parked near a tipping point where a nudge flips it out of one stable state into another, and there the small cause really does decide which one you get. So which of these fits July 1914 — what's the state space, what's the attractor, are the statesmen a perturbation or the people shoving a metastable thing over the edge? And tell me where the mapping breaks, because I half suspect it's carrying more weight than it can bear, and I'd rather hear where it goes silent than watch it get patched. Then the part I'm most curious about: does the episode say anything back about the theory, does unpredictability in my sense have anything to do with the historian's sense of contingency or am I just conflating two words, and does any of this bear on whether the war had to happen?
