I am trying to work through the second section of Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals, but I am reading it in a borrowed translation I cannot check against the German, so what follows is my own paraphrase of the stretch that has me stuck, not a quotation.

A maxim is the subjective principle of volition. The categorical imperative tells me to act only on that maxim through which I can at the same time will that it become a universal law. When I test a maxim by asking whether it could hold as a universal law of nature, some maxims fail because they cannot even be thought as such a universal law without contradiction; others fail because, although they can be thought as universal laws, a will that had them as its laws would contradict itself. Kant's four examples are the man who, from self-love, resolves to end his life when it promises more evil than good; the man who, needing money, resolves to borrow while promising repayment although he knows he cannot repay; the man who lets his talents rust; and the man who, being comfortable, resolves not to help others in distress. The first two are said to violate a strict or indispensable duty, the last two a meritorious one.

Help me understand what is actually going on in the distinction. What is the contradiction in the first two cases, and what kind of thing is it — a logical impossibility, an impossibility in the order of nature, or something about the will's own structure? Why is the failure in the last two cases supposed to be a different kind of failure, given that Kant runs all four through the same formula? And is the item under test the maxim, the particular act, or the agent's whole practical standpoint? Finally, apply the distinction to a maxim that Kant never discusses and that has no settled treatment in the literature: the maxim of accepting an offer of employment with the intention of quitting should a better offer arrive before the start date. Tell me which sort of failure, if any, that maxim meets, and whether your answer exposes a problem in the distinction itself.
