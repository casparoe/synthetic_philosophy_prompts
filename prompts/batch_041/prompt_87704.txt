You are a philosopher of mind and an experienced graduate instructor who has taught heterophenomenology many times. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation of the view itself. My audience is a ninety-minute graduate seminar in a cognitive science department: the students know some neuroscience and have encountered Dennett's name, but they are impatient with philosophy and split between two assumptions—that first-person reports are direct readouts of consciousness, and that reports are just noise to be ignored. I want to teach heterophenomenology as a method for turning reports into public, checkable data without settling the metaphysics of consciousness. Before drafting the plan, think carefully and brainstorm the conceptual dependencies and the likely misconceptions; then give me the plan in a clear sequence.

What I need from you: first, the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one—from the problem of the report, to the heterophenomenological stance, to the heterophenomenological world, to the relation between that world and the subject's brain and behavior. Second, the one carrying example that should run through the whole session. I am inclined to use a split-brain patient who verbally denies seeing an object while his left hand reaches for it, but tell me whether that is the right example or whether another case would carry the dependencies better; if you keep it, specify what the example must include and what it must not include, for instance that it must not presuppose an answer to the question of whether consciousness is split. Third, the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form—at minimum, that heterophenomenology is behaviorism, that it denies consciousness, that it takes reports at face value, that it makes reports incorrigible, that it is just introspection with a new name, and that it is a form of fictionalism—and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, with a sentence or two of what I should say. Fourth, a check for understanding at the end that would show whether they can use the method rather than merely recite it: something like asking them to separate questions heterophenomenology can answer from questions it cannot, or to produce a two-column reading of a sample report. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—the hard problem, neural correlates, illusionism, the details of Dennett's other commitments—and how I should say so without making the method look like a dodge.

Do not give me readings, citations, or bibliography; the plan must be self-contained and usable without internet access. Write as if you are coaching me through the session, with the dependency structure and the misconception map as the core of the deliverable.
