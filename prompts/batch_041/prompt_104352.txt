I teach a graduate seminar that sits between moral philosophy and the philosophy of memory, and I keep colliding with a framing that my students, and frankly a few of my colleagues, treat as self-evident: that a community bears its past the way a person does. On this picture a nation that has committed or suffered atrocities has something like a memory, which can be healthy or repressed and which it can either confront or bury; commemorating becomes a patient working through a trauma, a political amnesty becomes suppression, and the past stays pathogenic until the underlying material is dragged into the light. The framing is doing enormous work in live arguments about memorials, truth commissions, and whether a state can owe its victims an act of remembrance — I suspect it is precisely what lets people treat Margalit's duty to remember as a premise rather than a metaphor, and it is equally what Rieff's polemic against the therapeutic turn is aimed at. What bothers me is that my students take the conclusion — that communities carry exactly the memory-duties and memory-pathologies that persons do, no more and no less — as following almost automatically, and I am fairly sure the analogy is carrying the argument rather than merely illustrating it.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than evaluate the literature. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that is cleanest: what plays the role of the individual memory trace, of recall, of repression, of the unconscious, of the therapist or the therapeutic process, and of the subject who owns the memory in the first place. Then show me where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into two piles — those that merely make collective memory messier than individual memory (say, that it is distributed across archives, monuments, curricula, and habits rather than stored in a single head; that it is curated by whoever holds institutional power rather than retrieved automatically; that it is contested among subgroups rather than unified in one subject) and those that genuinely undermine the inference my students are drawing. With the disanalogies on the table, tell me what becomes of each of the two positions I mentioned: does a duty to remember survive the loss of the analogy or does it need to be re-grounded on something other than the person-writ-large picture, and does the charge that therapeutic remembering is itself a symptom rather than a cure depend on the analogy or can it stand on its own? Close by saying whether a better analogy is available for the relation between a community and its past — and be candid about which side, the remembering camp or the forgetting camp, the better analogy actually favors. Please keep this purely conceptual; I know the secondary literature and I do not need citations, reading lists, or reconstructions of what any particular author said. What I want is the mapping, the disanalogies that matter, and an honest verdict on whether the conclusion was ever doing anything but riding on the analogy.
