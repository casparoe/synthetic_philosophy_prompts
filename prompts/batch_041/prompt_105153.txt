I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've been working on a piece about A.J. Ayer — the logical positivist — and the odd fact that near the very end of his life he had a near-death experience (bright, painful red light, and he came away with the impression that the light was somehow responsible for the government of the universe) and admitted afterwards that it had loosend his confidence that death is the end of everything. But the anecdote isn't what I'm writing about, it's the argument he'd made decades earlier in Language, Truth and Logic about survival after death, which I've been trying to lay out cleanly and which seems to me to prove far more than he wanted it to. I've reconstructed it like this:

(1) A claim is meaningful only if some possible experience would count for or against it.
(2) Take the claim that I will survive the death of my body. What experience could verify it? A voice at a seance, a ghost, a resurrected body with my memories — each of these at most shows that some entity is present with certain features.
(3) To verify that *I* survive, you'd need a criterion for deciding when a person at one time is the same person as a person at another, since the surviving entity is supposed to be the same person as me.
(4) But there is no such criterion. Nothing observable settles identity across a gap like that; there's no fact of the matter to observe.
(5) So the claim isn't false, it's meaningless — not a bad hypothesis but not a hypothesis at all.

Here's my worry. Everything from (3) onward seems to eat ordinary survival too. I go to sleep tonight and wake up tomorrow — is there a criterion for tomorrow's person being the same person as me that's any firmer than the one I supposedly lack after death? If not, by (1)–(5) the claim that I'll exist tomorrow comes out meaningless as well, which is absurd and clearly not what Ayer thought. Same for the man who wakes from a coma still being my father, or my being the one who did the thing I remember doing. So either the criterion exists and the death case merely lacks the relevant observations, or it doesn't exist and ordinary persistence goes down with it.

So: which premise or step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd rather keep (1) if I can, since the whole verificationist project rests on it, but I don't know what's left of Ayer's position if I do. Also — does his own near-death experience do any philosophical work here, or is it just a biographical curiosity? From what I can tell he kept the philosophical line and only reported the psychological wobble, but I might be flattening something.

And please don't hand me a confident verdict dressed up as the consensus. Flag what's settled among people who actually work on Ayer and on personal identity versus what's genuinely disputed — including whether Ayer himself treated the missing criterion as a special feature of the death case or as a general problem, and whether anyone today accepts the verificationist premise at all.
