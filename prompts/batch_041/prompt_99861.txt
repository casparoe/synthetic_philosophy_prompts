ok so I need help writing a commencement speech, and I've made it weird for myself on purpose.

backstory: I've been reading rationalist blogs for like fifteen years — LessWrong, SSC, all the descendants — and the idea that's stuck hardest is the merit/desert one. The gist, as I've internalized it: "earning it" kind of dissolves on inspection. Effort is downstream of stuff you didn't pick — your temperament, your upbringing, whether one adult ever believed in you, whether you were born with a brain that can sit still. And if you say effort is the creditable part, fine, but then you need a second-order effort to have earned the first effort, and that either regresses forever or bottoms out in a dice roll. I basically buy this. Maybe I shouldn't. But I do, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise in a speech.

Here's the bind. I'm giving the commencement address at the reentry/returning-students program at our local community college. The graduates are mostly people who came back to school after prison, or addiction, or a decade of dead-end work. Their families will be there. So will the donors who funded the program, and they definately want to hear that these graduates earned this. And I don't think "you earned it" is quite true, and I don't want to stand up there and say a thing I think is false just because it's the nice thing.

But "none of you deserves anything, it's all luck" is also wrong for the room — it's cruel, and it's not even the point. So I need a speech where the philosophical view actually does structural work: it decides what the speech can be about, not just a paragraph I drop in and then get back to the normal graduation stuff.

My hunch is that the way out is to move the whole thing from backward-looking desert to something else — celebrating that this happened at all, gratitude, the forward-looking commitments people are about to make, the fact that a bunch of us got to be here for it. But I honestly can't tell if that's a real solution or a dodge that just relabels "earned it" as "grateful for it." I want you to figure out which, and if it's a dodge, say so and do better.

One concrete device I want the speech built around, because I think it does the work: the lucky twin. For any graduate, imagine the version of them with the same effort, same choices, same slog — but one worse roll of the dice. Same grit, but a body that got sick at nineteen, or a father who hit, or a judge who threw the book, or just a town with no program like this one. That twin is in the room in a sense, and my intuition is you have to hold the twin in exactly the same regard as the graduate. And then the question the speech has to actually answer, not just pose: if you honor the graduate but not the twin, what are you honoring — the person, or the dice?

Extra constraint I want to try, because it forces honesty: write the speech so it never once says anyone deserved this. Not the word, not the idea smuggled back in as "you did this yourself." See if it's still possible to make it genuinely moving and warm under that constraint. If it isn't possible, that's a finding — tell me.

Practical stuff: about 8–10 minutes spoken. Land it for the families and the donors, not just for me — the donors especially, since the version of the speech that flatters them is the one I most want to avoid. No naming philosophers, no reading lists, no citations; the people in that room don't need a syllabus and a model without web access couldn't produce one anyway.

Then, for my eyes only, a short note at the end: where this leans on the most contested version of the no-desert argument, where the reasoning is weakest, and what would actually change my mind — what argument or consideration would make me drop the lucky-twin device entirely and write a normal speech where people just earned things. I'd rather know now than find out from the faces in the front row.
