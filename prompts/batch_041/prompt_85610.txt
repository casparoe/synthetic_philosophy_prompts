I am a physicist, and until about a year ago Émilie du Châtelet was, for me, a professional ancestor: the translator and commentator of Newton, the person who took Leibniz's side in the vis viva dispute. Then a friend, knowing that I was in the middle of deciding whether to leave research, gave me her essay on happiness — written in the 1740s, never published in her lifetime. It has stayed with me in a way her mechanics never did, and I have now agreed to run a session on it, which is why I am writing to you.

The session: forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and roughly twenty graduate students and postdocs in the physical sciences. None of them has read any philosophy; all of them are used to claims that can be checked; several of them are at exactly the point in their careers where I was. I do not want you to explain her view to me. I want a plan for how I explain it to them, in the order the pieces have to come, so that it arrives as a position to be argued with rather than a list of opinions to be admired.

What has to be conveyed, roughly: she treats happiness as a project with conditions rather than a matter of luck, and she lays out those conditions — health, freedom from prejudice, virtue, strong passions and tastes, and a continued susceptibility to illusion. The last two are what I keep circling. She wants us clear-eyed about prejudice, which sounds like a demand for truth, and in the same breath insists we preserve the illusions that make a life bearable; she tells us to decide what we want to do and to be, to refuse regret, and to keep thoughts of death, including the deaths of the people we love, out of the room; and yet she also appears to counsel settling into the station one has been given rather than trying to change it — while her own life was one long refusal to accept hers. I have not decided whether these are tensions in her view, tensions in mine, or the whole point.

I want the plan to have that tension as its spine rather than as a footnote. So: the order of ideas, with the dependencies made explicit — what has to be in place before her demand for illusion can be heard as anything other than an invitation to self-deception, and where the biographical material belongs, if anywhere, given that it is both the most seductive thing I have and possibly the most misleading. The one example or case that should carry the whole explanation, and what it must have and lack to do that job. The misconceptions I should expect — my guesses are that they will hear a self-help author with a checklist, a hedonist, or a sophisticated liar — which of those is the real danger, and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The single question I can put to them at the end that would tell me the idea landed rather than merely registered. And what I should deliberately leave out at this level, saying so, including whether any of her physics belongs in the room at all.

Two constraints. No readings: they will not have read anything, I do not want the plan to depend on their reading anything afterwards, and I do not want to be pointed at secondary literature. And no reliance on her exact wording: I will be working from a translation, and I would rather the explanation not turn on a phrase. Give me the plan, not the explanation.
