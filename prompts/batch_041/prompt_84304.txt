I have no academic background in philosophy, though I read on my own and argue about it with friends, and I want to settle a question that keeps pulling me back. The case, usually called the toxin puzzle, goes like this. A billionaire offers you a large sum, say a million dollars, payable at midnight tonight, on one condition: at that moment you must genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin at eight the next morning. He is an excellent judge of intentions and pays only if the intention is real. The toxin will make you violently ill for about a day and then leave you completely unharmed. Nothing obliges you to drink it: at eight in the morning you may drink it or pour it down the sink, and either way the money is yours. The difficulty is that you can see ahead of time that drinking it would cost you a day of illness and gain you nothing, so you might well conclude that you will not drink it, and it is unclear whether you can sincerely intend, at midnight, to do something you are sure you will not do.

I would like a decision tree that starts from someone who accepts this description and leads them to a definite position on what a person in that position should do. Every branch point should be a question about the case that a person could answer without knowing which position it leads toward, so that the tree sorts people by what they say about the case rather than by the label they favor. No node should simply ask whether intentions are under our voluntary control, or anything of that shape; that distinction has to be reached through questions about concrete variants. Please build the variants into the tree itself: what you would do if a friend offered to hold you to it by force, if the billionaire's judgment of your intention were only about ninety percent reliable, if you could swallow a pill tonight that would make you want the toxin in the morning, and if he offered to pay not for your intention but for your being certain you will drink it. I would also like the tree to make visible where the question of what a person should do comes apart from the question of what the case shows about what an intention is, since those two seem to get run together in every argument I have had about this.

At minimum the tree needs to reach these leaves: form the intention, take the money, and drink; form the intention, take the money, and then not drink; turn the offer down because you cannot sincerely form the intention; accept and arrange for someone or something else to make sure you drink; deny that the case is possible as described; and hold that the money is really being paid for being a certain kind of person rather than for a passing intention. Give each position its standard name if it has one, and mark it unnamed otherwise; I do not want names required anywhere else. Where two leaves differ only in vocabulary, merge them and say so, and where the tree genuinely cannot separate two positions, flag that rather than forcing a split.

Finally, tell me which combinations of answers the tree leaves empty, and whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and give each leaf one sentence on the most awkward thing it has to say about the case. Six to nine leaves, please, and no citations, references, or reading lists: I want the structure of the disagreement, not pointers to where other people have put it.
