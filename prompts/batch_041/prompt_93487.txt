I've been trying to get my head around the debate over whether our beliefs, expectations, and prior knowledge can actually change what we see -- not merely how we describe or interpret things, but the raw look of them -- and I keep running into the feeling that the examples people cite aren't all doing the same kind of work. I have no training in psychology or philosophy; I've been collecting these cases from popular articles, podcasts, and my own experience, and I'd like you to act as a philosopher of perception who specializes in exactly this question. Please think carefully before you answer, because the tempting move is to treat every case as the same sort of evidence, and I suspect that is where the whole issue goes wrong. Begin by setting out, in your own words, what would have to be true for a case to count as genuine penetration of perception by cognition, and what neighboring phenomena it has to be distinguished from (attention, expectation, memory, post-perceptual judgment, plain guessing); about 150 words, under the heading "Setting the bar."

Then rank the following eight cases into tiers -- call them "Strong evidence," "Suggestive but contestable," "Probably not penetration," and "Clearly a different phenomenon" -- according to how much each one, taken at face value, supports the idea that cognition reaches down into perception itself rather than operating on top of it. Present the tier list as a table, one row per case, each row with a one- or two-sentence justification, and put every case in exactly one tier. The cases: (1) I know two lines in an optical illusion are the same length, and I still see them as different -- the knowledge simply won't budge the percept. (2) I have eaten bananas my whole life, and a banana in dim light looks to me a more saturated yellow than a gray patch that measures the same color. (3) A radiologist with years of training reports seeing a fracture immediately, as a visible feature of the scan, where I see only noise. (4) After the word "bread" is flashed before a blurry picture, the picture looks like bread to me and I cannot make it look like anything else. (5) A friend insists that after learning to tell two kinds of bird apart, the two now look obviously different to her, though they looked identical before. (6) When I am frightened in the dark, the shadows in a room seem to creep toward me. (7) I have been told a particular shade is called "blue" in one language and "green" in another; when I silently repeat the other language's word, the shade looks slightly more green to me than when I repeat my own. (8) If I stare at a common word long enough, it stops looking like a word and dissolves into meaningless marks, even though I know perfectly well what it says.

Finally, under the heading "Where the tiers are doing the work," write about 200 words on which cases gave you the most trouble to place and why, and under the heading "Bottom line" give me your verdict in two or three sentences on how much of the everyday "seeing is believing" intuition survives once the cases are sorted this way. Don't just tell me which case goes where -- the whole point is where the boundaries fall, so flag any case whose tier hinges on a contested assumption and name that assumption explicitly.
