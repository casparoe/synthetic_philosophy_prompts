I run a small reading group on consequentialism and I keep having the same fight with a friend — she says the separateness-of-persons objection is one objection, and I think by the time you've spelled out what it's actually an objection *to* you've already got four or five different positions that people then wrongly bundle together under the heading of the utilitarian reply. So I want to settle it with a decision tree I can hand out. Take as the starting commitment: someone accepts that the total or average welfare of everyone can in principle outweigh what happens to any one person, and that there's something troubling about that. From there, each internal node should be a single question a reader could answer without already knowing which named position sits at the leaf — for instance, what exactly the objection claims is wrong (that persons are being counted together at all, that aggregation lets the losers be sacrificed with nothing owed to them, that it ignores the fact that each life has its own point of view, or that it treats persons as mere receptacles of welfare), and whether the objection is meant as a metaphysical claim about the nature of persons or a straightforwardly moral complaint about fairness. I especially want the tree to force a verdict on the proves-too-much worry: if the objection says we can't trade one person's good off against another's, does the same reasoning forbid trading a person's earlier good off against their own later good — and if a leaf has to bite that bullet, say so right on the leaf. There's also the Parfit-style move that the objection presupposes a sharper boundary between persons than personal identity really supports; I want that as a branch, not as a leaf, since it's a reason to think the objection fails rather than a view about what it targets.

Two things I care about here that are probably not in any textbook. First, whether a position survives the compensation case: a policy that badly harms one person, but where that person is later made more than whole, or where the harm was consented to in advance — I want to see which leaves have to call that a violation of separateness anyway and which can allow it, because that's what actually separates the interesting views from the slogan versions. Second, the distinction between sum-ranking (the aggregative structure) and welfarism (the claim that only welfare counts) — a lot of people write as if rejecting one rejects the other, and I'd like the tree to keep them apart so I can see whether any leaf rejects only one of them. Aim for maybe eight to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves, each leaf labeled with the position (or unnamed, if nobody I'd recognize holds it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a flag on any pair of leaves the tree genuinly can't tell apart — I'd rather have an honest these-two-collapse than a fake extra question padding it out. And don't just give me a list of names with descriptions attached; build it out of the questions first, then tell me which combinations nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
