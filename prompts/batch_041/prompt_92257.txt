I've been reading about how the word 'genocide' got invented and it set off a pattern I keep noticing and don't have a name for. Lemkin's own idea was broader than what ended up in the treaty -- the drafters dropped the cultural-destruction part and left political groups out, and the definition everyone now fights over is essentially a compromise text from 1948. Here's the thing I mean. You get into an argument about whether some atrocity is a genocide, and everybody agrees on the facts -- the killing, the intent, the scale -- and then the whole conversation turns into a fight about the definition: does the targeted group count, does destroying part of a group qualify, does the intent have to be the only motive. And it never settles, because the two sides arent answering the same question. For a government, saying the word is a commitment; you're now on the hook to do something. For an activist, saying the word is the something. So 'is this a genocide' quietly replaces 'what should be done about this', and the descriptive question can't be pulled apart from what saying it would oblige you to do. I think it happens with other words too -- 'famine' has a threshold and a committee and an announcement that lands late and then gets treated as the response -- but genocide is the clearest case. Is there a name for this? I know about people stretching a term to cover too much, and I know the usual complaint that we argue about labels instead of acting, and neither is what I mean. What I want is the specific move where the word carries an obligation for whoever says it, so the factual question and the practical one fuse, and the compromises baked into a legal text end up as the default answer to a moral question. If there's no term for it, name it and define it, and tell me where the edges are -- what's the nearest thing that isnt this, and where the line runs.
