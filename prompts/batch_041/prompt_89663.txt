I host a small epistemology podcast — 400 listeners on a good week, but the guests are real — and next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent her career defending positive introspection. Her line, as I understand it: the KK principle, the claim that if you know something then you know that you know it, is true for a genuine and non-trivial class of cases, and the standard anti-luminosity argument only bites against conditions that come in degrees. Roughly: sure, you can't be in a position to know you're in a condition while you're stuck at a threshold, so marginal cases do kill luminosity for stuff like it being warm in here, or this being a heap. But she says the argument has no grip on all-or-nothing conditions — you either are or you aren't in them, there's no continuum — and that when your knowledge rests on one of those categorical conditions, you really do know that you know. I honestly don't know how much of this is her own idiosyncratic framing versus a recognized move in the debate, and that's part of why I want the questions to be good rather than just agreeable.

Write me 8 to 10 interview questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I dont want her just restating the view. Specifically, I want at least one question each on:

1. the gradable-versus-categorical distinction itself — what makes a condition all-or-nothing rather than threshold-y, and whether knowing that p is supposed to sit on the categorical side, because if it does the whole thing starts to look like it assumes what it's meant to prove;
2. the dialectic — whether she's conceding the anti-luminosity result for the degree cases and just narrowing the battlefield, and what she thinks that argument actually turns on (the margin-for-error reasoning) as opposed to degrees as such;
3. the step from luminosity to KK, since being in a position to know you're in condition C doesn't obviously amount to knowing that you know, and I suspect there's a gap there she'll want to paper over;
4. the old regress and over-intellectualization worries about KK — finite minds, animals, small children — and whether restricting it to categorical conditions actually gets her out of them or just relocates them.

Press her where the literature actually presses, not where it's easy. End with one question that could genuinely make her uncomfortable, something she can't answer just by narrowing the class again, and say in a line why you think it's the hard one.

One more thing: separate for me what's actually settled in this area from what's still genuinely contested among the people who work on it, and flag anywhere you'd be presenting one camp's line as if it were the consensus — I don't want to say something on air that the other side would just roll their eyes at.
