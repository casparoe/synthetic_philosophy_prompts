I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I apologize in advance if I misuse any terms. Over the past year I have been reading around the edges of computational complexity theory, and I keep noticing a pattern in how my colleagues and I actually talk and act that I cannot square with itself. On the one hand, we treat a problem's being NP-complete as a settled, almost physical fact about the problem: we say that scheduling, or protein folding, or the traveling salesman problem is intractable, and we mean that no efficient algorithm exists or ever will, not merely that nobody has found one yet. On the other hand, we routinely ship products built on SAT solvers and integer-programming solvers that handle instances of these very problems every day, and we build entire cryptographic systems on the assumption that certain functions are hard to invert — assumptions that, so far as I can tell, nobody has proved, and some of which would simply be false if P turned out to equal NP. It seems to me that we are simultaneously treating complexity results as deep discoveries about the world and treating their unproven cousins as engineering conveniences we are willing to stake security on.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the philosophical commitments that this set of practices presupposes. What view of what complexity theory is about — a theory of algorithms, of problems, of physical limits, or of our own epistemic bounds — does it take for granted? What does calling something intractable because it is NP-complete presuppose about the relationship between worst-case complexity and the difficulty of the instances anyone actually cares about, and about whether a proof of NP-completeness is a fact about the world or a fact about what can be proved in a given formal system? What theory of feasibility does the practice presuppose — does it need the identification of the feasible with the polynomial-time solvable, and is that identification something the practice depends on or merely something that happens to be convenient? And does grounding cryptographic security in unproven hardness assumptions presuppose something about the epistemology of mathematical conjecture — for instance, that a well-tested, widely-attacked assumption is rationally acceptable as a foundation for trust even absent proof?

Then I would like you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and to tell me whether its various parts presuppose consistent things or whether, as I suspect, there is a genuine tension between the hardness-is-a-fact attitude and the hardness-is-an-assumption-we-engineer-around attitude. I would also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several different theories of complexity, feasibility, or mathematical knowledge would license exactly the same engineering behavior. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival — say, one on which complexity classes are artifacts of a chosen machine model and an asymptotic idealization rather than robust features of the world, or one on which average-case behavior is what matters — what in our day-to-day practice would actually have to change, and what would stay the same because it never really rested on the theory at all?
