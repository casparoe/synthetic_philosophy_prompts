My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the puzzle Wigner raised, and I came away unable to accept his conclusion but equally unable to say where his reasoning breaks. I'd like you to help me diagnose it.

His argument, as he wrote it out for me, runs as follows.

(1) That our mathematics applies to nature stands in need of explanation only if the application is not the product of our having selected the mathematics for its applicability.

(2) It is largely such a product. We keep the formalisms that have worked on the phenomena we have tried them on and quietly abandon, or never revisit, the ones that did not; what we call mathematical physics is the residue of that filtering. He offered two supports here: a civilization whose physics were written in a nonstandard set theory or an intuitionistic logic would report the same "effectiveness," which suggests the miracle lies in the practice rather than the world; and any finite run of data can be fitted by a polynomial of high enough degree, so bare fit shows nothing.

(3) A tool retained because it worked on the cases it was tried on is no surprise when it works on those cases; that is a selection effect, not evidence of correspondence.

(4) Hence the effectiveness that struck Wigner as unreasonable is an artifact of selection and requires no further explanation.

(5) The same account holds for any body of theory we keep because it worked: its success on the data that selected it is no evidence of its truth.

(6) If success on the selecting data is no evidence of truth, then neither is success on data not used in selection, since new data differ from old only in not yet having been used to select, and a theory selected for its past fit has no claim on the future beyond whatever that fit already failed to establish.

(7) So the predictive success of a theory is never evidence of its truth.

(8) But predictive success—a theory's calling a phenomenon no one had yet looked for—is our best evidence of truth, and a view on which no experiment could ever confirm anything is not one I am prepared to hold.

(9) So the selectionist account must be wrong somewhere.

He reads (9) as a refutation of the realist gloss on mathematics and a vindication of his own deflationary one. I find the deflationary spirit of (2) through (4) attractive, and I also regard (8) as non-negotiable; I cannot tell whether his argument is valid with one premise I should abandon or whether it slips at a step, and I do not want to reject more than I have to. What I am after is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on realism: which step should go, why it looked acceptable, what giving it up would cost, and what the argument nonetheless gets right. If you think the chain is sound and the real question is which premise is least costly to surrender, say so and price the options.

Please give me your diagnosis in prose, and then a JSON object with exactly these fields: "step_rejected" (the number or numbers of the step(s) to give up, as a string); "argument_is_valid" (true or false); "why_it_looked_fine" (a few sentences); "cost_of_rejecting" (what one must give up or explain away); "rival_diagnoses" (a list of objects, each with "step", "cost", and "why_less_attractive"); "novel_prediction_verdict" (what becomes of novel predictive success on your reading); "what_the_argument_gets_right" (the insight worth keeping); and "confidence" (a number between 0 and 1).
