Im building a session on Hume's essay on taste — the one where he says beauty is a sentiment in the mind rather than a property of the object, but then still insists that some judgments of taste are absurd and ridiculous and that there's a standard we appeal to. I keep running into the same fork and I want a case that forces it.

The fork: Hume seems to offer two different candidates for what the standard actually is. One is the verdict of the ideal critics — the people who have the five virtues he lists (fine-grained sensitivity, lots of practice, wide comparison across works and eras, freedom from prejudice, sound understanding). The other is the general rules of art, which he says get discovered from experience of what has pleased people across times and places. Normally these two are supposed to agree, and the critics are supposed to be the ones who articulate the rules. I want a single work where they come apart.

So: construct a thought experiment with one work of art — describe it concretely, not as a bare hypothetical — where:

- the work has genuinely and durably pleased almost everyone who has ever encountered it, across cultures and centuries, and the pleasure isn't explained by ignorance, manipulation, novelty, or low attention;
- the ideal critics, satisfying all five virtues to a high degree, converge on the verdict that it is shallow — and they aren't being snobs, they can give an account of why it's shallow that survives contact with the work;
- both verdicts are stable under reflection: the crowd's pleasure isn't dissolved by the critics' explanation, and the critics' verdict isn't dissolved by the crowd's enthusiasm;
- and here's the wrinkle I care about most: the critics' own unguarded, first-pass response to the work is pleasure. Their considered verdict says shallow. So their sentiment and their judgment point in opposite directions, which is awkward for a view that ties the standard to sentiment.

One paragraph for the scenario. Then:

1. Which of the two candidates does Hume actually need to be the standard, given that he says the sentiment is in the mind and that the rules are empirical? Can he have both?
2. If the critics win, has he smuggled in aesthetic realism — a property the crowd fails to detect? If the crowd wins, can he still say some judgments are better than others, or does the standard collapse into majority taste?
3. Which horn is worse for him, and why. Say what a defender of Hume would say to wriggle out — e.g. that the case misdescribes one of the five virtues, or that the rules were never meant to be validated by actual popularity, or that the critics' unguarded pleasure just shows their sentiment is corrupted by the very refinement that makes them good judges.
4. Finally, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what the smallest change would be that makes the two standards converge again.

Dont worry about citing anything, I just want the case and the analysis.
