Two arguments in the diachronic rationality literature look like the same argument to me, but I suspect they arn't, and I'd like you to compare them. One is the money pump: someone with a cyclical preference — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — pays to be walked around the loop back to where she started, and every trade along the way is one she'd endorse on its own. The other is the self-torturer: a guy strapped to a dial with a thousand settings, where each notch up is an unnoticeable increment of pain plus a large cash payout, so he takes every step and ends up at a setting he'd have refused at the outset; no cycle anywhere, each step a strict improvement on the last. Is the pump just the second case with a cycle bolted on, or are these different diagnoses of the fault — intransitivity in the preferences vs. something about the structure of a sequence of choices in which no single choice is the mistake? And what does either case actually tell us about what diachronic rationality demands, e.g. whether an agent is permitted to turn down a locally improving trade in order to stick with a plan she's already adopted? Be concrete about where the two cases come apart.
