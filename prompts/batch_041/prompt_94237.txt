OK so I'm in this sort of reading group on memory and responsibility, and the woman who runs it — retired philosopher, sharp as anything — keeps handing down verdicts on cases, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually running so I can predict what she'll say about the one coming up. Her verdicts so far:

- The men who ran the killing units: guilty, obviously, that's the easy one.
- The ordinary people who voted the regime in and lined the parade routes and personally hurt no one: still guilty, she says — political guilt, the whole citizenry's, not a thing a courtroom is for.
- A woman who informed on nobody and hid nobody but knew exactly what was happening to the family two doors down and kept her curtains shut: guilty, she says, in a register no court or parliament could ever reach.
- Their children, born after: not guilty, she's adamant, you don't inherit it, and she gets genuinely angry at the phrase "born guilty."
- But — and this is the one that threw me — a man who was six years old when it ended, now eighty, who has spent his whole life insisting it never happened and toasting the old regime every year: guilty, she says, no question.
- And a grandson who emigrated, changed his name, and now spends his weekends campaigning for a memorial: not guilty, she says, and arguably owes less than the ones who stayed comfortable.

Now the case she's about to rule on. Different country, same shape of history, except: nobody alive did anything, the old regime's crimes are real and documented, the current state is the direct legal successor of the one that did them, it has never apologized, and the citizens today mostly just don't think about it — no pride in it, no denial either, they benefit from the property and the stability and never ask where it came from.

What's she going to say? I care less about the verdict then about the rule — lay out the rules that fit everything she's said so far (I assume it's some mix of the four kinds of guilt people always drag out of Jaspers, but I don't know which of them is doing the real work, or whether it's something else entirely), say what each rule predicts for the new country, give me your best guess and why, and tell me which case I should put to her next to pull the rules apart. And if two of the rules she might be running disagree about the new case, say which single earlier verdict pins her down hardest, and whether any of her past verdicts actually conflict with each other.
