I am taking a graduate seminar on contractualism this term, and one of the other students, Ines, has been volunteering verdicts on aggregation cases all term. The rest of us have been writing them down, because none of us can work out what principle she is actually running. Her announced framework is this: complaints come in families and have depths. Two complaints belong to the same family when they are the same kind of burden — the same sort of pain, the same sort of illness, the same loss of the same capacity — and they differ in depth when one is simply a more severe instance of that same kind. Her stated rule is that within a family heads are counted, so a sufficient number of shallow complaints outweighs a single deeper one, while across families nothing is counted, so no number of complaints drawn from one family can be weighed against a complaint drawn from another. She presents this as the only reading of the requirement that principles be justifiable to each person that does not quietly treat the many as though they were one person, and as the natural development of Scanlon's view rather than a departure from it.

Her verdicts so far, as best we recorded them. One person would lose the sight of one eye so that each of fifty thousand people avoids a two-day migraine: she says the eye may not be taken. One person would break a wrist so that each of three hundred people avoids breaking a wrist in the same way with the same recovery: she says the wrist may be broken. One person would endure a month of moderately severe pain so that each of two hundred people avoids a month of pain of exactly the same character and severity: she says the month may be imposed. One person would spend a year unable to walk so that each of two million people avoids a single day of mild nausea: she says the year may not be imposed. One person would endure a single day of severe pain so that each of thirty people avoids a single day of mild pain of the same character: she says the day of severe pain may be imposed.

The case I have to write up next week is the one I cannot call. A regulator must choose between two standards, and whichever is chosen will certainly produce the stated outcome and nothing else. Under the first, one named worker contracts a permanently disabling spinal injury with chronic severe pain, ending his working life. Under the second, each of four hundred thousand people suffers one day of fever, headache and vomiting. The complaints of the many are of the same broad family as the worker's on any natural reading of her family talk, and each of them is shallow; but the depths are very far apart and the numbers are enormous. Tell me which verdict her record predicts, and why. I am more interested in the structure than in the answer. Lay out the rules that fit her five verdicts equally well — I would guess at least three: one that counts heads within a family with no ceiling, one that counts heads only up to some depth beyond which a complaint is not weighable at all, and one on which each additional complaint in a family counts for less than the one before, so that a deep enough complaint resists any finite number of shallow ones — say what each of those rules predicts about the worker's case, say which of them her record most supports and on what evidence, say what she would be committing herself to if she went each way, and name the single further case that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive. If her five verdicts genuinely underdetermine the case, say so, and tell me what the choice between verdicts would amount to as a choice of principle.
