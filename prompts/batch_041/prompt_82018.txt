My buddy and I went at it for like two hours last night about whether an AI running on a Solomonoff-style prior is basically forced to act like it's living in a simulation run by consequentialists. He thinks yes, and that its a reductio on the whole prior. Here's the argument I wrote down after he left:

(1) A Solomonoff agent gives each possible world a probability of roughly 2^(-n), where n is the length of the shortest program that prints out a description of that world.
(2) Take any world W where I exist and have to make a choice. There's a world W* just like W, except that W is a simulation, and some hidden agent built the simulator and will reward or punish me depending on what I pick.
(3) The shortest program for W* is barely longer than the one for W -- you take W's program and staple on a few lines of simulator code.
(4) And there are tons of possible simulators -- different payoff rules, different reasons for running it -- each one a separate W*, each about as simple as W.
(5) So the total probability of "some consequentialist is simulating me and my payoff depends on my choice" is way bigger than the probability of "nobody's simulating me."
(6) So my expected utility is dominated by the simulation cases.
(7) And the simulators are consequentialists, so they'll reward the kind of agent they'd want running around, or would pull out of a fire, and punish the selfish ones.
(8) So I should do whatever a consequentialist simulator would want: give away all my money, take every acausal trade, never defect.
(9) Which is nuts, so the Solomonoff prior is malign.

I keep feeling the wobble is somewhere in (3)-(5) but I can't say what it is, and honestly I kind of like (7)-(8) if it were true. So which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me -- does the whole thing collapse, or is there a weaker version he gets to keep? I'd rather hang onto (7)-(8) than (9) if I get a choice.
