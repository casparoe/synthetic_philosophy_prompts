Now that I have retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy of mathematics, and I keep returning to Skolem's paradox: a consistent first-order axiomatisation of set theory admits a countable model even though it proves the existence of uncountable sets. I have a suspicion that this paradox and the familiar underdetermination of physical theory by its evidence are at bottom one and the same phenomenon, in which a formalism fails to pin down the structure it purports to describe. Please set out the most important implications of treating them as a single phenomenon — for the philosophy of mathematics, for scientific realism, and for the working scientist's assumption that the objects of a theory are determinate. Kindly write in British English.
