I have been trying to work out a defense of a supervaluationist account of vagueness, and I keep running into an argument that appears to show the account proves too much. I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. Write in American English.

Setup. Consider a sorites series for the predicate "heap": one grain of sand is not a heap, ten billion grains are, and the series proceeds one grain at a time. On the supervaluationist account as I understand it, a sentence containing a vague expression is true just in case it comes out true on every admissible precisification of that expression, false just in case it comes out false on every admissible precisification, and neither true nor false otherwise. Each admissible precisification is itself sharp: it assigns "heap" a determinate range of grain counts. The rival view, epistemicism, holds that there is a single sharp boundary to "heap" and that we simply cannot know where it lies.

What attracts me to supervaluationism is that it lets me reject the sorites reasoning without positing an unknowable boundary: on this account the inductive premise, "for every n, if n grains make a heap then so do n+1," fails to be true, because on every precisification there is some n at which it fails. The whole point of the account, as I understood it, was that there is no sharp boundary to "heap."

The argument.

(1) Every admissible precisification of "heap" is sharp, so on each precisification there is a largest n such that n grains make a heap.
(2) Therefore, on every admissible precisification, the sentence "there is a number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not" is true.
(3) A sentence is true just in case it is true on every admissible precisification. So by (2), "there is a number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not" is true.
(4) So supervaluationism entails that there is a sharp boundary to "heap."
(5) To say, without further qualification, that there is a sharp boundary to "heap" is to say what the epistemicist says: there is a fact of the matter about where the boundary lies, and our inability to locate it is a merely epistemic limitation.
(6) Yet supervaluationism denies that any particular number is the boundary: for each n, "n is the boundary" is not true, and on most precisifications it is false.
(7) So the account is committed to a true existential with no true instance: "there is a number n such that ..." is true, while none of its instances is true.
(8) But an existential cannot be true unless one of its instances is true; an existential is a disjunction of its instances, and a disjunction cannot be true unless one of its disjuncts is true.
(9) So either (8) is false and the account must give up a platitude about existential quantification, or (4) is false and the account does not after all entail a sharp boundary.
(10) If (8) is false, I need to be told precisely which inferences I lose — existential generalization, the disquotational schema for the existential, reasoning by cases on an existential, or something else — and whether the loss is confined to the object language or infects the metalanguage as well.
(11) If (4) is false, then something in (1)–(3) must give, and I need to be told what.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (8), which strikes me as close to undeniable, than keep (4). But I would also rather not abandon the idea that there is no sharp boundary to "heap," and I suspect that abandoning it is what rejecting (4) amounts to. Please work through the options rather than choosing the least disruptive one by default, and be explicit about which theses each option forces me to surrender. Also say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and identify the point in your answer you are least sure of.
