Question 3. Nadia is a climate scientist. She has strong but probabilistic evidence that a glacier will collapse within 50 years; she does not know it will. She believes it and asserts, 'That glacier will collapse within 50 years.' Omar has the same evidence but does not believe it; he asserts the same sentence because he thinks it is probably true and wants to secure funding. Which norms of assertion—knowledge, truth, belief, or some combination—best explain whether Nadia's and Omar's assertions are permissible? In your answer, distinguish the norms and say whether the truth norm can permit insincere assertion.

Student answer: The knowledge norm says you should assert p only if you know p. This is the norm Williamson defends. It explains why Nadia's assertion is problematic: she only has probabilistic evidence, so she doesn't know, so she shouldn't assert. Omar's assertion is also problematic under the knowledge norm because he doesn't believe it, and knowledge entails belief. The truth norm says assert p only if p is true. Under this norm, Nadia and Omar's assertions are permissible if the glacier really will collapse; truth is all that matters. But the truth norm can't permit insincere assertion, because if you assert something you don't believe, you're not really asserting it—you're lying or play-acting. The belief norm says assert p only if you believe p. This is the weakest norm; it doesn't require truth or evidence. So Omar violates the belief norm but Nadia satisfies it. Moore's paradox—saying 'p but I don't believe p'—shows the belief norm is the fundamental one. The knowledge norm is just the belief norm plus truth, so it's really a combination. In Nadia's case, the best norm is the belief norm, because she believes it and that's what matters for assertion; the knowledge norm is too demanding for science communication.
