I'm in a rational choice seminar and we just did the dollar auction, and the whole time I kept thinking about the 2007 ABN AMRO takeover, which as far as I can tell is the largest dollar auction anyone ever actually ran, except the twist is that in this one almost everyone at the table ended up ruined, including people who never held the second-highest bid. Quick refresher on the case so we're on the same page: Barclays agreed a friendly merger with ABN AMRO, then a consortium of RBS, Fortis and Santander came in over the top with a mostly-cash offer somewhere around 71 billion euros, the consortium won, and the plan was to carve the bank into pieces -- RBS taking the wholesale and Asian business, Fortis the Benelux retail side, Santander the Brazilian and Italian bits. The LaSalle carve-out that was supposed to grease the Barclays route got litigated and then folded into the consortium's deal anyway. Within about a year Fortis had been dismantled and nationalised across the Benelux and RBS was majority-owned by the UK government; Santander basically walked away fine; ABN's shareholders got paid.

What I want is a disciplined mapping of that episode onto the dollar auction and the escalation-of-commitment literature, and then a hard look at where the mapping fails, because I suspect it fails in an interesting place. In Shubik's setup both the top two bidders pay, the prize has a fixed and known value, and the auctioneer profits while the bidders spiral past the point where winning is worth anything. Here the "prize" had a different value to each bidder depending on synergies, bids were partly in stock and contingent on financing, one side was a coalition of three firms with different and partly conflicting valuations, and the auction actually terminated at a finite price instead of spiralling. So: who is the player, exactly? Is Fortis the second-highest bidder or a co-winner? Does the fact that the winners were the ones destroyed, rather than the losers, tell against the dollar-auction framing and in favour of a plain winner's curse or overbidding-under-common-value story? And what does the case do back to the theory -- does it confirm the escalation mechanism, or is it an anomaly the dollar auction never anticipated because its two-payer structure assumes the pain falls on the party that lost?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, and nothing else -- no intro, no wrap-up paragraph. Point one should fix the mapping (who's the player, what's the bid, what's the prize, what corresponds to the second-highest-payer clause). Point two should say what the dollar-auction structure genuinely explains here. Point three should say where it misdescribes the case, and be specific about which structural assumption breaks. Point four should say whether escalation-of-commitment or winner's curse does better on the residue, and why. Point five should say what the episode shows about the theory itself -- confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated -- and what you'd have to change in the model to accommodate it.
