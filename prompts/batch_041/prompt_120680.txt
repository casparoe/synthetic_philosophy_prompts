On this reading the age-graded drinking law — nothing before eighteen, wine in moderation but no intoxication before thirty, symposia permitted from forty — and the requirement that the drinking party be overseen by a commander who is sober and wise are not arbitrary customs but follow from the dialogue's own psychology: the image of the human being as a puppet pulled by many cords, with law as the golden cord of calculation that we are meant to grasp and hold to; the account of the two fears, one of which is shame, and the suggestion that temperance, like courage, must be practiced against genuine resistance rather than acquired without contest; and the claim that wine is a medicine given to soften the soul's hardness in old age, and that the greatest benefit of the gift of Dionysus is something the lawgiver hesitates to state to the multitude. On the reading I'm defending, the symposium is a laboratory: a controlled, low-stakes setting in which the drinker's character is exposed to himself and to others, where shame does its work, and where the habit of holding to the golden cord can be built up before it is needed in earnest.
