I present a long-form podcast on philosophy and the human sciences — a general audience, but one that will sit still for twenty minutes of epistemology — and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent a decade defending the view that credences are, in general, sets or intervals of probabilities rather than single numbers, and that the orthodox demand for one precise number is a special case masquerading as the norm. She is excellent on the big picture and, in my experience, rather too good at retreating to it when pressed, so I want this interview to be a sequence of specific pressures rather than a tour of the position. Our listeners will not know the technical literature, and I would much rather she explain the machinery in her own words than gesture at papers we cannot cite on air, so please write each question to stand on its own: spoken, direct, and free of jargon she would have to translate for a general audience.

Here is the ground I want covered, roughly one cluster of questions per item, each with a follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and turns the screw a little further. First, the phenomenon — I have seen it called dilation — in which conditionalizing a set of probabilities on a new item of evidence can leave the interval for some target proposition wider than it was before, so that a piece of news appears to make one less committal about something rather than more. I want her to say plainly whether that is a defect of the view; if she answers that the interval is answering to the evidence, and that the evidence genuinely fails to discriminate, the follow-up should ask whether dilation can occur on evidence that every precise probability in the set treats as uninformative about the target, and if it cannot, why the phenomenon is supposed to be so striking in the first place. Second, decision: under a set of probabilities, which rule ought to govern choice, why that rule rather than maximin or its more permissive cousins, and how she answers the charge that whatever rule she picks could equally be represented as maximizing expected utility with respect to some single member of the set — which would leave the interval with no work to do in action. Third, the nature of the state itself: is an interval-valued credence a doxastic attitude in its own right, or a representation of the agent's uncertainty about her own precise credence, or a stand-in for a committee of homunculi; and if it is a genuine attitude, what could count as holding it wrongly. Fourth, the diachronic question: how the view can honour the thought that one ought now to defer to one's better-informed future self when the future interval is not comparable in width or location with the present one. And finally the closing question, which I take to be the hardest one for any permissive epistemology: what would have to be true of the world, or of us, for her to conclude that sharp credences were the right ideal after all — with a follow-up that obliges her either to name a kind of case or to concede that none could do it. Eight to ten questions in all, follow-ups marked as such, and no question whose only effect would be to invite her to restate the view.
