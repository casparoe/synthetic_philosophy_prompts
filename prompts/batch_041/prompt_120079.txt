I'm in my high school's philosophy club, and for our film night I've been asked to give a short talk on Arrival (Villeneuve, 2016) and Quine's indeterminacy of translation, so I'd like your help working out the reading. On the surface the film looks like a textbook illustration of the gavagai scenario: Louise has no bilingual informant and no shared vocabulary, only the heptapods' behavior in context, and the circular logograms — one glyph carrying a whole proposition — make Quine's holism literal, since no part of the language can be pinned down until a manual for the whole has been committed to. What I want to get right, though, is a distinction the film seems to blur: between a translation that is merely underdetermined by the evidence we happen to have, which more fieldwork could settle, and Quine's stronger claim that rival manuals can fit all possible behavioral evidence equally well, so that there is no fact of the matter for a translator to get right. Which one is the film's crisis, where one government's translators read a key term one way and Louise reads it another, with the fate of the world depending on it? And when the film resolves the crisis through Louise's altered experience of time rather than through further evidence, is that a cheat, or is it the film's way of saying that the only route past the indeterminacy is to become a different kind of speaker than the manual was built for? Please also say where the film resists the reading: its Sapir-Whorf premise concerns what a language does to thought, not what fixes meaning, and its ending grants Louise the very access to the aliens' meanings that Quine says no translator can have. I'd like a reading that is fair to the film and to Quine, and that ends with a judgment about whether the film dramatizes something the argument misses.
