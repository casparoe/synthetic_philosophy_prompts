Last term our cognitive science reading group held a joint session with the philosophy department on the timing of conscious experience, and two people I know reasonably well — Mira, a vision scientist who works on time perception, and Daniel, a philosopher of mind — spent most of the two hours arguing past each other in a way I still cannot adjudicate. The session started from the color phi illusion: a blue dot is flashed at one position, then a red dot at another, and at certain combinations of spacing and timing viewers report a single dot moving smoothly between the two locations, with the color changing abruptly midway along the path. The puzzling part is that viewers report the change as occurring before the second dot has even been presented, even though the color of the second dot is settled only when that dot arrives. So the content of the experience seems to be about an event that has not yet happened at the moment the experience places it.

Mira took the phenomenon to show that the question of when the color change entered experience has no determinate answer, and that the two natural stories one could tell about it are empirically indistinguishable. The first — call it the Orwellian story, after the rewriting of history — says the subject really does undergo an experience with the unrevised content (the dot still blue as it arrives, say), and a later process overwrites the memory, so the report misdescribes what was experienced. The second — the Stalinesque story, after the staged show trial — says nothing is overwritten: the brain withholds the content from consciousness until the second flash has been processed, then presents a single, already-consistent experience in which the color changes in mid-flight. Mira's argument was that no possible experiment could tell these apart, since they predict the same reports and, she claims, the same priming, the same reaction times, and the same measurable neural activity; and that the whole dispute presupposes a single moment at which each content becomes conscious, a finish line the brain does not have. Daniel replied that the stories are not two descriptions of one thing but two different causal claims about the order of events in the head: on the Orwellian story there exists an internal state carrying the unrevised content which is then overwritten, and anything that exists and is then overwritten has causal powers — it could in principle be primed, probed, or leave a trace; on the Stalinesque story no such state ever exists. Different causal structures, he said, can be separated by intervention even when the reports are identical, and if you specify the revision process in detail the Orwellian model predicts specific interference effects that the buffer model does not. He added that Mira's no-finish-line argument proves too much, since it would equally show that there is no fact about whether a masked word was seen or whether a blindsight patient saw anything.

Mira came back with three points: that the unrevised content is defined only as what would have been experienced had the revision not happened, and we cannot produce that counterfactual situation because the revision is part of normal operation; that masking and blindsight are disanalogous because in those cases independent behavioral evidence discriminates, whereas here every measurable profile is the same; and that testability in principle is doing all the work, since she cannot see the difference between a prediction that no possible evidence could falsify and no prediction at all. Daniel's last word was that the indistinguishability is an empirical bet, not a conceptual truth, so the honest verdict is that the matter is currently underdetermined rather than that there is nothing to distinguish; Mira's reply was that if that is all that is left, the disagreement has quietly become about what the words experience and conscious mean rather than about the brain — to which Daniel said there is a third possibility she keeps ignoring, namely that the two stories sit at different levels, one describing what the subject underwent and the other describing processing, in which case asking which is true is a category mistake rather than a question for experiment. I have no stake in either side. Please go through the exchange and tell me who is right about which specific claim — rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right — what both of them are missing, and, if there is a fact of the matter here, what kind of evidence would settle it.
