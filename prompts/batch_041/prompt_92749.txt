I was recently removed from the board of a small local arts collective after a years-old tweet of mine resurfaced, and two people close to me have been arguing ever since about whether what happened to me counts as punishment. I honestly can't tell who is right. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of law and social norms and adjudicate their exchange point by point. Before giving a verdict, brainstorm the main fault lines in the concept of punishment—authority, jurisdiction, intention, aggregation, standing, expressiveness, and the difference between hard treatment and withdrawal of cooperation—then work through each numbered move below, rating it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating. End by saying who is more right overall and what both are missing. Don't cite literature; reason from the concepts.

They said:

(1) Alex: "It wasn't punishment. Punishment is a formal response by an authority with jurisdiction, after a finding of guilt, with a defined penalty. A group of people deciding not to share a stage with you is a boycott, not punishment."

(2) Sam: "That's a statist definition. Parents punish children; schools punish students; a spontaneous coalition can punish too. All you need is that a group imposes a cost on someone because they believe he did wrong, and the cost is meant to express condemnation and deter others. That's exactly what happened."

(3) Alex: "But the cost wasn't imposed by a single agent; it was the aggregate of many independent decisions. You can't punish by aggregation. And the organizers never said 'we punish you'; they said they couldn't platform you. Intent matters."

(4) Sam: "Intent is often mixed. If a bouncer throws you out because you're drunk, that's not punishment; if he throws you out because you insulted him, it is. Here the stated reason was your tweet, so it's punitive. And if aggregation were a bar, no institution could punish either, since institutions act through many people."

(5) Alex: "Then any withdrawal of cooperation for a reason is punishment. If I stop inviting a friend who is rude, I'm punishing him. That can't be right. The difference is that punishment claims authority to enforce a norm on behalf of the community, while a private refusal to associate claims no such authority."

(6) Sam: "The collective did claim authority—it runs the space, sets its rules, and announced the removal as a response to what you did. If that's not a claim of standing, nothing is. And even if it's not punishment in the narrow sense, it's a response to a past wrong that imposes a cost, so it does the same moral work as punishment; calling it something else is just relabeling."

Please adjudicate each numbered move: is it right, partly right, or mistaken, and why? Then answer: (a) Does what happened to me count as punishment on the best account? (b) Which of their proposed criteria are genuine necessary conditions and which are parochial to state punishment? (c) What would each of them need to concede to reach a defensible middle position?
