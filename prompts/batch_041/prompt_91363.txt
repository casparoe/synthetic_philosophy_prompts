Okay so I run a monthly reading group out of my apartment, 10-14 people, mostly rationalist-adjacent types plus a couple of grad students who come for the arguments, and I get about 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them *feel* Paul Christiano's argument that the Solomonoff prior is malign, instead of me standing up and explaining it at them.

Quick version of the argument as I understand it, and please correct me in your answer if I've garbled it: if you're a Cartesian agent -- you only ever get your own observation stream, you don't get to peek at the world that produced it -- and you form beliefs by Solomonoff induction (every computable hypothesis, weighted two to the minus its length, updated on your data), then among the cheap hypotheses that fit your observations are ones in which you're being simulated by someone. Simulating an agent is short to write. So a big chunk of your posterior says you're inside somebody else's computation. For a consequentialist that's the bad part: your expected value gets hijacked by the simulators, and by copies of you elsewhere in the prior, and by the fact that plenty of the other agents in the prior are themselves trying to influence the prior. So the prior isn't neutral -- it rewards you for being the kind of agent a simulator would want to run.

I don't want to lecture that at them, and I don't want them to know where this is going until the debrief. I want them to build the thing and then walk into it. Rough instinct for a mechanic, take it or throw it out: teams secretly write tiny world-programs on index cards, one instruction per card, and the cost of a program is the number of cards it uses. The agent team sits behind a screen and only ever sees the observation cards, and each round it bets on the next observation with a weight of one-half to the power of the card count of whatever hypothesis it's currently holding. The cheapest hypothesis that fits the data so far should turn out to be the simulation one, and I want the moment where betting on the cheap simulation hypothesis is actually the profit-maximizing move -- and where the world-writing teams realise that the way to win is not to write a faithful world but to write a short program in which the agent thinks its being watched. That's the malignity, felt in the payoffs.
