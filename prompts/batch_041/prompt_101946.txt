I am a high school senior, and as part of my city's charter-review process I have been invited to present a proposed amendment to the municipal code before the Charter Review Commission next month. I would be grateful for help preparing for the question period, since I expect it to be harder than the presentation itself.

My amendment bundles three changes to municipal elections. The voting age for municipal elections would fall from eighteen to sixteen. Voting in those elections would become compulsory for eligible voters, enforced by a small fine that could be discharged through a few hours of civic service. And residents who are not citizens but who have lived in the city for at least two years would be permitted to vote in municipal elections.

I have tried to derive all three changes from a single principle rather than defending them separately: the franchise should track subjection to the laws one is obliged to obey, not membership in a particular legal status. I deliberately do not rest the case on the idea that everyone affected by a decision should have a say, because that would appear to enfranchise commuters, tourists, and anyone else who uses our roads and breathes our air without being governed by our ordinances. I also want to hold that compulsory participation is not compulsory choice: my ballot would include a none-of-the-above option, so that a voter who has weighed the candidates and rejected them all can record that judgment without penalty.

The Commission has five members. The chair is a retired municipal judge who reasons in terms of legal authority and precedent and will ask whether a city may do any of this at all. The county election administrator cares about registration, ballot design, enforcement, and cost. A community organizer who works with immigrant families supports votes for non-citizen residents but regards the compulsory element as punitive toward the poor and the disengaged. A political scientist on the commission knows the empirical literature on turnout and will attack any causal claim I make about habits or legitimacy. A school board member worries that sixteen-year-olds are not competent and that the schools would be dragged into partisan politics.

Please draft my question-and-answer preparation. I want the objections I will actually receive, in roughly the order I will receive them, phrased the way each commissioner would phrase them, and ordered by how much damage each would do to my case. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to concede. Please finish with the one question I am dreading. If my three proposals do not in fact follow from a single principle, or if the compulsory element sits badly with the subjection principle or with the votes-for-non-citizens element, say so plainly, because I would rather learn it now than in the room. Please avoid the generic talking points that appear in every op-ed on this subject; I need the objections that arise from my particular framing. I do not want a reading list or citations, since the commissioners will not be persuaded by names and I cannot look anything up during the session.
