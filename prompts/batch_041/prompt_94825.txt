A colleague in the education faculty has asked me to vet a paragraph from a trade book on the epistemology of testimony that she intends to teach. The book says: "The reductionist holds that testimony is never a basic source of knowledge: the hearer must first establish that the speaker is reliable, so testimonial belief is really inference in disguise. The anti-reductionist replies that we are entitled to accept what we are told — testimony is a basic source, and the hearer needs no positive reason at all before believing. But since the reductionist's demand for evidence of reliability can only be met by relying on what others have told us, reductionism is viciously circular. In the end the dispute is really about whether children are credulous or discerning, since the epistemology merely tracks how we in fact learn from others." I suspect this is largely fair but that something has been quietly lost; I am inclined toward anti-reductionism myself, though I would rather that not colour the assessment. Which claims should she keep, which need qualification, and which are misrepresentations? Where it matters, say what a well-informed reductionist and a well-informed anti-reductionist would each say in reply. And be explicit about which of these claims are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on testimony — I do not want a contested position presented to her as the consensus.
