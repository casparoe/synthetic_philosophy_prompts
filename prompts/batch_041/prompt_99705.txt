Next month I'm presenting at our cognitive science seminar, and I'm defending a claim I think is going to get me in trouble: that "sphexishness" has been misapplied. Everyone uses it as a property of a creature -- the digger wasp that drags the cricket to the burrow and, if you move the cricket, just re-runs the whole inspection routine from the top, forever. My line is that it's not a property of the organism at all, it's a property of a control loop: a sequence that, once triggered, can only be exited by running to completion, and that can't be interrupted by anything the agent represents as a reason to stop. Once you frame it that way, humans are sphexish in a bunch of tiny domains too (I keep re-opening the same cabinet to check for the thing I already know isn't there), so the tidy picture of "wasp = machine, human = free" collapses into a much less tidy question about how much of the loop an agent can represent and revise from the inside. Rigidity vs. flexibility stops being a difference in kind and becomes a difference in coverage.

The audience is the problem. It's mostly experimentalists -- insect navigation people and a couple of bee-cognition folks -- who will hear this as philosophy-of-mind being done on the backs of animals they actually run experiments on, plus two philosophers of mind who've heard the wasp story a hundred times and think its a cliché. Write me the Q&A prep: what each side will actually object to, in their own vocabulary (the experimentalists will put it differently than the philosophers), ordered by how much damage each one does, what I should concede up front -- including whatever I have to give away about how solid the original wasp anecdote really is -- what I should hold firm on, and where the weakest joint in my argument is, the one I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. And tell me the one question I'm dreading.
