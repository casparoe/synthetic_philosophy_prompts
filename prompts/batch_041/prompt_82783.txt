Until last year I would have said there were two ways for a state to handle religion: the American way, where the state keeps its hands off the churches, and the French way, where religion is pushed out of public life altogether. Then I discovered that a church I last set foot in as a child has been receiving, on my behalf, a surcharge on my income tax that the state collects for it, and that the only way to stop the arrangement is to appear at a government office and declare that I am not a member; my daughter, meanwhile, takes a religion class at her state school that the state pays for and that the church has a say in staffing. I am told England is stranger still: an established church almost nobody attends, bishops sitting in the legislature, and no tax at all. I have stopped thinking of this as one spectrum running from establishment to separation, and I would like a decision tree that sorts the arrangements instead of the labels.

I host a small reading group — six or seven people, none of them specialists, one of whom teaches canon law — and what I want to hand them is a tree with branch nodes that are questions about a state's actual practice, phrased so that someone can answer them about their own country without knowing any of the vocabulary. The distinctions I care about, roughly in this order: who collects the money, and whether the state collects it for religious bodies, merely forgoes taxing them, or does neither; who appoints the personnel, and whether the state installs, approves, or crowns religious officeholders or leaves the bodies to appoint their own; whether the state hands over any of its own jurisdiction, as with marriage, family status, or religious arbitration that civil courts will enforce; whether religion is taught in state schools and on what terms, confessional with an opt-out, opt-in, replaced by a subject about religions, or absent; whether the state restricts religious display to protect a shared public space or protects religious display against restriction; and whether religious bodies as such get standing in the state — seats in a legislature, places on the boards of public broadcasters, treaties signed with the government — or whether the state deals only with individual believers.

Leaves should be positions rather than countries, six to eight of them, though each leaf should name the arrangement that comes closest to instantiating it: the English establishment, the German state-collected tax, the French separation of 1905 together with the exception it still leaves in Alsace-Moselle, where the clergy remain on the state payroll and religion is still taught in schools, the American non-establishment alongside tax exemption and legislative chaplains, and any hybrid you think those four miss. Each leaf gets a name, one sentence on the commitment that ought to embarrass whoever holds it, and a note on which of my six distinctions it is simply indifferent to. Where the tree cannot separate two positions — I suspect the American and the German arrangements are closer than they look on the question of who pays for religion — say so rather than forcing a branch. Where a combination of answers corresponds to nothing anyone has defended, mark the box empty and tell me whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; I would rather have three honest empty boxes than a tidy tree that pretends to cover everything.

Before the tree, in a single paragraph, settle something I keep failing to keep straight: whether neutrality here can mean two different things, the state declining to recognize any religion as against the state recognizing all of them equally, and which of the four arrangements each sense of the word would endorse. I don't need sources or further reading; I need the sorting to be right.
