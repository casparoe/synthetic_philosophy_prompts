Two years ago I would have called the question of who has a right to leisure a seminar abstraction. Then a colleague of mine — a team leader in his early fifties who had been working sixty-hour weeks for most of a decade — collapsed and died at his desk, and the company's response was a compulsory e-learning module on work–life balance. The Japanese have a word for what happened to him, karoshi; I had always assumed it named a problem somewhere else. I have since been elected as the staff representative on the committee renegotiating our working-time agreement, where management proposes to trade our thirty-five-hour week for "flexibility," and I keep finding that the arguments in the room do not fit the names I have for them. Both sides quote the same sentence from the Universal Declaration — "Everyone has the right to rest and leisure, including reasonable limitation of working hours and periodic holidays with pay" — and it settles nothing.

I want a map before the next bargaining round, not another slogan. Please write me a taxonomy of the positions on the question of who has a right to work and to leisure, and what those rights are held against — a decision tree rather than a list of labels. Each internal node should be labelled with the claim that splits it, phrased as a question a colleague could answer without knowing which position it leads to (not "are you a Weberian?" but something like "the team is offered a bonus for working through the public holiday: do you take it because the work is a vocation, because the money is worth it, or because you cannot refuse it without penalty?"). Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

These are the distinctions that matter for my purposes. First, whether a person's time is presumed to be their own or presumed owed to a calling, a household or an employer, so that leisure must be justified and work need not be. Second, whether leisure is valued intrinsically — as contemplation, festivity, play — or only instrumentally, as the recovery of the capacity to work. Third, what the ground of a limit on hours is supposed to be: bodily health, personal autonomy, the republican thought that whoever sets your hours holds power over you, fairness in sharing out the work that exists, or the claims of worship. Fourth, who holds the correlative duty — the state by statute, the employer or the collective agreement, the household, or the worker's own virtue. Fifth, whether chronic overwork is a vice of the individual or an equilibrium that nobody chose, since that determines whether the vocabulary of "balance" is even coherent. And sixth, whether the right to leisure and the right to work are allies or rivals.

The tree has to reach at least these leaves: work as a calling in which idleness is the characteristic vice; leisure as a contemplative or festive attitude rather than spare time; statutory maximum hours as a matter of right; hours as nothing but a bargained term; leisure as mere recovery time for labour power that should be abolished along with the wage relation; the civic argument that leisure is a condition of being a citizen; the structural-equilibrium account of overwork; the claim that the question is malformed while caring labour goes uncounted; and the view that there is no right to leisure and no right to work either. Where two of these land in the same leaf, tell me so rather than papering over it, and where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered.
