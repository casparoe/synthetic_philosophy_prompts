I'm in second year and I'm taking this course on Marx and political economy (it's cross-listed with history, which is why half the reading is 19th-century factory inspectors' reports), and I've somehow ended up agreeing to give a short speech next Saturday at the farewell do for the plant my dad worked at for twenty-nine years. They've finished installing the new automated line, the place is going down to about eleven staff, and the local asked me to say something because I'm the first one in the family to go to uni and they think I'll be able to "say something proper." It's going to be about sixty people in the old canteen: mostly blokes my dad's age who are taking a package and who will switch off the second anything sounds like a lecture, a few managers who've been told to put in an appearance, my mum and my aunties, and a reporter from the local paper. Four or five minutes, tops. What I really don't want is to give either of the two speeches that are just sitting there waiting to be given — the one where the robots took our jobs and it's all the machines' fault, or the one the site manager keeps doing about how this is just progress and everyone will be retrained into new roles. My prof reckons Marx thinks both of those miss the point: machinery isn't neutral, but it also isn't the villain, because what it does to people depends on the social relation it's sitting inside. Under capital, apparently, the same machines that could cut everyone's working week tend instead to make people work longer and harder — there's a whole section of Capital on the working day being prolonged and labour intensified by machinery, and another one right after it where he goes after the idea that the workers a machine displaces get "compensated" by new jobs, which is basically word for word the line management is using on us. He also made us read the notebooks from 1857–58, the Grundrisse, where Marx sounds almost excited about automation — the general intellect, social knowledge getting built into the machines, wealth stopping being a matter of labour time — before he says capital is stuck in a contradiction because it still measures everything in labour time anyway. And there's a line Marx quotes from John Stuart Mill at the very start of the machinery chapter that I can't get out of my head: "It is questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day's toil of any human being."

So can you write me the speech? It has to be warm — these are people I've known since I was a kid, and some of them will cry and a couple of them will be furious — and it obviously can't be a seminar, but I want Marx's actual position doing the structural work in it rather than just being name-dropped for decoration. Practically, that means: it shouldn't blame the machines or the engineers who installed them; it shouldn't blame my dad's mates for anything either; it shouldn't promise that retraining or the new roles fix it, because the compensation section is basically saying don't count on that; and it shouldn't pretend the automation is a pure tragedy with no upside, since the whole thing I'm trying to land is that something that could have meant shorter weeks for everybody is instead being used to mean fewer of us. I'd also rather not actually say the word Marx out loud if you can avoid it — the second anyone hears that name the older blokes will decide I've joined something — so the idea has to carry itself without the label. And it would be good if it didn't humiliate the managers standing at the back, because I think the machine isn't the villain and neither are they, and I want to say the true thing without it landing as an attack on particular people in the room. One more thing, probably obvious: I'd definately rather it didn't end on a slogan.
