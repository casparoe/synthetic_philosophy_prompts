I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on scepticism and the self, and I need a single essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The course is built around a two-axis taxonomy of dream-sceptical doubts, which I hand the students in the lecture and which they are expected to apply rather than recite.

Object of doubt. (a) World-doubt: I do not know whether the world I seem to perceive exists at all. (b) State-doubt: I do not know whether I am now dreaming rather than waking. (c) Author-doubt: I do not know whether I am the dreamer or something dreamt — whether my apparent agency, including the very act of asking the question, is original or derivative.

Recoverability. A doubt is recoverable if some experience available from within the doubtful condition would settle it (waking up settles state-doubt, or at least appears to); it is unrecoverable if every experience available from within the doubtful condition is equally compatible with both answers, so that no course of experience could settle it. Students should be told that recoverability is a property of the doubt, not of the doubter's confidence or sincerity.

The question must be built on Zhuangzi's butterfly passage: the anecdote in which Zhuangzi cannot tell whether he is Zhuangzi, who has just dreamt he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming that it is Zhuangzi. I do not want students to quote or cite anything, and I do not want the question to turn on scholarly interpretation; the taxonomy above is the whole apparatus they need. The question should nevertheless require them to do more than narrate the anecdote or run the familiar comparison with Descartes's dream argument.

Specifically, the question should require students to:

(i) place the passage's doubt in the taxonomy, and defend that placement against at least one rival placement — in particular against the textbook placement as state-doubt;

(ii) say whether the doubt, so placed, is recoverable, and what in the passage bears on the answer;

(iii) argue whether a doubt of that kind is scepticism at all, or whether it instead dissolves the question it appears to raise — for instance by treating the two conditions as transformations of one another rather than as competing candidates for reality, as the passage arguably does when it ends by insisting that the two must after all be distinguished and by naming the situation a transformation of things rather than an error to be corrected;

(iv) confront the objection that the reading reached in (iii) is not scepticism but a dogmatic metaphysics of transformation, and say what that reading would have to concede in order to survive the objection.

Please write:

1. The examination question itself, stating the time allowance (45 minutes) and the total mark (20).

2. A model answer of about 650 words, of the standard a strong candidate could plausibly produce in that time.

3. A marking scheme consisting of five must-hit points, the mark each is worth (totalling 20), and, for each point, one line on the characteristic way candidates lose marks on it — so that the scheme separates a solid B answer from an A answer rather than merely listing content that ought to appear.

Two constraints on the whole deliverable. First, the model answer must not depend on knowing who wrote what about Zhuangzi or on any particular translation; the taxonomy is the only apparatus, and the passage is to be referred to descriptively rather than quoted. Second, write everything in British English.
