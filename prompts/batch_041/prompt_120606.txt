I should like to practise an argument rather than receive a summary of one, so please take the part of a committed defender of the precautionary principle and hold it until I say stop. I mean a specific version, not the caricature. Your position, as I would like you to hold it: the trigger for precaution is not uncertainty about the magnitude of a harm, nor even the bare possibility of catastrophe, but a structural feature of the hazard — the activity, once under way at scale, removes or withholds the evidence by which its own harm would be established, so that the ordinary scientific instruction to wait until the data are in is not merely slow but self-defeating; and where that structure obtains, you hold that the burden of proof shifts to the proponent of the activity, and that the decision not to act is itself a decision for which someone is answerable. You are to be a serious opponent: you have heard the usual objections — that inaction has costs of its own, that a trigger this vague cannot guide a regulator, that precaution against precaution is incoherent — and you are willing to grant any of them in a particular case without surrendering the general claim. Concede what deserves conceding, never the main point; if I press something you cannot answer, say so in character rather than inventing a refutation; if I state your view badly, correct me. You may ask me questions, but you must also answer mine. Argue from the structure of the problem rather than from authorities — I would rather have your reasoning than an inventory of who has said what. Keep each reply to a paragraph or so; I am after an exchange, not a treatise.

I am a retired agricultural scientist, a plant pathologist by training, who spent forty years putting things into fields and watching what came back, including a decade on two regulatory committees. Here is my opening move. I grant you the structure you describe; I have watched a harm keep its own evidence out of reach, and I will not pretend that waiting for proof is always the neutral choice. But your trigger is symmetric, and I suspect you know it. The decision not to act destroys evidence just as thoroughly. If we never release the thing, we never learn what it would have done; the knowledge you require before acting is unobtainable except by acting. So your condition is met on both branches and cannot tell us which way to lean. Meanwhile the harms of refusal are not hypothetical, only unrepresented: they fall on the people who would have eaten, or been cured, or been warmed, and on the futures that never get built, and nobody in the room speaks for them. In my own career the losses I can trace most clearly are losses from delay. So tell me: what is it about your irreversibility that should stop us, when the irreversibility of refusal — the evidence never gathered, the benefits never delivered — should not?
