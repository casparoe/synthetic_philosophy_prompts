I am assembling case materials for a graduate seminar on responsibility-sensitive distributive justice, and I want a purpose-built case rather than another outing of the beach bum, the unrepentant driver, or the expensive taste. Construct a realistic scenario — a household, a workplace, a housing cooperative, nothing science-fictional — in which two versions of luck egalitarianism that differ only in the baseline they use to specify a person's shortfall (the position that person would have occupied but for the bad luck, versus an equal share of the relevant resource) deliver opposite verdicts about what is owed to one person. Hold everything else fixed: the shortfall must arise from brute luck that is unchosen, nobody's fault, and unrepaired by any prior transfer, so that the divergence cannot be explained away by disputing voluntariness, and the version that ends up owing nothing must still be a recognizable luck egalitarianism rather than a strawman. Nothing in the case may stipulate the verdicts; they have to fall out of the two baselines. The case is for making the choice of baseline visible as a substantive commitment rather than a technicality, so it should leave a defender of either version with something uncomfortable to say. Give the scenario in one paragraph, then do the check.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion — (1) the scenario; (2) each version's verdict and the reasoning that generates it; (3) the feature of the case doing the work; (4) what happens to the divergence when that feature is removed, for instance by making the bad luck partially reversible or by shifting who bears its cost; (5) the strongest attack a defender of the worse-off version could mount against the case.
