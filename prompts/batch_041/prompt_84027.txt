Two friends of mine, Ana and Ben, got into an argument at a decision theory reading group, and I was the only one not taking a side. The topic was the St. Petersburg paradox, but Ana had brought up a variant she thought dissolved it. In her variant, the casino does not have unlimited funds. Before you decide whether to pay the entry fee, you are told only that the casino's maximum possible payout is 2^K dollars, where K is an integer chosen uniformly at random from 10 to 100. You are not told K. The game is otherwise the usual one: a fair coin is flipped until the first tails, and if that happens on toss n, you receive 2^(n-1) dollars, except that the payout is capped at 2^K. Ana said that in this finite-bankroll version the expected payout is finite and, in fact, modest, so any rational expected-value maximizer should be willing to pay, say, twenty dollars to play. The paradox, she said, is an artifact of assuming an infinitely rich casino. Ben replied that this only relocates the problem. If the range of K were shifted upward, the fair price would grow without bound, so the paradox returns as a limit; and if the range were shifted downward, the game would just be boring. He said the real lesson is that expected monetary value is not a good guide to rational choice when the payoffs are extreme, because money has diminishing marginal utility. Ana answered that diminishing marginal utility is a separate fix; if Ben wants to say expected value is the wrong decision rule, he needs to say what the right one is, and she suspected he would end up endorsing a bounded utility function that makes the paradox disappear by fiat. Ben said a bounded utility function is not fiat; it reflects the fact that no finite being can have infinitely more well-being, and that any sane decision theory should be sensitive to the difference between a certain small gain and a vanishingly small chance of an enormous gain. Ana said that if you are going to cap utility, you can make almost any gamble look rational or irrational depending on where you put the cap, and that the original paradox was about money, not utility, so changing the subject to utility does not address it. Ben said the original paradox was always about rational choice, and money is only a stand-in for whatever it is we ultimately care about. At that point they asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. I want to know who is right about the finite-bankroll variant, whether it dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, whether diminishing marginal utility is an ad hoc fix or a genuine solution, and whether the dispute is really about money or about rational choice more generally. Also tell me what both of them are missing, and which of them, if either, has the stronger overall position.
