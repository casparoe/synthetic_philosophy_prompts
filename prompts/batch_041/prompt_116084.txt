I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of history, and the next session is given over to cliodynamics — Peter Turchin's project of making history quantitative — with attention to the "secular cycles" of his structural-demographic theory: the roughly centuries-long oscillation in which population growth outruns the productive base, the common population is immiserated, aspirants to elite status are produced faster than the positions available to absorb them, intra-elite competition turns violent, the state's fiscal capacity buckles, and the resulting instability resets the demographic and political variables. My students are strong on Hegel, Marx, and the hermeneutic tradition and have no quantitative social science at all; they will arrive both intrigued and suspicious. I do not want the theory explained to me. I want a teaching plan for ninety minutes.

Tell me the order in which the components must be introduced if each is to arrive as a consequence of what precedes it rather than as one more item in a list, and in particular what has to be in place before the word "cycle" can do any work for them, since I expect them to hear it as either a periodic law or a decorative metaphor. Specify the single historical case that should carry the whole session, and defend it against the obvious alternatives. It needs to be a case whose narrative they already possess, so that the theory's contribution is re-description rather than narration; but it must not be a case in which an exogenous catastrophe or one decisive confrontation does all the explanatory labor, because then the endogenous mechanism never becomes visible and the theory is never tested by the example. I had intended the French Revolution; tell me whether that is the right carrier or a trap, given that 1789 arrives pre-explained and any framework can accommodate it after the fact.

Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — that "elite overproduction" is a slogan about meritocracy or merely wealth inequality renamed; that this is vulgar Marxism fitted with differential equations; that a retrospective fit to pre-modern data is automatically unfalsifiable; that quantifying the mechanism amounts to denying that anyone ever acts — and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, and which of them I should instead leave standing so the students refute it themselves. Close with the question I should put to them in the final minutes to test whether they have grasped the difference between a mechanism with feedback and a covering law, and with a short list of what I should announce I am deliberately not covering at this level — the model's actual equations, the disputes about the quality of the underlying data, the Ibn Khaldun-derived notion of asabiya — together with the reason each omission is defensible rather than evasive.
