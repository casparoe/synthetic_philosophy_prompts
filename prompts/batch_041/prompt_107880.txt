I teach philosophy at a university, and my own research is in moral and political philosophy; I have lately been asked to take the probability and induction unit of a general-education course, which is how I came upon a pattern I cannot name and am no longer confident is a single thing.

Stated loosely, the pattern is this. A question about chance looks perfectly determinate until one notices that answering it requires a choice the question itself does not make, and that the candidate choices are all equally faithful to the question as posed. In each case the difficulty seems to be produced by the very feature that makes the question look answerable — the symmetry of the setup, or the thought that since nothing distinguishes the possibilities they must be treated alike.

Three specimens, in what I take to be increasing order of distance from one another.

First: a chord is drawn at random in a circle, and we ask for the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Choose the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk; or choose its direction and its distance from the centre independently; or choose its two endpoints independently on the circumference. Three procedures, three answers, and I cannot see that any of them misreads the instruction.

Second: a point is chosen uniformly on a sphere, and I learn that it lies on a particular great circle. How is it distributed along that circle? The circle is one set of points, but the same set is an equator in one coordinate system and a meridian in another, and the two descriptions give different distributions along it. Here it is not that one parametrization is better than its rival; it is that the conditional probability has not yet been fixed.

Third: the length of a coastline, measured with rulers of decreasing length, grows without bound. Nothing competes with anything here; the quantity simply has no value.

I am not after a label. I want to know whether these three are one phenomenon wearing three faces or three distinct failures, what the correct diagnosis is in each, and whether the family — or its most important member — has a name. If it does not, I would like a coinage with a definition, its boundary cases, and an explicit account of what it excludes: in particular, cases in which dependence on an arbitrary choice is harmless, and cases in which the right verdict is that the questioner has simply blundered. I have students who will ask, and I would rather not improvise.
