I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics and computability, and I've been set an essay on the Church–Turing thesis. In the lecture, my tutor offered a distinction that I found genuinely clarifying but that I have not seen stated quite this way anywhere else, and I should like you to take it as your starting point. She suggested that the thesis is best understood as the conjunction of two claims with quite different epistemic standing: a "ceiling" claim, to the effect that no function which is effectively calculable — calculable by an idealised human agent following a finite set of unambiguous rules, with unbounded time, paper and patience — falls outside the class of functions computable by a Turing machine; and a "floor" claim, to the effect that every Turing-computable function is indeed effectively calculable in that informal sense. Her view was that the floor claim is close to trivial, since a Turing machine is itself just a finite, unambiguous rule-set of exactly the kind the informal notion describes, whereas all of the philosophical interest, and all of the difficulty, sits in the ceiling claim, whose left-hand side is irreducibly informal and therefore cannot be captured by anything we would recognise as a proof.

Adopting the role of a philosopher of logic with a specialism in computability, please set out the most important implications of this epistemic situation. Before you begin writing, I should like you to spend some time brainstorming what could even count as an implication here, and to be explicit about whether each candidate follows from the thesis itself, from its contested epistemic status, or merely from the way the thesis is habitually used in other debates. Among the directions I have been mulling over, and which you should feel free to endorse, reject or reframe: what the situation implies for whether "algorithm" and "effective procedure" can be treated as terms with determinate extensions at all; what it implies for the philosophy of mind, given how often the thesis is invoked in arguments about whether thinking just is computation; what it implies for the limits of mathematical knowledge, particularly for the force of undecidability results that seem to presuppose the thesis; what it implies for programmes that posit computation more powerful than Turing machines; and what it implies for the wider epistemology of mathematical claims that are universally accepted yet in principle unprovable. Please rank the implications you identify by importance and justify the ranking, and flag any that strike you as resting on a reading of the thesis that a reasonable philosopher could reject.
