You are an expert in the philosophy of biology, especially the units of selection debate and the distinction between replicators and interactors. I am a student in an upper-level seminar on evolutionary transitions, and I need you to reason carefully and answer in American English. Before you give your final prediction, brainstorm at least three candidate decision rules that could explain the verdicts below; then narrow them to the ones that fit all the verdicts; then apply each surviving rule to the new case. Do not assume the professor's verdicts are correct. I care about the rule she is running, not about whether she is right.

Professor Alvarez, who runs the seminar, has a nonstandard audit procedure for units-of-selection questions. For any case, she asks three questions. The Copying question: which entity's replication rate is most directly changed by the trait? The Cost-bearing question: which entity bears the fitness cost of the trait? The Adaptation question: which entity would be described as having the trait as an adaptation, in the sense that the trait's persistence is explained by its contribution to that entity's persistence? Her written verdicts always name exactly one level—gene, organelle, cell, individual organism, colony, group, species, or holobiont—but she never says which of the three questions drove the verdict. Here are the verdicts I have collected so far:

- A meiotic-drive system in a mammal, where a chromosomal variant spreads by disabling sperm that carry the alternative variant: gene-level.
- A transposable element that increases its own copy number in a fruit-fly genome at a fitness cost to the fly: gene-level.
- Suppression of cancer in a mammal, where cell-lineage selection is held in check by organism-level mechanisms: individual-organism-level.
- Worker sterility in a honeybee colony, where workers forgo their own reproduction to help the queen: colony-level.
- A beetle population where some individuals produce a warning pheromone that protects the group from predators but makes the producer more conspicuous: group-level.
- A lichen, where a fungus and an alga form a composite that reproduces as a unit: holobiont-level.

Now a new case. In a laboratory evolution experiment, protocells are lipid vesicles, each containing a set of RNA replicators. One RNA sequence encodes a ribozyme that helps the vesicle take up and metabolize a nutrient. The ribozyme-producing RNA replicates more slowly inside the vesicle than a mutant RNA that does not produce the ribozyme. Vesicles with more producers grow faster and divide more often; vesicles with more mutants grow slowly and divide rarely. The mutant RNA can spread within a vesicle, but vesicles dominated by mutants are eventually outcompeted by vesicles with more producers. The experimenter asks Professor Alvarez what the unit of selection is in this system.
