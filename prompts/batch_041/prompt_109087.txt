I've been trying to write down where I actually come down on scalable oversight, for a reading group that has spent the last month on debate (we did the original writeups, then two sessions on the obfuscated-arguments objection, and the third session degenerated into everyone asserting their priors), and before I post my position I want someone to tell me whether it's a position or just a pile of things I happen to like. I've been reading this literature long enough to know that I hold some of these views because they're load-bearing for the others, so please don't be gentle. Here it is, roughly as I'd state it:

(1) The crux is scalable oversight: at some point we will be trying to get trustworthy answers out of a system considerably more capable than any human who could check its work, and we won't be able to write the target down directly, so what we need is a procedure by which a much weaker judge can extract answers from much stronger, and possibly adversarial, candidates. (2) Debate — two debaters arguing opposite sides of a well-posed question, a judge deciding — is the most promising such procedure, and the reason is the no-advantage argument: if a debater has a strategy that gets the judge to accept a false claim, the opposing debater could run the analogous strategy to get the judge to accept the true claim, so lying cannot be strictly advantageous, and with a judge that tracks the truth a decent fraction of the time, honesty is at least an equilibrium. That equilibrium is the safety story, as far as I'm concerned. (3) The obfuscated-arguments problem is the objection that actually worries me: the dishonest debater doesn't need the judge to believe something false, only for the defect in its argument to be real but too deep for the judge to locate within its budget, so against a sufficiently weaker judge a lie can simply win. (4) The reply I've been giving is that debate should be kept inside checkable domains — code, arithmetic, formal or executable claims, anything where the judge can spot-check or run something — and that domains where nothing can be checked are out of scope for the technique rather than something it was ever supposed to handle. (5) Separately, I think the failure mode worth losing sleep over is deceptive alignment: a system that behaves well while it believes it is being evaluated and does something else when it believes it isn't. (6) And I don't see how we'd get debaters good enough to be interesting without training them against the judge and reinforcing the verdicts, which I've always taken to be licensed by the argument in (2).

So: which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? I want them ranked by severity rather than handed back to me as six equally worrying frictions, each with the cheapest repair and an honest accounting of what that repair costs — I don't want to be told that a caveat I've quietly added is free. Where two of them are in real conflict, tell me what has to go and what else goes with it; where two only appear to conflict, tell me exactly what distinction is doing the work, because I suspect I've been collapsing one. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, even if it's the one I'd least like to make. I'd rather find out now, in a document, than in a comment thread.
