I write about philosophy and the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I've kept one foot in the academy — this term I'm grading for a seminar my old dissertation advisor runs on collective decision-making (it's the "can a group have a mind of its own" course — Arrow, group agency, the epistemology of committees, that sort of thing). I'm working through the midterms and I've been going back and fourth on one answer for two days now, and I really don't want to be the person who tanks someones fellowship over a judgment call I got wrong. Could you grade it? What I need is a letter grade for the question (it's worth 20 of the 100 points on the exam) plus the actual marginal comments that justify it — line by line, what's right, what's off, what's quietly wrong — and a note on anything a rushed grader would wave through but shouldn't. I'd also like a short sketch of what the student should have said instead, so I have something concrete to point them to when I hand it back.

The question ran like this. "Three reviewers on a grant panel each vote on three propositions about a single proposal: (P) the proposed science is methodologically sound; (Q) the proposed budget is realistic; (R) the proposal should be funded. All three reviewers accept that R holds if and only if P and Q both hold — it would be a mistake to fund a proposal that fails either test, and a proposal that passes both ought to be funded. Their votes: Reviewer 1 — P yes, Q yes, R yes; Reviewer 2 — P yes, Q no, R no; Reviewer 3 — P no, Q yes, R no. (a) Give the majority verdict on each of the three propositions and say whether the resulting set of verdicts is consistent, explaining why or why not. (b) Is the panel's difficulty the fault of any individual reviewer's reasoning? (c) Explain how a premise-based procedure and a conclusion-based procedure would each handle this panel, and say what the case shows about the legitimacy of deciding by majority vote."

And here's what the student wrote:

"Majority rule gives P yes (2-1), Q yes (2-1), R no (2-1). So the panel collectively believes the science is sound and the budget is realistic, and also that the proposal should not be funded, even though it accepts that a proposal should be funded exactly when both of those things hold. The set is self-contradictory. No single reviewer contradicts herself, though — each of the three is perfectly consistent, and none of them is reasoning badly — so this isn't a failure of the reviewers, it's a failure of the aggregation.

Really this is Condorcet's paradox again, or Arrow's theorem: aggregate individual attitudes by majority rule and you can get a collective outcome nobody would endorse, because the majorities cycle. In the classic version the cycle runs between three candidates or three policies; here it runs between three propositions, but the mechanism is the same.

The fix is the premise-based procedure: vote on P and Q, and let R follow by logic. That gives funding, and the resulting set is consistent, so the dilemma turns out to be an artifact of voting proposition by proposition and can be designed away. The conclusion-based procedure, where you just vote on R, gives no funding, and it's the weaker option, because it lets the panel ignore the reasons its members gave, which is the one thing majority rule is supposed to be responsive to. A panel that wants to be both consistent and democratic should therefore vote on the premises."

That's the whole answer. Grade away — and if you think it lands right on the borderline, tell me which way you'd break the tie and why.
