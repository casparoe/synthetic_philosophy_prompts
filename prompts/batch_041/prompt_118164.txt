So I just got my citizenship — six years, two lawyers, don't ask — and my parents threw a dinner for me. My uncle, who spent his whole career in the merchant marine and therefore considers himself an authority on boats, gave a little toast of his own about how a country is a lifeboat. Fixed capacity. People already aboard. Keep hauling swimmers in past that capacity and the boat swamps and everybody drowns. So strict borders are just good seamanship, and nobody gets to call it cruelty. I didn't want to have it out with him at my own party but I've been chewing on it all week.

What I actually want is an audit of the analogy, not a sermon. Correspondence table first — what plays the boat, the sea, the capacity, the passengers, the captain, whoever built the boat — then where the mapping holds and where it quietly breaks. The bit I keep circling is capacity: is that a fact about the world, or a decision made by the people already sitting in the boat, and does his conclusion survive if its the second one? He also likes to point out that even at sea the law makes ships help people in distress, and he treats that as proof that the limits are built in from the start. I think that actualy cuts against him but I can't articulate why. And if the lifeboat is a lost cause, is there a picture that does the same work without cheating?
