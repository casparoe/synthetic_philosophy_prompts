Next month my postdoctoral advisor retires after forty-one years in an evolutionary biology department, and I've been asked to give the toast at her dinner. She spent her whole career on the unfashionable side of a fight that most people in the room have a stake in: she argued, from her first grant to her last paper, that selection at the level of the group is a real and load-bearing force in evolution, and that the gene's-eye picture which dominated the field for decades made it too easy to explain group-beneficial traits away as bookkeeping for the interests of individuals. About half the guests are her colleagues and former students, people who will hear a single careless word about kin selection or the price equation and wince, and some of whom spent their own careers arguing that she had the level of selection wrong. The other half are her family, including three grandchildren who know her mainly as the person who grows the tomatoes.

What I want from you is the toast itself: five minutes, spoken, pitched at that mixed room. It has to be sincere. I'm not willing to stand up and pretend the debate came out her way, and I don't want the usual pioneer and ahead-of-her-time filler that would say nothing at all. The interesting thing she used to insist on, and which I think is the real hinge here, is that the question of which entity is the unit of selection is not one question but two: there is a difference between which entities are being selected and which entities the selected traits are for, and a trait can be for the good of a group without the group being the thing that selection is acting on. That distinction is what let her keep arguing with people who thought she was simply wrong, and I'd like the toast to be built on it rather than merely decorated with it. The speech should use that ambiguity to say something honest and specific about what she actually contributed, to an audience that will notice if I get the biology wrong, without losing the grandchildren at the table.

Please take a definite line: pick one reading of the units-of-selection debate and one account of what her career amounted to, commit to both, and give your reasons, rather than laying the positions out side by side and leaving the choice to me. And because this is a speech rather than a paper, close with a short, candid note on where it takes liberties, whether with the science or with a contested interpretation of what she was up to, so that I know exactly what I would be committing to if I read it aloud at the dinner.
