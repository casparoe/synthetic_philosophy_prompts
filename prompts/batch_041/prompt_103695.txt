I spend a lot of time reading rationalist blogs and comment threads, and one thread I keep pulling on is the project of heuristic arguments: the attempt to make rigorous the loose, structural reasoning mathematicians actually use when they guess at a result before they can prove it. The piece of that project I find most interesting, and most slippery, is the presumption of independence. The rough idea is that when nothing you know links two propositions or two unknown quantities, you are entitled to reason about them as if they were probabilistically independent. That is the move behind treating the truth of two unrelated open conjectures as independent random variables, or treating the digits of an irrational constant as uniform, or assuming that the accidental features of a large object are uncorrelated with one another.

I would like you to argue for a specific thesis about how this presumption should be cashed out. The thesis is that the presumption of independence is fundamentally a claim about the inductive situation of a computationally bounded reasoner — a norm telling such a reasoner which regularities it is entitled to project and which coincidences it should not treat as informative — and not a claim about any objective chance distribution or about the world's ontic structure. If that is right, then a whole class of formalizations are misfires: any account that fixes a single prior over a fixed hypothesis space, or that tries to read the presumption off one probability distribution, will either build in the very correlations the heuristic is supposed to license ignoring or will smuggle in substantive assumptions. The correct formalization, on this view, will have to be stated over a family of reasoners with different inductive biases, and will be a claim about what those reasoners can and cannot be surprised by. Please make the strongest case you can for this thesis, and do not merely assert it: motivate why the ontic and fixed-prior readings fail, and give at least one concrete toy example, spelled out with actual numbers or a small formal setup, in which the epistemic reading and the fixed-prior reading diverge.

The obvious objections are the ones I want you to take seriously. One is that once you refuse to fix a hypothesis space, the presumption becomes vacuous or collapses into plain subjective Bayesianism, where anything goes and the heuristic has no teeth. Another is that boundedness is doing no work: a computationally unbounded reasoner could adopt the same heuristic, so the appeal to resource limits looks like a distraction. A third is that the heuristic is simply false in general, since knowing one fact very often does tell you about a supposedly unrelated fact, and the interesting question is what licenses the inference anyway. I would like the argument to be self-contained — I do not want a survey of what other people have written on this, and I do not want it to lean on names or references I would have to go look up. I want the actual reasoning, in the weeds, including where you think the thesis is weakest.
