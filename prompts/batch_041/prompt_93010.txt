I'm running a session for my Indian epistemology reading group — 14 people, mostly philosophy postgrads but only two of them have read any Nyāya before, 90 minutes, one room with movable chairs. Every time we hit the list of pramāṇas it goes flat: someone reads out perception, inference, testimony, comparison, and everyone nods as if these were just four labels for things they already believe in, and then we spend the rest of the hour on whether testimony is really irreducible to inference and never get anywhere. What I want is an activity where they have to actually *produce* knowledge-claims under a classification scheme and then defend their classification against someone who is trying to collapse it, so that the question of whether a given route to knowledge is a genuinely separate means or just a disguised form of another one stops being a vocabulary exercise and starts being a fight. My hunch is that the bit they'll feel hardest is the comparison case — you're told what a gavaya is like before you've ever seen one, you later see the animal, and something has clearly happened that is neither bare perception nor a syllogism, yet it's maddeningly hard to say what — and I want them to run into that wall themselves rather than hear me describe it.

So: design me a runnable activity, roughly like a tribunal or an inquest, where the group splits into two or three investigating teams and a panel of classifiers, and each team gets a sealed card describing a scenario in which somebody comes to know something by a route I've specified (I'll write the cards, but tell me what the cards need to contain and give me the scenarios you'd use — I want at least one comparison case, one case where someone works out an unobserved fact from a puzzling combination of circumstances, and one case where someone comes to know that something is *not* there, plus a couple of decoys that really are just perception or just inference). The teams must state the knowledge-claim in a canonical form and nominate which pramāṇa it belongs to; then the classifiers — who are briefed to play a hard reductionist, the sort of opponent who says everything is perception or inference and the rest is bookkeeping — get to attack each nomination and force the team either to defend the route as distinct or to concede it collapses. Give me the rules, the timing across the 90 minutes, how the cards should be shuffled and dealt so the interesting collisions happen, what the panel is allowed to do to a team that just says it's obvious, and a scoring scheme that rewards a team for surviving a reduction attempt rather than for guessing the answer I have in mind. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual dispute between the schools — the ones who hold out for four means, the ones who want five or six, the ones who think two will do — and that shows them why the interesting question is not how many items are on the list but which reductions succeed and what it costs to make them succeed. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'd bet on the panel going easy on the comparison case because they can't articulate what's wrong with the inference reduction, and on one team just announcing that everything is inference and refusing to engage), how I should handle each of those, and which part of the real position the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in my own voice at the end — I'm thinking in particular that no game about classifying knowledge-arrivals will convey anything about why the Naiyāyikas thought the means of knowing needed to be *reliable* in a strong sense rather than merely truth-conducive, or about the realist metaphysics of absence that sits under one of the schools' positions, but tell me if you think I've got that diagnosis wrong. Please write it in British English.
