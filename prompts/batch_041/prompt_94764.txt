I'm putting together material for a grad seminar and I keep circling the same irritation. Whenever people in my department (or the CS lot down the corridor) model what an agent knows, the standard move is to hand the agent a set of possible worlds and then say it knows whatever is true in all of them — or, equivalently, a knowledge base that's just a pile of sentences, with the agent credited with everything you can derive from the pile. The upshot is an agent that knows every tautology, knows every logical consequence of what it knows, and treats logically equivalent sentences as interchangable. No real reasoner is like that — not you, not me, not the largest model anyone's trained.

So here's what I want. Treat that modelling choice as the practice, and tell me what it presupposes philosophically. What theory of knowledge, or of the epistemic subject, does it only make sense under? Something like: knowledge is a state closed under entailment and indexed to a fixed background logic. But be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Does the practice really commit you to the closure principle, or could you read the whole possible-worlds apparatus as a mere idealisation — frictionless planes, spherical cows — that says nothing much about the epistemology? Is it compatable with a picture on which the agent is a bounded, resource-limited reasoner, or does the very equation of what it knows with what's true at all its worlds quietly rule that out?

Then push on the inconsistency I can't shake. The same labs will cheerfully tell you the agent's knowledge is everything derivable, and then turn round and grade the thing on whether it can produce an answer inside a token or time budget, or fail it for not retrieving a fact it could in principle have derived. So one bit of the practice treats knowledge as unbounded and closure-closed, another treats it as a matter of what actually gets computed. Are those two commitments consistent, or is the second just engineering reality papering over the first? And tell me which parts of the practice are philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories of knowledge would license exactly the same design — as opposed to which parts force a substantive commitment.

Finally: if we dropped the omniscience assumption, what would have to change? Not the formalism — I know there are tricks with impossible worlds and awareness operators, you don't need to rehearse the menu — but what the practice itself would have to look like. Would you have to stop saying the agent knows anything and talk about retrieval, or degrees of accessibility, instead? Would the knowledge-based framing have to go altogether?

Write it as an essay. British English, please — I'll be marking these and I don't want to see any -ize endings. Don't bother with a reading list or citations, I know the literature; I want the argument.
