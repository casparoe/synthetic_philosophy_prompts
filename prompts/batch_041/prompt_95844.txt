I keep going back and forth on epistemic injustice and I want a principle that actually matches my verdicts, not the textbook one. Here's what I've got. A doctor brushes off a woman's report of chronic pain as dramatics — injustice, obviously. Same doctor brushes off a man's report because he's faked symptoms twice before, and this time the doctor happens to be wrong — not injustice, he had reason. A doctor quietly discounts a patient's account because booking an interpreter is a hassle and the clinic is running late — that feels like injustice to me and I can't point at any stereotype, just friction. Someone with a condition nobody has named can't get anyone, including herself, to register her pain as pain — injustice, and the weird part is nobody did anything wrong. Same person, except the name exists but only in a paywalled journal and a support group she'd never find — still injustice, but now I can't say the community is missing the concept. A Deaf person gets talked over because the meeting's turn-taking is too fast for anyone to wait — bad, and I genuinely dont know if its injustice. A man's account is discounted because his demographic really does have a higher rate of the thing he's reporting, the statistic is accurate, and he's telling the truth — I want to call it injustice and I can't see why, since the doubt is well-founded. And a woman who's been dismissed her whole life now hedges her own symptoms before anyone else can — injustice, but I can't find anyone who did it.

My attempt at a principle: you're wronged as a knower, not for what you said. Test it against these, tell me which of my verdicts it makes me give up, repair it if you can, and say what it predicts for a case I didn't list: someone from a marginalized group who gets over-believed, trusted on a medical question they had no business being trusted on, and it hurts them.
