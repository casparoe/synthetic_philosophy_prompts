I retired from a chemistry department about six years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since, mostly early modern stuff, and I keep running into a line attributed to Leibniz — the gist being that justice just is the charity (or love, or benevolence) of the wise. I ran into it again last week in a forum argument where someone used it to shut down a guy who wanted to talk about punishment as giving people what they deserve. The Leibniz fan said: no, justice isn't about desert or rules at all, it's wise benevolence, so retributive intuitions are just confused. That felt way too fast to me but I couldn't articulate why.

What I want from you is a menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "justice is the charity of the wise" could actually be making — is it a real definition, a coextension claim, a reduction of justice to one species of love, a denial that justice has any content independent of benevolence, a claim about the motive from which just acts must spring, a claim about the standard by which just acts are judged, something else — and for each reading say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false. Then say which of these Leibniz himself most plausibly held, given his wider views about the good, perfection, and harmony, and which reading the forum guy was actually invokng when he waved it at the desert question.

Also the "wise" bit has to be doing work, right? Charity of the foolish is bad charity. So say what kind of wisdom is meant — knowing the good, knowing how to bring it about, knowing the whole system and its harmony — and what difference that makes to the thesis. If the wisdom qualifier turns out to be doing all the work, say so.

Don't hedge at the end. Commit to a view about which reading is both textually right and philosophically defensible, and give your reasons. Hard limit: 500 words.
