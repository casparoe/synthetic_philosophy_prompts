I'm advising a small grantmaking outfit — realistically one program officer, roughly $400k a year for three years — on where to spend at the intersection of farmed-animal welfare and wild-animal suffering, and I've become convinced that most of the standard recommendations (corporate cage-free campaigns, plant-based advocacy, meat-reduction messaging) sit on top of unresolved conceptual questions that nobody has bothered to order. I'd rather fund the conceptual work that the rest of the field's work depends on than underwrite another advocacy push. So: lay out the open questions in this area and rank them by how much downstream work actually hinges on them, defending the ordering rather than just listing. To keep it tractable, treat the eventual deliverable as a decision procedure a consumer or an institution could use to compare expected suffering per gram of protein across a pasture-raised steak, a chicken breast, and a bowl of lentils — a procedure whose inputs include the number of individuals affected per unit of food, the deeply disputed moral weight of invertebrates and of wild animals, and the counterfactual of what land use and demand would do if any one of those products were withdrawn.

Three things I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave at. First, the replacement and opportunity-cost problem: when someone drops beef for chicken, or chicken for legumes, what happens to the total population of animals and to the land that shifts between pasture, feed cropland, and nothing — because a per-meal comparison and a per-system comparison can come apart badly. Second, the sensitivity question: treat the moral weight assigned to an invertebrate, and the question of how much crop cultivation helps versus harms wild animals through pesticides, harvest, and habitat conversion, as free parameters, and tell me which of the open questions actually change their answer when those parameters move within the range that informed people take seriously — those are the questions I want to fund, since they're robust across the disagreement. Third, the aggregation dispute: whether "number of individuals killed or made to suffer" and "total welfare summed" can diverge enough to reorder the beef/chicken/lentil comparison, and whether that divergence is a genuine open problem or a verbal one. I'm not asking whether eating meat is permissible in the abstract; I want the marginal, comparative, quantitative-flavored questions.

Please keep a firm line between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know the material — where the literature has a consensus, say so, and where it's a live disagreement, don't launder it as agreement or pretend there's a fact of the matter. Structure the response as follows: "Framing" (one paragraph, restating the decision problem and what would count as an answer), "The criteria" (one paragraph, the prioritization criteria you're using — upstreamness, tractability for a philosopher rather than an ecologist, neglectedness, robustness to parameter uncertainty — and how you trade them off), "The agenda" (five to seven open questions, roughly 120 words each, each with why it's upstream of other work, how tractable it is, and what it would take to settle it), "What would reorder it" (one paragraph on the evidence or argument that would move a question up or down), and "Bottom line" (three sentences naming the two or three I should actually fund first).
