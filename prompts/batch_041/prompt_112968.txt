Q3 (10 marks). In the unit we treated Gettier's paper as a problem about the sufficiency of the JTB analysis, and we looked at three families of repair: no-false-lemmas / defeasibility conditions; modal conditions (sensitivity, safety); and virtue-theoretic accounts.

(i) State the JTB analysis and construct a Gettier case of your own. Identify precisely the feature of the case that makes it a counterexample — i.e., say where the epistemic luck enters. (3)

(ii) Consider the following case: Ravi is driving through a valley he has never visited. He looks out of the window, sees a barn-shaped structure with a red painted roof, and forms the belief that's a barn. His eyesight is fine, the light is good, and the structure he's looking at really is a barn. Unbeknownst to Ravi, the valley was used as a film set last summer: every other barn-shaped structure in the valley is a cardboard facade with a painted roof, indistinguishable from a real barn from the road. Does this case tell against the no-false-lemmas condition? If so, does it show that condition to be too strong, or too weak? Justify your answer. (5)

(iii) Does the Gettier problem show that knowledge cannot be analysed? One or two sentences. (2)

Student's answer:

(i) JTB says S knows that p if and only if p is true, S believes that p, and S is justified in believing that p. Gettier showed that all three can hold at once without knowledge. Take the Smith and Jones case (I know we were asked to make one up, but this is the clearest): Smith has excellent evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket, so he infers that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. Smith gets the job, and he too happens to have ten coins in his pocket, so his belief is true. What makes it a counterexample is that his evidence is false — the reasoning runs through the claim about Jones getting the job — so although the belief is justified and true it isn't knowledge. The luck enters at the level of the evidence, then, rather than at the level of the conclusion.

(ii) This is a Gettier-type case. Ravi's belief is true, he holds it, and he's justified: his eyesight is fine, the light is good, and he's looking at a real barn with a red roof. But he doesn't know, because he'd have believed the same thing if he'd been looking at a facade — his belief isn't safe. Note that Ravi has no false beliefs anywhere in the story: the structure is a barn, the roof is red, the light is good. So the no-false-lemmas condition is satisfied. Still, I don't think this tells against the condition, and my answer to the question is 'neither'. The condition was designed for cases like Smith and Jones, where the reasoning runs through something false; the barn case is really a counterexample to the bare JTB analysis, and the natural fix is a modal condition like safety rather than another lemma condition. So the no-false-lemmas condition isn't too weak — it just wasn't meant to do that job.

(iii) No — the moral isn't that knowledge is indefinable. It's that an adequate analysis has to rule luck out entirely, so that no luck is left anywhere in the story. That's why I'd put my money on safety.
