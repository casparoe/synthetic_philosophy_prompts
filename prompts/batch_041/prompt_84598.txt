Liberal states now do two things that appear to answer to incompatible views about the value of memory. They require schoolchildren to study historical atrocities, fund memorials, museums, and official days of remembrance, and in several countries criminalise the denial of those atrocities. At the same time they grant individuals a right to have old and no longer relevant information about themselves erased or removed from search results, seal or expunge criminal records after a period of good behaviour, and restrict how far back employers may inquire into a person's past. Two writers I have read seem to disagree about whether such a combination can be defended at all: Avishai Margalit holds that we owe the dead a duty of remembrance grounded in respect, while David Rieff holds that collective memory of past atrocities is at least as often a curse as a blessing, and that there is a case for letting some things go.

I am not asking which of them is right, and I am not asking for a policy recommendation. I want to know what the practice itself presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and tell me whether its two halves presuppose consistent things. Does the combination require a division of labour in which remembrance is owed to the dead and to a community's identity, while forgetting is a right of the living individual, and is that division principled or a fudge that lets us avoid choosing? Note that the right to erasure is not absolute: it is drafted so as not to override historical and archival research, and it yields to freedom of expression. Does that carve-out show that the practice already treats memory and forgetting as answering to different values, or does it merely rank the same value differently in different settings? What does expungement presuppose about personal identity over time, that a person can cease to be the person who did the deed, or only that others may be required to treat him as though he had? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license exactly this set of rules. Finally, tell me what would have to give if the presupposed view were replaced by a uniform one: what would a regime built on a genuine duty to remember have to do about spent convictions and about delisting, and what would one built on a general right to be forgotten have to do about the memorials, the school curricula, and the prosecutions for denial?
