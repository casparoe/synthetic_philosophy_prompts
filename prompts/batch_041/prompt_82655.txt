I edit the quarterly newsletter of our department's rational-choice reading group, and for the spring issue — the one themed around growth-optimal betting — I'd like to commission a satire page. The idea I keep returning to is a problem-page advice column, the sort of thing a glossy magazine runs under a heading like "Ask the Actuary": readers write in with their dilemmas and the columnist answers with serene authority. Only this columnist is a true believer in the Kelly criterion and in what some people call geometric rationality, and answers every letter by treating a life as a bankroll to be grown. The theory I want the column to run on, in case it helps to have it stated: to maximise the expected logarithm of your wealth — equivalently, the rate at which your money compounds — you stake a fixed fraction of your bankroll on each favourable bet, and that fraction is generally well short of everything you could stake; ruin is not a bad outcome but the end of the sequence, so a small probability of total loss is priced out of all proportion to its size; when your edge is estimated rather than known you should stake less than the formula says; and insurance against a loss that is large relative to your wealth can be the log-optimal move even when the premium exceeds the expected loss. Geometric rationality, as I understand it, carries the same logic from a bankroll to utility in general: rather than maximising the average of your utility across possible worlds, you maximise its geometric mean, so a world in which you end up with nothing counts for disproportionately much and you should be willing to give up a great deal in most worlds to keep it off the table. The comedy should come from the advice being coherent, so that someone who knows the betting literature laughs rather than winces, while a reader who has never heard of any of this can still follow it.

Format: a title and a one-line standfirst, then four letters with the columnist's replies, then a short sign-off — roughly 800 words in all. Voice: deadpan, avuncular, faintly exasperated at how often readers ask permission to feel something the arithmetic doesn't license. The target of the satire is the habit of treating every choice — career, insurance, family, grief — as a bet to be sized, and the way the authority of a correct result travels to cases where its premises (a repeated bet, a known edge, a bankroll you will bet again) no longer hold. Nobody in the piece should break character, nobody should signal awareness of being funny, and the words "satire" and "parody" should not appear. Set it in an invented world: no real economists, authors, books or institutions, no citations, no figures that can't be derived from what the readers report. Two further constraints. First, internal consistency is part of the brief — the columnist must never contradict themselves, so where a later answer seems to conflict with an earlier one (it will, around the question of taking bets with negative expected value) the resolution has to be a genuine feature of the theory rather than a fudge. Second, at least once the columnist should be exactly right in a way a careless reader will read as a blunder, and at least once they should concede that the framework is silent on the question asked — and then answer anyway, without noticing that this is what they have done.

Use the following four letters verbatim as the readers' side of the exchange, giving the writers invented names or initials: (1) a reader whose uncle has offered even money on a coin that the reader has seen come up heads 60 times in 100 flips, and who has £5,000 saved for a house deposit, asking how much to stake; (2) a reader who has lost £40,000 of a £45,000 inheritance on a run of bets their own spreadsheet described as positive-expected-value, asking how to get back to where they were; (3) a reader whose flat has roughly a 1-in-500 annual chance of being destroyed by flood, who can buy insurance at a premium exceeding the expected loss, and who asks whether buying it contradicts what the columnist told letter (1); (4) a reader who has been offered a job at a company that will most likely fail, at half their current salary, and who observes that there is no repeated version of a career to average over — they get one run — and asks whether geometric rationality says to take it. Each reply should actually answer its letter: the first, for instance, should commit to a figure. A closing run of one-line questions with one-line answers is welcome if it earns its space, provided every answer is still something the columnist could have said in the replies above.
