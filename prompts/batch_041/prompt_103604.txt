Hey, so for my philosophy club project I'm supposed to write about vagueness, and I picked the fight between epistemicism and supervaluationism. Quick version of what I mean by those, just so we're on the same page: epistemicism says a sentence like "that pile is a heap" always has a definite true-or-false answer even when the pile is borderline, we just can't know which one. Supervaluationism says borderline sentences are neither true nor false, but they still come out true if they're true no matter how you make the vague word precise, and false if they're false on every precisification.

Here's the thing I actually want you to argue about, and I think its an angle people don't talk about alot. I'm calling it retroactive sharpening. Imagine my school has a rule that you have to be "on time" to first period or you get detention. Nobody knows where the line is. Then the school board votes and says: on time means your feet are in the door by 8:00:00.0 exactly, no exceptions. Now what about the kid who walked in at 8:00:00.4 last Tuesday, before the board ever voted? Under the new rule he was late. But was he late on Tuesday? Did the board find out something that was always true, or did they just decide it? And then a year later a different board moves the line to 8:00:30 — so which of those was the real line? This feels like it should matter for the debate but I can't get it straight.

Don't just say epistemicism is simpler or that supervaluationism has weird logic consequences — actually engage with what the school board case shows about whether there was a fact of the matter before anyone drew a line. And say what the epistemicist has to say about the second board vote, because that's the part I really can't figure out.
