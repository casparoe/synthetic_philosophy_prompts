ok so I want you to do a comparison for me, and I want it to be the real thing rather than the standard lecture-hall version. The pairing is Descartes' dream doubt in the Sixth Meditation and the butterfly passage near the end of the Qiwulun chapter of the Zhuangzi. Everyone puts these two side by side and says "both worry that life might be a dream," and then moves on. I want you to actually pull them apart.

Here's what I think is the interesting fault line, but don't just take my word for it — argue it out. Descartes' question is, roughly, "how do I know I'm not dreaming right now?", and his answer, late in the Sixth Meditation, leans on the fact that waking experience hangs together with the whole remembered course of my life in a way dream experience doesn't: dreams don't get woven into that chain, and there's a God who isn't a deceiver backing the whole thing up. Zhuangzi's passage doesn't ask that question at all. It asks which one I am — was I Zhuangzi who dreamt he was a butterfly, or am I now a butterfly dreaming I'm Zhuangzi? — and then it just stops, with a line about things transforming into one another, no criterion, no verdict, no God. So one of these is an epistemological problem with an exit, and the other may not be an epistemological problem at all.

Things I want you to work through, in no particular order:

Whether Descartes' memory-and-coherence criterion actually does the work he needs, or whether it's circular — the state whose reliability is in question is precisely the state that supplies the memories you'd use to certify it. Push on that hard. Also say what work the non-deceiving God is doing that the memory criterion can't do on its own, and whether dropping the God leaves the criterion standing.

Whether Zhuangzi's ending is a failure to answer, a refusal to answer, or a sign that he was never asking the question. There's a reading on which the butterfly passage is continuous with the rest of the Qiwulun — the whole chapter is about the mutability of perspectives and the way distinctions harden and dissolve, and the dream line is a metaphysical remark about transformation rather than a skeptical hypothesis. If that's right, then calling it "dream skepticism" is a bit of a projection. But if it's wrong, say why.

A case I want you to bring in yourself and use as a stress test: lucid dreaming. A lucid dreamer knows, from inside, that she is dreaming, and the dream carries on. Ask what this does to each position. My hunch is that it doesn't rescue Descartes — knowing you're dreaming doesn't tell you which of the two lives is the real one, and lucidity is itself something you can dream — but it also doesn't hand Zhuangzi his conclusion for free, because a lucid dreamer still has a self on both sides of the transition and still cares which one she is. See whether you can make that hunch either stand up or collapse. If it collapses, say so.

The asymmetry I keep coming back to: in Zhuangzi's story the butterfly has no doubt at all. The dream contains no anxiety about being a dream, which means first-person evidence of dreamhood may be structurally unavailable to the dreamer from inside — you can only ever get the news from the other side, and by then you're awake, and you have no guarantee the same isn't true in reverse. Does that make Descartes' doubt impossible to execute rather than hard to resolve? Does it make Zhuangzi's question the better-posed one, or just a different one?

Finally, a verdict: is "dream skepticism" one problem with two costumes, or two problems that happen to share imagery? And whichever you land on, tell me which of the two texts you think a philosopher today should actually be working from, and why the other one isn't it. Don't hedge at the end — pick.
