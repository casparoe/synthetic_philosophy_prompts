I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending origin essentialism — the thesis that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg — but who rejects what she calls the bare-particular reading of it. Her line is that what is essential is not a haecceity and not the particular gametes as such, but the individual's initial state: the complete causal starting point of its development. She draws two consequences and is happy to defend them. First, origin essentialism is not a special fact about humans, or even about organisms: it generalizes to anything with a determinate developmental history, so a machine assembled from parts on a particular occasion is equally origin-essential. Second, in cloning and nucleus-transfer cases there is no "same individual with a different origin": a transferred nucleus is a different initial state and therefore a different individual. She also insists that none of this bears on personal identity over time or on the ethics of reproduction.

Write me eight to ten interview questions for her, in the register of a spoken interview, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question must press on whether the initial-state version collapses into something trivial — the worry that any two distinct causal histories trivially yield distinct individuals, so that the view is no longer about origin at all. At least one must press on fission: if a zygote divides and two people result, what is each twin's initial state, and can she explain why the division belongs to the origin of both rather than of neither? At least one must press on whether her second consequence commits her to the view that a person is essentially the bearer of a particular genome, and whether that can be squared with her denial that any of this touches personal identity or reproductive ethics.

You are a philosopher working on essentialism and personal identity; start by thinking through her position and locating where it is genuinely weakest. Then be committed: don't hand me a menu of possible angles. Rank the questions, tell me which two would actually force her to give something up, name the reply she is most likely to make to each, and stake your own view on whether that reply works. Where you are unsure, say what would settle it, but make the call.
