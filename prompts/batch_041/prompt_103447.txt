I have been asked to give the student address at my college's commencement this spring. I am a returning student in my fifties, having left the degree unfinished in the 1990s and come back to finish it, and I have no academic background, only a couple of years of reading on my own. The thought I cannot put down is the one that says this particular stretch of history may be unusually decisive, because of technologies now arriving, in a way that ordinary decades are not. I want the address built on that thought, and I want it built honestly.

Write it: about seven minutes spoken, for an audience of families and local people who have never heard the idea, with a handful of faculty in the front rows who know the literature and will catch it if I oversell. Three constraints. First, the speech must not assume the thought is true; it has to concede that every generation has felt itself to be the decisive one, and that the feeling is not evidence, and still leave the graduates with a reason to act as though their choices carried unusual weight. Second, it has to face the objection that if this moment really is decisive, the decisiveness probably sits with a few governments, laboratories, and firms rather than with the person in the cheap folding chair, and it must say what follows for them anyway. Third, it must not sound like prophecy: nothing about a chosen generation, nothing about destiny, nothing that a religious family and a skeptical one would hear differently in a way that splits the room. The idea should govern the whole shape of the speech, deciding what I may claim and what I have to give up, rather than appearing as a flourish near the top. No authors, titles, or citations; I could not defend them if anyone asked.
