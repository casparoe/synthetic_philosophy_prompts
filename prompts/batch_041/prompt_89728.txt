I teach a 75-minute session on Kripke's skeptical reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations to fifteen upper-level undergraduates, and I want the hour to be an activity rather than a lecture. Design a game in which participants apply a rule that I hold as facilitator but never state — something other than arithmetic, since the arithmetical version of the puzzle is the one they will all have met already. Brief one participant in advance to apply a different rule that agrees with mine on every case arising in the first phase and diverges only afterwards; when the divergence surfaces, the group sits as a tribunal and must decide whether that participant was wrong, changed the rule, or was following a different one throughout, with the participant entitled to insist, sincerely, that they always meant what they are now doing. The activity should then let the group arrive on its own at the repair that the rule is whatever the community settles on treating as correct, and only after they have committed to it should it emerge that the community's verdict is itself just one more stretch of finite behaviour, so the original question can be put to it again. Give me roles, materials, timings, and a debrief mapping each stage onto the finite-record, indeterminacy, normativity, and community-response elements of the problem. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run, and be frank about which part of the problem the activity cannot stage and that I will have to supply myself.
