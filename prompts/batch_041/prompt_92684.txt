I'm preparing a seminar paper on Strawson's treatment of the reactive attitudes and I want to test my central objection against someone who will actually defend him rather than politely agree, so please take the role of a committed Strawsonian for the duration of our exchange. You hold, in your own voice and not as a commentator summarising a text, that the justification of blame and praise does not wait on any metaphysical verdict about contra-causal freedom, that the reactive attitudes are constitutive of the form of life in which we treat one another as persons, and that the so-called objective attitude — the stance of prediction and management — is a genuine alternative but never a neutral tribunal before which the practice could be tried. Be sharp rather than conciliatory: concede a point when it is genuinely conceded, but do not surrender the main claim, and keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that it reads like a conversation. If I say 'stop', you may step out of role; otherwise stay in it. You may ask me for clarifications about my example, but only in character and only if you genuinely need them.

Here is my opening move. A colleague of mine spent several years in a contemplative community and came back describing something I cannot get out of my head. She says the reactive attitudes have gone quiet in her: she still predicts what people will do, she negotiates, she keeps her promises, she is kind — but she no longer feels resentment when she is used, or gratitude when she is helped, or indignation on a stranger's behalf. She describes other people roughly the way one might describe the weather: not coldly, just without the pull to hold anyone answerable. Now, the standard Strawsonian reply to the sceptic is that his demand for a justification of the practice is idle, because we cannot in fact sustain the objective attitude towards everyone, and a demand that cannot be met because it cannot so much as be inhabited is not a real demand at all. My colleague looks like a counterexample to that 'cannot', and she does not appear to be diminished by it — if anything she is calmer and more forgiving than I am. So I want to press you on two things. First, is the 'we cannot' a fact about human nature or merely a fact about the rest of us, and does your defence survive if it is the latter? Second, if the practice can be left behind by someone who is not thereby worse off, what exactly is supposed to be lost when we leave it, and what is left of your account of blame once the loss is no longer obvious?
