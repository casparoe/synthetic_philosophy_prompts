I'm working on a seminar paper about Mill's feminism, and I keep running into an objection I can't answer well. A friend in the seminar argues that the empirical literature on the gender-equality paradox settles the question Mill left open in The Subjection of Women: Mill says nobody can know the nature of the two sexes while they have only ever been observed in conditions of inequality, and that the way to find out is to remove the inequality and watch what happens. If the most egalitarian societies are the closest thing we have to that experiment, my friend says, then the result is in, and the result is that the differences don't shrink — they get bigger. I'd like you to reason this through on Mill's behalf rather than in your own voice, and tell me whether he would accept the framing at all.

Here is the pattern as I understand it, and I want you to be careful about which version you're responding to. In countries that score highest on standard gender-equality indices — the Nordics, typically — average differences between men and women in the fields they choose to study and work in are reported to be larger, not smaller, than in countries with much more traditional gender norms. The flagship case is STEM: the relative share of women among STEM graduates, or the ratio of women to men entering those fields, tends to be lower in the most egalitarian countries. But this is a claim about relative sorting rather than absolute participation — the total number of women going into physics or engineering is generally not lower in those countries, and much of the reversal is about where women cluster instead, in health, education, and care work. Something similar has been reported for self-reported traits and values, such as competitiveness and appetite for risk. The literature is also genuinely contested: the most-cited version of the STEM finding was challenged on how its key measure was constructed and had to be corrected, and critics argue the whole pattern is an artifact of comparing relative rather than absolute measures across countries. Treat it as a live and partly unsettled hypothesis, and tell me how much of your answer depends on which version of it turns out to survive.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of Mill's response in his own argumentative idiom, not a survey of what contemporary philosophers have said. First, does he read this as the experiment he asked for, and if so, does it count as evidence for innate differences in interests or aptitudes? Second, if he resists — because the conditions still aren't clean, because preferences are themselves formed under residual norms, because the relevant comparison is the opportunity set rather than the ratio of women to men within a field — is that resistance a principled continuation of his argument or an unfalsifiable hedge, and what evidence would actually move him? Third, does any of this bear on the normative claim? He holds that the legal subordination of women is wrong in itself, and I want to know whether the naturalness question is doing any work in that argument for him or is strictly downstream of it. And fourth, if he thinks the egalitarian countries are still getting something wrong, I want to know what he thinks it is. Bring his associationist psychology, his utilitarianism, and his faith in the experimental method into play, and flag the points where the reconstruction requires you to guess. I'm not looking for citations or a reading list — assume I know the texts, and tell me where the arguments go.
