I'm an investigator at a regional ombudsman's office — we take complaints that the council or the housing association mishandled something, and roughly fifteen times a week I have to decide whether a complaint is upheld, not upheld or partly upheld, and then write the reasons so they survive being read by the council, the complainant, and occasionally a lawyer. I've got eight weeks from the day the complaint lands on my desk, no power to compel anything, my manager has to sign off on whatever gets published, and my two collegues have never read a word of philosophy. Everything we do is evidence: what I saw myself on a site visit, what the records show, what the complainant tells me, what the officer tells me, and what happened in the last case that looked like this one.

I've been reading around Indian epistemology for a book group and got oddly intrested in the Nyāya school, because they seem to have built an entire apparatus for exactly this — deciding when a cognition counts as knowledge. Four means of knowing, as I understand it: perception, inference, analogy or comparison, and testimony; the idea that testimony only counts when the speaker is actually competent and the sentence is intelligible in the first place (I've seen the conditions given as something like expectation, compatibility and contiguity — don't hold me to the wording); a five-step way of laying out an inference — state the claim, give the reason, cite an example, apply it, draw the conclusion; and a checklist of the ways a reason goes wrong, which I've seen given as five but with variants — the reason that's equally compatible with the opposite conclusion, the one that actually proves the opposite, the one that quietly assumes what you're trying to show, the one that needs as much proof as the claim itself, the one that gets overridden by something you can see for yourself, and one that is literally just the evidence arrived too late. That last one is my entire job some weeks.

What I want is something I can actually run: a one-pager, in order, that takes a complaint through the four means of knowing and then puts whatever inference I've built to the fault list, with each step saying what counts as enough to move on, where it stops with no case to answer, and where it hands the decision back to me as a judgement call rather than pretending the method made it. Write it for someone who's never heard of Nyāya and will be irritated if I make them learn Sanskrit — keep the distinctions that earn their place, drop the ones that don't, and tell me honestly where the analogy is doing too much work (I'm already suspicious about treating a case file as testimony and an earlier decision as analogy). Then run it on three complaints: a tenant who says a repair visit never happened while the council's log says it did; a resident who says she was never told about a planning decision when the file shows a letter sent to her old address; and the one where I'm fairly sure the method is going to make me write not upheld even though I'd bet money the complainant is telling the truth — because that's the case I need to see before I put this in front of the team on Monday. No reading list or citations, please, I won't follow them up; just tell me where I've mangled the ideas.
