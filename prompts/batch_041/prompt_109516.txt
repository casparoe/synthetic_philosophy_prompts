I teach a freshman seminar on punishment and I'm closing the term with Kesey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest plus the movie with Nicholson, and I've hit something I can't untangle on my own. The book is obviously set in a psychiatric hospital, not a prison, and yet every student reads Ratched's ward as a prison and McMurphy as an inmate, and honestly I've stopped resisting because the parallel seems to do real work. The whole place runs on a therapeutic justification for total control, McMurphy's treatment is punishment in a lab coat, and the lobotomy at the end looks like the logical terminus of a regime that wants to fix you rather than merely hold you. So read the novel through the punishment versus rehabilitation debate and tell me what it actually shows, not what the cliche says it shows.

My working hunch is that Kesey isn't attacking rehabilitation as such but attacking what happens when one person gets discretionary power over when you're released and that power gets dressed up as care. Which, as I understand it, is roughly why the rehabilitative ideal got savaged from the left in the seventies, indeterminate sentences and parole boards passing judgment on your soul, and why the fix was determinate sentencing, which then helped open the door to mass incarceration. A cure worse than the disease. Is the book a document of that critique, or is it doing something simpler and older, Romantic individualism versus the machine? Push back if I'm wrong.

Here's the part I really want you to take seriously. I want to set the ward against the Norwegian model, Halden and the rest. There the official line is the normality principle, the sentence is the loss of liberty and nothing more, and the comfort of the place is usually defended by something like the principle of less eligibility, the idea that prisoners should never be living better than the worst off people on the outside. That's a strange mixture, half humanitarian and half don't-you-dare-treat-them-better-than-the-honest-poor. Does Kesey's ward make the Norwegian defense look naive, on the grounds that any institution with that much power over people will reproduce domination no matter what it says its aim is? Or does the comparison actually cut the other way, showing that Ratched's problem isn't rehabilitation at all but the absence of any real constraint on her discretion, which is a design flaw and not an argument against the ideal?

I don't want Kesey reduced to a civics lesson and I don't want the penology bent to fit the novel, so be honest about where the book resists this reading. Point me to the scenes that would do the most work (the fog, the tub room, the fishing trip, whatever you think is load-bearing) and end by saying what the novel adds to this argument that the criminology papers don't. Also tell me whether the hospital/ prison distinction, which my students keep collapsing, is a distraction or is actually the sharpest thing about the book.
