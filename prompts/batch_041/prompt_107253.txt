I am a high school student and the student representative on my school district's Historical Commemoration Review Committee. The committee meets monthly and receives proposals to rename buildings, remove plaques, retire mascots, or add contextual labels. Each proposal gets about forty-five minutes, and our recommendation goes to the principal and the school board. We have no professional historian on the committee, and we cannot conduct deep archival research; we work from a short briefing packet, public comment, and whatever we already know. The decisions are consequential: they affect how students, alumni, and local community members see the school, and they often attract local news coverage.

Our recurring problem is the man-of-his-time defense. When a proposal targets a figure such as Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus, someone always says that we cannot judge them by our standards, that everyone then held those views, that they were products of their era. I have read enough philosophy to suspect that this defense is sometimes a legitimate excuse and sometimes a rationalization, and that it is doing several different jobs at once. I want a decision procedure my committee can actually run, not an essay about historiography.

Please write that procedure. It should be a reusable checklist or flowchart in words, with questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to retain, remove, or contextualize, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Build in the distinctions that matter: between an excuse and a justification; between the epistemic condition of blame, meaning what the figure knew or could reasonably have known, and the control condition, meaning what they could have done at acceptable cost; between the descriptive claim that a view was widely held and the normative claim that it was permissible; between judging the person and judging the act; between the figure's own life and the later institutional use of their name; and between honoring a person and teaching about them. Include a test for whether there was a live, morally serious contemporary critique of the conduct, and a test for whether the commemoration currently functions as an endorsement of the wrong rather than a recognition of a specific contribution. The procedure should not let a successful excuse for the person automatically settle whether a public honor should continue.

Then run the procedure on three cases: Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus. For each, give the answers the procedure would elicit, the recommendation it produces, and the points where the committee would have to exercise judgment. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like; I am inclined to keep Jefferson because of the Declaration of Independence, but I want the procedure to follow its own logic rather than my preference. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do, and where it is most likely to misfire when used by a committee of high school students and community members who are not specialists.

No citations, reading lists, or bibliography are needed; I want the procedure itself, with the hard cases and the value judgments marked as such.
