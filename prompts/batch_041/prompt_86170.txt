Essay: Assertion as Burden Transfer

The debate about the norm of assertion has settled into a three-way contest. On the knowledge account, one may assert that p only if one knows that p. Its principal attraction is that it explains why it is improper to assert what one merely believes, or what one has strong evidence for but does not know, and why a challenge to an assertion is, in the first instance, a request for the speaker's grounds. On the truth account, one may assert that p only if p is true. Its principal attraction is that it explains the impropriety of false assertions without importing the demanding notion of knowledge, and it handles the case in which a speaker with excellent evidence asserts a falsehood and thereby does something criticizable. On the belief account, one may assert that p only if one believes that p, with some versions adding a justification condition. Its principal attraction is that it treats insincerity as the fundamental fault and locates the norm in the speaker's state rather than in the world.

I want to argue that the contest is malformed. All three accounts treat the norm as a threshold condition, either on the speaker's epistemic state or on the proposition asserted. I claim that assertion is better understood as an act whose primary normative significance is the transfer of an epistemic burden, and that once we see this, the apparent competition among the three accounts dissolves into a division of explanatory labour.

When I assert that p, I do two things at once. I invite my audience to treat p as settled for the purposes at hand, and I take on a defeasible liability. That liability has two strands, which I want to keep apart: a discursive liability to answer challenges and supply grounds, and a practical liability to bear some responsibility for the consequences if p proves false and my audience relied on it. The norm of assertion, on this view, is not a condition the speaker must satisfy before speaking but a condition on the distribution of liability that the act creates. An assertion is proper when the discursive liability it generates is one the speaker can bear and the audience can reasonably be asked to accept; the practical strand bears on whether asserting is wise, not on whether it is licensed.

The three standard accounts then reappear as partial descriptions of what it takes to bear the burden well. Knowledge is the best available guarantee that the burden will not come due, which is why the knowledge account captures so much of the data. Truth matters because a false assertion is precisely the case in which the burden comes due. Belief matters because sincere assertion is the paradigm of a speaker who accepts the burden rather than merely pretending to. None of the three is the norm; each identifies a feature that bears on whether the burden can be borne.

Consider deferred assertion. A climate scientist announces that a particular glacier will have disappeared by 2050. She cannot know this, since the future is open. On the knowledge account her assertion is improper, which seems too harsh. On the truth account it is proper if the prediction is true, which seems too lenient, since she is answerable now for a claim whose truth cannot now be checked. On the burden-transfer account her assertion is proper just in case she accepts the standing discursive liability to defend the inference, to revise in light of new data, and to be answerable later. That, I think, is what we actually demand of her.

The obvious objection is that this makes the norm of assertion consequentialist or instrumental, and that the knowledge intuition is not about liability but about warrant: an assertion represents its content as known, and the fault is misrepresentation, not unmet liability. I have a reply, but I would rather hear the objection put as strongly as possible before I give it. I would also like to know whether the account, once the two strands of liability are separated, still has anything distinctive left to say that the truth account cannot say more simply.
