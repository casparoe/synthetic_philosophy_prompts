Ok so I teach a 2nd-year undergrad seminar on practical reason, about 18 students, and I've got one 80-minute session I want to blow on the spectrum arguements — the ones that seem to show 'better than' can't be transitive, where you inch from something clearly great to something clearly awful and every single step looks like an improvement. The Parfit stuff, you know the drill. My worry is they've half-heard it, or they nod along and it slides right off, and I don't want to just lecture the sorites analogy at them.

What I want is a game, not a discussion — something where they build the chain themselves and only realise where they've ended up at the end, and then actually feel the cost of it. My half-formed idea is to run it like a little trading floor. Sketch: everyone starts holding a 'world' at one end of a spectrum, and there's a broker (me) who offers to swap your world for the next one along, for a small fee, and every swap is one you'd obviously take because you yourself judge the next world better than the current one. You walk all the way down the chain, paying a fee at each step. Then the broker offers to swap you back to where you started — also for a fee, and also a swap you'd take, because you yourself think the world you started with is better than the world you're sitting in now. And round you go.

That's the shape. Now make it actually good. I need:

- A concrete spectrum that isn't one of the tired ones — vivid and a bit unexpected so they don't just recognise it. My instinct is a friendship / attention one: start with one deep friendship, and each step you add one more casual acquaintance and spread yourself a touch thinner, until you're at 10,000 acquaintances and nobody you're actually close to. Each step reads as 'more friends, barely any less deep, sure, better.' Endpoint: obviously worse than the one deep friendship. But pick or adapt whatever spectrum will really bite.
- Rules, materials (no real money — tokens, chits, whatever), the payoff structure, and how many rounds.
- A timeline for the 80 minutes with 18 people.
- The debrief: map each moment of the game onto the actual structure of the argument — the monotone chain, the endpoint verdict, the resulting cycle, and why intransitivity is supposed to be a problem (the pump, the getting bled dry). They should only hear the names and terms afterwards.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time and how I should handle it.
- And be straight with me about what the game can't reproduce. I suspect it makes intransitivity feel like an obvious defect to be fixed, when part of the point of the spectrum arguments is that maybe it's the value relation itself that's intransitive and the thing to give up is one of the 'obvious' steps — I doubt the game can give them the pull of that option. Tell me which feature I'll have to supply myself in the debrief, and where the game might accidentally over-teach.

What I want them walking out with is: a cycle they generated themselves, and a felt sense that you can't keep all of it and stay unexploitable — without me having told them any of that up front. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I've got that covered.
