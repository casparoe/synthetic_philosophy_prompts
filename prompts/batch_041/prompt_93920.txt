ok so this has been bugging me for a while and I want a diagnosis, not a survey. I keep reading around the uniqueness/comparability fight about the Holocaust and it never resolves. Roughly: on one side the uniqueness camp — the thing is singular, no other genocide is like it, it's a caesura in European history, and (this is the part I stumble on) the singularity is supposed to do moral work, it's supposed to ground a special obligation to remember, a special German burden. On the other side the comparativists: Nolte in the eighties arguing that there was no moral difference between Soviet and Nazi mass murder and that the Nazis acted out of fear of what the Soviets would do to Germany, plus the whole later wave who compare it to the Armenian genocide, to slavery, to colonial atrocity — not to diminish it, but because refusing to generalise seems to them an historiographical dead end, you can't explain anything if every case is sui generis. And then there's a parallel fight about modernity that I think is the same fight wearing a hat: Bauman saying the Holocaust wasn't a regression to pre-modern barbarism at all but a product of modernity — bureaucratic rationality, instrumental reason, the division of labour into little tasks, the taxonomic sorting of populations, obedience to rules experienced as morally good — versus the people who say no, that gets you the machinery but not the targets or the zeal, you need the ideology and the antisemitism, and modernity was betrayed rather than expressed.

Both of these have been running for decades and nobody moves an inch. So what kind of disagreement is it, actually? My hypothesis — and I'd rather you test it than humour me — is that it's not historical at all. 'Unique' is true of any event under a fine enough description and false under a coarse enough one, so the real dispute must be about which descriptions matter and for what, and meanwhile the two sides keep refuting each other's comparisons in the wrong currency: one side's comparison is causal (what made this possible), the other's is about magnitude (how much suffering, how many dead), a third is about meaning (what it signifies), and nobody says which they're trading in. And if the load-bearing part is really the normative one — the memory politics, the special obligations — then the historical thesis may never have been doing the work people think it does, which would explain why the fight keeps restarting whenever someone threatens to normalise the past. Tell me where that's wrong. And whatever the diagnosis, say what would count as progress here: a proper typology of comparisons (in what respect, for what purpose, against which comparison class), a split of the thing into two questions with different truth conditions, some formal point about uniqueness predicates, or nothing at all — and if nothing, say why no amount of good history or good philosophy will settle it.
