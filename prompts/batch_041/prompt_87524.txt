I teach an upper-level seminar on liberalism and empire, and I am drafting the final examination. I want a single essay question on John Stuart Mill's views on India and colonial rule — one that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar claim that Mill was a liberal imperialist who quietly exempted peoples he deemed uncivilised from the harm principle of On Liberty (1859).

The question should require students to distinguish, and then adjudicate among, three ways of reconciling Mill's anti-paternalism at home with his defence of British rule in India. On the scope-restriction reading, the harm principle is framed from the outset as governing only members of a civilised community, so colonial coercion falls outside its reach and there is no inconsistency to explain. On the tutelage reading, despotism is justified instrumentally, as a school meant to make itself unnecessary by educating a people into the capacity for self-government. On the non-ideal-theory reading, Mill's practical work on Indian administration was conducted under conditions in which the operative question was not the legitimacy of coercion within a self-governing community but the competence and good faith of an alien administration that could not be expected to meet the harm principle's conditions.

Students should also be pressed on two points that summaries of the debate usually skip. First, is the civilised-community qualifier a conceptual restriction — only communities of a certain kind can be self-governing, so the principle simply does not apply — or a prudential one, on which the principle applies but cannot yet be enforced without harm? Second, does the tutelage argument have a determinable terminus, or does it risk becoming unfalsifiable? If the criterion of maturity is set by the tutor, what independent check constrains the tutor's judgment, and what follows if the tutor has an interest in the school's continuing?

To give the question real bite, add a third demand: students should consider whether the harm principle, being a principle about the legitimate exercise of power over the individual within a community, is even the right frame for assessing relations between communities. A student who concludes simply that Mill was inconsistent, or simply that he was consistent, should not be able to reach full marks; the question should reward a student who can say what would count as consistency here, and why the framing of the question may itself be doing work.

Please provide (a) the essay question as it would appear on the examination paper, (b) a model answer of roughly 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around five must-hit points, giving the marks for each and a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs candidates marks on that point. End the marking scheme with one or two sentences on what distinguishes a first-class answer from a solid second-class one. Candidates have 90 minutes and no texts; the model answer should be self-contained and should not depend on any particular passage being quoted. Do not ask for citations or a reading list.
