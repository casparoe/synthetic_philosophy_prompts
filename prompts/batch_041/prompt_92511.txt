I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized social platform, and through a chain of reorganizations I've ended up on the small internal group that drafts our community guidelines, which has turned into a year-long argument I can't stop having. My degree is in computer science and I have no formal philosophy training; everything I know about this subject I've picked up from arguments, a few long essays, and a colleague who used to work in trust and safety at a much larger company and treats one idea as decisive — that a democracy doesn't have to let its enemies use its own freedoms to destroy it, so a state may suppress movements that would use free speech to dismantle the arrangement that protects it. I find that too neat, and I find that I don't hold either of the standard positions cleanly: not the American one, which I absorbed growing up here and which says the state essentially cannot punish speech no matter how vile, and not the European one, which seems to hold that some speech is intolerable because of what it does to the people it is aimed at. I'd like to know what view I actually do hold, so below are nine cases with my honest gut reactions.

(1) A man in a public square delivers a long, sneering speech about a racial minority, telling the crowd they are a plague and should go back where they came from; no individual is threatened. My reaction: legal, the state should do nothing, though I would cross the street. (2) The same man, same speech, but he names a specific family standing in the crowd and says they should be run out of town. My reaction: illegal — that is a threat, not an opinion. (3) A publisher brings out a slick, footnoted book arguing that a historical atrocity never happened, aimed at persuading ordinary readers. My reaction: legal here, and I feel no pull toward prosecution, but if I lived in a country that had lived through that atrocity I think I would want it banned, and I cannot tell whether that is a principled difference or just squeamishness. (4) A podcast recites only true crime statistics about a particular ethnic group, in a tone of pure contempt, week after week. My reaction: I want it gone more than I want the denial book gone, even though everything it says is arguably true — and that bothers me, because I thought my objection was to falsehood. (5) A comedian's routine mocking a religion, funny and cruel in equal measure, performed in a club people choose to enter. My reaction: legal, and prosecuting it would be absurd, even though I think the routine makes life worse for the people it is about. (6) A teacher tells her class that one group of students is naturally inferior. My reaction: she should be fired and never teach again, but not prosecuted. (7) A stranger calls someone a slur to their face, once, on the street, with no threat attached. My reaction: this should be a small crime — and I cannot square that with my answer to case 1 except by saying the person is trapped and cannot leave, which is equally true of the crowd in case 1. (8) A country bans a party whose platform calls for expelling a minority, and enforces the ban by shutting down its rallies. My reaction: I am for it, which troubles me, because I am against banning the book in case 3 and both look like a state suppressing a viewpoint it despises. (9) My own platform removes the speech in case 1 under a hate speech policy, while the law leaves it alone. My reaction: the removal is fine, even good, and I don't think of it as censorship in the sense I care about — but I cannot say what the difference is between the state doing it and us doing it, other than scale.

I tried to write a principle myself and it keeps failing. The best I have is: restrict speech only when it denies someone's standing as an equal member of the political community, rather than merely offending them or expressing contempt for their beliefs. That seems to get 1, 2, 5, 6, and maybe 8, but it does something wrong with 4, which denies standing and is true, and with 7, which I want criminal even though a one-off insult is not a denial of standing. A friend who reads a lot of this says the real distinction is between speech that harms by offending and speech that harms by silencing — by driving its targets out of public life — and that only the second is regulable; another friend says it is entirely about whether the audience is captive; a third says it is all about who is doing the restricting, state or platform, and that I am confused because I keep switching between the two; and my trust-and-safety colleague proposes that a slur or a sneer is closer to a slap than to a statement, so it should be regulated as conduct rather than as opinion. What I want from you is the principle, or the small family of principles, that actually fits the pattern, stated precisely enough that I could implement it as a policy and know what it does at the edges; for each clause, the case that forces it; an honest statement of which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should give that verdict up; and its verdicts on four cases I have not listed — (a) a campaign ad that is accurate about a group's unemployment rate and ends with "and you know what to do about it"; (b) a trans employee's colleagues repeatedly and pointedly using the wrong pronouns at work; (c) a newspaper column arguing, politely and with citations, that a religious minority should be barred from immigrating; and (d) a platform's automated system that suppresses a minority-rights hashtag because that hashtag is disproportionately used in abusive replies. Please do not give me a reading list or cite court cases or statutes — I cannot look them up and I will not remember them — just the principle, the reasoning, and where it leaves my reactions.
