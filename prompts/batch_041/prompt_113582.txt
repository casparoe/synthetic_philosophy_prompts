For the final meeting of a graduate seminar on historical explanation, I want to reopen an argument that ended badly last term. A colleague closed a discussion of the Industrial Revolution with the remark that it was only a matter of time: given the technical and demographic preconditions, something of the kind was bound to happen, and the question of what would have happened had it begun in France, or half a century later, is idle. Pressed, he added that such a counterfactual is not even well posed, since the causes in question were structural and mutually supporting, so removing any single one leaves the outcome unchanged, and there is no fact of the matter about the alternative. A student then objected, in what sounded like the opposite spirit, that no one can rerun the tape, and the session dissolved.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct precise claims that it was only a matter of time could be making here — modal, probabilistic, causal-structural, explanatory, epistemic, and narrative readings, and any others the idiom admits — ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the radical. For each, say what it asserts; whether it is a claim about the past itself, about our evidence concerning it, or about how historians should write; whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or unfalsifiable; and, crucially, whether it would actually license treating the counterfactual question as idle.

Two further matters I want worked out. First, the idiom appears to fix the occurrence while leaving only the timing open: say whether that split is coherent, and what would have to be true for an occurrence to be robust while its timing is free. Second, say whether my colleague's auxiliary argument about structural causes establishes idleness or only some weaker conclusion, and whether inevitable in kind but not in instance, or inevitable somewhere but not there, is a stable position or a way of having it both ways.

Close by telling me which reading I should attribute to him if I want to be fair to him, and whether the student's objection supports his conclusion or undermines it. Lay the whole thing out as a numbered list I can put on the board, each entry ending in a one-line verdict.
