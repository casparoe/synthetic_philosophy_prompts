I am midway through a novel whose protagonist is a Kantian moral philosopher — rigorously consistent, emotionally glacial, and, I have begun to fear, in possession of an argument that will not survive the scrutiny of the reader I most want. In the scene I am drafting she stands before a hostile seminar and claims to have settled the question of how we may treat animals. I would like your help before I commit it to the page, because if her argument is sound then my plot is wrong; and if it is not sound, I need to know precisely which step gives way and what it costs her to surrender it.

Her argument runs as follows.

(1) A being can be the object of a direct moral obligation only if it can also be bound by one — only, that is, if it is a rational agent, capable of recognizing the moral law and acting from it.

(2) No non-human animal is a rational agent in that sense.

(3) Therefore no non-human animal can be the object of a direct moral obligation. Strictly speaking, we owe nothing to a dog or a horse.

(4) It is nevertheless genuinely wrong to be cruel to an animal — wrong, not merely imprudent or distasteful.

(5) Since the wrongness cannot reside in any claim the animal has upon us, it must reside in the effect of the act upon the agent. Cruelty coarsens one's feeling for suffering, and a man whose sympathy has been blunted is the more likely to fail in his duties toward human beings.

(6) The duty to refrain from cruelty to animals is therefore indirect — owed not to the animal but to ourselves, or to humanity.

So far, so Kantian; and I am content to let her have (1) through (6). My trouble begins with what follows from them.

(7) If (5) is the whole truth, then the wrongness of any particular cruelty is contingent on its actually doing the coarsening. The man who drowns kittens but weeps at the opera, his sympathies quite intact, has done nothing wrong — which I find intolerable, and which she would too.

(8) And the reasoning licenses an expansion she plainly does not want: whatever tends to corrupt the agent's moral feeling is forbidden on the same ground, so that desecrating a corpse, or wantonly destroying a beautiful thing, or (should the psychology ever run that way) mistreating a machine, becomes wrong in just the way that cruelty to a dog is wrong.

(9) Worse still, the reasoning of (1) through (3) applies with equal force to human beings who are not rational agents — the newborn, the profoundly demented, the irreversibly comatose — and would strip them of direct moral standing. Yet she, and Kant, plainly intend to hold that we owe such people everything.

The argument thus appears to prove too much in two directions at once: it lets cruelty off the hook whenever the agent's psychology happens to be spared, and it either inflates the class of things we may not injure — corpses, artworks, machines — or collapses the class of persons we must not injure, the infants and the demented, down to the class of rational agents.

Tell me which premise or step to give up. I would prefer to keep (1) and (4), since her austere charm depends on the first and my own conscience on the second; but if one of them must go, I want to know what it costs her to abandon it. And I should be glad to be told whether there is a reading of Kant's own texts on which she could keep both — so that the scene, and the novel, can stand.
