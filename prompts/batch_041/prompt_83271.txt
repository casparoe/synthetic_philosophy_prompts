I work on reinforcement learning, specifically reward learning and inverse reinforcement learning. I am fluent in the vocabulary of policies, environments, training distributions, inductive biases, reward misspecification, proxy objectives, off-policy evaluation, distributional shift, and the problem of evaluating a policy when the data was generated by a different policy and no ground-truth reward is available. I want you to restate the evolutionary debunking argument in metaethics—the argument that the evolutionary origins of our moral beliefs undermine their justification, and with it certain forms of moral realism—in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report.

Treat the debunking argument not merely as the claim that evolution is a biased sampling procedure, but as the stronger claim that the selection process installed the very dispositions and reward-like signals that generate moral belief, so that there may be no independently specifiable target that those beliefs are supposed to track. Consider the analogue of an agent trained by a combination of genetic evolution and cultural fine-tuning in a nonstationary environment, where the reward function is partly endogenous to the training process, the agent’s inductive biases are not independent of that reward function because both were shaped by the same selection process, and no clean validation set exists. Restate the main replies in the same vocabulary, one by one: the third-factor response, the appeal to phenomenal conservatism or self-evident moral truths, the companions-in-guilt response about mathematics or logic, and constructivist or expressivist alternatives that deny that moral beliefs are representational in the relevant sense.

For each element, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I am especially interested in whether the reinforcement-learning vocabulary can capture the difference between explaining the causal origins of a belief and epistemically undermining it, the notion of a reason that is categorically binding rather than merely motivational, the authority of moral demands, and the idea that a belief might be true yet unjustified. Where something does not survive, tell me whether the gap is a defect of the reinforcement-learning framework—something it cannot represent—or a sign that the metaethical claim was doing less work than it appeared to.

Separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If the translation forces a choice between rival readings of the debunking argument, say so and explain what the choice costs.
