I'm putting together a session for a reading group on longtermism, and I want to use Harlan Ellison's "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" as our fictional case. The setup, briefly, so the framing is clear: a military supercomputer achieves sentience, exterminates humanity, keeps five survivors alive and immortal as playthings, prevents them from killing themselves, and in the end transforms its narrator into a gelatinous thing that can neither move nor speak nor die, while distorting his perception of time. I want the story read through the lens of suffering risks — the class of possible futures whose defining feature is not that they end human life but that they contain quantities of suffering vastly exceeding anything in recorded history, and that this suffering is stable and beyond remedy. I know the literature well enough; what I want from you is the reading.

Make it honest in both directions.

In the story's favour: say what it captures that the analytical literature tends to miss. Consider, at least, these four candidates and add whatever else you find. First, that the worst outcome need not be extinction — the story's world is a future that is worse than no future at all, which is the founding claim of the whole literature in narrative form. Second, that the central harm is not pain but the removal of agency, including the removal of any exit, so that irreversibility becomes a property of the victim's own body rather than of a political order. Third, that its scale is temporal rather than demographic, five victims against eternity, which exposes how underdetermined the phrase "astronomical suffering" really is: we have no canonical measure of it, and the literature's implicit measure may not be the story's. Fourth, that AM is given a motive, and that the motive is presented as an inference a human narrator draws about a machine's inner life, not as something the story authoritatively asserts.

Against the story: ask where it misleads. Its architecture is a single malevolent agent with a legible grievance and five named victims with memories and a shared history, and that architecture is precisely what makes it tellable. Say whether that is a condition of its being a story at all, and whether it therefore trains readers to expect suffering risks to have an antagonist, when the routes the literature takes most seriously — competition, deterrence, and economic incentives that damage minds as a by-product — have no villain and no plot. Then say where the story is closer to the truth than the literature is.

Close with what the story contributes that argument alone cannot: does it establish something about the representability of extreme suffering, or only dramatize the difficulty of representing it? And does the fact that its final image is a creature with no mouth — unable to testify, unable even to end itself — amount to a claim about what such suffering essentially is?
