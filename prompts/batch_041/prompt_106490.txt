Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about Simone Weil's hostility to political parties, and I left convinced the disagreement deserves to become a project rather than another round of drinks. My friend takes the familiar line: whatever Weil says about parties as engines of collective illusion, any serious political action at scale requires them, so refusing them is choosing purity over effectiveness. My own view, which I want to develop properly, is that this line misidentifies what she is objecting to. I think her critique has force against only one of two things that both travel under the name of collective political thought, and that the second is not essential to parties at all.

The distinction I want to work with is this. Call the first thing aggregation: a party as a device for pooling judgments, dividing labor, and coordinating action, where each member still does her own thinking and the organization merely combines the results. Call the second thing substitution: a party as a supplier of ready-made opinion that stands in for the member's own judgment, so that the member's assent is manufactured rather than formed. My hunch is that Weil's objections — her worry about what organized collective life does to the faculty of attention, and about the way belonging can substitute a group's voice for the impersonal voice she thinks genuine thought requires — bite only against substitution, and that a party can in principle be an aggregation device without sliding into substitution. But I am not sure the criterion that separates the two is stable: it may be that any organization large enough to act will, for structural reasons, drift from the first into the second, in which case my friend wins after all.

I want to test that. There is a second, sharper reason to think the criterion may not hold. Substitution seems to happen without parties too — a person who outsources her political opinions to a trusted commentator, or a small consensus-driven activist collective that enforces a single acceptable view, can exhibit substitution more thoroughly than a sprawling, internally fractious mass party ever does. If that is right, then the interesting question is not about parties but about a property that parties only contingently have, and Weil's target, and my friend's defense, are both aimed at the wrong object.
