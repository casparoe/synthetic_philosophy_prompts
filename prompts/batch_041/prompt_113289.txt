everyone in my reading group keeps leaning on the same picture for self-deception and I want to know how far it actually holds. The picture: the reasoning part of you is basically a lawyer, and the rest of you is the client. The client has an interest in a particular verdict -- I'm not drinking too much, my partner is the unreasonable one, the project died because of the market -- so they retain the best advocate available, and the advocate goes out and builds the strongest case for that verdict out of whatever material is lying around: a cherry-picked study, a flattering comparison, a technicality. Nobody has to be lying to anyone. No mysterious homunculus hiding a truth in a locked drawer, just a professional doing their job for a paying client. My friend uses this to say the old paradox of self-deception is a fake problem, and also that we should stop moralizing about self-deception, since a lawyer defending a guilty client isnt doing anything wrong.

So: how far does it hold? Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the client, the lawyer, the case, the evidence, the jury, the opposing counsel -- then the places the mapping breaks, sorted into the ones that matter for my friend's use and the ones that dont. The spot I keep getting stuck on is who knows what. A real lawyer usually knows the truth about the case and argues anyway, and so does the client, so either both parties know (nothing is hidden from anyone, which makes this insincere advocacy rather than self-deception) or neither does (and then where is the deception?). Work out whether the analogy quietly presupposes the very thing it was supposed to explain, and which of its two possible readings it needs in order to do the work my friend wants -- two real sub-systems that hold different information, or one person playing two roles. Then run it on two hard cases: (a) the lawyer who argues the case for years and ends up believing the client is innocent -- is that still the same picture, or has it turned into something else? (b) someone who has been told the truth by people who love them, repeatedly, and still sincerely denies it -- my friend's picture seems to say they must be insincere, which is exactly the answer he says we should stop giving. Finally: does the anti-moralizing conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if theres a better picture, what is it and what does it fix -- someone in the group suggested a spokesperson who invents a rationale without ever having been in the room where the decision got made, and I want to know whether thats definately an improvement or just the same problem in a new coat.
