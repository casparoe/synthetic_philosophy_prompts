I'm building a case study for a grad seminar on markets and political legitimacy, and I want to use the DARPA Policy Analysis Market — PAM, the FutureMAP thing that blew up in July 2003. You probably half-remember it as the terrorism futures market that Congress killed, and thats fine, but I want you working from these facts rather than guessing at them, so here they are. It was a DARPA project run out of the Information Awareness Office (Poindexter's shop) to build a real-money futures exchange on the future of the Middle East. It was going to cover eight countries, with contracts on each country's military activity, political instability, economic growth, US military activity and US financial involvement, plus US aggregates like GDP, world trade, US military casualties and western terrorist casualties. The plan was to let traders bet on combinations of those, including conditional stuff — what happens to stability in one country if US troops leave Saudi Arabia, what that does to its neighbours, what that does to oil. A sample screen on the project's website listed the assassination of Arafat and a North Korean missile attack as miscellaneous items. On July 28, 2003, two senators held a press conference denouncing the whole thing as a federal betting parlor on atrocities and terrorism; the Pentagon cancelled it within a day; and note that the Senate had already voted to cut the office's funding over a different project (Total Information Awareness, the privacy one), so the money was shaky before the scandal even hit. Also worth knowing: the team had been running lab experiments on price manipulation from the start, and their first combinatorial simulation with a dozen student traders over a few days produced about a dozen trades total, which they called a serious failure.

Now the theory to run it through: Hanson's futarchy, the proposal that we vote on our values and bet on our beliefs. Take the machinery as three parts. (1) An ex ante welfare metric W, fixed by vote, that everyone accepts as the thing to maximize. (2) For any proposed policy P, conditional markets that price W-under-P against W-under-not-P. (3) A decision rule: adopt P just in case the market says it raises W. Thats the whole thing.

What I want from you:

The mapping, done honestly. Which parts of that machinery did PAM actually instantiate? My suspicion, which you should test rather than assume, is that PAM was a forecasting market with no decision rule bolted to it — it was meant to inform policy, not select it — so calling it a futarchy is generous. If thats right, say exactly what would have had to be added to make it one.

The counterfactual problem, which I take to be the sharpest objection and which I want worked through properly rather than gestured at. In a real futarchy only one branch of the conditional ever gets settled, so the W-under-not-P leg has to be settled on something other than an outcome — a forecast, or an explicit causal model, or a comparison across jurisdictions. What does that do to the mechanism? Does it just relocate the expert judgment it was supposed to replace?

The sabotage problem in its sharper three-way form: price manipulation (cheap to describe, expensive to do, self-limiting), metric manipulation (you hold the losing side, so if P gets adopted you profit by making W worse), and agenda manipulation (you control which policies ever come up for a vote). Futarchy has an answer to the first. Say whether it has one to the second and third, and whether PAM's history bears on any of them.

The reverse direction, which is the part I actually care about. What does the PAM episode show about futarchy that futarchy cant show about itself? In particular: the objection that killed it was that some things shouldn't be traded at all, regardless of what the market would predict. Is that an objection futarchy can even hear, given that its values-half is just a vote over the metric? And notice what the vote actually did here — it happened, through Congress, inside an appropriations fight bundled with an unrelated privacy scandal. Vindication of the values-half, reductio of it, or something futarchy never modelled?

Finish with the negative space: what futarchy is silent about, and whether PAM is a genuine anomaly for it or just a case it was never built to handle.

No lit review, no citations, I know the literature well enough. Just dont smooth over the places where the mapping strains.
