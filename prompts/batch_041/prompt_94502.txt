I'm designing the final essay question for a graduate seminar I teach on conditionals. I want one question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard triviality result, so I am building it around a repair to Stalnaker's Thesis that I have been developing in my seminar notes. Here is the setup I want the question to use; treat it as fixed content, not as something to research.

Background the students will have: Lewis's triviality argument purports to show that no indicative conditional can both (i) be a proposition with a probability, and (ii) satisfy the equation P(A→B) = P(B|A) for every probability function P. The argument runs, roughly, by applying the equation to the conditionalized distribution P(·|B) and to P(·|B̄), which forces P(A→B) = P(B), and then equating that with P(B|A) to yield the absurd conclusion that A and B are probabilistically independent for arbitrary A and B. Students will also have seen the usual escape routes: deny that conditionals express propositions (expressivism), restrict the equation to a privileged probability function rather than all of them, deny closure of the equation under conditionalization, or restrict the class of sentences to which the equation applies.

The repair I want them to assess is what I have been calling the Simple-Conditional Restriction. Its two clauses are: (1) the equation P(A→B) = P(B|A) is asserted only for conditionals A→B in which neither A nor B contains a further conditional; and (2) the truth value of a conditional need not be a function of the truth values of its parts, so the semantics may be a selection-function semantics on which A→B is true at a world just in case B holds at the nearest A-world.

The question should ask students to determine, by tracing the triviality proof step by step, whether the Simple-Conditional Restriction blocks the argument, and if not, to say exactly which assumption of the proof it fails to touch and which additional commitment a proponent would have to abandon instead. It should also require them to sort the four escape routes above by whether each one targets an assumption the proof actually uses, and to explain why at least one popular-sounding route is a red herring because the proof never relied on the assumption it denies. The question should be answerable in ninety minutes by a student who understands the proof but has not memorized the secondary literature, and it should not require naming or citing any particular paper, author, or date.

Deliverables, in this order:

First, the essay question itself, phrased as it would appear on the exam, including the setup above in whatever compressed form keeps it readable.

Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words. It must walk the triviality proof far enough to show which premises each step consumes, give a definite verdict on the Simple-Conditional Restriction, name the assumption that must be surrendered if the restriction fails, and classify all four escape routes. Where a step of the proof is load-bearing, say so explicitly rather than gesturing at the literature.

Third, a marking scheme as a table with exactly these four columns: Criterion; Marks (out of twenty, with the marks summing to twenty); What an A-range answer must do; Common error that should cost marks. The criteria should be the discrete moves a strong answer has to make, not general virtues, and each common-error entry should name a specific identifiable mistake rather than a vague weakness.

Fourth, a second table, the Escape-Route Audit, with exactly these three columns: Escape route; Does it target a premise the proof uses? (Yes/No, with the premise named); Consequence for the triviality result. Include a row for each of the four routes plus a row for the Simple-Conditional Restriction, so the two deliverables agree with each other.

Fifth, a short note of no more than one hundred and fifty words, addressed to me as the examiner, flagging which single feature of the question is doing the real discriminating work between an A and a B answer, and stating honestly whether the question as posed has a unique defensible answer or leaves a genuine interpretive choice open.
