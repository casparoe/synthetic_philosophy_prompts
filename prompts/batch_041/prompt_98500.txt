I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on the arrow of time next week, ten minutes, and I'm stuck on how to write them. The room is a nightmare: about half are physicists — stat mech people and a couple of cosmologists — who think the "problem of the arrow" was basically closed decades ago and that anyone still puzzled is just refusing to accept a special initial condition; the other half are philosophers of physics plus a couple of historians who think the physicists are smuggling in that initial condition and calling it physics. And a handful of grad students who just want to know what the actual fight is.

What I dont want is the standard tour — laws are time-symmetric, entropy goes up, therefore the past is special. I've been pushing a particular line in my own work and I want the opening to actually commit to it, so the next two days have something to chew on. The line is this: there isnt one arrow of time, there's a small family of them — the thermodynamic one, the cosmological one, the radiative one, and what I've been calling the record arrow (the bare fact that we have traces of the past and none of the future) — and the real question is which of these are independent and which are downstream of which. My bet, and I want the remarks to say it plainly, is that the record arrow and the thermodynamic arrow come apart, and that the felt fixity of the past versus the openness of the future is not something you can read off the second law. And the usual fix — bolt on a low-entropy initial condition, what people call the past hypothesis, and declare victory — I want to call a promissory note, without just sneering at it.

So write the remarks. They have to do real work: set the puzzle up so both camps recognize it as theirs, state my thesis without pretending the case is closed, and leave everyone productively annoyed for two days. Two constraints. First, no citations and no "as So-and-so showed" — I cant verify anything and you can't either, so if you need to gesture at an idea, just describe the idea. Second, do NOT let the phrase "the second law says entropy must increase" into the remarks as if the second law were a law of motion — that's precisely the move I'm pushing against, and half the room will come for my throat.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered beats, each a couple of sentences, no intro and no wrap-up.
