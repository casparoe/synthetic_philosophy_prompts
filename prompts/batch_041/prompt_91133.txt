I am designing a pre-mortem protocol that a regulator or a research funder could run before adopting a performance target, and I have concluded that I cannot write the protocol until I know what a theory of proxy corruption would have to deliver. Please write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate account of the phenomenon usually filed under Goodhart's law has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a verdict on which requirements are genuine and which are artifacts of a particular camp's interests. Do not give me a reading list or attribute views to authors; I want the argument in your own terms.

Set the list against at least the following cases, which I have been collecting. Several are constructed rather than reported, so treat them as stipulations, and say so if one is incoherent or already presupposes an answer.

1. The straightforward case: a proxy is a stable correlate of the goal while unoptimized, and the correlation breaks once agents are rewarded for moving the proxy, because there are ways to move the proxy that leave the goal untouched.

2. The counting effect: nothing about the underlying phenomenon changes, but making it a target improves its measurement, so the number rises. Here a rising number is equally consistent with corruption and with success, and the account owes some way of telling them apart.

3. The decoy: a metric that everyone, including its designers, regards as a crude stand-in is nonetheless published as a target, is used mainly to coordinate effort, and improves the goal precisely because nobody treats it as a description of the goal. An account that identifies targeting with corruption must explain why this is not a counterexample, or concede that it is.

4. The inverse proxy: a cheap metric negatively correlated with the goal over the relevant range. Targeting it reliably makes things worse, and it is not obvious that this is the same phenomenon rather than ordinary bad measurement.

5. Metric monoculture: a single statistic is adopted across many institutions for many purposes and becomes unusable for all of them, not because any one agent games it, but because the aggregate weight of optimization on it exceeds what its measurement channel can bear. Corruption here is a congestion externality on a shared resource, and no one need intend it.

6. No independent goal: a target that constitutes the goal rather than representing it, so there is nothing for the metric to have been a good proxy of. The account must say whether it applies at all, and what would count as gaming.

Beyond the cases, I want the list to include the requirements usually smuggled in without argument: that the account distinguish deliberate gaming from non-intentional distributional drift; that it distinguish the metric's ceasing to track the goal from the institution's substituting the metric for the goal; that it be predictive rather than diagnostic, able to say before the fact which targets will degrade; that it not be refuted by the many cases in which targeting a proxy works fine; and that it respect the difference between a proxy's error being random and its error being selected by the optimization itself. Add whatever else you think belongs, and cut whatever you think is a prejudice dressed as a requirement.

Two things I most want from you. First, whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, I suspect that demanding prediction from features of the proxy-goal relation alone, while also demanding a principled distinction between corruption and legitimate redefinition of a goal, cannot both be had; if that is right, say which has to go and what is lost. Second, which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and what it excludes.

The purpose fixes the bar: this is a tool for an auditor who must decide, before a target is adopted, whether to expect degradation, and who will not have access to the agents' intentions. Weight the list toward what such a user needs.
