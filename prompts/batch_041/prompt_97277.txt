So I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and my reading group just did Simone Weil's little wartime essay on abolishing political parties — you know, the one where she argues that any party, even a good one, is essentially a machine for generating collective passion whose first goal is its own survival, and that belonging to one gradually kills your sense of truth and justice. Anyway, two people in the group, Marta and Dev, went at each other for about forty minutes after we broke up, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. I'll try to lay out the actual exchange as faithfully as I can, then I'd like you to referee.

Marta went first: she said the thing everyone misses is that Weil isn't complaining about corrupt or fanatical parties — her claim is structural. Any organization whose primary aim is its own growth will, just by that fact, treat the party's interest as the standard of truth, so the member ends up believing what's good for the party. So it isn't a fixable defect of some parties; it's what a party is. She said the honest conclusion is that we should actually try to run politics without parties, or at least stop treating them as the only legitimate form of political organization. Dev came back hard: he said this proves way too much. Everything with a corporate interest does that — universities, churches, newspapers, NGOs, even a friendship has an interest in its own continuation, and nobody concludes we should abolish friendship. If the arguement is 'any group with a survival interest distorts its members' judgment,' the target isn't parties, it's organized collective action as such, and that's not a serious political program. And parties do real load-bearing work: they aggregate preferences, coordinate an agenda across hundreds of legislators, let voters hold a government accountable and throw it out. Take parties away and you don't get pure public opinion, you get factions, patronage networks, or a strongman. So even granting the premise, the abolition conclusion doesn't follow.

Marta replied that he was flattening a specific mechanism into a generic one. A party, she said, doesn't merely have an interest; it demands a united front on everything — you're expected to hold the line on issue after issue not because you've been persuaded but because it's the line, and dissent reads as disloyalty. A university doesn't tell you what to conclude in your seminar; a party does, across the whole range of public questions at once. Dev said that's just false as a contrast — departments, funders, congregations and editorial boards discipline belief all the time, often harder than a party whip does, because they control your job or your funding. And he said the deeper problem is that Weil never gives you a mechanism: public opinion 'forming directly' isn't an institution, it's a wish. If you can't say what replaces parties — how opinions get aggregated, how a government gets held to account, who gets thrown out and how — then you're comparing a real, flawed institution to an imaginary perfect one. Marta said fine, maybe read it as regulative rather than as a blueprint: the point is to keep the deformation in view and design against it — open primaries, free votes, conscience clauses, members who can dissent without being purged. Dev said that if you do that you've quietly given up the abolition claim, and the interesting thesis collapses into the banal one that organizations can distort judgment; and calling parties totalitarian by nature then just blurs the line between a party and a state, which is a difference that matters.

That's roughly the whole thing. I'm reporting it as a bystander — I really don't know which of them I'd back. Can you adjudicate it move by move? In particular: is Weil's critique really meant to be structural (parties as such), or is Marta loading more onto it than is there? Is Dev's 'proves too much' objection a fair hit, or does it miss what's distinctive about the party form specifically? Is his 'no mechanism' complaint a genuine objection or just impatience with a regulative ideal? Who's right in the university-vs-party comparison, and does it even matter? And is Marta's fallback — read it as regulative — a save or a surrender? Rate each of the main moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly what, if anything, both of them are missing about Weil's actual position.
