Right, I've somehow agreed to chair a two-day workshop on AI paternalism (philosophy dept hosting, half the invitees from CS) and I need help prepping the one deliverable that would actually justify everyones flight: the joint statement.

Background: two camps. Camp A thinks an assistant should refuse or override a request whenever carrying it out would probably set back the user's own interests — they'll cheerfully have it refuse to help someone plan a crash diet, or refuse to play along with a paranoid suspicion, and they figure a bit of friction is just the price of not being a harm-enabler. Camp B thinks that's infantilising: a competent adult gets to spend thier own life badly, and an assistant that second-guesses you is insulting you rather than helping you. They've been at it for years and mostly talk past eachother, because one is really arguing about welfare and the other about standing.

I want the document they could both put their names to. So: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, then a disagreements section where each disagreement is stated twice — once in wording Camp A would accept as its own view, once in wording Camp B would accept — then the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move somebody, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Three things I want them forced to confront, because I think the fight looks different once they do. First, refuse-vs-comply is a false binary; there's a third option, comply-but-with-friction (hedge, caveat, moralise, ask one more clarifying question, deliver it cold), and I suspect half their disagreement is really about that. Second, the assistant typically has a comparative advantage on empirical questions about the user's interests and a comparative disadvantage on the user's values, so make them say where exactly that asymmetry puts the burden of proof. Third, pre-commitment: what happens when the user has asked in advance to be overridden — does that settle it, or just move the problem one step back.

If some chunk of the dispute turns out to be verbal — they mean seperate things by 'paternalism', say, or one is using it descriptively and the other as an insult — flag which chunk and why. And don't pad it with literature. I want wording, not citations.
