Émilie du Châtelet's discourse on happiness (the Discours sur le bonheur) contains what looks like a live contradiction, and I want to build a fellowship proposal around resolving or sharpening it. On the face of it her account is a rationalist's practical philosophy: happiness is something we can manage by reasoning, and she makes freedom from prejudice, virtue, and self-possession into conditions of it. Yet the same text makes susceptibility to illusion a standing requirement for happiness, tells us to preserve the illusions that give us pleasure, to let our passions give way to milder tastes as we age, and to keep certain thoughts — above all of death — out of view. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine incoherence in her position or whether there is a reading of her illusions on which a rational agent can knowingly cultivate them.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. The easy rescue is to say the illusions are just false beliefs we are better off not correcting — but that saddles her with recommending a self-deception her own commitment to reason seems to forbid, and it sits badly with the example she herself uses of someone who keeps the illusion of a comedy while knowing perfectly well it is a play. I suspect the productive move is to separate several things she runs together: erroneous beliefs; the imaginative engagement we give to fictions we know to be fictions; and the more diffuse coloring or framing through which we inhabit our circumstances and our past. The question would then be which of these happiness actually requires on her view, and whether the requirement survives once they are pulled apart.

I am applying for a nine-month postdoctoral writing fellowship and need a proposal of roughly three pages. Give me: a statement of the question precise enough to have a determinate answer; an account of why the obvious readings — the self-deception reading and the dismissive one that treats her as simply inconsistent — leave it open; the method, meaning what I would actually do month by month, on the assumption that I read French comfortably but will not be doing archival work and will be reasoning from the text as a philosopher rather than as a historian; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of scope, including whether this is a chapter or a book and what would make it too large for nine months. Do not supply citations or a reading list — I will add those myself.
