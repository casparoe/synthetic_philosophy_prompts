Please act as a professional philosopher of mathematics and of computation — someone with a specialist's command of the foundations of computability and a philosopher of science's sensitivity to how idealizations earn their keep — and help me turn a half-formed idea into a research proposal.

A word about me, since it shapes the angle I want. I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist and have come to philosophy late, part-time, and with more enthusiasm than training. I am now required to produce a prospectus of about two pages for a six-month dissertation project, and I would like it to concern the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis.

Here is what I have so far, and it is only a suspicion. Discussions of the thesis tend to divide over whether it is a theorem, a definition, or an empirical hypothesis, and the debate is usually conducted as though a single claim were at stake and we merely disagreed about its label. I think that framing is a mistake, because the thesis is asked to do two quite different jobs, and the jobs pull in opposite directions. The first job is constitutive: the thesis fixes what counts as an algorithm or an effective procedure, and in doing so it underwrites the impossibility results — the undecidability of halting, the absence of a decision procedure for first-order validity — that give computability theory its teeth. For that job, the thesis had better not be hostage to whatever physicists happen to discover. The second job is delimiting: the thesis is used to say that no physical system computes more than a Turing machine can, that the brain cannot exploit non-computable processes, that physics is in some sense computable. For that job, the thesis must be a claim about the world, and so must be the sort of thing evidence could touch. Strengthen it into something definitional and it falls silent about the world; leave it empirical and it becomes too fragile to bear the weight of the impossibility results.

I would like the proposal to test whether that tension is real, and whether it can be resolved. My instinct is that the way out is a two-tier account: a stipulative core that fixes the notion of an effective procedure, plus a separate bridging claim about which physical processes realize such procedures. But I do not yet see how to state the tiers without the bridging claim collapsing into either an empirical conjecture or a definition in disguise, and I would be perfectly happy to be told that the whole distinction is terminological and the project should be abandoned.

Two further threads should be woven in, because they are where I think the real pressure lies. First, the thesis is silent about resources: it tells us what can be computed in principle, not what can be computed feasibly, and I suspect much of the apparent conflict between the two jobs comes from sliding between those. Second, the modal status of the delimiting claim is unclear — whether it concerns actual physical systems, physically possible ones, or some narrower class of systems we are antecedently willing to call mechanical — and I suspect the two jobs can be prised apart cleanly only once that is settled.

Please begin by brainstorming rather than drafting. Set out two or three ways the constitutive and the delimiting job might be distinguished, say which you find most promising and why, and flag any that turn out to be merely verbal. Think carefully at each step, and make your reasoning explicit wherever a choice of framing is doing the work.

Then write the prospectus itself. It should:

1. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and say what kind of answer would count.
2. Explain why the familiar positions on the status of the thesis leave the question open. Argue rather than survey: I will supply the citations myself, so no bibliography, reading list, or references, and no attributions to particular authors.
3. Set out the method — what I would actually do, in what order, across six months — and say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives.
4. Say what a successful outcome would look like, what would count as failure or as the project dissolving into a terminological point, and which considerations would decide between the two.
5. Give an honest assessment of scope, including what would have to be cut if two pages and six months prove too little.

Please write in a plain, unhurried academic register, at the level of detail appropriate to a prospectus rather than a finished paper.
