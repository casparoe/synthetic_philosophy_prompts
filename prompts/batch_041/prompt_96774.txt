Neither of them did anything at all. What, if anything, does this case show about whether omissions can be causes? What, if anything, distinguishes the gardener's omission from the stranger's? And here's what the student wrote:

"Both cases turn on the same thing, which is counterfactual dependence. If the gardener had watered the plant it would have lived, and if the stranger had watered it, it would have lived too. So on the counterfactual theory of causation both omissions are causes of the death, and I think both verdicts are correct. In the gardener's case in particular we get the verdict we want, since he was the one who was supposed to water it.

The obvious objection is that this makes everything a cause of everything. My neighbour didn't water the plant, the Queen didn't water it, and infinitely many other people didn't water it, so if the test is applied consistently then the death has infinitely many causes. This shows that counterfactual dependence can't be a sufficient condition for causation. But it doesn't show that omissions aren't causes, because a process account that requires a flow of energy or a conserved quantity from cause to effect has literally nothing to say about a case in which nothing happens, and that's a cost to the process account rather than to the view that omissions cause things.

What separates the gardener from the stranger, then, isn't metaphysics but our explanatory and blaming practices. We single out the gardener because watering was his job, and the stranger's omission isn't salient to anybody. So causal selection is pragmatic. It follows that the sentence 'the gardener caused the plant to die' isn't really a claim about the causal structure of the world, it's a claim about which omission we happen to find interesting. The metaphysics of omission and the pragmatics of causal talk can't be pulled apart, and there's no fact of the matter about which omissions are causes, only facts about which ones we choose to mention."
