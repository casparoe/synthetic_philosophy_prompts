I am a high school student working on a philosophy of art unit, and I have become fixated on the Brancusi customs case of the late 1920s. My friends and I were arguing about it after class and could not agree, so I am bringing the question to you. The facts, as I understand them, are these: Constantin Brancusi shipped a polished bronze sculpture titled Bird in Space to New York in 1926; American customs officials, judging that the tall, thin, near-featureless form did not resemble a bird or anything else they recognized as sculpture, classified it as a manufactured metal object and levied a duty of forty percent of its sale price, whereupon the photographer Edward Steichen, who had bought the piece, appealed, and the matter went to trial, with sculptors and critics testifying on both sides about whether the object was art at all. I would like you to analyze this episode through the lens of competing theories of what makes something a work of art: the institutional view, on which a thing is art when the artworld collectively confers that status upon it; the family-resemblance view, on which art is a cluster of overlapping similarities rather than a definable essence; the formalist view, on which art is distinguished by significant form; and the expression view, on which art is the outward rendering of an artist's inner state. For each theory I want a disciplined mapping: who counts as the relevant agent or institution, what evidence each theory treats as decisive, and what each would say the customs officials, the testifying experts, and the judge were actually doing. Then tell me where each theory falls silent or misdescribes the case. For instance, does the institutional view make the customs officials' initial refusal simply a mistake about what the artworld had already decided, or does it turn the trial itself into the moment of conferral, and does that distinction matter?

I also want the analysis to run in the other direction, from the case back to the theories. The court, as I understand it, eventually ruled in Brancusi's favor, and I have seen it said that the decision helped loosen the legal definition of art in the United States; but I am suspicious of the inference that a customs ruling settles a philosophical question. Does the case vindicate the institutional view, since a status was in fact conferred by an authoritative body, or does it instead show that the law has its own purposes, such as revenue, classification, and administrability, that make it a poor test of any theory of art? Finally, I would like you to consider a variant I have not seen discussed: suppose the same shipment had contained not a bronze cast but an ordinary manufactured object accompanied by a signed certificate from the artist declaring it art, in the manner of the readymades that had been exhibited a decade or so earlier. What would each of the four theories predict about the customs officials' proper verdict, and which theory, if any, can accommodate both the Brancusi case and the readymade case without special pleading?

Please write your answer in British English, and please be honest about which parts of the case the theories genuinely illuminate and which parts they merely redescribe. I would rather have a careful account of the limits of each theory than a tidy verdict that one of them wins outright.
