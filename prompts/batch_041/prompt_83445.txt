I teach an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of belief and want to close the term with John Patrick Shanley's *Doubt*, a play whose entire dramatic engine is the question of what one person may believe about another on the evidence she has. (The 2008 film follows the play closely, so either can serve as the text.) It opens with Father Flynn telling his congregation that "Doubt can be a bond as powerful and sustaining as certainty," and it ends with Sister Aloysius in doubt about the very conviction that drove her to force his removal. What I want from you is a reading of the play against the exchange between William James, in "The Will to Believe," and W. K. Clifford, in "The Ethics of Belief" — not a summary of either essay, and not a reading that quietly turns the play into an illustration of one of them.

Put the play's pressure on both positions. Does Aloysius's situation satisfy James's conditions for a genuine option — live, forced, momentous, and undecidable by evidence — or is it a case his argument has to exclude, given that the question is in principle answerable (the telephone call she reports having made to his previous parish is one she never made) and that the cost of her error would fall on the priest and on a boy whose mother has told her, in the play's most uncomfortable scene, that she does not want the accusation pursued? Clifford's opening cases are the shipowner and, more useful to you, the agitators who accuse prominent citizens on insufficient evidence and are shown to have been mistaken; his point is that the question "has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it ... not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." Say whether the play's refusal to settle Flynn's guilt is a fidelity to that point or a dramaturgical evasion that lets the audience off. Note too that the play is set in 1964, with the Second Vatican Council's changes already unsettling Aloysius, and ask whether her suspicion is a belief formed from evidence at all or a conviction powered by taste and institutional resentment — which is, for Clifford, the whole question.

Then bring in the machinery the two essays leave out. First, the distinction between believing a proposition and accepting it as a premise for action: acceptance can be voluntary, is relative to a context and a decision, and does not require the feeling of conviction — so consider whether Aloysius ever believes anything, or only accepts a conclusion and acts. Second, the question whether belief is voluntary at all; if it is not, what is the "will" in James's title, and what does the final scene suggest about the stability of a willed conviction? Third, the manualist moral theology in the play's background, which separates doubt about a fact from doubt about a duty and asks not what one may believe but what one may do — whether one may act on a solidly probable opinion favoring freedom, must follow the more probable opinion, or must take the safer course.

For the seminar: name the scenes worth assigning and what each one does to the argument; mark the places where the play resists the philosophical frame and the honest critic has to say so; and end by saying what the play adds to this debate that James and Clifford between them cannot supply.
