At my school's philosophy club two of my friends got into a proper row about epiphenomenalism and I said I'd referee but I definately couldn't follow who was winning, so I'm asking you instead. The setup: Sam reckons experiences are caused by brain goings-on but don't cause anything themselves, they're just along for the ride. Jess says the standard comeback is that this cuts its own throat — if my pain can't cause me to say "ow", then my report about my pain is produced by whatever else caused the pain, and it's a fluke if it ever matches what I'm actually feeling, so I can't know my own qualia at all. Sam said that only bites if you think knowing needs a causal chain; he said a shadow is caused by the thing casting it and doesn't cause anything back, yet you can read the object off the shadow, and reliabilist knowledge only needs a reliable link, not a particular direction of arrow. Jess said a shadow is a bad analogy because the light does the causal work, whereas in the judgment case the experience is supposed to be the only thing on offer, and if it's inert there's nothing for the judgment to latch onto. Then Sam came up with this thing on the spot: he said a judgment could represent an experience without being caused by it, the way a map can be about a city that never touched the map, and representing isn't causing. Jess said that just moves the problem — how does the brain know which experience to draw a map of, if not by being affected by it — and that the map analogy smuggles in a mapmaker.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right on each point rather than just who wins, rate each move from clearly wrong to clearly right, and say what both of them are missing? British English please — I might use this for my EPQ so keep it serious.
