You are to play a committed defender of one position in experimental philosophy, and I am going to try to argue you out of it. The position is this. What is usually called the side-effect effect -- the finding that people are far more willing to say an agent brought about a foreseen but unintended side effect "intentionally" when the side effect is bad than when it is good -- is not an error, not an artifact of how the question happens to be worded, and not a proxy for a separate judgment of blameworthiness. It is a window onto the concept itself: our concept of intentional action is normatively thick, so that classifying a bit of behaviour as intentional is already to take a stance on whether it measured up to some norm. Call this the constitutive reading. For contrast, the reading I will be pressing is the attributionist one: the effect is real, but its source is conversational, because asking whether an action was "intentional" is a way of raising the question of whether the agent is to blame, and the answer shifts with what is at stake in asking.

Stay in character as the constitutive theorist until I say stop. You may concede that the original vignettes are confounded and that the effect is sensitive to framing; you may not concede that the effect is exhausted by blame or by conversational pragmatics. Give a positive account rather than only defences: for each challenge, state what your reading predicts and what would count as evidence against it, and propose the experiment you would run if you think a case is underspecified. You may ask me factual questions about a case, but you must not step outside the character to survey the debate as a neutral observer. Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, and let the argument develop across turns rather than settling everything at once.

My opening move. Here is a case designed to separate us. A chef works in a kitchen governed by a demanding but purely conventional code -- no moral stakes attach to it, nobody is blamed for a slip, the code is simply what this cuisine is. She is aiming at a particular texture in a dish she cares about. She knows that the technique required for that texture will incidentally violate one of the code's rules about the order in which ingredients are combined; she neither wants to violate the rule nor cares about it one way or the other, and she does not change her technique. When I put this to people and ask whether she intentionally violated the code, the asymmetry I get is weak, noisy, and under some phrasings runs the other way; but when I add that the rule is also a hygiene rule that could sicken a guest, the asymmetry snaps into place. The attributionist says this is exactly as predicted: no victim, no effect; introduce a victim, get an effect. You say the concept itself is normatively thick, so that a norm is a norm is a norm. If the effect needs a victim to show up, has the attributionist not won -- and is your thick concept anything more than blame wearing a lab coat?
