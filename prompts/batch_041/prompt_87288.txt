I run the research desk at a small human rights documentation outfit — we publish reports on ongoing conflicts and atrocities, and one of our recurring nightmares is the word "genocide." Field teams send us dossiers and everyone wants us to either say it or never say it, depending on who's in the room and what they think will get a reaction. Right now the call gets made by vibes. I need something better, because once we print the word we can't take it back — it changes what courts, funders, and governments do with us, and sometimes it changes whether our people on the ground are safe.

Can you build me a decision procedure my desk can actually run on a dossier? I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says "not genocide — here's the label that fits", and where it kicks the decision upstairs to a human because the thing being decided is values or politics rather than fact. It needs to be runnable by a smart researcher who has no legal training, in maybe an hour, off a dossier of testimony, satellite imagery, casualty lists, and whatever a field coordinator typed up in a hurry.

Ground it in the actual concept, not the vibes. Lemkin invented the word and his version was much wider than what the UN later wrote down — he was after the destruction of a group's whole social existence, its political, social, cultural, economic and religious life, not only its bodies. Then there's the much narrower 1948 Convention definition, which is what actually has legal teeth (it needs intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such, and it lists specific kinds of acts; note it drops political groups entirely, which was Lemkin's thing). The procedure has to keep clear which of those two questions we're answering at any moment, because honestly half our internal arguments are people using the moral word and the legal word as if they're the same word.

Things it has to survive:

- The intent problem. We almost never have a memo saying "destroy them." So what do we actually do — infer intent from scale, from who gets targeted, from official rhetoric, from what the killers leave alone? Give me a usable version of that, and tell me when the inference is too thin to lean on.
- The distinction between destroying a group and just clearing it out of a place. Ethnic cleansing isn't a category in the Convention at all, and I need the procedure to say clearly when that's what we're looking at and why it still might be awful without being the G-word.
- "In part." How much of a group is enough, and does it matter whether the part is geographically defined?
- Cultural and economic destruction on its own — burning archives, banning a language, closing churches, starving a region. Under Lemkin this can count; under the Convention it mostly doesn't. The procedure should say what we do with that gap rather than pretend it isn't there.
- The naming-is-politics problem. Sometimes the law will point one way and the moral judgment the other, and sometimes the honest answer is "we believe this but our board will not publish it this year." I don't want the procedure to launder that into a factual finding. Mark it as a hand-off.
- Our own house rules: one part-time legal advisor, no litigation budget, and we publish on a monthly cycle so the call can't drag on forever.

Then run the whole thing on two dossiers so I can see it work:

(a) A region where a minority has been driven out almost completely — villages emptied, a distinct dialect's schools and a handful of old churches destroyed, but the killings were mostly of men of fighting age during a messy insurgency, and there are credible claims of looting for profit and of a land grab by settlers. Half my desk is sure it's genocide; I'm not.

(b) A different region where a group is being killed across the board including children and elders, its mosques are being demolished, survivors are being forced to convert or marry out, and a minister has said on record that these people have no place in the country. This one looks open-and-shut to me, and I want the procedure to either confirm that or make me eat my words.

At least one of those two should come out in a way I won't like — don't tell me what I want to hear. And at the end, tell me plainly what this procedure can't do: which parts are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and where a researcher will definately just be guessing.
