I host a small podcast where I interview people about the less glamorous corners of AI safety, and next month my guest is an independent researcher who's spent years defending a claim I find half-mad: that you can define "low impact" / "avoiding side effects" without first writing down a full specification of what the agent is supposed to value. Her own proposal, which she calls the reversibility-budget view (dont hold me to the name), goes roughly: an action's impact is how much it narrows the set of futures that some reference agent could still reach using only operations we'd call reversible, and this is meant to be value-neutral because it never ranks futures, it only measures how much gets foreclosed.

I want questions that actually dig into that, not softball. Draft me 9 to 11 of them. I need at least one on the baseline problem (reversible relative to what set of operations, and who gets to pick that set without smuggling in values), at least one on whether preserving options is itself a value -- since some options are things nobody should be able to do -- and at least one on the omission problem, i.e. that doing nothing is also a choice with side effects, so what stops the view from collapsing into "the safest agent is the one that never acts." I also want a question on whether labeling something a side effect rather than an intended effect already presupposes a ranking of outcomes, and one on how you compare two option-sets at all, given that any way of measuring "how much was foreclosed" is going to disagree with the others.

For each of the sharp ones, add the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- she tends to retreat to "that's a choice of reference frame, not a value judgment," so I want a follow-up that makes that move harder to reuse. Finish with one question about what could actually count as evidence against her view, and one that makes her say what her measure does with a concrete case: an agent told to cure a disease that, on the way, quietly rewrites an entire local economy that some other people cared about. Keep it conversational enough that I can actually read it aloud.
