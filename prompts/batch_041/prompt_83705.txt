Some friends and I have a long-running argument about the direction of time, and one of us has finally gone off the deep end in a way I'd like to exploit. His position, roughly: the fundamental laws don't distinguish past from future (he knows about the tiny T-violating effects in the weak sector and waves them away as a red herring, which I think is basically right but he is insufferable about it), so the arrow of time isn't in the dynamics at all -- it's inherited from the boundary condition at the early end, the low-entropy start, and "entropy increases" is a statistical claim about whatever coarse-grained description you happened to choose, not a law. He's extremely pleased with himself about this and has started a little wellness brand around it, called Downhill, and now he wants me to write the launch essay for his newsletter, in his voice.

So that's the job: write it. First person, deadpan, the register of one of those "I tried X for 30 days" columns, 700-800 words, no winking and no scare quotes around the physics, funny to someone who's read the actual literature and readable to someone who hasn't. All the comedy should come out of the physics being correct, so please don't let him slip. He's genuinely consoled by the fact that the equations don't care which way you run them; he's started a "coarse-graining journal" where he re-describes his day at finer and finer resolution until the numbers stop going up; he has a little demon that keeps having to erase, and pay for the erasing; he explains why his memory only points one way and treats it as a life lesson; and there's a passage where he tries to be serene about the recurrence time and about maybe being a Boltzmann brain, and fails, without noticing. He should offer two or three "practical tips" that are just the second law in the imperative mood. He's a physics grad who's read the real literature, so he can be technical when it suits him, but no citations, no reading list, and nothing that hangs on a paper I'd have to go look up. The one thing he must never do is accidently solve his own problem -- by the end the reader should be left holding the actual puzzle, how a time-symmetric law plus one special initial condition yields a world with a direction, while he insists he's made his peace with it.
