ok so I'm in a senior metaethics seminar, about 15 of us, one 90-minute block a week, and I've somehow been volun-told to lead one of the sessions. I want to do the wrong kind of reasons problem. You probably know the shape of it: there seem to be reasons to have an attitude that have nothing to do with whether the attitude is correct or fitting — the demon who'll torment you unless you believe some proposition, the boss who'll promote you if you find her joke funny. And the fitting-attitude / buck-passing picture says a thing is good just when it's fitting to admire it, so the objection is that these wrong-kind reasons are reasons to have the attitude but they clearly don't make the object good, so the picture looks like it breaks somewhere.

The thing is I really don't want to stand up there and explain all that for the first 20 minutes and then run discussion questions. I want them to walk straight into it and feel it before I name anything. So I want an in-class activity — a game, a role-play, something staged with real (small) stakes — where they generate right-kind and wrong-kind reasons themselves, and then hit the wall: both kinds of reason are, in some thin sense, reasons to have the attitude, but only one kind makes the object worth admiring. And ideally they also feel the weirdness of not being able to just decide to admire something because there's a reward in it.

Here's a half-formed idea so you know the flavor, but throw it out if you've got something better. Split the room into three juries of five. Give each jury three short fictional bios of people who've done something admirable but are also flawed. They have to crown one as the most admirable. The twist: at the start everyone draws a secret card assigning them a "stake" — if their jury ends up picking the candidate on their card, they get a small prize. Then the deliberation gets contaminated, because people have a reason to want to admire one of them. Make them write down privately why they admire their pick, rate how sincere it feels, and vote. Then maybe flip the payoffs — now the prize goes to the jury that picks the *least* admirable — and make them redo it, so they can feel that if a reward can move their admiration, it was never really about the candidate.
