I am preparing a podcast episode on whether large language models understand anything, and my guest occupies a deliberately awkward position in that dispute. She argues that the two familiar camps are answering different questions and each declares victory on its own terms: one asks whether the model possesses a grasp of its subject matter, the other whether its outputs are reliably apt, and a third question — whether we are entitled to treat it as an understander — gets quietly absorbed into whichever of the first two suits the speaker. Her own contribution is a criterion she proposes in order to break the deadlock: a system understands a domain, she says, only if it can recover the problems that the domain's distinctions were introduced to solve, and not merely manipulate those distinctions correctly. Draft eight to ten interview questions that compel her to earn each of these claims rather than restate them. Include at least one question on the split among the three questions, one that tests whether problem-recovery is a genuine necessary condition or merely a marker of especially deep understanding (and whether a system trained well enough could satisfy it by accident), one anchored in a concrete case — for instance, a model that succeeds on a task only when the task is phrased as it appeared in training and falters when it is re-described — one on what evidence could in principle settle the question of internal grasp, and one asking whether her entitlement move resolves the debate or merely declines to have it. For each question, add a follow-up aimed at the reply she is most likely to give.
