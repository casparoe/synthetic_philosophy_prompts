I'm a science and ideas reporter at a mid-size regional outlet, and about two years ago I also got handed our weekly ideas desk, which means I'm the one who decides what happens to the pitches that come in about possible harms: a small local study finding a pesticide residue in the water supply, a preprint suggesting a popular reading app is slowing kids down, a chemist who says a chemical in food packaging is something he "wouldn't want in his kitchen," that kind of thing. We get four or five a week and I get maybe twenty minutes each, and right now my method is vibes plus two questions I inherited from the last person in this chair: does anyone propose a mechanism, and who paid for the study. I want to replace that with an actual procedure grounded in the precautionary principle -- not the bumper-sticker version, but the thing as it's been spelled out by people who take it seriously, where precaution is supposed to kick in when the science is uncertain but the potential harm is serious, and where it comes with built-in discipline: the response has to be proportional to the harm you're worried about, you can't treat like cases differently, you have to weigh the costs of acting against the costs of not acting, and you have to revisit the call as evidence comes in. I also half-remember a risk-studies distinction between risk, uncertainty, ambiguity and ignorance -- probabilities known, probabilities unknown, contested framings, and not even knowing what you're missing -- and I think our desk badly needs something like it, because "we don't know" covers four very different situations in our pitch inbox. So: write me the procedure. The ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it has to hand the call to a human. It has to run in twenty minutes, it'll be used by two junior editors and a freelancer who don't have science backgrounds, we publish daily so "wait for more evidence" costs us the story, and anything that names a local company or that would read as a health alarm goes to the standards editor and in the second case the editor-in-chief, who is the one who actually eats the legal risk.

Two things I want it to handle honestly. First, someone on the desk keeps saying the precautionary principle is empty because any story we run also carries risks -- it scares people off a treatment that works, it gets picked up and mangled by worse outlets, it damages a small business on thin evidence -- so precaution cuts both ways and can't tell us anything. If that objection sinks the whole idea, say so; if there's a version of the procedure that survives it, show me which step is doing the surviving. Second, consistency, because I know we'd run the pesticide story in a heartbeat and bury the identical one about a product our readers happen to like, and I don't have a principled answer for why. Then run the thing on three pitches: (a) the water-supply pesticide residue, one small study, no mechanism, one epidemiologist quoted as "concerned"; (b) the reading-app preprint, decent sample but it contradicts two earlier studies and the authors are selling a competitor product; and (c) a tip from a researcher that a widely used over-the-counter remedy he thinks is useless is being overprescribed at a clinic in our coverage area -- no study, a handful of patient anecdotes, and a plausible-sounding biological story. I'll be straight with you, (c) is the one I want to run, it's the kind of story that makes a career, so if the procedure tells me to sit on it or to kick it upstairs, fine, but I want to see exactly which step does that and whether I'm just being flattered into the answer I like. Mark the points where a judgment call is dressed up as procedure, tell me what the procedure can't do, and at the end tell me which step you'd personally bet on least and what would change your mind -- which consideration would flip your verdict on any of the three, or what would have to be true about our desk or our readers for a different procedure to be the right one.
