A friend of mine — a philosopher of science who works on causal inference — and I have spent the better part of a month arguing about the causal exclusion argument, and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped defending the standard replies and started defending a position of my own. I have now been invited to present that position to the mind and brain seminar, a joint meeting of the philosophy department and the cognitive neuroscience group, in three weeks. The talk is fifty minutes with forty minutes of questions afterwards. I need to be ready for the question period, not for the talk.

Here is the position as I intend to present it. The standard exclusion argument, as Kim and his critics both state it, is valid. But it rests on an assumption that has gone unexamined: that for any effect there is a single, exhaustive ledger of causes, so that any candidate cause not entered on that ledger is either redundant or epiphenomenal. Call this the single-ledger assumption. I argue that it is a modelling choice rather than a metaphysical datum, and that dropping it opens a third option between exclusion and overdetermination, which I call stratified causation. On this view, the physical realiser fixes how the effect is brought about — it determines the mechanism — while the realised mental property fixes which of the outcomes that mechanism could have produced is the one produced. Screening-off is therefore stratum-relative: the physical property does not screen off the mental property, because the two do not range over the same contrast class of outcomes. I also want to run the parity argument properly rather than rhetorically: if the exclusion argument worked, it would exclude the properties of epidemiology, ecology and most of neuroscience, and since nobody accepts those conclusions, the fault lies with the argument. And I want to insist that none of this is a retreat to epiphenomenalism, because mental properties still pass a difference-making test — just one indexed to a stratum.

The room will contain roughly the following people. Four philosophers of mind: three are non-reductive physicalists who take the exclusion problem to be the strongest argument against their own position and who know the standard replies and their weaknesses; the fourth is an eliminativist who thinks the whole debate is folk psychology refusing to die. Two philosophers of science who work on causal modelling in the interventionist tradition and who think most of the philosophy of mind literature on causation predates the interventionist framework and has never caught up. Three neuroscientists, two cellular and one systems, who will want to know what any of this changes about what they do on Monday. And one colleague from classics who will ask, politely, whether stratified causation is Aristotle's four causes with the serial numbers filed off.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis:

- eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them rather than by how famous they are;
- each phrased as the person who will raise it would phrase it — the interventionist's objection should sound like an interventionist's, the cellular neuroscientist's like a cellular neuroscientist's;
- for each, a reply I can deliver in two minutes, plus an honest note on where the reply is soft;
- what I should concede in the first minute, before anyone has asked, in order to take the wind out of the strongest objection;
- the single objection that has to land well for the position to survive the session at all, and why that one rather than the others;
- the question I am hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if it comes.

Some rules. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or 'as so-and-so argues' — I know the literature and I will not be able to look anything up while I am standing at the front. Do not hedge about what a particular participant would say; reason from their commitments and commit. If my position turns out to be a relabelling of a familiar move — supervenience is not causation, the parity argument, the appeal to multiple realisability, contrastivism — say so plainly. I would rather learn that from you than from the eliminativist in the second row.

You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who has argued both sides of this debate for a career and has chaired this kind of seminar many times. Think carefully about what this particular room will do to this particular thesis. Before drafting the sheet, spend some time brainstorming: what does each of these audiences actually care about, what does my position look like from where each of them sits, and what would each of them regard as the decisive question?
