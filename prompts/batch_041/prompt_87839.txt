I retired two years ago from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, and since then I have been sitting in on graduate seminars in the philosophy department of the university near my home. This term I offered to help the instructor of a seminar on rational choice and the philosophy of economics by grading the short-answer portion of the midterm, on the theory that thirty-five years of arguing with referees about error bars had made me a tolerably careful reader. The students had read Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 paper and Aumann's 1976 paper, among other things, and the instructor and I settled on a question worth 25 points, divided as 10 for part (a), 7 for part (b), and 8 for part (c). The question, and one student's answer, are below.

Question 2. Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade result is often summarized by the claim that if traders are rational and share a common prior, differences in information alone cannot generate trade. (a) State the theorem's assumptions and its conclusion in your own words, and explain the intuition behind the proof. (b) Explain the role played by the common prior assumption, and say what happens to the result when that assumption is relaxed. (c) Does the theorem show that speculative trade is impossible in real markets? Explain your answer briefly.

Student's answer. (a) Milgrom and Stokey (1982) consider an exchange economy in which the traders share a common prior over the states of the world, are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers, and begin from a Pareto-efficient allocation of the available goods, and in which it is common knowledge that they are rational and that the information structure is as it is. Under these assumptions they show that no trade takes place at all, even though the traders hold different private information about the state. The proof is an unravelling argument. If the initial allocation is already efficient, a trader who proposes a transaction cannot be offering me a share of the ordinary gains from trade, so the only remaining explanation for his willingness to take the opposite side is that his information is better than mine. Because everyone reasons in this way, and because the reasoning is common knowledge, any proposed trade reveals something unfavourable to the person who would accept it, and so every proposal is declined. The equilibrium is therefore the no-trade allocation. (b) The common prior is what makes the unravelling work, since it is only because the two traders interpret the same piece of information in the same way that my counterparty's willingness to trade can serve as evidence about the state for me. Relax the common prior and the argument collapses, since two traders who weight the states differently may both expect to gain from the same bet; the heterogeneous-expectations model of Harrison and Kreps exploits precisely this to generate speculative trade in a stock market. The result is the trading counterpart of Aumann's agreement theorem, which says that two rational agents with a common prior must agree about the probability of any event. Its upshot is that private information is the engine of speculative trade: where traders know nothing that the others do not, there is nothing to trade on. (c) The theorem does not show that speculative trade is impossible in real markets. Real traders trade for reasons that have nothing to do with information — to hedge an exposure, to raise cash, or to rebalance a portfolio — and transactions of that kind can be mutually beneficial without any disagreement about the state. The theorem should therefore be read as a statement about the informational channel only.

I have deliberately not told you my own impressions of the answer, since I would like an independent reading of it before I write anything in the margin.
