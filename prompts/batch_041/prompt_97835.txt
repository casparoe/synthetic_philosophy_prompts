I've been arguing with a friend who works in foreign-policy research, and he's built an arguement that historical analogies are never actually evidence for anything — no lesson from appeasement, no quagmire lesson, no lesson about rising powers and ruling powers, none of it. It's annoying because every premise sounds reasonable when he says it, and I also refuse to accept where the thing lands. Here's his chain, close to verbatim as he states it:

(1) For an analogy between two situations to give me any reason to expect outcome A rather than outcome B, the two situations have to be relevantly alike. (2) What counts as relevantly alike is fixed by a theory of the domain — some account of which features are actually doing the causal work. (3) In statecraft there's no such theory: the feature one school treats as decisive (say, regime type) is background noise to another (say, the distribution of power), and each school defends its own account largely by pointing at cases. (4) So the set of features that counts as relevant is chosen by the arguer, not discovered. (5) If the relevant features are chosen, then any pair of situations can be made alike or unlike at will — pick the features that line up and you get the lesson, pick the ones that diverge and you get the opposite. (6) An inference whose premise can be turned toward either conclusion by a choice the arguer makes is worthless as evidence. (7) So no lesson of history is evidence for anything; the analogies are rhetoric. (8) But that same reasoning would sink medical case reports, legal precedent, and every inference from similarity, none of which anyone can seriously give up. (9) Therefore one of (1) through (6) is wrong, and I can't tell which one.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since without them I don't see what an analogy could even be, and I obviously want to keep (8). If I have to surrender something in the middle, I want to know the bill before I pay it. So: which step or step-pair should go, why did it look innocent until you push on it, and what do the alternatives cost?

Please give me the diagnosis as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "step_to_give_up" (the step number or pair, as a string), "flaw_kind" (one of: equivocation, scope-or-quantifier shift, modal slide, overstrong premise, valid-but-costly), "diagnosis" (two to four sentences naming what goes wrong and why the step looked fine at first), "price_of_giving_it_up" (what the argument loses and what the rest of the chain can still deliver without it), "alternatives" (an array of objects, each with "step", "price", and "why_i_dont_prefer_it", one entry per other step I could plausibly sacrifice), and "residual_worry" (the part of my friend's point that survives no matter which step we give up).
