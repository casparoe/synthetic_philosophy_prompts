I am in my third year of a philosophy degree and taking a formal epistemology paper this term; we have spent the past few weeks on accuracy-first treatments of credence, so I am reasonably at home with proper scoring rules and with the dominance argument for probabilism, but the package stops looking like a package as soon as I test it on cases. A classmate and I have been trading examples, and my own reactions pull in two directions at once — towards the thought that what makes a credence good is accuracy itself, and towards the thought that what makes it good is something about how it hangs together with the others. Take accuracy to be the sum of squared errors over whatever propositions are scored, assume the agents below share their evidence unless I say otherwise, and assume the worlds mentioned are the only ones in play. Here are my verdicts. (i) Two agents await a fair coin toss: the first holds 0.5 that it lands heads, the second holds 0.9, having arrived there by wishful thinking she would be the first to disown. It lands heads, and the second agent's credence is the more accurate by a distance (0.01 against 0.25). I want to say hers is nevertheless the worse credence, and worse epistemically rather than all things considered. (ii) Two agents hold credences in three propositions — that it rains, that it is cold, and that it rains or is cold. The first holds 0.4, 0.4 and 0.9; the second 0.4, 0.4 and 0.8. Only the second is coherent. At the world where it rains and is cold, the first agent scores the better of the two (0.73 against 0.76 — I have checked the arithmetic). I still want to say she is the worse off, and that her incoherence is a defect a lucky score does not repair; I note in passing that she cannot even say what she expects to score, having no distribution to take the expectation with, and I take that to be part of the point. (iii) Suppose an agent is scored on those same three propositions and on nothing else. My classmate says the dominance argument then delivers coherence over the scored propositions only, full probabilism being an artefact of scoring over an algebra. My verdict is that she is right about what the argument delivers and wrong that it matters: the requirement should still be full coherence, and I would rather say the restricted set-up is under-described than that rationality is relative to the scoring sheet. (iv) A three-person committee must announce a single set of credences. Its members hold 0.2, 0.2 and 0.9 in some proposition and 0.8, 0.8 and 0.9 in its negation, so one member is incoherent; the committee announces the means, 0.43 and 0.83, which are not a probability function either. My verdict is that the committee should announce those means and not tidy them up, its business being to represent its members rather than to be a well-behaved agent in its own right. (v) An agent who knows the objective chance of a proposition to be 0.2 holds 0.2 in it; a second agent, who knows the same, holds 0.4. The proposition turns out true, so the second has been the more accurate of the two. I want to say the first agent's credence is the better one, and better because it answers to the chance rather than because of how it scored.

My tentative principle is that a credence is better the more accurate it is in expectation, where the expectation is taken by the agent's own lights. That seems to get the fifth case; on the first it tells me the wishful thinker is already doing the best she can, which is precisely the verdict I want to reject; on the second it is either silent or vindicated by a trick, since the incoherent agent has no expectations to take; and on the fourth it treats the committee as a single agent whose incoherence is straightforwardly a defect, which is the verdict I am least willing to give up. Please test it, repair what can be repaired, and give me the principle — or the small set of candidates — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to an agent neither of us has described, saying for each clause which of my cases forces it. Tell me what it implies about two cases I have deliberately left out: an agent whose credences are perfectly accurate because she has simply been told every truth, and a pair of agents whose priors differ while their evidence does not, one of whom ends up much the closer to the truth. Finally, if the only principle that saves all five verdicts is a conjunction of four clauses with nothing to do with one another, say so, and tell me which single verdict I should surrender for the sake of a more unified principle, and why that one rather than another.
