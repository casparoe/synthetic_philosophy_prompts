Hey, this might be a bit of an odd one. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, but something at work has been nagging at me.

Our whole process is: take a big scary job ("move billing off the legacy service"), chop it into tickets small enough that one person can do each one and one reviewer can check it, then trust that if every ticket is right the whole thing is right. It works way better than having one senior person try to hold everything in their head. I read something in the AI safety blogosphere once — I think its called factored cognition, or HCH, I forget whose idea — that seemed to be the same trick applied to thinking in general: picture a person with unlimited time who can split any question into sub-questions, hand each one to a copy of themselves (or someone just as good), and staple the answers back together. The pitch was that this is a decent stand-in for what it would mean to think a thing through perfectly.

So what does that picture actually have to assume? I want the hidden commitments laid out — that judgment splits cleanly at all, that a pile of reliable small answers composes into a reliable big answer, that there's some fact of the matter each little answer is tracking, that "the same question" even survives being cut up, that checking an answer is cheaper than producing it. And I want to know which of those are load bearing versus just conveniences of how we organize work, and whether our practice really requires them or is just a good fit for them.

Because I've watched it fail. The bug that's an interaction between two modules that each pass review on their own. The ticket that says "is this design good" and nobody can tell you what would count as verifying it. The vague ticket where every sub-answer is fine and the whole is still wrong because the split itself was the mistake. And we also insist that one person owns the whole system and sits in design review holding all of it in their head, which seems to cut against the decompose-everything story.

So: reconstruct the theory of thinking our process is quietly betting on, say whether its parts are consistent with each other, tell me what it rules out (does it just lose anyone who says real thinking is holistic and can't be cut up at all?), say where it's underdetermined — what other views of deliberation would license the same practice — and then tell me whether the failures above just mean the tree is fuzzier in some places or actually break the idealization, and whether theres a repaired version that survives. American English please.
