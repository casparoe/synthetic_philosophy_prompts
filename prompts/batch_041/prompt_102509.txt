I'm building the decision policy for an AI system and two of my colleagues won't stop fighting about it. One's a utilitarian, one's a deontologist, and the fight is basically: when the system has to make a call on its own, should it reason like a utilitarian or like a deontologist? I want you to write the dialogue between them. Not a debate where one side wins and the other is a moron -- both of them are smart and neither should be a strawman. Real back-and-forth, where each one responds to the actual point the other just made, concedes when the other lands a hit, and shifts ground when they're stuck.

Please have them get into the stuff I think is actually where it gets interesting, because I'm tired of the trolley stuff (no trolleys, please):

- The deontologist's usual defense of constraints is that agents have their own lives, projects and integrity to protect, and that's supposed to be why they get agent-relative prerogatives and why they can't just be used as means. But an AI doesn't have a life to live or anything it cares about, so that whole story seems to fall apart for it. Does the deontologist have a reply here, or does she have to re-ground constraints somewhere else -- in the person on the other end, or in what kind of system you can trust? I want the utilitarian to press this hard.

- Flip side: impartiality is normally something humans have to struggle toward, and for an AI it's just the default, it comes free. Is that a feature or a bug for the utilitarian? Does an agent that feels no partiality make utilitarian reasoning too easy, or actually cleaner and more honest?

- The system gets copied, versioned, rolled back, run in parallel. It isn't one continuous agent with a single track record. What does that do to deontology's picture of a unified agent whose act is really *hers*, and to utilitarian accounting when there are a bunch of forks of the same policy all acting at once?

- And the level-of-analysis thing, which I suspect is the real crux: when the deontologist says the system shouldn't deceive a user to make them feel better, and the utilitarian says but the welfare comes out higher if it does, is the utilitarian secretly just a rule-consequentialist defending the same constraints by other means? Or are they genuinely disagreeing? Let them fight about that one.

Let them actually argue it out, and by the end give me some sense of whether the disagreement is about which theory is true, or about which theory is easier to implement and audit inside a system -- because I have a hunch those are two seperate questions and everyone keeps conflating them. You don't have to resolve it.
