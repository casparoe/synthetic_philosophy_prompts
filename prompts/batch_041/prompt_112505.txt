My old physics mentor is retiring this month and his department asked me to give the toast at the dinner. I did a physics degree and then bailed into software, so I'm not a philosopher, but I've spent enough evenings in his office getting lectured at that I know the shape of the fight.

He's basicly spent his whole career on string theory and eternal inflation, which is the part of physics where you can't point at an experiment and go, there, that settles it. Half the room will be his former collaborators, who will absolutely notice if I say something dumb about whether that work is even science, and the other half will be his kids and grandkids, who are pretty sure grandpa proved we live in a multiverse.

I want five minutes that lands in both rooms. The hard part is this: I want to say something honest and warm about a career spent on ideas that haven't been confirmed and might never be, without (a) talking like it's all basically settled, (b) doing the thing where I wave it off with science is about the journey, or (c) saying anything that only makes sense if you can already assign probabilites over universes. He spent years complaining that people do (c) in the first sentence of every talk and he'd hear it instantly.

Also he has this argument he's been making forever, roughly that when a theory is the only game in town and it keeps not falling apart under every test you can actually run, that has to count for something even when you can't test the thing itself. I know there's an obvious counter that we've been fooled that way before, so I don't want to just assert it and sit down.

The only frame I've come up with on my own is git. He's been maintaining a branch that nobody can merge, and the real question is what makes a branch worth working on when you can't merge it. Use that if it helps, toss it if it's bad.

Write the toast. No quotes from him, I don't remember anything exactly anyway, and don't cite papers or books. And don't make the family feel stupid.
