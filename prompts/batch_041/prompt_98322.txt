I sit on the secretariat of a parliamentary committee that screens pronatalist proposals — birth bonuses, childcare and parental-leave expansions, immigration measures justified as population policy. We see about a dozen a year, each with real money attached, and I have to hand the minister a one-page recommendation: adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions. I know cost-benefit analysis and demography; I do not know population ethics.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run in an afternoon, built on what population ethics establishes rather than on one school. Sequence the questions to ask: does the policy change how many people exist, or only how well existing people live; are the added lives worth living, and by whose standard; are we comparing totals, averages, or some threshold below which an added life does not count toward the good; what to do when a policy looks better on totals and worse on averages; what to do when the people a policy benefits would not have existed at all under the alternative, so that we cannot say the policy made anyone worse off. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a rejection, and where it must hand the decision to a value judgment — for instance, choosing the threshold at which an added life stops counting, or deciding whether the interests of people who do not yet exist may be discounted. State plainly where the procedure cannot be made non-arbitrary, and what it cannot do at all.

Two things to fold in. First, our chair has proposed a working rule that no policy may reduce the average wellbeing of people already living, whatever it does to totals; the procedure should say whether that rule can be accommodated, and at what cost. Second, the procedure has to survive the case of a policy that adds many lives that are only barely worth living.

Then run it on three proposals: a monthly cash bonus per birth; a childcare subsidy that improves the lives of existing families without changing how many children are born; and an expansion of skilled immigration that raises both the number of residents and their average wellbeing. At least one should come out the way I will not like — say so plainly and explain why that does not by itself discredit the procedure. Keep the deliverable usable: questions, stopping rules, and residual judgment calls, in that order, with the reasoning compressed.
