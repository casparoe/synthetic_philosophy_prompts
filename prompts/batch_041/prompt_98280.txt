I'm the head TA for an undergraduate course in the philosophy of biology, and I'm grading the take-home midterm for the unit on biological function. The unit covers the selected-effects (etiological) account associated with Wright, Millikan, and Neander; the causal-role (systemic) account associated with Cummins; and Godfrey-Smith's critique of the etiological program. Because students can recite the stock examples (hearts, eyes, vestigial organs) from memory, I built the exam around a constructed case and a hybrid proposal I wrote myself, so that the answers have to be reasoned rather than recalled. I'd now like you to grade one student's submission. I'm not looking for a general impression: give a letter grade, then the specific marginal comments that justify it, working through the answer point by point and distinguishing errors that are central to the question from errors that are peripheral.

Here is the exam question. A hypothetical freshwater snail, which I'll call the ridgeback snail, has two features of interest. Trait A, toxinase, is an enzyme that catalyzes the breakdown of a plant alkaloid; comparative and sediment evidence indicate that the ancestral population was under strong selection when an alkaloid-bearing plant colonized its habitat, and the variant responsible for efficient catalysis swept to fixation. The plant has since gone locally extinct, and although the enzyme is still synthesized and still catalyzes the same reaction, it no longer makes any measurable difference to survival or reproduction. Trait B, the dorsal ridge, is a thickened ridge along the shell; developmental and phylogenetic evidence indicate that the ridge arose as an unselected byproduct of selection for an overall thicker shell, and that nothing has ever selected on the ridge itself. A piercing predator arrived within the last few generations, and the ridge now incidentally deflects it, so the ridge currently contributes to survival; there has been no opportunity for selection on the ridge to have occurred. The question has two parts. (a) For each of A and B, state whether it has a function under (i) the selected-effects (etiological) account and (ii) the causal-role (systemic) account, and justify each verdict. (b) A colleague proposes what she calls the retention-plus account: a present trait token has a function just in case (i) the token was selected for producing that effect somewhere in its lineage and (ii) the effect still makes a difference to the organism's current survival or reproduction. Does this account classify A and B differently from the standard etiological account, and does the additional clause do any work? Evaluate.

Here is the student's answer. The two accounts differ in what makes something a function. On the selected-effects account, a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected; functions are historical, and ascribing a function makes a claim about a trait's evolutionary past. On the causal-role account, a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system, where the system is chosen by the investigator; this account is ahistorical and relativizes function to a level of analysis. For trait A, the selected-effects account says that A has the function of detoxifying the alkaloid, because that is the effect for which the ancestral variant was selected, and the plant's extinction does not matter since the account looks backward; the causal-role account also assigns A a function, since within the metabolic system toxinase contributes to the capacity to process the alkaloid. So A has a function on both accounts. For trait B, the selected-effects account says that B has the function of deflecting the piercing predator, because the ridge now protects the snail and so contributes to survival, and this is the effect that explains why the ridge is retained; the causal-role account likewise assigns B a function, since the ridge contributes to the shell's defensive capacity. So B, too, has a function on both accounts, and the two accounts agree on both traits. Turning to the retention-plus account, it is really just the selected-effects account with an extra clause: clause (i) already requires selection, and clause (ii) is satisfied whenever the trait is doing anything at all, so the proposal changes none of the verdicts, and A and B both come out as having functions exactly as under the standard account. The clause therefore does no work, and the account should be rejected as an unmotivated complication.

Please give the student a letter grade from A through F with plus or minus, and then the comments I can hand back, going through parts (a) and (b) in order. For each part, tell me what a fully correct answer would have said, identify which of the student's claims are correct, which are subtly wrong, and why, and note which of the errors a quick grader would most likely let slide. Judge the reasoning itself rather than the confidence of the prose, and be explicit about whether the mistakes are incidental slips or go to the heart of what the question was testing.
