I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book set in the second half of the nineteenth century. Its two central figures are a physicist of the old school and a geologist who has staked his reputation on deep time, and I have the historical furniture I need: the physicist's estimates of the age of the Earth and of the Sun, computed from the rate at which a hot body loses its heat, allowed him tens of millions of years and no more; the geologists and the Darwinians, reasoning from the thickness of strata and the slow arithmetic of natural selection, needed hundreds of millions and said so; the two sides went at each other in print and in private letters for the better part of forty years; and the quarrel was overtaken in the end, not by anybody's changing his mind, but by the discovery of a source of heat that neither party had thought to put into the calculation. What I cannot write is the middle of the argument — the long stretch in which two intelligent men, neither of them a crank, each familiar with the other's work and neither with any interest in being wrong, simply failed to converge. A friend of mine who reads philosophy tells me there is a theorem, due to Robert Aumann, that speaks to precisely this situation: two agents who share a prior and whose posterior probabilities are common knowledge cannot agree to disagree, so a persistent disagreement between them must be traceable to something other than the evidence. I am not a philosopher and I do not trust my grasp of it, so I would like you to use it as a map of my characters' impasse.

Map it carefully, and then tell me where the map fails. Treat the physicist and the geologist as the two agents, and say what has to be true of them — about the prior they share, about what each knows of the other's evidence and reasoning, about what is common knowledge between them — for the theorem to apply at all; then say which of those conditions actually obtained in this dispute and which did not, and what the theorem would have told each man to do with the disagreement: what he is entitled to infer about his own reasoning from the fact that a peer he respects will not move, and whether either of them drew that inference. I also want you to take seriously a possibility that I have not seen put this way. It seems to me that the two men did not merely assign different probabilities within a common space of possibilities; they were working with different spaces. The physicist's inventory of admissible sources of heat did not contain the thing that would eventually settle the question, and no exchange of posteriors, however complete and however honest, could have repaired this, because neither man could make common knowledge with the other a possibility that he had never imagined. If that is right, say so plainly, and then say what it does to the theorem: whether the case is a counterexample, a merely uninteresting failure of an idealization that was never meant to cover it, or the ordinary condition of scientific disagreement rather than an exotic one. Finally, turn it around. What does this episode show about the theorem — about what the shared-prior and common-knowledge conditions are really doing, and about whether a theorem of this shape can be of any use to people who are in the middle of a disagreement, as opposed to economists describing one after the fact?
