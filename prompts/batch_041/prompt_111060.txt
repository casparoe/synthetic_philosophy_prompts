I teach a seminar on punishment and social sanction — the literature that distinguishes hard treatment from symbolic condemnation, asks whether deterrence, retribution, or the authoritative expression of censure is the essence of punishing, and argues about who has standing to condemn — and I want to end the term with Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter (1850), because it is the one canonical novel I know in which the continuing content of the sanction is almost purely significatory. Hester Prynne is tried, convicted, and sentenced by a lawful authority, but what she is sentenced to is a public exposure and then a sign to wear for life; there is no fine, no prison term beyond the pre-trial cell, no corporal penalty. What I want from you is a critical reading at the level of a seminar handout, not a survey of themes: does the novel dramatize the expressive tradition's central claim that a punishment can consist entirely in the meaning it publicly fixes on an offender, and does it also expose the theory's weak points? Take the scenes seriously as evidence — the opening scaffold and the magistrates' demand that she name the father, the matrons in the crowd who want the mark burned or branded and who have no legal authority whatever, the letter she embroiders in gold rather than wearing as a dun rag, the night on the scaffold when Dimmesdale's apparition of a fiery letter is read by him as his own crime and by the town as an angel or an omen, Chillingworth's private and unaccountable campaign of revenge, Dimmesdale's self-scourging with no social censure at all, the town's later willingness to say the letter stands for able, and the grave the two of them finally share.

Two conjectures of mine that I would like you to test rather than assume, because they are the reason I am teaching the book instead of an article. First, I suspect the novel is a natural experiment for the view, common in the current arguments about deplatforming and public shaming, that informal social sanction is not really punishment because it involves no deprivation, no authority, and no due process — Hester has all three of the latter and suffers anyway, which suggests the sanction's bite lies in its expressive content rather than in any hard treatment, and that the crowd's standing to condemn is the contested thing rather than the absence of punishment. Second, I suspect the book undermines the same view from the other side, because Hester eventually takes over the meaning of her own censure — she re-authors the sign, and the community that meant to brand her ends up praising her — which would mean that the expressive content of a punishment is hostage to the offender's counter-interpretation in a way a theory of authoritative censure cannot easily allow. I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not turn the novel into an illustration of a position it was not written to hold, and do not bend the philosophy to fit the plot. So I need you to say plainly where the novel resists my framing — its theological grammar of sin, guilt, and conscience before God rather than before a community; Dimmesdale's case, which locates the real tribunal inside the man and makes the town's censure almost beside the point; the narrator's irony, which refuses to settle whether the community's judgment is vindicated or merely cruel; and the possibility that the able-reading is the town domesticating Hester rather than Hester triumphing over the town. Close by telling me what the novel contributes to this debate that the argument alone cannot, and what the honest critic has to concede on both sides. I do not need citations, page references, or reading lists — I know the novel and the literature, and the seminar has the texts.
