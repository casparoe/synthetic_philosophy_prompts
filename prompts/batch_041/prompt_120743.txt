For the past two years I have been reporting on conservation, rewilding, and the economics of land use, and I have arrived at a position I find awkward to say out loud: that habitat destruction, on balance, reduces wild animal suffering. I want to be clear that this is not the cartoon version. I am not claiming that clearing a forest is painless, and I am not claiming extinction is good. The claim is narrower. The animals caught up in land conversion are overwhelmingly short-lived, high-fecundity species, most of whose offspring die young — of starvation, exposure, parasites, predation — and conversion tends to substitute a very large number of small, rapidly turned-over individuals for a much smaller number of larger ones. If the quantity that matters is sentience-weighted suffering integrated over time, then reducing the number of animals can reduce the total even when the survivors have a worse time of it.

What I need from you is the case against that position, and I want it built the way a working scientist would build it rather than the way a critic would. Specifically: which empirical findings would count against it, which of them are already in hand, and which measurements would settle the question if someone actually went and took them — stress physiology, body condition, parasite loads, breeding success — for the animals that remain in fragmented and degraded habitat as against those in intact habitat. I am especially interested in the load-bearing step, the substitution of fewer animals for more, and whether it survives contact with source–sink dynamics and ecological traps, where habitat that looks like habitat keeps drawing animals in and either kills them or, worse, keeps them alive and breeding at a deficit. A sink is not obviously better than no habitat on the accounting I am using, and I want to know whether that cuts against me or for me. And I want to know what the invertebrate case does to all of this, since the numbers there swamp every estimate I have seen and are the least known.

There are two distinctions I keep tripping over and would like you to disentangle. The first is between counting individuals and counting animal-time: a colleague argues that the right unit is the animal-hour spent in aversive states, that the number of deaths is a red herring, and that the whole question reduces to what fraction of animal time is spent hungry, frightened, or in pain. If he is right, tell me what that does to my position, and what data would estimate that fraction. The second is the population-ethical one: whether the claim is even well-formed depends on whether one holds a total, average, or prior-existence view, and I suspect the verdict changes entirely across them. Say where my position sits and which axiology it needs in order to be true.

Then rank the objections. Separate what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward, say which of them a sympathetic defender would concede rather than dismiss, and tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect or simply the price of a claim about aggregate welfare that nobody can measure. I would rather hear that the view is well insulated and that this is a problem than be handed a list of talking points. No citations needed; I want the argument, not the reading list.
