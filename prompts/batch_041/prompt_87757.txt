I'm a senior engineer at a ~60 person startup and I've somehow ended up owning our docs and commit-message conventions, which is a weird amount of power over what people are allowed to say. I did a CS degree, never took a philosophy class, so forgive me if I get terms wrong.

The thing that keeps biting us: someone writes in a README "safe to call from multiple threads", or a postmortem says "the outage was caused by the cache eviction change", or a commit says "fixes the duplicate-charge bug" — and it turns out nobody actually knew that, they believed it or thought it was probably true. I've read enough philosophy-adjacent stuff to know there's a real debate about what you're allowed to assert: only what you know, or is truth enough, or is having good evidence the bar. I think that debate is exactly our problem, but every version I've seen is written for philosophers and I need something my team will use in a code review.

So turn that into a decision procedure I can put in our docs. The questions a reviewer or author runs through in order: what kind of claim is this, what would have to be true for it to be assertable, what evidence moves it between buckets, when do we downgrade to "we believe" or "we've seen no evidence of", and where does the procedure just stop and say this is a judgment call, escalate. Ten minutes in a code review is my budget, and I'm the one who has to sign off on the doc.

Two wrinkles I want handled because they're the ones that actually come up: (1) a lot of what we "know" is team knowledge — no single person knows the cache change caused the outage, but the team's evidence together does, so does a knowledge bar even make sense at the level of one author; and (2) we constantly imply claims we never state — a README that lists "handles retries" gets read as asserting retries work under load even if nobody wrote that down.

Then run it on three cases: the thread-safety line in the README, the causal claim in the postmortem, and the commit claiming a fix for a bug that hasn't reproduced since. At least one of those should come out with an answer I won't like, and tell me straight which steps are just vibes dressed up as rules.
