I'm giving a talk at our mind/cog-sci reading group in a few weeks and I want you to write my Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of the talk.

Heres my thesis, in three bits. (1) The sphex wasp story -- the digger wasp that drags the cricket back to the burrow mouth, drops it to go re-inspect the burrow, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while shes inside she just resets and runs the whole sequence again, over and over -- gets used as the scare story about how we might all be automata (Hofstadter coined the word, Dennett leaned on it). I want to argue the story gets the moral backwards. The wasp isn't a special case of rigidity; she's the ordinary case of acting under a fixed description of the situation. When I follow a recipe, or a rule of inference, or a traffic law, I'm doing exactly what she does -- running a routine keyed to a description of what's in front of me, and I don't re-open the description unless something forces me to. (2) So "are we sphexish?" is a badly posed question. Sphexishness is a property of the pair (agent, description), not of the agent. Nothing is sphexish simpliciter; a thing is sphexish relative to a description it can't revise. (3) What freedom amounts to, then, isn't the absence of routines -- it's the capacity to notice that the situation doesn't fit the description you're acting under, and to revise the description from the inside. That capacity comes in degrees, and it's continuous with whatever the wasp is missing, not a different kind of thing.

Now the audience, because I know the objections depend on who's in the room. Its about a dozen people: two hard compatibilists from the phil dept who think I've just re-described responsiveness to reasons and will ask why the wasp earns its keep at all; two people from the biology side who study insect behaviour and are sick of philosophers using the wasp as a prop -- one of them has read Keijzer's attack on the example and will tell me the wasp actually does adapt after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest is arguably adaptive behaviour anyway, so the whole metaphor is rhetoric rather than fact; an eliminativist who'll say the description/mechanism distinction is doing no explanatory work and that the question is folk-psychology residue; and a Kantian who'll insist the real line is between acting from a rule and acting on a maxim, and that "revising the description" is just a fancier heteronomy. Plus one sharp grad student who works on LLMs and will probably ask the question I'm dreading.

What I want: the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each person would phrase them, sorted by how much damage they do rather than by how likely they are, with a two-minute reply for each and a one-line note on where the reply is soft or hand-wavy. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes so I don't spend the whole hour defending the wasp's good name. And be blunt about which of my three claims is load-bearing: if the entomologists take out (1), do (2) and (3) survive on their own, or do I actually need the wasp and just don't want to admit it? Finally, give me the objection I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have a feeling its the grad student's -- and tell me honestly whether my "revise the description from the inside" criterion is anything more than flexibility with a longer name, because if it isn't, someone in that room will say so before I do.
