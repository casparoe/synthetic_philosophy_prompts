I have spent enough years on committees, review panels, and volunteer boards to have noticed a pattern for which I have no name, and I would like to know whether it corresponds to something established — or, if it does not, to have it named and defined sharply enough that I can tell it apart from the ideas that surround it.

What I keep seeing is this. A group of a dozen people is shown a proposal that is, on any careful reading, a bad one. Nobody objects. Afterwards, five of them tell me privately that they thought it was bad. Each gives a version of the same account: everyone else looked at ease, so I assumed they saw something I had missed, and I did not want to be the one who had missed it. The detail that interests me is that none of them was deferring to any identifiable person's judgment, and none could name an expert in the room whose competence they trusted. What they were treating as evidence was the absence of objection.

A second case, from a different setting. A proposal goes out to a large mailing list and draws forty messages of support and not one of criticism. A newcomer reads that as a verdict — the matter is settled. But the criticisms did not appear because the list's software buries replies arriving after the first day, and because the last person who raised an objection in that forum acquired a reputation for being difficult. Silence and consent look identical from the outside here, and, more to the point, they look identical from the inside as well.

A third: a working practice — a way of framing a question, a reporting convention — that no one has examined in decades, not because anyone believes it sound, but because proposing to examine it is heard as an admission that one does not understand why it is there. The practice survives because questioning it is expensive, and the expense is what keeps the absence of questions in place.

The structure I am trying to isolate is a group settling into a state that no member endorses, where the evidence each member uses for the state being endorsed is the absence of visible disagreement — an absence that is produced by every member's reliance on that same inference. The loop is the whole of it. It is not simply people following one another's choices: no one is following a choice, they are following a non-event. It is not mere timidity either; the members need not fear anything in particular, and several of them are brave in other settings.

There are several neighboring ideas, and I want the boundaries drawn rather than a label handed to me. How does the case above differ from one in which people are copying the observable decisions of whoever moved first, and from one in which each member simply misreads what the others privately think? Where does it sit relative to the suggestion that people misrepresent their opinions in public while holding different ones in private, and relative to the suggestion that a group can collectively commit to something that no member wants? Is there a name for the inferential step itself — taking the absence of dissent as evidence of assent — as distinct from any of those? And is the arrangement stable or unstable, given that the cases I have watched ended abruptly and completely, with the same people who had stayed quiet afterwards insisting that they had always had doubts?

A term would satisfy me only if it earns its keep: it should come with a statement of what the thing is not, a verdict on which of the neighboring concepts is the nearest relative and where it falls short, and a ruling on the boundary cases — whether it matters that some of the silent members were merely indifferent rather than privately opposed, and whether the one indifferent person who speaks anyway has broken the pattern or merely been an accident that broke it.
