I am a graduate student in a population ethics seminar, and next week I have to present on the version of negative utilitarianism that my supervisor favours: suffering is lexically prior to pleasure above a threshold, and commensurable with it below. A classmate and I constructed the following argument to embarrass the view, and I am now worried that it proves far too much, because I cannot see where it goes wrong. (1) There is a threshold magnitude T such that any suffering at or above T is lexically prior to pleasure: no quantity of pleasure, however large, justifies bringing about or permitting such suffering. (2) Suffering below T is commensurable with pleasure: some finite quantity of pleasure does justify a suffering below T. (3) Let S+ be a suffering of magnitude T+ε and S− a suffering of T−ε, where ε is arbitrarily small. (4) S+ and S− are subjectively indistinguishable: no one could ever tell which of the two they were undergoing, and they differ only infinitesimally in magnitude. (5) No requirement of reason can turn on a difference that no possible experience could detect and that amounts to an infinitesimal difference in magnitude; so S+ and S− cannot differ in their status as tradeable or non-tradeable against pleasure. (6) Hence if S− is tradeable for finite pleasure, so is S+; and if S+ is lexically prior to all pleasure, so is S−. The threshold at T therefore marks no normative boundary. (7) By repetition of the same step, either every suffering, however severe, is tradeable for some finite quantity of pleasure, or every suffering, however mild, is lexically prior to all pleasure. (8) The first implies that there is a finite price at which any atrocity becomes worth committing; the second implies that a world containing one mild itch is worse than a world containing no suffering at all, however much happiness it also contains. (9) Both are unacceptable, so the threshold view is false.

I would rather keep the lexical priority of serious suffering than give up either (1) or (2), so I am not inclined to accept either horn of (7); but I also cannot tell which premise is doing illegitimate work, and I am not confident that the fault is merely the one I keep half-seeing. Please diagnose the argument: name the step or steps that should be rejected, explain why they looked compelling in the first place, and price the alternatives — for each repair, say what the threshold view must give up to make it, and whether the view survives in a recognisable form. If the answer is that the threshold must be relocated, sharpened or made vague rather than abandoned, I would especially like to know what stops the same argument from simply running again at the new location or against the vague version, since that is the objection I expect from the room.

Please write your answer in British English, as I shall be circulating it with my seminar notes.
