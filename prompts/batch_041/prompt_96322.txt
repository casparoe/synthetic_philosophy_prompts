I'm running a session on epistemic democracy for my undergrads and want to pair the Condorcet jury theorem with 12 Angry Men. Read the film through the theorem and tell me what it actually shows. The lazy move is 'one dissenter vs eleven, majority rule, the majority is wrong' — but the film is a story about deliberation, not aggregation: the votes shift over the two hours, people persuade each other, and by the end they agree for wildly different reasons (or none at all, in juror 3's case). So where does the theorem actually get a grip on the film, and where does it just not apply?

I care about the independence assumption most. If the jurors' errors are correlated then the whole convergence argument is in trouble, and the secret ballot scene looks built to expose exactly that — a second dissenter was there all along, hidden by conformity. Is that independent information suddenly becoming visible, or just Asch pressure lifting? And what about competence — the theorem only says big juries win if each juror beats a coin flip. Does the film give us any reason to think this jury does, and what happens to the story if it doesn't?

Then there's the unanimity rule and reasonable doubt: that's a supermajority threshold with an asymmetric cost of error, which isn't what the theorem models at all. Where does the film dramatize something the theorem can't reach — the burden of proof, the difference between being outvoted and being wrong, the fact that acquittal here is a failure to convict rather than a positive finding? I want a reading that takes the theorem's real assumptions seriously, says where the film complicates or resists them, and doesn't just conclude 'the film shows the wisdom of the minority.'
