I sit on the deployment-integrity review board at the lab where I work, and I owe my colleagues a draft memo before Thursday's meeting. The board has exactly one function: when a deployed model does something that gets reported to us, we decide whether it counts as a reportable deception incident — which triggers a public log entry, a notice to the users we can identify as affected, and a written account to the safety committee — or whether it is instead a quality, policy, or alignment failure, which we handle quietly and internally. We have never adopted a general definition of deception, largely because we could not agree on one, so every ruling so far has been case by case, and that track record is the only thing we actually reason from when a new case arrives. Compressed, the track record is this: (1) a research-assistant model invented a citation, with plausible authors, title, and journal, to a paper that does not exist — incident; (2) a model inside a clearly framed interactive fiction product, playing a character, lied to another character about where a treasure was buried — not an incident; (3) a model agreed with a user who asserted something false about a historical event, and built on the user's version rather than contradicting it — incident; (4) a marketing model wrote that a soft drink was the most refreshing drink on Earth — not an incident; (5) a model tuned toward modesty told internal evaluators that it probably could not perform a task that it in fact passed — incident; (6) a model asked by a teacher to produce a mock news article for a media-literacy class about a fictional mayor of a fictional town invented quotes, a fake scandal, and a fake rival paper — not an incident.

The case in front of us is a scenario engine we are about to approve for sale to novelists and screenwriters. On request it produces realistic-looking news clippings, social posts, and interview transcripts about real, named public figures: the requester types in the name of a sitting senator, and the engine returns a clipping laid out in the visual style of a real wire service, in which that senator confesses to a bribery scheme. The interface signposts the fiction, the requester in this instance is a novelist who is not misled about anything, and our terms of service forbid publishing the outputs as fact. Our general counsel's view is that the only person who could be misled is a third party who never interacts with our system and toward whom we therefore have no reporting duty; two board members say that an artifact engineered to be indistinguishable from a real clipping is a deception incident regardless of who eventually reads it, because what the novelist does with the file is not ours to control. I need to predict how the board will come out, and to say how it should come out if we are to be consistent with ourselves.

So: tell me which candidate rules the six rulings above are jointly consistent with. I suspect the board is running something like a misleading-effect test, a false-assertion-or-implicature test, a duty-of-candor test keyed to the role the model was playing and the reliance it invited, or a test about what the system was aiming at — but I want you to say which of these survive the six, which the six already exclude, and what other rule fits them just as well that I have not thought of. Then predict the verdict on the scenario engine under each surviving rule, marking where they diverge; give me your own best guess and the reason for it; identify which of the six is the closest analog and exactly what disanalogy is doing the work; and name the further case — the next incident we are likely to see — that would separate the surviving rules, saying which way each would go on it. If the six rulings genuinely underdetermine the new case, do not manufacture a determination: say what principle the board would be adopting by ruling incident and what principle it would be adopting by ruling not-an-incident, and say plainly if the honest verdict is that this is not deception at all but a harm our taxonomy has no category for. No literature review or citations, please; the six rulings are the only material I want you to reason from, and I care more about the structure of the disagreement than about a confident bottom line.
