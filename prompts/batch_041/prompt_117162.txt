I'm a third-year PhD student working on moral uncertainty — mostly the formal end of it, though both my supervisors are straight ethicists, which is its own kind of fun. In three weeks I'm giving a 40-minute paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop (about 20 people, half philosophers, half economists) and I'm already dreading the discussion more than the talk. Here's the thesis I'm defending. The orthodox way of handling moral uncertainty — stick credences on the rival moral theories, then do whatever maximises expected moral value — isn't just under-specified, it's incoherent, because it silently assumes that the "choiceworthiness" scores handed out by different theories sit on a common cardinal scale, and there's no non-arbitrary way to construct that scale. What I propose instead is a settlement model. Each theory ranks the options in its own terms and we never try to convert those rankings into numbers. An option is permissible only if it survives a qualified-majority test: no theory holding, say, a fifth of my credence may rank it below that theory's own median option. Among the survivors we take whatever is top-ranked by at least one theory and tolerable to all the rest. The picture is a parliament with a veto threshold, not a calculator, and the payoff is that it explains why I shouldn't sacrifice one person's basic interests for a large pile of mild benefits even when the expected-value arithmetic says the pile wins. I think the interesting bit — and the bit I'll get eaten alive for — is that the threshold does real work rather than just being risk aversion in fancy dress.

The room is the problem. There'll be two decision theorists who work on imprecise probability and ambiguity aversion, one of whom will definately have a representation theorem ready before I'm off my second slide. There's a Kantian who I'm fairly sure thinks putting credences on moral theories at all is a category mistake and will say so in the first question. There's a friendly social choice economist who will want to know what my aggregation rule does about cycles and strategic misreporting of credences. There's a moral realist who will insist the real trouble isn't intertheoretic comparison but that credences in moral theories aren't credences — you can't have 0.3 belief that lying is wrong. And my flatmate, who does contract theory, keeps telling me the whole thing is a bargaining problem and I should just say so and stop pretending otherwise.
