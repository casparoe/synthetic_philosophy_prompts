A certain family of legal rules interposes a mandatory delay between a person's decision and its taking effect, and lets the later decision override the earlier: a short right of rescission for a purchase made at the door or in a high-pressure sales pitch; a waiting period before a handgun may be taken home; a cooling-off interval before a divorce becomes final; a reflection period before a request for assisted dying is granted. In each case the rule is triggered by how the original decision was made — in haste, under pressure, in a heightened emotional state — and the remedy is time, after which the agent may confirm or withdraw. Yet the same legal system treats coolness as aggravating in the criminal law: premeditation raises the grade of a killing, while a killing in sudden passion is reduced to manslaughter. So a heated decision is treated as not fully the agent's own when it produces a bargain, and as less culpable when it produces a crime; but an impulsive assault is still an assault.

What theory of action, and what theory of the self, does this pattern presuppose? I am especially interested in whether it presupposes a two-stage picture of agency in which the initiation of an action and its reflective endorsement or veto are distinct events — the picture the Libet experiments invite, since a readiness potential in the motor cortex precedes the subject's reported awareness of the urge to move, and Libet himself proposed that consciousness, while it may not initiate action, retains a veto in the interval before the movement. Does the practice require that reading of the experiments, or merely fit it? Does it presuppose that the later, cooler self is the authentic self and the earlier one a usurper, and if so on what grounds, given that the earlier self is ordinarily held fully responsible for what it does? Does it presuppose any indeterminism at all, or only that reflection changes what one does?

Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. Could a wholly forward-looking rationale — protection from manipulation, or from regret, or from decisions one would later disavow — license exactly these rules with no metaphysics of the will at all? And if we replaced the presupposed picture with one on which no temporal stage of a person has authority over the others, or on which deliberation is itself sub-personal and there is no privileged moment of conscious endorsement, which of these rules would have to go, and which would survive on other grounds?
