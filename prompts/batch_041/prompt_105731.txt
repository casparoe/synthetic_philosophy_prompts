You are an expert in formal epistemology and the philosophy of science, with a particular command of the literature on truthlikeness — verisimilitude — and of the logical and epistemological problems it raises. I would like you to help me turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal. Please think carefully, and brainstorm openly before committing to a framing.

The context: I am applying for a one-year research fellowship and I need a proposal of roughly two pages. I work on the problem of verisimilitude, that is, the problem of saying what it is for one theory to be closer to the truth than another, and of whether there is a fact of the matter about that. As I understand the state of play, Popper's original attempts, which compared theories by their classes of true and false consequences, were shown to be formally untenable; and the various successors — comparing theories by the likeness or distance of the worlds they permit, or restricting comparison to those parts of a theory's content that are 'relevant' to the questions at issue — each buy their success at a price. Decades of refinement have left us with a menu of definitions rather than a settled concept. What interests me is less which definition is correct than whether the question 'which of these two false theories is closer to the truth?' has a determinate answer at all, independently of who is asking and why.

My half-formed idea is this. The impossibility results and the proliferation of definitions may both be symptoms of a single mistake: treating truthlikeness as an unconditional relation, holding between a theory and the whole truth, as it were, from nowhere. I want to distinguish unconditional truthlikeness from what I have been calling issue-relative truthlikeness — closeness to the truth with respect to a designated set of questions that the theory is meant to answer, where those questions are represented by a partition of the space of possibilities, a 'target partition'. My conjecture is that once truthlikeness is relativised in this way, the competing definitions converge on the same ordering, and what looked like a deep disagreement about the concept turns out to be a disagreement about something else: whether there is any privileged, interest-independent way of fixing the target partition. The obvious objection, which I want the proposal to face head-on, is that this is not a solution but a change of subject — that it makes truthlikeness an artefact of a partition chosen to yield the answer we wanted, and so forfeits the realist intuition that motivated the problem in the first place.

Please write the proposal. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, given in terms of positions and arguments rather than citations, since I will add the references myself; a statement of the method, saying what I would actually do — whether the work is formal (defining the relativised ordering and proving what it does and does not deliver), conceptual, or both — and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives; a description of what a successful result would look like and, just as importantly, what would count as the project failing or collapsing into the triviality I mentioned; and a realistic assessment of scope for the time available, including an honest note on the risk that the formal work swallows the philosophical point. The proposal must also make the case that the project is worth doing and can be done.

A few constraints. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by description instead, and I will attach the references. Do not pad the proposal with a survey of the field; the conceptual work I need from you is in the framing — finding a formulation of the question that is both open and tractable, and an angle on it that is not the standard one.
