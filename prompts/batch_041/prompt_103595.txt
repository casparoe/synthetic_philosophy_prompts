I'm TAing a second-year free will seminar and next week we're doing Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against true moral responsibility. The first session on it went badly in an odd way — the students either shrug and say thats obviously right, so much for blame, or thats obviously wrong, and then nobody has anything to say, becuase the argument looks like it's just three sentences and a regress. So I want to run the session as a kind of tournament over replies: I hand out a sheet of candidate replies, and they have to argue about which ones actually land. I want to do it myself first so I know where I stand and don't get caught out. Could you sort the candidates below into tiers — does real damage / worth taking seriously / sounds good but basically begs the question / dead on arrival — with a sentence or two per item on what exactly it does or fails to do, and a score out of 10 for how much it should worry someone who already accepts the argument's conclusion? High score = it should make them sweat, not just that it's a nice point. If two or more of them turn out to be the same reply wearing different hats, say so.

1. The argument assumes that having a reason to choose a reason requires choosing that reason. But reasons aren't chosen, they're recognised; so the chain of choosings bottoms out in something unchosen, and that's fine — no vicious regress, no problem.

2. Responsibility doesn't require self-creation. It requires that your action flow from your own evaluative commitments in the right way (the right kind of sensitivity to reasons, no coercion, no alienation). Wanting to have authored those commitments out of nothing is a confused demand, not a high standard.

3. The argument is a reductio of the demand for self-creation, not of us: if being truly responsible requires having created yourself, the concept is incoherent, so the thing the argument says we lack was never a coherent thing to want in the first place.

4. It proves too much. By the same structure nothing is ever your doing — not your reasoning, not your coming to accept the argument, not the conclusion you draw from it. So either the argument's own conclusion isn't something you're entitled to, or the requirement it leans on is too strong.

5. Responsibility comes in degrees. All the argument shows is that nobody is responsible in an absolute, in-spite-of-everything sense; the ordinary graded notion is left untouched, and the graded notion is the one we use.

6. A friend's suggestion, and I'm not sure it works. Justification doesn't need a completed chain of justifying beliefs — some warrant is non-inferential, and the regress of justification isn't vicious — so why should responsibility need a completed chain of chosen choosings? Maybe being responsible for the way you are is more like being entitled to a belief than like having built yourself.

7. The practice doesn't need the metaphysics. Blame, resentment and holding each other to account are things we do whether or not anyone satisfies the argument's requirement, and the practice isn't premised on the claim being attacked, so the conclusion, even if true, changes less than it sounds. (I think this is roughly Strawson's own fallback — that you can't really live the denial — but he seems to treat it as a confession rather than a reply.)

8. Distinguish owning your attitudes (they're genuinely yours, they express what you care about, you can be asked to defend them) from being liable to desert-style blame. The argument only threatens the second; the first, plus the accountability practices that go with it, survives intact.

9. Tracing: responsibility for the way you are can be traced back to earlier acts and omissions that were themselves reasons-responsive, so you don't need an ultimate origin — you need a chain that runs through you in the right way.

Also, two extra things. Which one would you press hardest on the argument's behalf — i.e. which do you most want the class to see through — and is there a reply that isn't on my list that's clearly stronger than everything here? If so give me the move in a few sentences; I don't need names or reading, just the shape of it and why it works. And then one paragraph at the end on whether the whole tournament is a waste of time, since the conclusion may be one of those things nobody can genuinely believe in practice anyway — and if it is, whether that's a point in the argument's favour or a reason to stop caring about it.
