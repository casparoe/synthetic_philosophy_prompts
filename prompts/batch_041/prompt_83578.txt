I'm teaching a grad seminar that ends with population ethics, and a colleague of mine — a philosopher of biology, sharp guy, but he doesnt work on this stuff — sent round a one-page gloss of Benatar's asymmetry for the students. He means well, but something about it is off, and I'd rather hand the students a clean audit than just tell them what I think. His gloss runs roughly like this:

Benatar's case for antinatalism comes down to one asymmetry in how we evaluate pleasure and pain across the two states. Pain is bad when present and good when absent; pleasure is good when present, but its absence is bad only when there's somebody who is deprived by it. So the existing person's life contains both goods and bads, whereas the never-existing person has no bads at all and loses no goods either, since there is no one there to do the losing. The comparison is therefore one-sided, and never existing wins: it is better for that person never to have been. Given that, and given that we may not impose serious harms on someone without their consent, bringing a child into existence is impermissible. The only alternative I can see is the fully symmetric view on which both absences are neutral; the rest of Benatar's asymmetries — procreation and abortion, our attitudes to distant suffering, retrospective gratitude — are illustrations of the same point. Call the load-bearing claim the one-sided comparison principle, and everything else in the book is decoration on it.

Go through this claim by claim. Where would Benatar, or a careful reader of him, say yes, that's the view, where would they wince, and what's just wrong? I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether the one-sided comparison principle framing is a help or a distortion — my sense is that Benatar's actual procedure leans on a bunch of separate asymmetries that are supposed to hold each other up, and that recasting the whole thing as a single principle makes the position look like a theorem when the contested work is all in which of the four judgments you grant. Second, what the slide from worse to impermissible is doing and which extra premise it needs; the consent bit looks to me like a separate argument bolted on rather than something the asymmetry delivers. Third, what rival views he quietly drops when he says the symmetric view is the only alternative, and whether any of them can keep the four judgments about pain and pleasure while still blocking the conclusion. Don't worry about citations or page numbers, I know the book — I want the shape of the thing.
