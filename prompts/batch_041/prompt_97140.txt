Independence has stopped being a hypothetical where I live. What was for years a fringe cause now commands enough support that a referendum within the next decade is a realistic possibility, and since someone close to me has become active in the campaign and I have been asked to take a public position, I have spent the past few months trying to work out what I actually believe rather than what side I am expected to be on. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures my verdicts on the cases below; where nothing captures all of them, I want to know which verdicts have to be given up, and why.

My verdicts run as follows. Scotland's referendum in 2014 was a legitimate exercise, and Westminster was right to agree to it; had the vote gone the other way, independence would have been legitimate as well, even though the rest of the United Kingdom was never asked. Quebec's referendum in 1995 was likewise a legitimate process, notwithstanding that the question was ambiguous and the result turned on a fraction of a percent; if anything, that near-tie tells me the threshold should have been higher, not that the exercise was illegitimate. Catalonia's referendum in 2017, and the declaration that followed it, were wrong. I do not think Madrid's response — suspending self-government, prosecuting the organizers — was proportionate, and I think it made matters worse, but neither of those facts makes the secession itself legitimate. Kosovo's declaration in 2008 was legitimate, even though Serbia never consented and no negotiated settlement was reached. Crimea's vote and annexation in 2014 were illegitimate; the coercion and the foreign involvement settle the matter, and I do not think the preferences of the people there, whatever they were, could have made it valid. South Sudan's independence in 2011 was legitimate, but that seems to me to rest on the decades of war and the negotiated agreement rather than on the ballot alone. A region that is well treated, shares a language and political culture with the rest of the state, and wants out purely to stop paying taxes or equalization to poorer regions should not be permitted to leave, even if a clear and durable majority wants it. And if Scotland had voted yes, the island groups that voted heavily against independence should have been allowed to stay in the United Kingdom if they wished.

The principle I have been working with, which I suspect is too crude, is this: a group may secede if it forms a durable majority within a defined territory, if the parent state has had a genuine opportunity to address its grievances, and if the new state can be expected to protect its own minorities. That principle appears to license the wealthy region leaving to escape its obligations, which I deny, and it appears to license Catalonia, which I also deny; yet tightening it to require serious injustice, as in Kosovo or South Sudan, would make Scotland and Quebec look like lucky exceptions rather than exercises of a right. It also does not tell me what to say about the islands, where I seem to be applying the majority principle one level below the level at which I just refused to apply it.

Please repair this. State the principle or principles precisely, tie each clause to the verdict that motivates it, and tell me how they come out on cases I have not listed: a region that suffers no injustice but whose central government lawfully refuses, indefinitely, ever to permit a referendum; a secessionist region that would itself mistreat a minority within it; and a region whose majority wants independence but whose opponents would become a permanent minority in the new state with no route back. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best version of the principle cannot accommodate, and whether it is the verdict or the principle that ought to give way.
