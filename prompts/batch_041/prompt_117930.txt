I want a diagnosis of why the epiphenomenalism debate never resolves, and my own hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the two sides are not actually disagreeing about causation at all but about what it takes for a judgment to be *about* an experience; if that is right, say so and draw out what follows, and if it is wrong, tell me what the real axis of disagreement is.

Some background, in case it helps you aim. I have been reading philosophy of mind blogs and the rationalist forums for about fifteen years, long enough to have watched the consciousness threads circle the same loop many times, and I have never once seen anyone change sides in it. The loop runs roughly as follows. Someone defends epiphenomenalism: experience exists, it is caused by brain states, and it causes nothing, because the physical story is causally closed; the qualitative residue rides along, and our talk about it is produced by the same physical machinery that produces the experience. Someone then raises the objection that Chalmers treats as a constraint on any theory of consciousness in *The Conscious Mind* — that a theory must be able to dispel epiphenomenalism rather than entrench it. If experiences are causally inert, my judgment that I am currently undergoing a reddish experience cannot have been caused by that reddish experience, so either the judgment is not about the experience at all, or it is about it only by accident; and if by accident, then the epiphenomenalist's own assertion that experience exists is unwarranted, and the position stultifies itself. The epiphenomenalist has two standard replies. The modest one: the judgment is caused by the neural correlate, which is reliably correlated with the experience, and reliable correlation is enough for warrant, even without a causal path from experience to judgment. The ambitious one: the phenomenal state fixes the content of the judgment by something other than causation — the judgment is answerable to the experience, and that answerability, not the experience's causal efficacy, is what makes the judgment about it. The critic then says the modest reply quietly converts the judgment into a purely physical state whose content has nothing to do with experience, so the self-undermining charge survives intact, while the ambitious reply merely relabels the explanatory gap as a content relation and asserts that it obtains. Everyone nods, everyone restates, and the thread dies until it is reborn six months later.

What I want is not a survey of positions but a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. Is it verbal, turning on whether *cause* means difference-making or productive generation, such that the two sides could both be right under their own reading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — some result about what reliability without tracking can support, or a genuine inconsistency rather than a mere implausibility in the epiphenomenalist's assertion? Are the sides answering different questions under one name, one about the etiology of the utterance and one about the grounding of its aboutness? Or does it bottom out in a bedrock principle that one side accepts and the other simply does not — a no-coincidence constraint to the effect that an acceptable theory may not make our reliability about X a brute accident — which would explain the total absence of movement? One further seed, from a reading group I sit in on: a friend proposed splitting the self-undermining objection into an etiological question (what caused the utterance) and a constitutive question (what makes the utterance about experience), and claimed that the entire impasse consists in nobody specifying which one the charge needs. I found that promising and also suspect it may just be a redescription. Tell me which, and tell me what would count as progress here — a formal result, a distinction, a decision about what a judgment's being about something requires — or whether the honest answer is that nothing should be expected to move it and why.

For the response: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question.
