I'm a senior in a seminar on social epistemology, and my term paper is an attempt to turn the unilateralist's curse into something a working researcher could actually use, rather than just another puzzle I can recite. The idea, as I understand it, is this: when many similarly placed people each independently act whenever they judge the expected value of acting to be positive, and their judgments are noisy, the probability that at least one of them does something that turns out to be harmful grows with the number of people — so your own confident estimate that an action is beneficial is weaker evidence about the action than it feels like, once you realize you are only one of many who are weighing it.

My intended user is a researcher facing a one-off, hard-to-reverse decision whose consequences depend on what others in the same position choose to do — publishing a protocol with obvious dual-use potential, say, or running a small outdoor experiment. They face this kind of call a few times a year, the stakes are high, they are not a decision theorist, and they have about half an hour and one colleague to consult before committing.

Please write a decision procedure they can run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it returns a firm "do not act," and the point at which it must hand the decision back to a person because the dispute is about values rather than facts. I want it honest about the assumptions the argument leans on — in particular, I suspect the independence of the other agents' errors is doing more work than anyone admits, and I'd like the procedure to state plainly what it can and cannot do when errors are correlated, or when the action is secret and no one can observe whether others have already moved. Then run it on two cases: a lab that believes publishing a synthesis route for a dangerous pathogen is net positive, knowing several other labs are close to the same result; and a handful of researchers each independently weighing a small geoengineering field trial, where acting is visible and only partly reversible. At least one of these should come out "do not act" against the user's own prior, and I'd like you to close by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
