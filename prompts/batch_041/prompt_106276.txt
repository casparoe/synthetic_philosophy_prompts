I'm a postgraduate student, and this term I'm working as a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate module in contemporary moral philosophy. I've been asked to mark the short-answer section of the mid-term paper, and since I'm new to marking I'd rather get a careful second opinion than simply scrawl a number at the foot of the page. The module spent a fortnight on moral disagreement and cooperation, and one of the ideas we covered was that people with quite different moral outlooks can sometimes reach agreements about what to do that leave both of them better off. I want to be fair to the student and also to be able to justify the mark if the convenor asks me about it.

The question, worth ten marks, was: "Two people hold different moral views. Explain what is meant by a 'moral trade' between them, why such a trade can leave both parties better off by their own moral standards, and assess whether the overall benefit of a trade can be expressed in a single common currency. Illustrate your answer with an example." The mark scheme allots up to four marks for a correct account of what a moral trade is and why it can be mutually advantageous, up to three marks for a relevant and correctly analysed example, and up to three marks for the treatment of the common-currency question.

One student wrote: "A moral trade is an agreement between two people who hold different moral views, in which each party performs an action that the other regards as morally valuable. The trade is possible because the two parties attach different moral weights to the same actions, so an action that is a serious sacrifice for one may cost the other almost nothing. Suppose, for instance, that Ava believes animal suffering to be of great moral importance, while Ben believes that relieving global poverty is the overriding priority and is largely indifferent to animals. Ava agrees to make a substantial donation to an anti-poverty charity; Ben agrees to give up eating meat. Ava regards her donation as worthwhile but would rather have kept the money; Ben regards giving up meat as no moral loss at all, merely a mild inconvenience. Each party has secured from the other an action that they rate highly, at a cost that they themselves rate low, so both are better off by their own standards, and the trade is a Pareto improvement. Because both parties gain, it follows that we can place their gains on a single scale and add them together. If Ava gains four units and Ben gains six, the trade has produced ten units of moral value in total; it is therefore not merely good for them but good overall. This is why moral trade is sometimes described as a kind of offsetting, rather as carbon offsetting allows one to neutralise one's own emissions by paying for reductions elsewhere: each party compensates for the moral cost of what they do by securing a benefit on the other side. The only real precondition is that the parties disagree about the moral value of the actions in question; if they agreed entirely, there would be nothing to trade. It is worth stressing that neither party need give up their own moral view — Ava still holds that animals matter and Ben still prioritises poverty; they merely act, for instrumental reasons, in ways that the other approves of."
