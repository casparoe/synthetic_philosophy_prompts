I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about binding one's future self, and about the political version of the same thing, and my verdicts do not obviously hang together. Here is where I land, case by case.

Ulysses has himself lashed to the mast and instructs the crew to ignore his later pleas and, if need be, to tie him tighter. I think the crew should do exactly that. The man who gave the order is the one whose will counts, and the shrieking man is not to be obeyed.

A friend who knows he makes ruinous purchases after midnight pays a company to hold a sum of his money in escrow and to release it only at year's end, with a heavy fee if he asks for it early. In March he wants the money for a holiday; the company refuses, as the contract provides. I think the company is right and he has no complaint. He bought precisely this constraint.

But when a third friend merely installs blocking software on his own laptop, with no one else involved and no penalty attached, and a month later wants to uninstall it, I think he may simply uninstall it. Nothing binds him. He made himself a promise and he may break it.

A woman, while lucid, signs a directive: if she becomes severely demented, she is to receive no antibiotics for pneumonia or any other life-prolonging treatment. Years later she is demented, seems placid and even cheerful, and when the pneumonia comes she pulls away from the needle without understanding what she is refusing. I think the directive should be honored and she should be allowed to die.

A man, in a low period, signs the same kind of directive. Two years later, in another low period, he takes an overdose and then, half an hour in, calls an ambulance himself and says he wants to live. The paramedics find the directive. I think they should treat him. This is the one I keep going back and forth on.

Now the political cases. A legislature passes a statute containing a clause that it may be repealed only by a two-thirds majority of a future legislature. A later legislature repeals it by simple majority. I think the later legislature is entirely within its rights; the earlier one did not bind it.

A country's constitution entrenches a bill of rights so that it can never be amended, not even unanimously. A later generation, by an overwhelming majority, wants to amend one provision. I think they are stuck with it through ordinary politics, though I would allow a genuine constitutional convention to start over from scratch.

But when a constitution entrenches the permanent disenfranchisement of a religious minority, and a later generation wants to restore the franchise, I think the entrenchment is void and they may restore it, whatever the amendment clause says.

So earlier selves bind later selves sometimes and not others, and the pattern is not simply that individuals are bound while collectives are free: the demented woman's earlier self binds her, yet the later legislature escapes its predecessor. I suspect the real variable is something about whether the later self counts as a competent agent, but the political cases then seem to pull the other way, and the suicide case pulls against the dementia case.

What I want is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible. State it precisely enough to be applied to new cases, tell me which clause each part is doing work for, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save and which one I should give up. I would also like to know what the surviving principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a psychiatric patient who, when well, signs an advance agreement consenting to a depot injection that is to be given even if, when unwell, he refuses it, and who then refuses it. If no single principle can cover the ground, say which two I would have to choose between and what turns on the choice.
