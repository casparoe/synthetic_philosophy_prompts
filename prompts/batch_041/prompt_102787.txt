You are asked to write an essay of roughly 1,500 words on a controversy in metaphysics: whether the question of why there is something rather than nothing admits of a genuine answer or is in some way defective.

So that the essay engages a specific proposal rather than rehearsing the standard positions, consider the following.

The Principle of Explanatory Symmetry (PES): For any contingent fact F and its negation not-F, F and not-F stand in exactly the same relation to explanation. Neither is intrinsically more intelligible than the other, and neither calls for explanation any more than the other does. It follows that the cosmological question is ill-posed. The question presupposes that nothing is the default or privileged condition and that something is a surprising departure from it; but existence and nonexistence are perfectly symmetric, so there is no surprise to be explained. The universe's existing is no more remarkable than its not existing would have been, and the demand for a reason is a leftover of a habit of thought that treats being as an achievement and nonbeing as a baseline.

A critic replies that PES proves too much. If F and not-F are always on a par, then no contingent fact is ever in need of explanation, and the entire practice of asking why things are as they are is discredited. Yet that practice is not obviously discredited; the defender of PES therefore owes an account of why the symmetry is fatal for the cosmological question but harmless for ordinary explanation.

Write an essay that weighs this dispute and reaches a definite verdict. It should do all of the following, though not necessarily in this order.

First, state clearly what would have to be true for the cosmological question to have a genuine answer, and what would have to be true for it to be defective. Make the criteria explicit enough that they can be applied, rather than leaving them as a general impression.

Second, assess PES on its merits. Is the symmetry it asserts real, or does it smuggle in an asymmetry of its own? Consider whether the critic's objection is decisive, and whether PES can be restricted in a principled way rather than simply stipulated to apply only where it is wanted.

Third, examine at least two attempts to give the question a real answer and say whether they succeed. Candidates might include the claim that the world exists of necessity and so needs no explanation; the claim that it is explained non-causally, for instance by its value or goodness; the claim that explanation is always internal to the world and so cannot be demanded of the world as a whole; and the claim that the question is answered simply by identifying what there is. You may choose others, but treat each seriously enough that a defender would recognize it.

Fourth, address the coherence of nothing. Distinguish, if you can, an empty world — a world with no concrete objects, but still a world with its own structure — from no world at all, and say whether the question, and PES along with it, conflates the two. Consider also the difference between asking why there is anything at all and asking why there is this rather than something else, and whether the two questions stand or fall together.

Finally, reach and justify a conclusion. Possible verdicts include that the question has a genuine answer of some kind; that it is answerable only by a brute fact and that this is acceptable; that it is defective because of the symmetry PES describes; or that it is defective for some other reason. Do not end on a noncommittal note, and do not treat the choice among these as merely a matter of temperament.

Argue from reasoning rather than from authority. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and do not attribute positions to named authors; present and evaluate the arguments themselves. Define any technical terms you introduce, and make clear when a step is a substantive claim rather than a definitional one. Write in American English.
