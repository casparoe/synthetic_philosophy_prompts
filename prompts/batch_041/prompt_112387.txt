I retired from physics six years ago and have been trying to make up for lost time by reading philosophy, mostly philosophy of math, and I've fallen in with a small reading group of other retirees that has spent the last three months crawling through the axiom of choice. I must have used Zorns lemma a dozen times over my career (functional analysis mostly, once in a statistical mechanics paper I would rather forget) without ever once asking whether it was true, so the whole business has been a shock to the system. Here's my problem. At our last meeting one of the group, a retired topologist who really ought to know better, handed round a short argument which appears to show that Zorn's lemma is a theorem of ordinary set theory — no choice principle needed — and hence, since everyone tells me Zorn's lemma and the axiom of choice are equivalent, that the axiom of choice is provable too, and that all the independence results I keep reading about must be about something else. I've been staring at it for a week and I cannot find the line to give up.

(1) Let (P, ≤) be a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound in P. (2) Let C be the collection of all chains in P, ordered by inclusion; since every chain in P is a subset of P, C is a set. (3) Every chain in C has an upper bound in C, namely its union: if D is a family of chains of P that is totally ordered by inclusion, then the union of D is again a chain of P, because any two of its elements lie in some single member of D, and that member is a chain. (4) So by Zorn's lemma, C has a maximal element, i.e. there is a chain M in P that cannot be enlarged. (5) By (1), M has an upper bound u in P. (6) If u were not maximal in P there would be some v in P with u < v, and then every element of M is ≤ u ≤ v, so M together with v would be a chain strictly larger than M, contradicting (4); hence u is maximal in P. (7) Therefore every nonempty poset in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element, which is just Zorn's lemma. (8) But Zorn's lemma implies the well-ordering theorem, which implies the axiom of choice, and the axiom of choice is supposed to be independant of ZF. So either (1) through (6) prove far more than anyone thinks they can, or something I've been told about independence is wrong.

Tell me which line to reject, why it looks so innocent on the page, and what I actually pay for rejecting it. A couple of us think (3) is where the sleight of hand is — that the union of infinitely many chains need not be a chain — but once I noticed that the family itself is a chain I convinced myself that's a dead end, and my own money is on (6) or (7), though nothing I try holds up when I write it out. I would like to keep (2) and (3) if at all possible, since they strike me as pure bookkeeping, but if the price is that one of them has to go then say so plainly and tell me what else falls over with it. And if it turns out this thing does prove something, I want to know exactly what it proves and why that is not the same as proving Zorn's lemma.
