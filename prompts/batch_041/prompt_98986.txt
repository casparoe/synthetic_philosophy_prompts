I teach a second-year undergraduate course on practical rationality — roughly, the philosophy of action and intention with some decision theory mixed in — and I have just finished setting and marking the mid-term. One of the questions concerns Kavka's toxin puzzle, and before I finalise the marks I would like an independent read on a script, partly because I want to be sure I am not being either too generous or too harsh. Our marking scheme rewards, in order of weight: an accurate reconstruction of the case; a clear statement of what exactly is supposed to be puzzling about it; and careful handling of the question whether intending to drink the toxin requires believing that one will drink it. The scale is out of 20, where 17–20 is excellent, 14–16 good, 11–13 satisfactory, 8–10 a weak pass, and below 8 a fail.

The question I set was: "In Kavka's toxin puzzle, an eccentric billionaire promises to pay you one million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but will do no lasting harm, and the money will be in your account before the time for drinking arrives, whether or not you drink. Explain why the case is thought to be puzzling, and assess the claim that a fully rational agent can both form the intention at midnight and then rationally decline to drink the toxin the next day. In your answer, address whether intending to drink requires believing that one will drink."

The script I am working through reads as follows:

"The toxin puzzle, which comes from Nozick's early work on Newcomb's problem, runs as follows. An eccentric billionaire will pay me one million dollars if, at midnight tonight, I intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make me painfully ill for a day but will do no lasting damage, and the money will be in my account before the time for drinking arrives, whether or not I actually drink. I am free to change my mind once I have been paid.

The case is puzzling because two compelling lines of thought pull in opposite directions. On the one hand, intending to drink looks like the rational thing to do: it is the only way to get the money, and at the moment of intending I need not do anything unpleasant. On the other hand, drinking the toxin is plainly pointless. Once the money is paid, the illness buys me nothing, so a rational agent would not drink.

I think the puzzle's force comes from the second line of thought. A rational agent knows that drinking the toxin is pointless, and so a rational agent knows that she will not drink it. But one cannot intend to do what one knows one will not do: an intention of that kind would not be an intention at all, merely a wish. So the agent cannot form the intention at midnight, and the million dollars is out of reach.

The obvious objection is that I could intend to drink at midnight and then simply change my mind after the money arrives. I think this is the most interesting part of the case, and I am not sure how to answer it: the offer pays for the intention alone, so an intention that I know I will abandon seems to be exactly what the case forbids, but it is hard to see why I could not form it. Perhaps the case is simply under-described at this point.

So the puzzle shows that intending is not rational in the way that acting is: an intention is only as good as the action it leads to, and here the action is one that no rational agent would perform."

I would like a grade out of 20 together with the specific comments that justify it, organised as a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. In particular, please separate the things the answer gets right from any claims that are inaccurate, and within the latter distinguish errors of fact (including misattributions) from steps in the argument that do not follow; where a claim is ambiguous or merely under-developed rather than plainly wrong, say so and treat it accordingly, and note what a stronger answer would have said at each of those points.
