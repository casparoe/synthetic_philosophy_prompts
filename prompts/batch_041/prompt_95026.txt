I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have sat on the advisory board of a small private foundation that makes grants to public-history, memorial, and oral-history projects — roughly two dozen a year, mostly outside the United States. Our board meets on Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting. I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

Approach the problem as an expert in comparative memory politics and transitional justice: someone who knows how Germany, Japan, Turkey, and the American South have each handled — and each failed to handle — the memory of large historical crimes, and who also knows the philosophy of what a funder can legitimately demand of a project it does not itself carry out. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a few paragraphs of notes to yourself in which you lay out the competing considerations and work out where you land; the notes are for your own thinking and are not part of the deliverable. Then write the memo. Structure it as a document I can hand to colleagues: addressed to the board, dated, a bottom line up front, then the reasoning.

The case. A municipal cultural association in a city in southeastern Turkey has applied for about $180,000 over two years for an oral-history archive and a small public installation concerning the deportations of 1915. In every document the association has submitted, the events are called "the events of 1915" and the deportations "the relocations." The words "Armenian" and "genocide" appear nowhere in the application. In a phone call I sat in on, the association's director explained that naming the events plainly would expose her staff and the municipality to prosecution under the law that makes it a crime to insult Turkishness, and would end the project before the first interview is recorded. She says the archive is the argument: several hundred hours of testimony from survivors' grandchildren, recorded in their own villages, in their own words, while the last generation who heard the stories firsthand is still alive. A foreign board's demand for the correct vocabulary, she says, would buy us a clean conscience and cost the project its existence.

Our grant guidelines were rewritten two years ago after a review of our German portfolio. They require applicants to "name the events their project concerns without euphemism," and we have enforced that requirement twice, once against a Hungarian applicant and once against a Japanese one. Two board members have already said they will vote no if the euphemism stands: they argue that a foundation that funds a project calling a genocide "the events" is funding the denialist frame, whatever the archive contains, and that the testimonies themselves will be read for decades through a label we agreed to. A third board member argues the opposite. Our naming rule, she says, was written with Germany in mind — with what the Germans call coming to terms with the past, where the state has already conceded the crime and the struggle is against forgetting. In Turkey the state still denies it and the struggle is against silence. A rule that demands the vocabulary of countries that have already repented guarantees that the countries that haven't will be the ones with no archive at all. She calls our guideline a parochial standard dressed up as a universal one, and I cannot dismiss the point.

The director has proposed a compromise of her own: fund the oral-history archive only, deposit the recordings with a university repository abroad under a multiyear embargo, and either drop the installation in Turkey altogether or build a modest municipal memory garden that names no events. Our bylaws permit us to condition a grant but forbid us from "materially altering the scope of a funded project" without the applicant's written consent. The director has said she will not consent to any wording that puts her staff at risk; she would rather withdraw the application and try again in five years.

One more thing, which is why I am asking you rather than writing this myself. I have spent a decade writing fiction about people who live inside regimes that require a particular vocabulary, and I am aware that my instinct to protect the archive may be the instinct of a novelist who would rather have the testimony than the truth in capital letters. If you think that instinct is distorting the recommendation, say so plainly.

What I need from you:

- A concrete recommendation — fund as submitted, fund with conditions, fund only part of it, or decline — stated in the first paragraph, with the exact conditions if there are conditions.
- A timeline: what we tell the applicant, when, what we do if she refuses, and what the board should be prepared to say publicly.
- The reasoning, set out so that a colleague who disagrees can engage it rather than just reject it.
- The strongest case against your recommendation, stated at full strength before you answer it. Do not soften it to make your answer look easy.
- A short section proposing the general standard the foundation should adopt for projects in countries that still deny the events in question, and a test of that standard against the three other settings we fund in — Germany, Japan, and the American South — no more than one paragraph each, so that I can see whether the standard gives me an answer I can live with everywhere.
- A frank note on which part of this is a question of values that no standard will settle, and, if your recommendation turns on some fact about the local situation that I have not given you and that you cannot check, say which fact it is and how it would change your answer.

No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I want an argument I can circulate, not scholarship. Write in American English, and keep it under about 1,200 words, since my colleagues will read it on a phone in the ten minutes before the meeting.
