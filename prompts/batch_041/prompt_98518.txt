Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences I have been helping to run a graduate reading group on the foundations of probability, drawing about fifteen students from philosophy, statistics, and the sciences; we meet for a single 90-minute session. Having spent my working life reporting intervals rather than point estimates, I want an activity that makes the group feel the force of imprecise probabilities rather than merely hear the case for them, and in particular that forces them to run into two things a single-number credence conceals: first, that a rational agent may hold an interval of credences and defend it against a bookie; second, that this latitude has costs, because conditioning on new evidence can widen rather than narrow one's interval for a target proposition (the phenomenon the literature calls dilation), and because once an interval is in play the agent must still act, so a further decision rule has to be chosen, with maximin, maximality, and E-admissibility giving different verdicts on the same set of options.

Please design the session as a game with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that the participants meet dilation and the choice-rule problem themselves, in the doing, before either is named. If you build a concrete dilation example into the mechanics, work the arithmetic through and confirm that conditioning really does widen the interval. Include a debrief that maps what happened onto lower and upper probability, credal sets, and the decision-rule menu; say plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how I should handle it; and be candid about which feature of the imprecise-probability view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion myself. No lecture up front, and they should not hear the term imprecise probability until the debrief.
