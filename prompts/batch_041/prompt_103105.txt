A colleague of mine, a sociologist who works on labor markets, has taken to announcing in seminars that generational labels are just astrology for social scientists. Her case runs roughly like this: Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z and the rest carve a continuous variable, namely year of birth, into a handful of bins whose boundaries no one can justify except by appeal to folk usage; the personality sketches attached to each bin are vague and flattering enough that almost anyone can find themselves in whichever one they were assigned; and the whole apparatus seems built to resist falsification rather than invite it. From this she draws a practical conclusion — that we should purge generational categories from our survey instruments and our regression specifications and speak only of age, period, and cohort effects in the technical sense — and she treats the analogy as what licenses that conclusion. I find the parallel striking but I doubt it can carry that much weight, and I would like to know precisely what it does and does not establish.

Please audit the analogy. Start with an explicit mapping: say what corresponds to the twelve signs, to the birth date, to the horoscope column, to the astrologer, to the reader who finds the description uncannily apt, and to the astronomer who debunks the whole business. Then tell me where the mapping holds and, more importantly, where it breaks — and among the breakages, distinguish the incidental ones from the ones that actually matter for the use my colleague is putting the analogy to. I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the analogy illicitly imports a feature of astrology that generational claims do not share, namely a purported causal influence of the classifying variable itself, and whether that changes the verdict on whether a generation could be a real social kind even if it is not a natural one. Second, whether the charge of unfalsifiability is doing any independent work here, given that generational claims are at least in principle testable against longitudinal data in a way that horoscopes are not. Third, whether the fact that people act on generational labels, and are treated differently because of them, can make the categories self-fulfilling in a way that no star sign ever is — and if so, whether that tells in favor of generations being real kinds or merely in favor of them being effective fictions.

Finally, tell me whether her practical conclusion — drop the categories — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether that conclusion was being carried by the analogy all along. If the analogy is the wrong one for her purpose, propose a better one, give me the same kind of mapping for it, and say what it would license instead.
