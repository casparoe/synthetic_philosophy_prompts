Write a syndicated advice column — one reader's letter and the columnist's reply — in the voice of an agony aunt who has made a certain decision theory the rule of her life. Before you write, hold the theory in mind in roughly these terms: an act is not judged by what it yields in the world you happen to inhabit; it is judged as the output of a policy, and the policy is to be chosen as though from behind a veil, before you know which branch of the world you will occupy — including branches in which you never learn the outcome, never exist, or are the victim of a coin you never see. On this view, "what should I do now?" is close to a category error, because the decision was already made by the policy you carry around; a promise binds you even in the world where the other party did not keep theirs; and you may owe something to a person who pays you only in a world where you cannot pay them back.

The letter: a reader writes that their partner has proposed a "reciprocal commitment" — each will sign a contract binding them to a costly act precisely in the branch where the other does not reciprocate — and asks whether to sign. The same reader adds, almost as an aside, that a stranger has demanded $100 on the ground that he flipped a coin determining whether the reader would receive an unexpected windfall, and has warned that "the others like him" are watching how the reader answers.

Now write the reply. Its humor must come from taking the logic seriously rather than from inventing absurdity. Requirements:

- The reply is deadpan, unhurried, and warm. It never winks at the reader, never names a theory, a philosopher, or a book, and uses no technical jargon. A reader who has never encountered the theory should nonetheless be able to reconstruct it from the column alone.
- The columnist follows the argument where it goes, including to conclusions a reader will find distasteful, and does not soften them into ordinary common sense. If she is tempted to flinch — at the blackmail, at the contract, at the prospect of being bound by a branch of the world she will never see — she may notice the temptation and decline it, but she must not break character or dissolve the difficulty with a platitude.
- Invent the columnist's name and the title of her column; period pastiche is welcome. Do not name any living person.

Format the response with these headings, exactly as given, in this order:

1. "The Letter" — about 180 words.
2. "The Reply" — about 450 words.
3. "A Note on the Choices Made" — about 200 words, in your own voice as the writer, explaining what was satirized, which implications you chose to press, and where you had to work hardest to keep the columnist internally consistent.
