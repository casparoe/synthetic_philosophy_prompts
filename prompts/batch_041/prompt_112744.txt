I am trying to adjudicate a disagreement that keeps splitting along national lines: whether the Scandinavian disposition to trust government is a rational accomplishment or merely a fortunate equilibrium, and whether the American disposition to suspect it is a civic virtue or a failure of nerve. I do not want a verdict yet. I want the specification that any verdict would have to satisfy.

Write the desiderata: the requirements that any adequate theory of the reasonableness of political trust must meet before it is entitled to say that one of these attitudes is more reasonable than the other. For each requirement, give the case, phenomenon, or consideration that motivates it; mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which merely reflect the commitments of a particular camp; and flag any pair that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. That last part is what I most want to see.

Build the list so that it is not vacuous. In particular, make it able to handle each of the following, which I treat as constraints on the theory rather than as optional extras:

1. The object of trust is an institution, not a person. Institutions have no single will, no face, and no capacity for the kind of reciprocal regard that personal trust presupposes. The theory must say what trust in such an object even is, without collapsing it into prediction on the one side or approval on the other.

2. Trust and reliance must be kept apart. A citizen may reliably predict that a tax authority will process a return while trusting it in no sense that could be betrayed, and may trust an institution whose behaviour he cannot predict at all. The theory must locate the difference and say why it bears on reasonableness.

3. Trust is dimensionally uneven. The same institution may be rationally trusted for competence, distrusted for goodwill, and trusted again for procedural fairness, and a citizen may be right to hold all three attitudes at once. A theory that treats trust as a single scalar fails here.

4. Trust is unevenly distributed across institutions within a single polity. The Scandinavian pattern is not uniform trust but a specific profile: high confidence in some agencies, suspicion of others, and a large component of trust that is really trust in other citizens as mediated by the state. The theory must be able to decompose a national attitude into its parts rather than score it as one number.

5. Reasonableness must be assessable without the assessor knowing the outcome in advance. If the theory can certify trust only after the trusted institution has proved trustworthy, it has said nothing about whether trust was reasonable when it was extended, and it cannot handle the bootstrapping problem: suspicion can be self-fulfilling, and so can trust.

Now a case that I think does the most work, and that I have not seen discussed in these terms. Consider a small, homogeneous, high-trust polity — call it Nordia — in which the observed trust is sustained less by institutional performance than by mutual monitoring and social sanction: citizens trust the state in part because they trust one another to punish defection, and the state's good behaviour is itself a by-product of that mutual surveillance. From the outside, Nordia looks exactly like a society whose institutions have earned their trust. Is the Nordian attitude reasonable? What does your list of desiderata force you to say about it, and does any requirement have to be relaxed in order to say it?

Finally, briefly — a few sentences per family at most — say how interest-based accounts (trust as the trusted party's stake in continuing the relationship), moral-attitude accounts (trust as a stance that outruns the evidence and can be betrayed), and evidence-based accounts (trust as a calibrated credence in institutional performance) fare against the list, and name the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise popular answers. Assume throughout that the theory is meant to adjudicate a real disagreement among citizens of different polities, not to satisfy an ideal observer.
