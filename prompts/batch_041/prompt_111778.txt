Hi — I'm a philosophy professor, and through a series of bad decisions I'm now chairing our university's commemorations committee, the body that handles petitions to rename buildings, pull down statues, and rescind honorary degrees. We meet twice a year, we get alot of petitions, and each one gets about twenty minutes of discussion by people who arent philosophers — a dean, two students, a historian, and me — after which we send a recommendation to the president, who has the actual authority and who mainly cares about not ending up on the front page. Right now we decide by vibes, and I don't like it.

What I'd like to try is grounding the whole thing in Anscombe's attack on Truman — the campaign against Oxford handing him an honorary degree, the flat claim that he was a murderer, which went over like a lead balloon at the time. I think her distinctions are doing real work and I want to see whether they survive being turned into something a dean can actually run. So: give me a decision procedure for the committee — the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the matter to the president because it's a values question rather than a question of moral fact. It has to keep what she actually holds: that intending the death of the innocent as a means is murder rather than something to be weighed; that intending differs from foreseeing; that good consequences don't launder the act; that the claim of no alternative is usually just false; and that whether the thing worked, or shortened the war, is beside the point. Tell me which of those I have to soften or drop to make it runnable, where softening it betrays the view, which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and where a non-philosopher applying this will reliably get it wrong. Then run it on three petitions we're actually holding: (a) rescind an honorary degree from a wartime head of government who ordered a strike on a genuine military target knowing it would kill a large number of civilians; (b) take the founder's name off a building because his fortune came from the slave trade — not a killing at all, and maybe just outside the framework entirely; (c) remove a plaque honoring a mid-level official who signed the paperwork for a mass deportation and has always said he was only doing his job. (c) is the one I expect to be hardest, and (b) is the one where I suspect your procedure will tell me something I don't want to hear, namely that I'm on my own. Also tell me what the procedure can't do. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.
