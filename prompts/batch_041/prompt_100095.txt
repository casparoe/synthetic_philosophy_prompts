I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm working up a piece on the philosophy of testimony. I want to run a popularizers take past you before I lean on it, because something about it smells.

Here's roughly how a guy I'll call a philosopher-journalist summed the whole thing up to me. Hume showed that testimony can never be a basic source of knowledge, because for anything you're told you first need independent evidence that people who say things like that tend to be right. Reid flipped it around: we're built to believe what we're told, credulity comes first and doubt is the thing we learn later, so testimony hands us knowledge with no supporting reasons needed. So the whole debate is Humeans who want evidence before they'll trust anybody vs. Reidians who take people at their word, and since none of us can check everything for ourselves, Reid basically wins.

That last move felt like a fast one to me, but I don't know enough to say where the problem is. So: which parts of that would someone who actually works on this sign off on, which are the sort of half-truth you'd flag, and is any of it just wrong? I'm suspicious of two things especially. First, the way he's split everybody into two camps and then declared a winner -- is that even the right axis? Second, whether "Reid says believe what you're told" is really Reid, or whether the anti-Hume side is being made to say something cruder than anyone holds. And does his closing line -- we can't verify everything ourselves, therefore testimony is basic -- actually follow from anything true in the rest of it, or is he smuggling a conclusion past a much weaker premise?

Please be committal here. Don't just lay out the positions and leave me to pick. Give me a definite call on which claims survive, which don't, and what a fair one-paragraph version of his summary would say instead. I'd rather have something I can argue with than a menu.
