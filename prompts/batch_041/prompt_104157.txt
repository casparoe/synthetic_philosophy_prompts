I cover science and ideas for a metro daily, and next Thursday I've got to give the closing toast at a small dinner — maybe thirty people — for the campaign that just got ranked-choice voting passed here. Half the room is the volunteers who knocked doors for two years and are still buzzing. The other half is the little crew of mathematicians and election nerds who kept telling us what could go wrong, and who I've been pestering for months. So picture me, a reporter with a glass in my hand, needing to say something warm and congratulatory that the math people won't quietly smirk at, and that won't turn into a seminar for the volunteers.

You're a philosopher of social choice, so help me out. I keep circling this result — Gibbard–Satterthwaite, I think, seventies, maybe someone conjectured it even earlier, a philosopher and a mathematician if I remember right — and my loose version of it is: any ranked voting rule with three or more possible outcomes that can't be gamed (where your best move is always just to honestly rank what you want) has to be a dictatorship. If that's roughly the shape of it, then standing up and saying we finally made voting honest, or that strategy is dead, is exactly the thing I can't say. My friend, an economist, has this phrase she uses — the "manipulation tax" — for the idea that no rule ever removes manipulation, it just decides who gets to be strategic and who gets punished for being sincere. I want to build the toast around that thought without lecturing anybody.

Two things before you write. First, brainstorm out loud for a bit about what the theorem actually lets me claim and what it forces me to give up, and about how to keep the whole thing warm rather than defensive. Second, if I've mangled the theorem in that paragraph, quietly fix it as you go instead of repeating my sloppy version. Then give me the toast itself, around five hundred words, with maybe one joke that the door-knockers and the mathematicians can both laugh at. The theorem has to do real work in it — shaping what I can honestly promise this room — not just get name-dropped.
