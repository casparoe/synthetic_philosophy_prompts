I'm a graduate student organizing a reading group on the combination problem for panpsychism, and for the handout I want something better than a list titled "responses to the combination problem." What I'd like from you is a decision tree that begins from the one commitment every party to this dispute shares — that consciousness, or something very close to it, is fundamental rather than a late product of the physical — and branches, node by node, until every leaf is a named position on how many micro-experiences come to constitute one macro-experience. Please build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of authors: at each node, pose the single question that genuinely divides the positions beneath it, phrased so that a reader can answer it from the armchair without already knowing which label the answer leads to. I want the nodes to be concrete and committal — for instance, whether the fundamental items already possess a point of view or only qualities; whether the macro-experience is literally composed of micro-experiences or is something over and above them; whether any such composition requires an added primitive relation or follows from the natures of the parts themselves; whether grounding runs upward from micro to macro or downward from a single cosmic subject; whether micro-experiences survive as such once absorbed into a larger one; and whether the felt unity of a single experience is veridical or an illusion.

I need the following positions to appear as leaves: constitutive panpsychism; the variant of it that adds a primitive bonding relation between micro-subjects; cosmopsychism; panqualityism; panprotopsychism; emergentism; neutral or dual-aspect monism; and the illusionist or eliminativist line that denies there is any macro-experience to combine into in the first place. For each leaf, give the position's name and a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders are forced to swallow. Then do the two things a flat list cannot: first, flag any pair of leaves your tree fails to separate with the questions you have used, and say why they collapse; second, mark the regions of the logical space that no one occupies, with a brief diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, reading lists, or appeals to who said what, since the readers will be advanced undergraduates working without the literature in front of them, and I want a map of the positions rather than a bibliography.
