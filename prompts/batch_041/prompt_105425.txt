I want a credence, and I want the reasoning that produces it laid out term by term rather than delivered as a verdict.

Start with a case I'll stipulate rather than borrow. Take a work of art — call it the Original — and a second object, the Copy, that is physically indistinguishable from it at every level of description any observer could ever access: same materials, same microstructure, same surface, same pattern of wear and aging. Stipulate further that the Copy is never detected, never traded, never entered into any record, and that the whole of its causal footprint in human experience and in our institutions is indistinguishable from the Original's. The only fact that separates the two is provenance: the Copy was made later, by a different hand, as a deliberate imitation.

Now the proposition I want a number on:

There is a kind of value — call it authenticity value — that the Original possesses and the Copy lacks, and this value is not reducible to (i) any difference in what any possible observer could ever experience, (ii) any difference in what anyone believes or knows, or (iii) any difference in price, prestige, or institutional standing.

Give me your credence that this proposition is true, decomposed. I expect the number to rest on at least the following sub-questions; give a rough probability and one line of justification for each.

1. Are works of art, in the relevant sense, repeatable types that can have more than one fully authentic instance, or are they individuals whose identity includes the history of their making? If the former, the Copy may simply be a further instance of the same work rather than a lesser one.
2. Does aesthetic value supervene entirely on what can be experienced, or is the maker's achievement — the difficulty, the invention, the fact that this was done first, and by this person — a component of the work's value in its own right?
3. Is there a dimension of artistic value (originality, achievement, expression) distinct from aesthetic value, and does the Copy's lack of originality count against it on that dimension?
4. Is the intuition that the Original is superior tracking a genuine value, or is it an error — a projection onto the object of our contingent interest in causal history and personal connection?
5. Is the question partly verbal, i.e. does the word forgery already carry a defect as part of its meaning, so that redescribing the Copy as a forgery settles nothing?

Then combine the terms into a single number, tell me which term the number is most sensitive to, and say what would have to change about that term to move you by more than 0.2.

Two variants, please, and say how much each shifts the number:

- The self-duplicate: the same artist, decades later, produces a materially and perceptually indistinguishable replica of their own early masterpiece. Authorship is not in question; only originality in time is.
- The sole survivor: the Original is destroyed and the Copy is the only object left standing for the work, so every future encounter with it is an encounter with the Copy and no one knows or could know otherwise.

Finally, I'll put my own cards on the table. My credence in the proposition is about 0.3; a colleague who works on the metaphysics of art puts hers near 0.8. Given your own number and decomposition, tell me where our disagreement most plausibly lives — which sub-question we are most likely answering differently — and then tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where in your own reasoning you would expect the error to lie if you turn out to be wrong.
