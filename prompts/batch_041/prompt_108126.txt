I'm a first-time TA for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on minds and machines. The course is not a logic course, but the students have had one lecture on Gödel's incompleteness theorems. Last week's discussion became heated when a student claimed the theorems prove that no computer can match the human mind, and several students are now writing their final essays on that claim. The professor asked me to grade the take-home answers. One student's answer is below. Please give it a letter grade and the specific comments that justify it, point by point. I need to know what is right, what is wrong, and what a hasty grader might miss. The students are not expected to prove the theorems, but they are expected to state them accurately and to assess the philosophical claim carefully.

Question: Explain what Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems actually establish, and assess the claim that they show that the human mind cannot be a formal system. In your answer, distinguish what the theorems do and do not imply.

Student answer: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal system that is strong enough to express basic arithmetic is incomplete. That is, there is a sentence in the language of the system that is true in the standard interpretation but not provable in the system. The sentence is constructed by diagonalization and effectively says of itself that it is not provable. If the system is consistent, it cannot prove the sentence; and since the sentence says it is not provable, it is true. The second theorem says that such a system cannot prove its own consistency. So if we want to prove that arithmetic is consistent, we have to use a stronger system, and we can never prove consistency from within.

This matters for the philosophy of mind. Suppose the mind were a formal system of the same kind. Then there would be a true sentence that the mind could not prove. But human mathematicians can recognize that the Gödel sentence is true: we can see that the system is consistent, or at least we can see that if it is consistent, the sentence is unprovable and therefore true. So the mind can do something no formal system of that kind can do. Therefore the mind is not a formal system at all, and no machine can fully capture human mathematical understanding. The only way to resist this is to say that the mind is inconsistent, which is implausible, or to deny that we can know the Gödel sentence, which would undermine our mathematical knowledge.

The theorem applies to any formal system, not just arithmetic. Any axiomatic system with a finite number of axioms is incomplete. So the argument generalizes: no machine can match the human mind.
