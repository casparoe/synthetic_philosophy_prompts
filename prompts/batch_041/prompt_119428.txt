I am defending a paper at a workshop next month whose thesis is that the separateness of persons is not an independent objection to utilitarianism. My line: I grant that persons are distinct loci of value, and that a loss to one cannot be made good by a gain to another the way losses and gains can be netted within a single life. What I deny is that any of this entails something anti-aggregative. The objection, I argue, borrows whatever force it has from an unstated premise — the mid-century ordinalist refusal to compare welfare across persons — and so it either collapses (if cross-person comparisons are allowed) or proves far too much, indicting every cost–benefit analysis and every redistributive institution its proponents favor (if they are not).

The audience: a political theory workshop, mostly Rawlsians and Scanlonians, plus two welfare economists who work on social choice and cost–benefit analysis, and one Kantian ethicist. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six or eight objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I already know from the literature — ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in the vocabulary of whoever will raise it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede in the first thirty seconds, and end with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
