Ok, I teach a seminar on punishment and every year I do this exercise where I list off my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and then have to admit I don't know what theory they add up to. Last year a student finally called my bluff and told me to just write them all down. So here goes — I want you to tell me if there's a principle that gets most of them, which ones it can't save, and what it says about a couple of cases I haven't thought about.

My verdicts:

1. Two guys with identical intent and an identical plan each shoot a stranger at point blank. One victim dies, the other survives because the bullet glanced off a rib. I want the guy whose victim died punished a good deal more. The only difference between them is luck and I still feel the difference is real.

2. Drunk driver who runs a red and kills a kid in the crosswalk vs. a drunk driver who runs the same red at the same speed and nothing happens to be there. I want these two treated almost identically — maybe a hair more for the one who killed, but basically the same. And I notice this seems to flatly contradict #1.

3. A guy builds a bomb, plants it in a train station, the wiring is faulty, it never goes off, nobody's hurt. I want him punished as hard as if it had gone off. So now I'm with #2 and against #1 a second time.

4. The classic one: a town is about to riot and a lot of people will die unless we convict and hang someone we know is innocent. I say no, never, full stop, even if the arithmetic favors it.

5. A man commits a terrible crime at 22, serves no time because it's only discovered now, and he's 68, spent 45 years being a decent person, is dying of cancer. I don't want to lock him up now. Not because he didn't do it — he did — but because... I don't know, something about what punishment is even for at that point.

6. But: a guy who killed someone and then had a head injury that turned him into a genuinely different, gentle person — I do want him confined. Not punished exactly, confined, kept away from people, but I don't feel I'm punishing the person he is now. And I can't say why 5 and 6 come out differently for me.

7. A negligent parent forgets the kid in the car on a hot day. An intentional killer of a child. The gap between these is enormous and I'm confident about it.

8. Someone is punished under a law that didn't exist when he acted. I think that's wrong no matter how good the law is or how much punishing him would help.

So my half-baked stab at a unifying idea is something like: outcome-luck matters when it reflects something about what the agent actually did or risked, but not when it's just... the world's coin flip? Except #1 and #3 both involve a coin flip and I split them, so I already know that's not it.

Can you tell me what principle (or small set of them) actually captures the pattern, which of my eight verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it predicts for (a) a doctor who gives a lethal dose by accident vs. on purpose, and (b) a country deciding whether to prosecute an old man for what he did as a soldier decades ago. Please don't just tell me I'm a closet retributivist or a closet consequentialist — I want the actual clauses, the case that motivates each one, and an honest note on where the whole thing starts to creak. I've been teahing this for fifteen years and I'd like a seperate answer for once.
