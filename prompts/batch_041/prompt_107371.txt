Six weeks ago I resigned from a stable position to join a five-person effort pursuing an outcome I put at perhaps one chance in ten thousand of ever materializing, though if it did it would outweigh anything else I could hope to influence. I have not been able to stop turning over the argument that convinced me, and at this point I want a diagnosis rather than reassurance. Here it is, reconstructed as carefully as I can manage.

(1) An option is rational to choose exactly to the degree that it maximizes expected value; the variance of its outcomes, the certainty it forgoes, the magnitude of its worst case, and the identity of whoever bears its costs are not independent reasons for or against it.
(2) The expected value of committing a share f of my total resources to a project is f times the project's expected value; expected value is linear in how much I commit.
(3) My project carries a probability p > 0 of a benefit of magnitude W, and p·W exceeds the expected value of every alternative use of my resources.
(4) Therefore I should commit all of my resources to it, and none to anything of merely finite value.
(5) Since p need only be positive, the conclusion is robust to making p as small as I like, provided W is enlarged to compensate; there is no floor below which a probability may be ignored.
(6) The reasoning is indifferent to what the resource is, so it covers not only money and time but my health, my attention, and the claims of the people who depend on me.
(7) It follows that no finite sacrifice is too large if it raises p·W at all, including sacrifices that make my own survival less likely.
(8) Yet the same reasoning would have bound me just as tightly to any other long shot with a comparably small p and an incomparably large W, and would then have required me to abandon the first; the two cannot both be the unique demand.
(9) A principle that demands unbounded sacrifice, that cannot rank two long shots except by magnitudes no one can estimate, and that makes every finite commitment look like a betrayal, is not one I can act on.
(10) So (1) is false: expected value is not the whole of practical rationality.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (9) — I do not want to give up on expected value, only on whatever in the argument is doing the damage. Tell me which step to reject. For the step you name, explain why it looked irresistible while it was doing its work, and what rejecting it costs: which of my other commitments have to be surrendered or rewritten for the rejection to stick, and whether what survives is still a decision rule I could actually run on a Tuesday afternoon.
