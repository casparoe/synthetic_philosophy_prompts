I'm a PhD student in Buddhist philosophy writing on whether Buddhist ethics can be read as a form of consequentialism. The version I'm interested in is not the familiar maximizing picture but the karma-theoretic one: actions are assessed by their consequences, yet the action itself is identified with its intention (cetana), so the relevant consequence is the karmic fruit of a mental act, and the no-self doctrine makes it unclear whose welfare is being aggregated. Read Groundhog Day through that lens. Phil Connors is trapped in a loop where his actions have no lasting worldly consequences; what persists is his character and, if one accepts the Buddhist frame, the intentional quality of his repeated acts. Does the film dramatize an intention-consequentialism, or does it quietly vindicate a virtue-ethical reading of Buddhist practice? Point to the scenes and choices that most illuminate the tension -- his early hedonism and manipulation, the suicide attempts, the piano and ice-sculpture arc, the final day -- and say where the film resists the consequentialist reading. I would also like a closing paragraph on what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not.
