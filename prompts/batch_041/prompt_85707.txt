For the past term my school's ethics seminar has been going around and around on a single question, and I am writing to ask for help in diagnosing it rather than settling it. The question is whether the history of morality is a history of progress, and the two camps in the room have hardened to the point where each side can predict the other's reply before it is made.

The first camp holds that the record speaks for itself. Practices that were once unremarkable — holding persons as property, punishing people for what they believed, treating animal suffering as morally irrelevant — are now widely regarded as wrong, and this, they say, is not merely a change of taste but an improvement. Their image for it is a circle of moral concern that has been widening for centuries, taking in more beings as the class of those who count grows larger. The second camp does not dispute most of the historical facts. They dispute the shape of the story. On their account every widening has come with a redrawing: the "we" that becomes more inclusive is itself reconstituted in the process, so the later circle is not simply a larger version of the earlier one but a differently centred one, and the people inside it do not apply the earlier circle's standards. They add that the metaphor of expansion quietly assumes a single centre shared by all the circles, which is precisely what is in dispute.

What I notice is that the argument never stays in one place. Sometimes the two sides disagree about the historical record — whether inclusion has actually increased on balance, or whether the gains are offset by new exclusions that the winners cannot see. Sometimes they agree about every fact and then disagree about whether the result should be called progress at all. And sometimes they seem to be asking different questions: one side about whether our moral views are better than theirs, the other about whether there is any standpoint from which "better" means more than "ours."

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about what makes a change an improvement, a hidden empirical question about the record, or two separate questions wearing one name? Which parts of it could be settled by evidence, or by drawing a distinction that neither side has drawn, and which parts rest on intuitions that neither side can argue for? My own hypothesis is the one I sketched above — that the image of an expanding circle conceals a choice between concentric growth and re-centring — but I hold it loosely, and if it is the wrong diagnosis I would rather be told so than have it flattered. Finally, tell me what progress in this debate would actually consist of: a distinction, a measure, a piece of history, or an argument that the question ought to be abandoned. And if the honest answer is that the impasse is simply what happens when a normative question is asked in descriptive clothing, please say that plainly.
