For the past year I have been splitting my time between a philosophy department and a systems neuroscience lab, and I have noticed that one sentence does an enormous amount of work in both places: the brain is a computer. In the lab it is offered as a licence — we should treat neural activity as information processing, we should hunt for the algorithm the brain implements, we should not be distracted by the wetware. In the seminar room it is offered as a thesis, sometimes as an obvious truth and sometimes as an outrageous one, and it gets used to settle disputes it seems far too thin to settle: whether a neuron that no one can show computes anything really computes or is only described that way, whether a mind could survive being run on silicon, whether psychology should be replaced by a description of the brain's program, whether a thermostat or a liver counts as a computer too. I have started to suspect that the people deploying it are not deploying the same sentence, and I would like to know what it actually commits you to.

Please lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making, arranged as a menu running from the nearly empty to the nearly incredible, and tell me for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or confused. I am especially interested in how the various senses of "computer" do the work — a system whose behaviour is capturable by some computable function, a digital device, a programmable general-purpose machine, a symbol manipulator, a machine implementing one specific program, a device whose states are read as representations — and in which of these senses is in play when someone reaches for the slogan to close an argument rather than open one. For the readings that are substantive, say what they would predict or forbid that their rivals do not, so that I can distinguish a genuine commitment from a metaphor that has quietly hardened into a premise.

I am not asking for the history of the idea or for who said what; I want the anatomy of the sentence itself, so that the next time it is dropped in a lab meeting or a seminar I can ask which of its readings is on the table and whether that reading is actually doing the work it was recruited for. Where a given reading would support the use it was put to, and where it would not, please say so explicitly; and where two of the readings are being run together in a way that makes the slogan look stronger than any single reading could be on its own, point that out as well.
