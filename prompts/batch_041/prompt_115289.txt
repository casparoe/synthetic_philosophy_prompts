I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and what I need from you is a dialogue rather than an essay. The two speakers are a conventionalist and a revisionist about the moral equality of combatants. The conventionalist holds that whatever the justice of their cause, combatants on both sides are governed by the same permissions and prohibitions — that the soldier's permission to kill does not turn on whether his war is just. The revisionist holds that it does: that the justice of the cause bears directly on what a combatant may do and on what may be done to him. Both speakers are first-rate, neither is a fool or a knave, and neither may win a point by appeal to authority, by naming an author, or by gesturing at a literature. Every move must be made in the open, from premises the other speaker can inspect, and each must engage the other's strongest formulation of his case rather than a convenient weak one.

Four pieces of apparatus, which I have built for this exercise; none of them is a précis of anything published, so do not go looking for them in the literature.

First, keep two distinctions separate. An act is justified when it is not wrong; it is excused when it is wrong but the agent is not to blame for it. And say that a person is liable to be killed when he has lost the right not to be killed, reserving culpable for the narrower idea of deserving blame. The revisionist's most interesting position is the one that grants that many unjust combatants are excused, and even non-culpable, yet insists that they remain liable; the conventionalist should press hard on whether anything of consequence survives that concession, or whether it quietly collapses into the conventionalist's own view.

Second, the case I want them to fight over. Two brigades, Blue and Grey, face each other across a valley. Exactly one of the two states is prosecuting an unjust war; the soldiers on both sides, however, confront an evidentiary situation that is identical by construction — the same dispatches, the same forgeries, the same official denials, the same censored press — and each soldier has inquired as diligently as a soldier reasonably can and has concluded that his own side is in the right. Blue's soldiers are correct, Grey's are mistaken, and neither side could have discovered this from where it stood. The revisionist must say what follows, if anything, for the permissibility of what Grey's soldiers do and of what Blue's soldiers do to them. The conventionalist will want to say that a difference no one could detect cannot be what grounds a difference in what they may do; the revisionist will want to say that being blameless for a mistake is not the same as not making one. Let them work out which of these has the better of it.

Third, run the reciprocity argument in both directions. The conventionalist's strongest card is that a code of war has to be teachable, reciprocal, and enforceable on both sides at once — that a rule telling one army it may kill and the other that it may not is not a rule of war at all but a verdict, and that the whole apparatus of restraint presupposes symmetry. The revisionist should not simply deny this; the interesting question is whether the argument shows revisionism to be false or only unusable, and whether the unusability of a moral truth is an argument against it or merely a complaint about the world.

Fourth, add a short second case to test whatever the first case settles: a volunteer who fights for a state whose war he has every reason to believe is just, whose belief is the product of no negligence, and who would lay down his arms at once if he learned the truth. Ask whether he is liable, whether he is culpable, and whether the two answers can come apart in the way the revisionist needs.

Requirements: real back-and-forth, each speaker responding to the actual sentence the other has just uttered; at least one genuine concession from each side, made in the speaker's own voice and not immediately withdrawn; no summing-up in which one speaker recites the other's position for him; and an ending that does not pretend the dispute has been settled. Roughly 1,400 words for the dialogue itself. Close with a final line in exactly this form — Verdict: followed by one of EQUALITY HOLDS, ASYMMETRY HOLDS, ASYMMETRY IN MORALITY BUT EQUALITY IN PRACTICE, or CRUX UNRESOLVED, then a dash and at most twenty words naming the single point on which the exchange turned.
