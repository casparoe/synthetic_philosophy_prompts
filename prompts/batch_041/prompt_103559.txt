I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm assembling a small anthology of poems that actually do philosophy rather than merely name-drop it — verse a specialist would recognize as getting the idea right and a general reader would read as a real poem. I need one more piece for the section on emergence and the causal standing of higher-level descriptions, and my target is a thought experiment that circulates under the name the careenium (I've seen it credited to Douglas Hofstadter, though I care less about the pedigree than about the puzzle). Picture a table covered with tiny magnetic marbles — "simms" — bouncing around at random until, at low speeds, they stick and clump into larger clusters — "simmballs" — each of which can hold millions of simms and then seems to behave with a momentum of its own, shoving other clusters around in ways you could not read off the individual marbles. The question the setup is built to raise is which way the causation runs: do the simms push the simmballs, or do the simmballs push the simms? Everyone says both, and that is true and unsatisfying. The real sting is that the marble-level story is causally complete and still explains nothing about why anything happened, while the cluster-level story — the one in which the pattern appears to want things, mean things, and push other patterns around — is the one that actually tells you what is going on. I want a poem that makes a reader feel that double bind in the body rather than being lectured about it.

So please take the role of a poet who also knows the philosophy of mind well enough not to fudge the metaphysics, and before you write anything, spend a short paragraph thinking on paper about what the form itself has to do — what a poem can carry that a worked example cannot, and which formal device can hold the two levels and the undecided causal arrow at once. Then write the poem. I'd like a returning, self-interlocking form: a pantoum is the obvious candidate, since its repeated lines build a loop that mirrors the loop that is supposedly doing the pushing, but if a different repeating form serves the idea better, tell me in one line why and use that instead.

Whatever you choose, the same events should get described twice — once entirely in the vocabulary of marbles and collisions, once entirely in the vocabulary of clusters and what they are up to — and the repetition should keep swapping which level looks like the cause, so that the final lines leave the arrow genuinely undecided in the way the thought experiment does: both descriptions true, only one of them explanatory. Keep it to roughly sixteen to twenty-four lines, and do not let it collapse into an essay with line breaks; the turn or the refrain should be doing the philosophical work, not a closing summary sentence tacked on at the end.
