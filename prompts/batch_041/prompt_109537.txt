So my friend runs a second-year seminar on rationality and games, and she's roped me into guest-teaching one session — I write novels, I'm not a philosopher, but she says I'm good at staging scenes with constraints, and she wants the students to *feel* something before she names it. The topic she's handed me is the superrationality stuff: the Platonia dilemma and the luring lottery. 20 students, one 70-minute slot, and I get to run it however I want as long as it's a game they play, not a lecture.

Can you design the activity? Here's what I'm after.

Round one should be the telegram thing: a mysterious benefactor (me, in character) offers a big single prize to the one and only person who acts by some deadline, and if nobody acts or more than one acts, no one gets anything, and talking is forbidden. I want the room to *hit* the failure themselves — either everybody sends or nobody does, and I want to be able to tell those two failures apart in the debrief. Round two should be the lottery: everyone secretly writes a whole number on a card, the pot gets divided by the total of everyone's numbers, and a winner is drawn from the entries (so writing a bigger number buys you more chances but shrinks what's left to win). I'm expecting a runaway, a couple of people writing absurd numbers to spoil it for everyone, and some resentment — which I'd like to redirect at the structure rather than the jokers.

I need: the exact rules for both rounds, what materials to bring (cards, envelopes, a box, candy or tokens for the pot — no real money, and nothing that smells like gambling for the undergrads), the payoff scheme written out so it's actually playable, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 70 minutes, and a second pass at round one where they're allowed a private randomiser (a die, a phone) so we can compare what changes. Then the debrief, mapping each moment of what happened onto the ideas — including the symmetric-reasoning move where you ask "what if everyone reasons the way I'm reasoning," and why that lands on randomising with a probability tied to how many people are playing, and why the lottery's best-response number is "however many entries everyone else submitted," which is what makes it explode.

Two things I especially want from you. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment — including the case where the students try to cheat by silently coordinating, and the case where the small stakes make them goof around in a way the real thing wouldn't have allowed. Second, be honest about what this activity *can't* reproduce and what I'll have to just tell them afterwards. I know I can't fake a billion dollars, and I know the toy version can't really make anyone feel an astronomically large number. Say it straight.

Also, dont name superrationality at the top — they should be in the middle of it before they hear the word. And if it helps, I'm happy to bring my laptop and run the lottery draw off a three-line script, since doing it by hand with big numbers sounds miserable.
