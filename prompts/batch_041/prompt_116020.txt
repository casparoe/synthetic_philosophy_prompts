I'm in a graduate seminar on social ontology, and we have spent several sessions circling a question I still cannot answer cleanly: whether generations are real kinds, or whether labels like "Gen Z" and "the boomers" name anything more than a demographic bucket with a marketing department attached. I have now read enough defenses and debunkings that I can predict the shape of the next one, so I do not want a verdict — I want the specification. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of generations must satisfy. Treat "generation" as the thing to be theorized, covering both the ordinary usage in which people attribute a shared outlook to a birth window and the social-scientific usage in which generations are supposed to be units of cultural transmission, and take the purpose to be a theory good enough to carry weight in social-scientific explanation rather than cultural commentary alone. I want the requirements list: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it cannot contradict, each with the consideration that motivates it.

To save us both time, here are the considerations I keep returning to, and I would like each either vindicated as a genuine requirement or dismissed as a prejudice of one camp — realists about social kinds, constructionists, demographers, cultural historians. First, a cohort (everyone born in some window) and a generation (a group with a shared formative experience and some measure of self-consciousness as a group) seem to come apart: one cohort may contain several generations, and one generation may cut across cohorts, so the theory must say how the two are related rather than sliding between them. Second, it must explain where the boundary falls — why the window opens here and not a year earlier — without making the boundary either a brute convention or a metaphysical joint in nature. Third, it has to survive the fact that in the usual data one cannot separately estimate the effects of age, of period, and of birth cohort, since the three are linearly dependent; any claim that some difference is generational rather than an effect of aging or of the times therefore rests on an identifying assumption the theory should state outright. Fourth, the labels circulate and are taken up by the people they label, so the kind may be partly constituted by its own name — a looping effect that makes "is it real?" and "did we invent it?" hard to keep apart. Fifth, generational schemes are not portable: a label coined around one national experience does not map onto the same birth window or the same formative events elsewhere, and a theory should not smuggle in a single global timeline. Sixth, any generation is stratified internally by class, race, region, and migration status, so the theory must say whether the supposed shared experience is a statistical tendency or a defining condition. And seventh, it should say what generational categories are for — prediction, cultural explanation, identity, marketing — and whether one theory can serve all of those masters.

Then, on the basis of the list: flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since I expect the interesting result to be a tension rather than a tidy scorecard; tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the currently popular accounts; and close with a brief note on whether the distinction between a generation as a mere location and as an actuality — with smaller generational units nested inside it — buys the theory anything, or just relabels the problem. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will do the scoring against particular authors myself.
