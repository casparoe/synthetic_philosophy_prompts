I teach a mid-level seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I want to run a session that dislodges a confusion I keep finding even in good students: the assumption that a heritability coefficient is a fixed property of a trait, a number that tells you how genetic something is and therefore how modifiable it is. I have fourteen students and seventy-five minutes, and I would rather they feel the force of the population-and-environment dependence of heritability than hear me assert it. So what I want is a runnable in-class simulation — rules, materials, a scoring or measurement scheme, and a timeline — in which the group itself generates the data and computes the estimate, and in which the number they compute moves for reasons they can see with their own eyes. Please design it so the instructive reversals arrive as consequences of the rules rather than as announcements from me: the activity should arrange things so that the class first reaches a confident conclusion about how "genetic" the trait is, and then watches that conclusion collapse when the setup changes, before I ever name the fallacy.

Here is the scaffolding I have in mind, though you should feel free to improve on it. Give each participant a plant (or a bean, or a simulated organism) with a fixed genotype value and a phenotype that is produced by adding the genotype to whatever environment they draw that round; then have the class estimate, from the spread of phenotypes, how much of the variation is attributable to genetic differences. In one round let everyone grow in an identical environment, so that essentially all the variance is genetic and the estimate comes out near one; in a second round let the environments vary, so the genetic share drops sharply; in a third round restore a single common environment but shift its quality for everyone at once, so the estimate climbs back toward one even as the average phenotype jumps — the point being that a high heritability coefficient sits perfectly happily alongside a large environmental effect on the mean. Then introduce, in a final round, one or two genotypes whose response to the environment differs from the rest (a reaction norm, if you like), so that the tidy additive bookkeeping the class has been doing visibly breaks down. I would like the debrief to map each reversal back onto a claim about heritability — that it is a ratio of variances within a population across a range of environments, that it says nothing about individuals, that it licenses no inference about malleability or about differences between groups — and to be candid about which features of the real concept the simulation cannot reproduce, in particular the assumptions and confounds that dog actual twin and family estimates (equal environments, gene-environment correlation, the gap between the model and the messy biology). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially the misconception the students are most likely to carry out of Round One before Round Two corrects them, and how I should handle the student who insists the simulation is rigged.
