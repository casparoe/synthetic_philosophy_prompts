Please answer as a philosopher of science who is also at home in stratigraphy and in historiography — someone who can move between the two — and think carefully before committing to anything. Begin by setting out the positions you take to be genuinely in play, and only then give me your considered answer.

Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend who trained as a geologist, and I have come away unsure whether I was defending nonsense. His case is that the Anthropocene is finished. The Anthropocene Working Group's proposal was voted down by the subcommission, and the bodies above it, the ICS and the IUGS, then confirmed the rejection; so, he says, there is no Anthropocene in geology, only a word that humanities scholars and journalists use for a mood. He also has a technical objection I could not answer: a geological boundary has to be isochronous, and the industrial revolution arrived at different times in different places, so the proposal was doomed regardless of the politics. My own view — which he treats as a concession — is that the Anthropocene is either a real epoch or a metaphor, and since the geologists have said no, it must be a metaphor.

So: when a period like this begins, who decides, and on what basis? Is that a scientific question that stratigraphic bodies settle by procedure, or a historiographic one that historians settle by convention? And is my friend right that a boundary which is not globally synchronous cannot be a boundary at all — or does the mid-century marker he waves away, the plutonium spike in those Canadian lake sediments, answer him on his own terms?

Tell me, finally, what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be sound, would lead you to a different verdict, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail?
