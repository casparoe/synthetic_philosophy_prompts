I am the outgoing leader of a political party, and I am to give my resignation speech at the party's annual conference. Over the past year I have become persuaded by Simone Weil's critique of political parties — her argument that the party form itself, not merely this or that party, is inherently oppressive: that it demands unanimity, substitutes collective passion for attention to truth, treats the party's survival as the highest good, and makes justice subordinate to the party's interests. I intend to tell my own party, in public, that I believe we should work toward the abolition of the party form rather than its reform. The audience will be mixed: several hundred delegates who have given their lives to this organization and who will hear my words as a betrayal; a press corps that will seize on every inflammatory line; and a small group of family members who have no interest in political theory and who have come to support me. Write the speech. It should run about eight minutes, roughly 1,100 words. It must not rely on the rhetorical devices Weil condemns — the construction of us and them, the invocation of enemies, the generation of collective passion, the treatment of the party as sacred. Yet it must still be a speech that can be delivered to a crowd and that can move them. It must answer the practical objection that without parties there can be no organized action for justice, and it must not itself become the manifesto of a new party. At some point it should acknowledge the performative contradiction of delivering an anti-party speech to a party conference, and it should say what it can and cannot do about that contradiction. It should end with something concrete that the delegates could do the next morning. Write the speech itself, in the voice of the leader who is giving it; do not quote Weil directly, and do not write an essay about her.
