I've been rereading Edwin Abbott's Flatland (1884) against the literature on conceivability as a guide to possibility, and I want a reading that treats the novella as a serious piece of modal epistemology rather than a charming geometry allegory. The standard move is that the Square's inability to conceive of the third dimension is no evidence against it, and the Sphere is right to insist on this—but the book immediately turns the tables: the Sphere, having just lectured the Square on dimensional prejudice, dismisses the Square's proposed fourth dimension as nonsense, and the Square himself, back in Flatland, can reportedly grasp height only by faith rather than by sight or reason. I'd like you to use that symmetry as the crux: what exactly does a Flatlander's failure to conceive of height show about height's possibility, and does the Sphere's own failure strengthen or undermine the conceivability-to-possibility inference? Work in the distinction between negative and positive conceivability, and between imaginability and conceptual coherence, using the Lineland monarch, the Pointland solipsist, the Sphere's point-to-line-to-square analogies, and the Colour Revolt as test cases for whether the constraints on conception here are epistemic or merely psychological and social. End by saying what the fiction adds that the zombie argument and Kripkean necessary a posteriori cases cannot—whether it dramatizes a modal defeat that is incapacity rather than ignorance, and whether that helps or hurts the inference. No citations needed; I want the argument, the reading, and an honest note on where Flatland resists the analogy.
