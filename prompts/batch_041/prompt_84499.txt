I retired from a chemistry department three years ago, and having run out of other things to be annoyed about I've spent most of the time since teaching myself philosophy — epistemology mostly, a bit of decision theory, enough of the technical material to follow an argument on a page. Through an old colleague I've ended up as one of the volunteer forecasters for a small alumni outfit that runs prediction questions every quarter, and every quarter the same thing happens: four or five of the questions are about matters that are, in principle, already settled — an open conjecture, a number nobody has bothered to compute, whether a short program halts on all its inputs, whether some cryptographic assumption still holds — and I sit there with the form in front of me and no idea what to write. My first instinct is that this isn't a matter of probability at all, the thing is either true or false, which I'm told is naive. My second instinct is to write 0.5 and hedge, which I'm fairly sure is worse, and which in any case feels like lying with extra steps. What I want from you is a procedure I can actually sit down and run, twenty minutes a question, pencil and paper, no literature and no oracle: an ordered set of steps for arriving at and defending a credence on a claim whose truth value is fixed by logic or arithmetic but which I can't settle, with what counts as enough of an answer at each step, the points where the procedure stops and tells me to refuse to price the question at all, and a clear mark on the steps that are really just me making a judgement call in a lab coat. I'd like it to take seriously the thought that my prices shouldn't be free money for anyone who reasons better than I do — if some obvious chain of logical steps would let a sharper colleague take my numbers apart, the procedure should catch that before I post them — and I'd like it to seperate two cases that feel quite different to me: a claim that is just a finite computation nobody has done, where the answer is out there and it's a matter of arithmetic, and a claim that needs mathematics that doesn't exist yet, where I'm really guessing at what people will eventually manage to prove.

On the stakes: the numbers go up under my name and the group's, the coordinator posts them, they're scored at the end of the year against how the questions resolve, the leaderboard is visible to the whole alumni list, and last year I was asked to explain my entries over dinner, so I'd rather not have to defend a figure I can't reconstruct from my own notes. Please run the procedure on three of these: whether a famous open conjecture in my old field gets resolved within thirty years; whether the decimal expansion of pi contains a run of a hundred consecutive zeros somewhere before the billionth digit; and whether a small program I could write down in ten lines halts on a specified input. For each, give me the number to write, the one sentence to write beside it, and the first kind of news that should move it. I should say in advance that I'd rather not post anything between about 0.05 and 0.95 on the conjecture, so if the procedure tells me to write 0.5 and stand behind it, tell me so plainly and tell me what I'm supposed to say to the person who asks why I bothered — I'd sooner have an answer I don't like than be flattered.

Two last things. Keep visibly apart the places where you're describing something that people who actually work on this would agree is settled and the places where you're describing a view that is still genuinely contested, because I've already been burned once by treating a live dispute as a textbook result in front of people I wanted to impress. And tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it can't do at all, rather than letting me find out at the dinner — skip the names and reading lists, I'd rather have the reasoning than the bibliography.
