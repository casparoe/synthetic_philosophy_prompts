I keep getting stuck on a disagreement among Spinoza readers about his politics, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is, not another summary of the positions. The texts pull two ways. Spinoza equates right with power — each thing has as much right as it has power — which makes political legitimacy sound descriptive, a regime having right just insofar as it is effective; and he calls democracy the most natural form of government, the one that comes closest to preserving natural right. Some readers take this as a robust endorsement of popular rule, even the form in which freedom and reason are most fully realized. But the same author insists that most people are led by passion rather than reason, treats the multitude as volatile and easily managed, places the outward expression of religion and the limits of public speech under the sovereign's authority, and leaves his systematic Political Treatise unfinished at exactly the point where democracy was to be treated. Readers who weight that strand treat him as a Hobbesian in disguise, or as praising democracy only instrumentally — for stability or mildness, not because it is intrinsically tied to freedom. What I can't tell is what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal, about the word 'right' — one side using the naturalistic sense Spinoza defined, the other smuggling in a normative sense he rejected? Is it about whether the democratic commitment belongs to the philosophical argument or to a polemical context, so that the camps are really answering different questions under one heading? Is it about what 'freedom' means in his system — absence of external constraint versus living under the guidance of reason? Or is there a genuine tension in the texts that one side is misreading? My hypothesis is that it comes down to whether right can be both coextensive with power and a standard of legitimacy. Diagnose it for me: say which of these it is, give reasons drawn from what each side says about natural right, freedom, and the multitude, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a better account of how the two political treatises relate, or a reason to think the question is ill-posed. Take a definite position rather than laying out the options.
