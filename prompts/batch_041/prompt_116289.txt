I have spent the past year learning to read my own body. I have a condition that flares without much warning, and I have been trying to develop a feel for when one is coming — a faint premonitory shift, a heaviness behind the eyes, an ache that sometimes means something and sometimes does not. Mostly this has meant keeping a diary, guessing, and being wrong. I have improved, but the diary and the flare itself are the only scorecards I have.

Lately I have been reading about an old puzzle in epistemology, and it has unsettled me more than the illness has. The puzzle, as I understand it, is that a faculty cannot be used to certify its own reliability. If I want to know whether my sense of an oncoming flare is trustworthy, the only instrument I possess for checking it is that same sense; the moment I use it to confirm itself I am moving in a circle. I have seen the point made under the name of the bootstrapping problem, and also in the guise of a complaint that certain views make knowledge too easy: if you allow someone to argue from the deliverances of a faculty to the reliability of that faculty, they acquire knowledge on the cheap and can even dispose of skepticism in a sentence.

The argument seems to me airtight precisely because it does not depend on any particular account of what justification is — it is simply a fact about circles. Doesn't it therefore show that my confidence in my own read of my symptoms is not merely unreliable but unwarranted, that I have no epistemic right to it however well calibrated I happen to become? And doesn't the same reasoning generalize, since the only way to improve at seeing or hearing or tasting is to trust what one sees, hears, and tastes? I would like to know whether I should stop treating my own sense of an oncoming flare as evidence about anything, and whether the puzzle really carries the sweeping consequence I have taken it to carry.
