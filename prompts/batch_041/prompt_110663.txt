Ok, so I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science and I've got a weird problem: I use causal talk constantly (I ablate units, I write "the population is what drives the readout"), but I've realised I don't actually have a spec for what a theory of causation is even supposed to deliver. I've just been accumulating fragments — a bit of Lewis here, a bit of Woodward there — and I can't tell whether the disagreements are about the phenomena or about what everyone's trying to build. So before I read one more paper on preemption, give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of singular/token causation have to get right? I want the cases it must handle, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (causing vs. enabling vs. merely being a background condition; causing an event vs. explaining it; being a cause vs. being a difference-maker; degree or proportionality vs. on/off; the fact that causal asymmetry doesn't just read off the equations), and the constraints it can't violate (it shouldn't make causation everywhere or nowhere; it shouldn't presuppose that everything relevant is deterministic, or that everything relevant isn't). For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of a camp — I suspect 'no causation by omission' and 'causation is transitive' are the latter, but tell me if I'm wrong about that.

The reason I want the list and not another theory is that the cases I actually care about seem to pull in different directions, and I need to know which of them are genuine tests of a theory and which are just my confusions. Four that I keep hitting. (1) I zero a unit in a trained network and the class prediction flips — but zeroing it also perturbs everything downstream, and there's a redundant pathway that would have taken over if the thing had been trained on a slightly different sample, so the "intervention" isn't clean and I can't tell if that's a real problem or just old preemption wearing a lab coat. (2) I have a recurrent circuit where unit A's sustained firing depends on B's and B's on A's, both are but-for causes of the readout, and neither is upstream of the other. (3) The behaviour is carried by a group of about forty units: knock out any one and nothing changes, knock out all forty and it's gone — is the group a cause, and if so is that a different kind of causation or the same kind at a coarser grain? (4) In a detector I study, what the downstream reader uses is a unit's silence — zero spikes at a particular moment — and if that unit had fired, the animal would of made the other choice. That looks like causation by absence, which half the theories seem to want to outlaw by fiat.
