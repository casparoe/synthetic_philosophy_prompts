I have spent the past three years reporting on the politics of shame — on online pile-ons, on regulators who name and shame corporate wrongdoers, on truth commissions in post-conflict states, on programmes that try to replace honour-based punishment with restorative conferences — and I keep running into the same argument, in newsrooms, in NGO seminars and in the academic literature, without either side moving. One camp treats guilt as the mature moral emotion: it attaches to what an agent did rather than to what the agent is, it tracks conduct rather than the self, and it issues in apology and repair. Shame, on this account, is heteronomous — parasitic on the real or imagined gaze of others — and it produces concealment, withdrawal, rage and the shifting of blame. The other camp replies that this is a provincial picture, an artefact of a Protestant and individualist moral psychology: shame, they say, can be elicited by one's own standards rather than by an audience, it is the more relational and the more realistic of the two emotions, and guilt is the thinner and more easily rationalised. The old guilt-culture versus shame-culture contrast, which anthropologists now treat as far too coarse, keeps reasserting itself in these exchanges. Meanwhile everyone concedes that shaming institutions and guilt-based institutions produce different behaviour, and nobody agrees whether that difference is a fact about the emotions or a fact about the institutions.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute over a word that names at least four different things — public humiliation, the experience of exposure, a self-directed judgment of failure, and a social sanction — or is it a substantive disagreement that has been misplaced? Are the parties answering different questions under one heading: one about what these emotions are, one about what they do, and one about what they are worth? Is there a hidden empirical question — about the effects of shaming on recidivism, on cooperation, on the willingness to admit fault — that would settle the matter if someone would specify the population and the outcome? Or does the impasse rest on an intuition, which neither side can argue for, about whether a motive that depends on others seeing us can count as autonomous?

I would like you to take up a second question as well, because I suspect it is where the real difficulty lies. The debate is conducted as though a culture could choose its moral emotions: drop shame and cultivate guilt, or, in the current revisionist mood, recover shame and discard guilt. Is that framing coherent? Emotions are learned and socially scaffolded, and historians who write about regimes of feeling describe official repertoires that states and churches cultivate through ritual, pedagogy and law; but it is not clear what it would mean for a legislature or a school system to select an emotion. If cultures do shift their emotional repertoires, does that happen by design or as a by-product of changes in law, religion and economy? Tell me whether the choice framing is a genuine possibility, a category mistake, or a useful fiction, and whether answering that dissolves or sharpens the first question. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a taxonomy, a specified intervention with a measurable outcome, a criterion for what makes one emotion morally better than another, or an argument that nothing should be expected?
