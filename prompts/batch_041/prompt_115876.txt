I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'm building an interactive flowchart for a lunchtime discussion series at my company. The tool takes a colleague who has never read a page of moral philosophy and routes them, through a series of concrete questions, to the position they actually hold on why promises bind — not to the label they'd pick if you simply asked them to choose one.

Act as a philosopher who works on the normativity of promising and who is good at building diagnostic instruments. Think carefully before committing to any branch. Start by brainstorming, in a few lines, the distinct ways one could answer "what makes a promise binding?" and the distinct ways one could answer "what, exactly, does the promisor owe?" Then build the tree from that space of possible combinations, and only afterwards place the familiar positions inside it — do not start from a list of school names and invent questions to separate them.

Requirements for the instrument:

1. Every internal node must be a concrete case or a concrete claim with two or three answer options, phrased in ordinary language a non-philosopher could answer without knowing where the branch leads. No node may be a disguised version of "do you think promises are social conventions?" Cases worth using, at minimum: a promise made to someone who has since died; a promise to do something the other party no longer wants done; a promise to a stranger you will never meet again; a promise to do what you would have done anyway; a promise extracted under pressure; a promise to keep a secret whose keeping now harms a third party; a promise made to something that cannot hold you to it (a pet, a group that never learns of the promise); a promise whose performance has become pointless; a promise you are physically unable to keep.

2. The first question must be able to route someone who says promises bind only through reputation and prudence to its own leaf. The tree must contain at least these leaves, described by content rather than by author: the promise binds because the other party now relies and expects (and the wrong is disappointing that expectation); because it is a move within a social practice whose rules constitute the obligation; because a general rule of promise-keeping has the best consequences; because the promisor has bound their own will; because fidelity is a virtue and the "obligation" is the demand of good character; because there is a basic duty of fidelity that needs no further grounding; because the obligation is to be trustworthy rather than to perform, so apology or compensation can discharge it; and because promises add nothing at all and merely predict what you would have done. Add two to four further leaves of your own choosing, and label each leaf with a short descriptive name, plus a defender's name in parentheses only if you are confident it is correct.

3. Every node must change the outcome — no decorative questions — and every leaf must be reachable; trace at least one full answer path to each leaf. Target roughly twelve to sixteen nodes and eight to twelve leaves, written for an audience of smart non-specialists.

4. Deliver: (a) the tree as an indented list with node IDs; (b) a machine-readable edge list or JSON object I can drop into a web app, with node ID, question text, option, and target ID; (c) for each leaf, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

5. Finish with a candid diagnostics section: which pairs of positions your tree cannot separate, and which combinations of answers are unoccupied — saying in each case whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Do not include citations, reading lists, or quotations; I have no way to check them and will not use them, and neither will the tool's users.
