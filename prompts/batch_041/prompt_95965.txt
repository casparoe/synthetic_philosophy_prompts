I'm a graduate student working on the ethics of suffering, and I've been trying to get clear on a family of views I'll call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The core commitment is that there is some threshold of suffering such that any instance of suffering at or above it takes priority over — is not to be traded off against — any amount of sub-threshold suffering or sub-threshold pleasure: a million mildly happy lives cannot justify one life of agony above the line. In my notes I've been drawing a distinction that I can no longer tell is doing real work or is just a verbal manoeuvre, and I'd like you to explain the conceptual issue as carefully as you can. Roughly: a view can be lexically strict, in which the exchange rate between above-threshold suffering and everything else is infinite — no finite quantity of sub-threshold good or ill ever balances a single above-threshold ill — or merely effectively lexical, in which the exchange rate is finite but so steep that no quantity of sub-threshold good that could ever be realised in practice would outweigh an above-threshold ill. I've been calling the second "effective lexicality," and my worry is that it either collapses into ordinary commensurability — since a finite rate means that some amount of mild pleasure, if you could amass enough of it, would outweigh an agony — or else that the difference between the two is normatively idle, in which case the rhetorical force of "lexical" priority comes for free and the threshold itself is doing no work.

Please explain the conceptual issue here: what exactly turns on the strict/effective distinction, whether effective lexicality can be made principled rather than an arbitrary large number, and whether the "some finite amount of pleasure would outweigh an agony" objection to effective lexicality is decisive or confused. I'd also like you to say how this interacts with the deeper question of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable at all — whether the whole framing presupposes a common currency that a defender of incommensurability would simply reject — and whether a threshold view can avoid positing a sharp discontinuity, i.e. whether lexical priority can be had without a cliff-edge in the weighting function.

To make this concrete, take the kind of case I keep coming back to: an intervention that moves one person from a hair above the threshold to a hair below it, which the view must count as an enormous improvement, while simultaneously moving a very large number of people from a hair below to a hair above, which it must count as catastrophic. Whether that verdict is a reductio or is simply what lexicality means seems to me to be exactly the question, and I'd like your explanation to confront it directly. Please don't just survey the literature; I want a worked-out account of where the real crux lies, what the strongest version of each side would say, and what would have to be true for the distinction to matter — something I could use as the backbone of a seminar presentation.
