I'm a science-and-design journalist, and last month I was at a type-and-typography conference where, after the talks, two people I know ended up going at it at the hotel bar for like an hour. One is a working type designer (I'll call her Marta) and the other is a cognitive scientist who studies reading and categorisation (I'll call him Deshawn). The fight was about what makes every 'a' an 'a' — whether there's some kind of essence to a letter, or whether that's a romantic myth. I've been meaning to write it up but I honestly couldn't figure out who was winning, so I'd love a blow-by-blow. Here's roughly how it went. Marta opened: 'There's no essence. An "a" is a convention we all learned. Look at the history — the Roman "a," blackletter, a geometric sans where the thing is basically a circle with a stick on it, a single-story "a" that's just a loop and a stem. Nothing survives all of those. It's family resemblance all the way down: a chain of overlapping similarities, no common core. And every real typeface reinvents the letter anyway, which is the whole point of the job.' Deshawn came back that if it were only resemblance with no core, you couldn't tell a botched 'a' from a 'q' or a smudge, and you couldn't look at some wild new display face and say 'that glyph is their "a."' He said there has to be an abstract skeleton — a bowl, a stem, a closure, the right orientation, the fact that it can drop into a word-slot — that stays fixed while the style changes, and that this is exactly what that old Indiana project (Hofstadter's crew, the one that tried to get a program to design a whole alphabet in one coherent style) was chasing: pull the letter-concept apart from the style.

Marta said that's circular. You only get to call one stroke 'the bowl' after you've already decided the glyph is an 'a' — the anatomy is the conclusion, not the evidence. And anyway half the examples don't have a bowl at all, so he's just describing the prototypical 'a' (double-story, serif, the one you'd draw if someone asked) and mistaking the prototype for the essence. Deshawn said fine, the prototype isn't the essence, but the roles he meant are relational and functional, not shape-y: an 'a' is defined by the way it contrasts with its neighbours in the alphabet, against 'o' and 'e' and 'd' and 'q' — a bundle of contrastive features inside a system. That's why you can distort an 'a' a mile and we still read it, but nudge it just a little toward another letter and it snaps. Marta said that's just moving the bump under the rug — now the essence is 'the whole alphabet,' which is another way of saying there's no essence of any single letter, only a network of differences, and the network isn't fixed either, because we keep redesigning it.

Can you referee this properly, move by move? I don't need a verdict on 'who won' so much as, for each thing each of them actually said, whether it's right, half-right, or wrong, and where the real disagreement is hiding underneath. And if there's a formulation that gets at what neither of them quite said — something about essences versus prototypes versus contrastive systems that doesn't collapse into either of their corners — I'd love that, because right now I've got two smart people talking past each other and a deadline.
