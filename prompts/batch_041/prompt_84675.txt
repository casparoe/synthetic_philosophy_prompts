My mate Dan and I spent most of Saturday evening going at each other about split-brain patients while his flatmate kept trying to watch the match over us — Dan's convinced that once you sever the corpus callosum you've got two centres of consciousness in one skull, two subjects sharing a body, and he kept saying "well, obviously" as if it were a lab result rather than one reading of the data; I think that's a wild over-reaction and that what you've actually got is one subject whose consciousness has come apart at the seams, disunified in various modality-specific ways, with the left hemisphere doing the talking and confabulating reasons for things it never saw. We got nowhere, mainly because we kept sliding between "two streams of experience" and "two persons" and neither of us would say what we meant by a subject. So I want it done properly: draft the joint statement that the two of us — call him the two-streams man, me the one-subject man — could actually both put our names to after a serious adversarial collaboration. Start with a numbered list of claims we can both sign: what the surgery really severs (including that the corpus callosum isn't the only commissure and that leaving some of the others intact is a genuine variable), what the standard split-visual-field demonstrations do and don't show, how far we can trust the patient's own report of feeling unified, the cross-cueing problem, and the awkward fact that these patients mostly live perfectly ordinary lives. Then the residual disagreements, each one worded the way its own side would want it worded, in a table with the claim on the left and which of us owns it on the right.

After that I want the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move one of us, not the ones we just enjoy shouting about. I think there are at least three, though tell me if I've got them wrong: (i) whether having a single self-model, or a single global workspace, is what makes something one subject, or whether that's just one more feature a hemisphere could turn out to have; (ii) whether the left hemisphere's confabulated explanations are evidence of a second agent, or just of the only agent's interpretive machinery chewing on an input it can't see; (iii) whether the unity that matters is synchronic — one experiential field at a time — or diachronic — one life, one autobiography — because if we mean different things by unity we'll never agree about anything else. For each crux I want the case or test we'd both accept as bearing on it, with both our predictions written down before anyone looks. The Wada procedure is an obvious one, where you put one hemisphere to sleep with an intracarotid injection and see what's left: I predict what's left is still the same subject, temporarily thinned out, whereas Dan predicts you've unmasked a second subject running on its own. Same treatment for the partial-cut patients, and for the old trick where one hemisphere knows something the other denies knowing, plus whatever test would genuinely separate "the right hemisphere had an experience the left couldn't reach" from "there was one experience that nobody could report". And if part of this turns out to be purely verbal — I suspect the two-subjects bit is just the two-streams bit with a heavier word bolted on — then say which part, and why, and what each of us should count as evidence against our own view. I'm definately not after a reading list, I want the document.
