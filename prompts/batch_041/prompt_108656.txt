I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and have one ninety-minute session to spend on heterophenomenology, the method Dennett proposed for making consciousness scientifically tractable by treating a subject's verbal reports as a special kind of data — neither certified as true nor dismissed as noise — and constructing a theory of the world as the subject takes it to be. Sixteen students. I want them to discover the method by using it, and to feel at once its attraction and its cost, before I say anything about what it is. Design the activity. My own rough sketch is that students work in pairs, one as subject and one as experimenter, but replace it if you have something better. Whatever the design, I want a few constraints built in, because they are where I think the real lesson lives. First, the subject must report under a tight budget — a small fixed vocabulary, or a hard cap on words or answers — so that the experimenter is forced to infer rather than transcribe. Second, the reports should be collected in a form that makes them unusable as first-person evidence: sealed, anonymized, or routed through a second experimenter, so that nobody in the room can appeal to what it was like. Third, the experimenter should be scored, not on whether the subject's reports are true, but on whether the model predicts the subject's next reports; and at some point a confederate subject should be introduced whose reports are generated by a rule the experimenter can fit perfectly without the underlying experience being anything like what the fit suggests. I want the group to end up unable to say whether the method has captured experience or only built a tidy account of what the subject will say next — and to see that this is a feature of the method, not a failure of their execution.

Give me the full runnable thing: setting, materials, roles, the exact rules, the scoring or payoff scheme (no real money), a timeline that fits the ninety minutes, and the debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the steps of the method — the recording of utterances, the treatment of them as a text, the construction of the subject's world, and the neutrality that refuses both to verify and to debunk. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself: in particular, that in real science the reports are disciplined by other data, and that bracketing experience is not the same as denying it.

Write the answer as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — the way you would write a long letter.
