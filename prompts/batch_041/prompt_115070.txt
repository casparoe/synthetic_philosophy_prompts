Ok, I need an outside verdict on an arguement I got stuck in the middle of, and I'm not going to say which side I lean toward because honestly I couldn't tell.

My flatmate Ravi and his sister Priya were over and we ended up on gambling games with weird expected values. Ravi's been obsessed with this one for weeks. Call it Game A: you flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if the first heads lands on flip n, the payoff is 2^n/n dollars, positive when n is odd and negative when n is even. So heads on the first flip wins you 2 dollars, first heads on the second flip loses you 2, third wins 8/3, fourth loses 4, and so on, the amounts getting bigger in both directions. The chance the first heads is on flip n is 1/2^n, so outcome n contributes plus or minus 1/n to the expectation, and those contributions run 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which adds to ln 2, about 69 cents.

Ravi says that 69 cents is an illusion. The series only converges conditionally, so adding the terms in a different order can give you any number at all, a thousand, minus a thousand. And there's no privileged order for adding up the outcomes of a random variable, so the game has no expected value. Not unknown, undefined. He adds that the positive contributions total infinity and the negative ones total infinity too, so it's infinity minus infinity, which isn't a number.

Priya says that's a fact about a formula, not about a game. Her line: every real play of the game is finite, you always stop at some flip, so the value should be the limit of the values of the finite versions, and that limit is ln 2 however you cut it, because the flips come in an order and the game is played in that order. She also says undefined expectation can't mean there's nothing to say, because look at Game B, which Ravi invented as a follow-up: same coin, same payoffs, except B adds 1/n^2 dollars to whatever A pays out, so a dollar extra on the first flip, a quarter on the second, a ninth on the third. B pays more than A in every single outcome with no exceptions, so whatever you think about expected values, B is better than A. She says a theory that shrugs and calls them incomparable because the expectation is undefined is broken. (On the usual adding-up, B comes to about a dollar thirty, for what it's worth.)

Ravi's reply: fine, let dominance do the work where expectation can't. But B's series is also only conditionally convergent, so he can add B's terms in an order that makes it worth minus a thousand while adding A's in an order that makes it worth plus a thousand. Now you're saying B is better than A and also that B is worth less than A. He says that isn't a cute counterexample, it's the same disease, and it shows you can't bolt dominance onto a framework with a hole in it to save it.

Priya's comeback: rearranging the sum isn't something you do to the game, it's something you do to a formula, and you don't get to invent a value for B by summing its terms perversely and then announce a contradiction. She also said the infinity minus infinity argument proves too much, because it would make every game with an unbounded payoff incomparable with a sure amount, and that can't be right since the truncations give you comparisons.

They went around like this for an hour and I lost the thread.
