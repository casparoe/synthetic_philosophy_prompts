You are grading a final examination in a course on social choice and collective decision-making. The examination question and a student's answer are reproduced below.

Question

A three-member tribunal must decide whether a defendant is liable. Liability, L, holds if and only if both P and Q hold, where P is the proposition that the defendant signed the contract and Q is the proposition that the contract contained an enforceable liquidated-damages clause. Each judge submits a complete and consistent judgment set over P, Q, and L. Their votes are:

Judge 1: P yes, Q yes, L yes.
Judge 2: P yes, Q no, L no.
Judge 3: P no, Q yes, L no.

The tribunal uses simple majority on each proposition.

(a) Explain why each judge is individually consistent and how the collective result nevertheless violates logical closure. (4 points)

(b) State the outcome under a premise-based procedure and under a conclusion-based procedure, and explain why they diverge. (3 points)

(c) A commentator claims that this example proves majority rule is self-contradictory and that the only remedy is to replace majority voting with a consensus procedure. Evaluate that claim. (3 points)

Student answer

Each judge is individually rational because each one's judgment set is deductively closed. Judge 1 affirms P and Q and therefore affirms L. Judge 2 affirms P but denies Q, so denies L. Judge 3 denies P but affirms Q, so denies L. No judge holds an inconsistent set. The collective result is different. On P, Judges 1 and 2 outvote Judge 3, so P passes. On Q, Judges 1 and 3 outvote Judge 2, so Q passes. On L, only Judge 1 votes yes, so L fails. Thus the tribunal accepts both premises but rejects the conclusion they entail. This is the discursive dilemma.

If the tribunal uses a premise-based procedure, it will first decide P and Q by majority and then apply the logical rule, so it will accept L. If it uses a conclusion-based procedure, it will decide L directly, so it will reject L. The two procedures therefore yield opposite results.

The commentator is right that majority rule is self-contradictory here, but wrong that consensus is the only remedy. The problem is not majority rule as such; it is that majority rule is being applied to logically connected propositions without a procedure for ensuring consistency. A premise-based procedure solves the problem by making the premises authoritative. This shows that the discursive dilemma is really just Arrow's impossibility theorem applied to judgments rather than preferences. Arrow showed that no voting rule can satisfy all reasonable conditions, and the present case is an instance of that. The best response is to choose a procedure that tracks the truth, and premise-based voting is generally superior because it preserves logical closure and respects the majority on each premise. Consensus would avoid the inconsistency but at the cost of giving every judge a veto, which is why it is usually worse.
