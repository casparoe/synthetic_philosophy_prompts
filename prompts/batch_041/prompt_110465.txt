My metaphysics reading group has been stuck on the two-spheres case for three weeks now — the universe that contains nothing but two iron spheres, a mile or so apart, identical in size, shape, mass, colour, temperature and every other intrinsic respect, with the whole world perfectly symmetric about the midpoint. Half the group says obviously there are two things there, the other half says we've just described one thing twice, and one guy keeps saying "but I can call them A and B," which everyone finds either decisive or beside the point depending on the day. I've realized I don't actually know what I'm asking of an answer here, and I'd rather have the spec nailed down before I read one more paper about it.

So write me the desiderata. What does any adequate account of numerical distinctness — or any adequate defence or rejection of the claim that things differing in no way can't be two — have to deliver? I want the cases it has to get right: the two spheres themselves; a variant where the spheres are indistinguishable in their histories too, except that one came into existence a year before the other, so only temporal/relational facts separate them; a variant with indeterministic laws where at some later time one of them turns red and the other doesn't, so it looks like something has to single one of them out; three indiscernible spheres in a symmetric ring; and a world that's a perfect duplicate of ours shifted a mile to the left. Then the distinctions it can't collapse: numerical vs qualitative identity; being indiscernible vs being indistinguishable by any possible test; individuating by intrinsic properties vs by relations; identity being a fact of the matter vs being a convention of our descriptions; and the weak vs the strong reading of the principle. Then the background results it can't contradict: that fundamental physics never has to label its particles; that in quantum mechanics permuting two identical particles doesn't give you a different state of affairs; that no experiment could detect an absolute shift of the universe. For each requirement, say what case or consideration is behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste.

The part I most want is where you flag pairs of these that can't be jointly satisfied. My suspicion is that the moment you let relational properties do the individuating, the principle either becomes trivially true or trivially false, and I want to know if thats the real fault line or whether I'm just repeating something I half-remember. Tell me too which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise-attractive options. If it's cheap to add, close with two or three lines on how bundle theory, bare particulars, primitive thisness, and a structuralist line each fare against the list — but keep that short, the criteria are the deliverable. No citations or reading list, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the spec.
