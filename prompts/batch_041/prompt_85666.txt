Question 3 (short answer, 20 marks). We distinguished ontological individualism — social facts are nothing over and above individuals and their interactions — from explanatory individualism — social facts must be explained by facts about individuals. A colleague writes: "Since institutions are just patterns of individual behaviour, any explanation that stops at the level of institutions is a placeholder; the only real explanations name individuals."

(a) Reconstruct the colleague's inference. (4)
(b) Is the inference valid? (6)
(c) What, if anything, does the multiple realizability point do for the colleague's case? (4)
(d) Briefly: how would Durkheim and Popper each respond? (6)

Student's answer:

Ontological individualism says there's no group mind: when we talk about a state, a bank, or a norm, we're talking about individuals and their interactions and nothing else. Explanatory individualism adds that explanations have to terminate in facts about individuals, because there's nothing else for them to terminate in.

The colleague's inference is: (i) social facts are composed of individual actions; (ii) a composite can't explain what its components don't; therefore (iii) explanations that stop at the social level are incomplete. I think (iii) doesn't follow from (i) and (ii). You can grant (i) and still think "the interest rate rose because the central bank raised it" is a perfectly good explanation, since explanatory relevance isn't the same as metaphysical basicality, and nothing about supervenience tells you which level of description does the explaining. Multiple realizability makes this sharper: "high-trust society" is realized by quite different individual-level arrangements in different countries, so citing it doesn't pick out any determinate individual-level cause, which means the individual-level story is the one carrying the explanatory load. So the colleague's conclusion is probably right even though his argument doesn't get him there.

Durkheim would reject (iii) while accepting (i). His claim about suicide rates is that a rate is explained by social integration and regulation — one social fact by another — and he's explicit that this is about explanatory order, not about society being a big person. He'd say individual-level facts are simply the wrong place to look for the cause of a rate, and I think that's the strongest version of the anti-individualist position.

Popper is the mirror image. Methodological individualism for him means that social phenomena are the intended results of individual actions, which is why he's so suspicious of explanations that appeal to unintended consequences. Weber is the cleanest individualist of the three: explanation means recovering the subjective meaning an actor attaches to her own conduct — not necessarily a self-interested or even a rational one — which is why his ideal types don't need any social-level entities.
