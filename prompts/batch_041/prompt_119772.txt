I've been working through chapter 25 of Machiavelli's The Prince and I keep getting stuck on the same tangle, so what I want is help untangling it rather than a summary. Here is the passage in the English translation I'm using:

"I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her."

A little later he adds that Italy is an open country without barriers, and that "if it had been defended by proper valour, as are Germany, Spain, and France, either this invasion would not have made the great changes it has made or it would not have come at all." The rest of the chapter, as I read it, goes like this: princes rise and fall without any change in themselves; a man prospers when his fixed way of proceeding happens to match the times and is ruined when the times turn, yet no one can change his nature to suit them; and the chapter closes by preferring the impetuous man to the cautious one and calling fortune a woman who must be beaten and struck.

What I can't make fit is this. The river simile turns resistance to fortune into a matter of foresight and preparation — dikes built during quiet times — which sounds like precisely the patient, calculating disposition the chapter later says is doomed when the times change; and the valour that has failed to prepare seems to belong to whole countries rather than to individual men. Is the virtù in the simile the same thing as the virtù of the man whose methods ride the times, and the same again as the audacity that beats the woman? If so, is the chapter coherent, or is it sliding between an institutional sense of virtù and a personal one? And if the only virtù an individual can have is to have been born with a nature that happens to match the times, does the advice to build dikes amount to anything more than a recommendation to be lucky? I'd also like to know what work the passing appeal to free will is doing: is "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions" a claim about what causes events, or only about what can be foreseen and countered? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, or tables — I want it to read like a letter, not a set of notes.
