I'm not at uni or anything, just someone who got into decision theory through blogs and podcasts, and I've got an argument that a mate of mine swears proves we all have to maximise expected utility. The trouble is it seems to prove something much stronger, and I'm pretty sure the stronger thing is rubbish. Here it is:

(1) If your preferences can be represented by a utility function and you always pick the option with the highest expected utility, then no sequence of trades can leave you worse off for certain.
(2) If your preferences go in a cycle — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — then a clever trader can offer you a series of swaps, each of which you'll take because it moves you up your own ranking, and you end up back where you started minus a small fee. That fee is the pump.
(3) Any preference pattern that lets a trader pump you for certain is incoherent, so coherence demands that your preferences be transitive and complete enough to be represented by a utility function.
(4) But you can build the same kind of pump for someone whose preferences are transitive and complete yet violate the independence axiom — the Allais-style case where you choose the safe option in one pair and the risky one in another just because of how the common prize is framed.
(5) So coherence rules those preferences out too.
(6) And if you ever revise your preferences after new evidence or reflection, a trader can pump you as well: they buy low from your current self and sell high to your future self, or the other way round. My mate says adopting a policy or resolution doesn't help, because then the policy is the thing being chosen and the same argument applies to policies.
(7) Therefore coherence requires a fixed, complete, transitive, independence-satisfying preference ordering over everything, and you can never change it.
(8) But that's absurd. It means you can't become a better person, can't learn from experience, and can't have the sort of open-ended values where you find out what you care about by living.
(9) So something in the argument must be wrong, but I can't see what. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd like to keep (1) and (3) if I possibly can, but not if the price is (8). Also, if the answer is that the trader can't really pump a preference-changer because the future you isn't bound by the present you, does that also let the intransitive person off the hook, or only the changer?
