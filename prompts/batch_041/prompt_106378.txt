I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I keep running into the same line whenever a historical controversy breaks: a fight over how a country should commemorate its founding, or over what a newly opened archive really shows, or over whether a museum label should be rewritten. Someone on the panel — usually a historian with a lot of institutional weight — will say, with the air of settling matters, that the historian's only job is to show the past as it actually was, and that everything else is commentary, present-day moralizing, or politics. The line is normally attributed to the nineteenth-century German historian Leopold von Ranke, and I know enough to know that the sceptics treat it as either naive or impossible: the tradition running through E. H. Carr, who argued that the historian's selection is partly constitutive of the facts rather than a neutral window onto them, and through Hayden White, who argued that historians unavoidably impose literary plot structures on a shapeless past. What I cannot do — on a deadline, or in front of an audience, or when a colleague has just used the line to shut down a question about whose story gets told — is say what the promise actually amounts to.

So what I want from you is not a defence or a refutation of Ranke but a menu: the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, laid out as separate readings rather than collapsed into one. I suspect there is a spectrum. At the innocuous end, it might mean only that historians should not fabricate, distort, or suppress what the evidence supports. Somewhere in the middle, it might mean that a period admits of a single best description that does not vary with the historian's interests or selection. At the far end, it might mean something metaphysical: that the past is simply there, complete and determinate, waiting to be copied rather than reconstructed. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude no working historian would deny, a substantive and defensible position, or something actually false — and, this is the part I most need, tell me which readings the sceptical objections require in order to bite at all. If Carr and White are only refuting the strongest reading, then the person on my panel can retreat to a weaker one and keep his authority intact; if their arguments also threaten the weaker readings, the retreat fails. I want to know which way it goes and how to say so in a paragraph.

One further wrinkle to fold in: the German word in the original can be read as meaning actually or as meaning essentially, or properly — the first pointing at correspondence to what occurred, the second at grasping a period on its own terms rather than through later categories. I would like to know whether that distinction is doing real work, and if so what work, or whether it is a translator's red herring. When you tell me which reading the phrase is most likely performing when someone uses it to close down an argument — as it was used to me last month, against a proposal to teach a national founding story as a story of both settlement and dispossession — give me both the short version I could put in the piece and the single follow-up question I should ask to find out which reading the speaker is actually committed to. Don't bother with sources, citations, or a reading list; I will add those myself, and I would rather have the distinctions clean than the scholarship surveyed.
