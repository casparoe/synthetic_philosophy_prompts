I need a joke about the absent-minded driver problem, and I want the humor to come from the actual structure of the problem rather than from the phrase "absent-minded." The setup I have in mind is the standard one: a driver travels a road with two intersections, and at each one he may either exit or continue. Exiting at the first intersection yields nothing, exiting at the second yields the large payoff, and continuing past both yields a small consolation payoff. The driver cannot distinguish the two intersections, and he knows in advance that whatever he decides to do at an intersection, he will do the same thing at the other one, because the decision is made once and the memory of having made it does not survive to the second occasion. The usual answer is that he should continue with probability two-thirds, which yields a better expected payoff than either always continuing or always exiting; part of what makes the case interesting is that it is a single decision encountered twice under imperfect recall, so the two "occasions" cannot be treated as independent choice points without distortion, and this is where the disagreement between decision theories tends to surface.

Write one joke, or a short pair of jokes, that a reader who already knows this problem would find funny, with the punchline turning on something real: the indistinguishability of the intersections, the fact that the same policy governs both encounters, the mixed strategy and its odd status as something the driver cannot condition on his location, the difference between what maximizes expected payoff from the ex ante perspective and what seems rational at the moment of choosing, the impossibility of precommitting in the ordinary sense when the agent who would carry out the commitment is also the agent who forgets it, or the fact that the driver's uncertainty about where he is is partly uncertainty about his own future behavior. Jokes that merely observe that decision theorists disagree, or that absent-minded people forget things, will not do; the punchline has to be earned by the structure. After each joke, add two or three sentences explaining exactly which feature of the problem it exploits and why that feature is the funny part, so that the explanation itself could be used to check whether the joke is actually about the absent-minded driver problem or only decorated with its vocabulary.

If you can, include one variant in which the situation is slightly altered -- for instance a third intersection, a passenger who can tell the intersections apart, a driver who can leave himself a note but cannot be sure he will trust it, or two drivers who can coordinate beforehand but not during the drive -- and say what the variant changes about the joke and about the problem. I would rather have one joke that lands than three that gesture at the topic, so do not pad the answer.
