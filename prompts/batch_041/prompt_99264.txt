I'm leading a discussion group on Robert Bolt's A Man for All Seasons next week and I want to read it through Plutarch's idea of what biography is for. The thing that's always stuck with me about Plutarch is that he says he isn't writing history, he's writing lives, and that some small remark or gesture tells you more about a man's character than a whole battle does, and that the whole point is that you might improve yourself by watching these people. Bolt's More is basically a saint -- he goes to the block for his conscience while almost everyone around him is a timeserver, a fool, or a betrayer (Richard Rich especially). So: is this a Plutarchan life or is it just straight hagiography, and does the difference matter for what the play is doing?

A few things I want the reading to actually deal with. Plutarch pairs his lives up, a Greek against a Roman, so you can weigh one man's virtues against another's -- Bolt gives us one hero and a chorus of moral inferiors, so what does that do to the moral lesson, if there even is one? Does the play want me to imitate More or just admire him, and is that a real distinction? And then the thing I keep tripping on: I've been reading about the situationist critique of virtue ethics, the experiments suggesting people's behavior is driven more by the situation they're in than by any stable character trait. If that's right, doesn't it gut the whole Plutarchan project? If More's courage is just one man in one very specific corner, what am I actually supposed to learn from him?

Point me to the specific scenes that carry this, say where the play resists the reading, and dont let it turn into an illustration of Plutarch. I'd like to end by saying what the play shows about learning from lives that the Plutarch and the situationist arguments by themselves dont.
