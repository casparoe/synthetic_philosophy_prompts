I am building a program that designs letterforms. Give it a style — a handful of sample letters, or a described aesthetic — and it should generate the rest of the Roman alphabet in that style, so that every shape it produces is recognizably the letter it is meant to be and yet plainly belongs to the same family as its siblings. The project is in the lineage of the Letter Spirit work in Hofstadter's Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies, but I am not asking you to reconstruct that work. Before I write any of the machinery, I need a specification for the single concept the whole thing turns on: what makes a shape count as the letter a.

Write the desiderata for a theory of letter identity. State what any adequate account of "this shape is an a" must deliver, give the case or consideration that motivates each requirement, and mark each as non-negotiable or negotiable for my purpose. Build the list around the following cases, which I take to be the ones any account has to survive:

- The span of things a competent reader accepts as a. Serif and sans, italic and upright, the double-storey a and the single-storey a, the forms in cursive, in blackletter, in a child's handwriting, in a wildly stylized display face. Whatever the theory says, it may not draw the boundary so tight that it starts rejecting ordinary typefaces.
- The near-misses it must still reject. A reflected a, an upside-down a, a d or a q read as a rotated a, an o or an e with a stray stroke, the at-sign, the Greek alpha, the script a of the phonetic alphabet, a bowl with its stem attached at the wrong place or on the wrong side. Orientation matters here in a way it does not for most objects — a mirrored cup is a cup, and a mirrored a is, for most readers, not an a — and I want to know what the theory does with that.
- The homoglyph problem. A glyph can be visually identical to the Latin a and still be a different letter: the Cyrillic а is drawn the same as the Latin a, and the Greek, Cyrillic, and Latin capitals are often indistinguishable. So two shapes that are the same shape can be different letters, and two shapes that are visibly different — the double-storey a and the script a — can be read as the same letter in the same script. Any theory has to say what it is that a pure description of shape leaves out, and how recognition gets away with ignoring that thing.
- Graded membership and typicality. Some a's strike a reader as more a-ish than others; some are marginal, sitting near the edge of the category; and there is a point at which further deformation stops producing an a at all. A theory must either explain the gradient or explain it away, and either way it must locate the boundary or explain why there is none.
- Identity versus quality versus legibility. "Is this an a?" is not "Is this a good a?" and neither is "Is this a legible a?" A smudged or clumsily drawn a is still an a; a beautiful shape in the style of an a may still fail to be one. The theory of identity must not smuggle in aesthetics or legibility, though it should say how the three are related.
- The alphabet as a system. Letters come in alphabets and constrain one another. The a a designer draws has to sit alongside the b, the g, the o, the q, the u of the same style, and the a and the g share a structural motif that the theory cannot ignore. Identity may therefore be partly relational — a is what it is in part by contrast with its neighbors — and the theory has to handle both the invariants internal to the letter and the contrasts that run between letters.
- The category-by-style grid. The same category appears in many styles, and the same style appears across many categories. Two a's in different styles are the same letter; an a and a b in the same style are different letters. The theory must make identity separable from style, and explain how a single generative process can vary one while holding the other fixed.
- The normativity of the letter. A shape is an a because competent readers and writers of the script treat it as one, including a disposition to produce it correctly and to correct others. The theory should say where this norm lives — in the shape, in the community of users, in the letter's function in the writing system, or in the letter's history — and whether the answer is part of what makes the shape the letter it is or merely a fact about how we come to know it.
- Context. The same mark can be an a in one setting and not in another, and some shapes are disambiguated only by the letters around them. A theory must say how much work context does before the identity of the shape dissolves into its surroundings.
- Psychological and developmental fit. The account should be consistent with how children acquire letters, with how quickly readers recognize a's in wildly varying handwriting, and with the fact that skilled readers appear to code letters in a form abstract enough to survive enormous changes in shape and yet sensitive enough to distinguish d from b. It should not posit representations that no reader could plausibly be carrying.

Then do three things. First, flag any pair of these requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why — I expect at least one such pair to fall out of the homoglyph case, and I would rather learn it from you now than from a broken generator later. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate otherwise-plausible accounts, and name the accounts it eliminates — I am especially interested in whether it kills every account that identifies the letter with its shape, and every account that identifies it with its code point or its script membership, leaving something in between. Third, tell me which requirements are genuine and which are, on reflection, prejudices of a particular camp — of typography, of linguistics, of cognitive science — that I could drop without loss.

Weight the answer toward the criteria and the cases behind them; I will do the scoring of particular theories myself.
