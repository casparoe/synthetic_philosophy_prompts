I'm TAing a first-year grad seminar on mental causation and the causal exclusion arguement, and I want to hand out a one-page decision tree instead of giving yet another lecture on it. The idea: everyone starts at 'the mental does some real causal work and I'm not giving that up', and then walks down branch nodes until they land on a named position.

Each node has to be a question they can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so phrase them as concrete commitments, not as labels. Stuff like: do you accept that every physical event with a cause has a sufficient purely physical cause? Is a mental property identical with the physical property it rides on, or just correlated with it? If the mental property and the physical property are each enough on their own to produce the effect, is that overdetermination, genuine competition, or neither? Here's a concrete case I want one node built on: a subject's pain and the simultaneous firing of her C-fibers both precede her wince — what does your view say actually did the causing, and does it matter that the two aren't independent? And: is causation fundamentally a matter of making a difference or of producing something, because I think half my students' confusion lives right there.

I want the branches to be the premises of the exclusion argument plus the ways of denying each one: causal closure, property distinctness, the no-competition/no-systematic-overdetermination premise, realization or supervenience of the mental on the physical, and the difference-making vs producing thing above. Leaves I need to see: reductive identity theory; non-reductive physicalism that still grants mental causation; property-dualist epiphenomenalism; interactionist dualism; the token-identity view where mental events just are physical events but mental properties aren't causal as such; panpsychist/Russellian monism where the mental is the intrinsic nature of the physical; emergentism; eliminativism; and the deflationary option that says the whole fight is a verbal dispute about what we mean by 'cause'. Also throw in one leaf that nobody actually defends but that the logic of the tree makes room for, and tell me whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And put in a node about whether the same arguement would gut biology, economics, etc., since that's where my students always get stuck.

For each leaf: the position name (or 'unnamed'), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and which earlier answer it hangs on. If the tree can't pull two leaves apart — I suspect non-reductive physicalism and emergentism are going to be trouble — flag it and say why instead of pretending the questions separate them.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words, American English, formatted as an indented tree with the notes hanging off the leaves.
