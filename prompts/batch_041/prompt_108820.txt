I run a 90-minute session for a grad seminar in philosophy of science, about 12 students, and I want a hands-on activity that makes them feel the force of the problem of identifying an ontology from inaccessible information. Not a lecture up front: they should run into the problem themselves. Here's the rough setup, but please turn it into a runnable game with rules, materials, roles, a timeline, and a debrief. The game is a black-box reverse-engineering contest. There are three sealed boxes. Each box has a hidden internal ontology, something like a small set of entities with states and transition rules. The teams can press one of a few buttons on the box and get a coarse output, maybe a colored token or a number. They can query as much as they like, but they can never open the box. Each team has to submit two things: a prediction for a held-out batch of button presses, and a one-page ontology report saying how many kinds of entities are inside, what states they can take, and what causal relations hold among them. Score the prediction part, not the ontology report, so the incentive is to be empirically adequate rather than ontologically right. The catch: I want at least two of the boxes to be behaviorally indistinguishable under every query the teams can make, but to have genuinely different ontologies. One might be, say, three coupled oscillators; another might be a single field with a memory. Better still, one might be naturally read as a bunch of objects with intrinsic states and the other as a pure relational structure with no intrinsic properties, even though all the accessible statistics are the same. The teams won't know which is which, and they'll have to decide whether to say the ontology is underdetermined or to pick one on some other ground like simplicity. I also want them to feel the difference between a hidden fact that a cleverer experiment could reveal and a fact that no accessible information could ever settle, so build in a moment where they realize that more data won't help. The point they should come away with is that accessible information plus a theory of what counts as a good explanation still doesn't uniquely identify the ontology; the identification is an extra posit, not a readout. Include roles per team, maybe a theorist, an experimentalist, a skeptic, and a reporter, and a whole-group referee who challenges the reports. The debrief should map what happened onto the idea of underdetermination, the difference between empirical adequacy and ontological commitment, and the temptation to treat parsimony as if it were evidence. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong teh first time and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the problem the game can't reproduce, e.g. a sealed box is only contingently inaccessible, so I'll need to say something myself about in-principle inaccessibility. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs like an email. And dont lean on named philosophical positions or citations; if you need a label for something, make one up inside the activity and let me supply the real names in the debrief.
