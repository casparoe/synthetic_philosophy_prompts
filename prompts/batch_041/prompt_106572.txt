I host a monthly interview show for a science-and-ideas outlet — mostly forty minutes with a researcher about a new book — and next month's guest is an ethicist who has spent the last several years arguing that we've been thinking about moral disagreement all wrong. Her line is that when two people hold different values they can often make a mutually beneficial exchange: a vegetarian and a carnivore agree that the carnivore will cut her meat eating in half and the vegetarian will kick in money to a farm-animal charity, with the split picked so that each of them, judged by her own values, prefers the deal to the status quo. She calls it the moral analogue of gains from trade, says it's the reason compromise isn't always a zero-sum concession, and she extends it to logrolling between voters, to "moral offsets" between people backing different causes, and even to paying a political opponent to tone things down. She's very good at making it sound obvious and I want to make her work for it. If it helps, her framing is roughly: trade doesn't require you to agree about what's good, only that each side has something the other wants more than what it gives up.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (she's a friendly guest, she won't stonewall, but she does like to say "that's a separate question"). I need at least one question on each of these three, and add whatever else you think earns its place. First, the baseline problem — an exchange presupposes that each side already owns something it can trade away, so who decided the carnivore was entitled to eat meat in the first place, and if she wasn't, is there anything left to swap? I want a follow-up that keeps her from just saying the baseline is whatever the law happens to be. Second, the worry that most real moral conflict isn't an exchange but a division — one indivisible thing (a border, custody of a child, whether a referendum passes) that both sides want whole — and that her model only looks good because she keeps picking examples with two independent goods in them; if she says trade needs nothing more than ordinal preferences, press her on what happens when one side's ranking is lexicographic. Third, the corruption and incentive worries: does buying someone's compliance taint the thing you bought, and does making values tradeable give everyone a reason to hold more extreme ones so they have more to sell?

Two more things I'd like in there somewhere. One question about whether the model is meant to describe how people actually negotiate or to tell them what they should do — she slides between those and I want it on the record. And one that turns the thing on her own side: whether knowing a trade is available changes what you're allowed to do unilaterally before any deal exists (if I know I can offset my flight, does that change whether I should take it, and does it change it for the better?). Keep the phrasing in my voice — first person, spoken, nothing that sounds like an exam question, and no "walk me through how you got interested in this" filler. Ive done enough of these to know the first thirty seconds decide whether anyone listens.
