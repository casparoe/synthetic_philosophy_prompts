I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm at work on a long feature about the 2006 demotion of Pluto — not the sociology of the affair, which has been covered to death, but its afterlife as a live problem in metaphysics. What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions: the problem of universals read into the episode, and the episode read back onto the problem.

Please take the role of a philosopher of science with a serious grounding in metaphysics — someone at home in the literature on universals, natural kinds, and classification, and also fluent in the actual planetary science. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short brainstorming section, roughly 300 words, in which you set out the candidate positions and, for each, one sentence on what it would predict about a dispute of this shape. Then write the analysis proper.

The facts to work from. You won't be able to look anything up, so the load-bearing material is here; treat it as given rather than trying to supplement it from memory, and if you think something I've supplied is odd, say so rather than quietly repairing it.

In August 2006 the International Astronomical Union, meeting in Prague, adopted a definition: within the Solar System, a planet is a body that orbits the Sun, is massive enough for its self-gravity to make it nearly round, and has "cleared the neighbourhood" around its orbit. A body satisfying only the first two conditions — Pluto, for instance — is a "dwarf planet." A rival resolution that would have made dwarf planets a subcategory of planets was also put to the vote, and defeated. The trigger for the whole exercise was a run of discoveries beyond Neptune beginning around 2000 — Quaoar, Sedna, and a body comparable to Pluto in size and greater in mass, later named Eris. The choice looked like this: either all of them come in, or Pluto goes out. The Union's own preparatory committee had floated three candidate definitions — a cultural one (a planet is a planet if enough people say so), a structural one (big enough to be round), and a dynamical one (dominant enough to clear its orbit). The Assembly took the dynamical criterion, added roundness, restricted the result to our own Solar System, and kept a separate, purely mass-based working definition for bodies orbiting other stars. Mercury, which is not in hydrostatic equilibrium, is nonetheless explicitly counted as a planet; and because the third criterion is location-dependent, an identical body further from the Sun could fail it. A rival, purely intrinsic definition — any non-fusing body massive enough to be round, wherever it orbits — is now widely used by working planetary scientists. The 2006 decision was carried by a vote of the members present at the closing session, a small fraction of the Union's membership, and was contested immediately by astronomers who held that a category of nature is not something a committee can vote into existence. There is a precedent worth having in view: Ceres was called a planet after its discovery in 1801, and by the 1850s, as more small bodies turned up between Mars and Jupiter, astronomers were cataloguing them separately and calling them asteroids. Ceres did not change; the category did.

H. Price; predicate nominalism or plain conventionalism; the cluster-kind views on which a kind is held together by a bundle of properties rather than an essence, of the sort Richard Boyd develops under the heading of homeostatic property clusters; and the pluralism about kinds that travels under the name promiscuous realism. For each, say what it implies about the dissenters' complaint that the Union had no authority to settle the matter.

Second, test two less obvious readings rather than assuming the standard menu is complete. The philosophy of biology has a well-known thesis that biological species are individuals — spatiotemporally located historical entities — rather than kinds; consider whether "planet" behaves more like a species in that sense than like a kind, and what would follow for the realism question. And consider whether the disagreement is substantive at all, in the way the quantifier-variance line associated with Eli Hirsch would have it: is this a case where two parties mean different things by "planet" and are therefore not really disagreeing, or is it a genuine disagreement about what there is?

Third, be honest about the traffic in the other direction. Which positions does the episode fit badly, not just well? In particular, say whether the record supports the realist's sense that there is a fact of the matter here or the nominalist's sense that the move from Ceres to Pluto shows a change in our conventions and nothing more — and then say plainly whether the episode can adjudicate between them or merely illustrates that both readings can accommodate the same facts.

Fourth, tell me what this case shows about the problem of universals that the usual examples — colours, species, the chemical elements — do not. Consider that the operative criterion is relational rather than intrinsic; that the definition is purpose-relative, being adopted for the Union's usage and varied for bodies around other stars; that a body can enter and leave the category without any change in the body; and that the dispute is partly about who has standing to decide, which looks like a social fact intruding on a metaphysical one. Does the episode amount to an argument for pluralism, or does it look like pluralism's failure of nerve?

Fifth, say where the problem of universals is simply silent. If the theory has nothing useful to say about why institutional authority, textbook conventions, and public sentiment figure in what presents itself as a question about the world, say so rather than forcing the machinery to cover it.

Finally, give me your own verdict, held to the evidence: which reading of planethood the episode best supports, and what would have to be true of the world for the rival reading to win.
