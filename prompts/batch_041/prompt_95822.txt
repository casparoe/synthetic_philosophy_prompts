I'm a second-year PhD student writing a thesis chapter on promissory obligation, and I've been asked to frame a departmental workshop on a dispute that I think has gone stale in the literature: that between the practice conception, on the broadly Humean line that promising binds only because there is a social convention of promising and that individual promises are moves within that practice, and the principle conception, on the Scanlonian line that the obligation rests on a moral principle requiring no convention at all, so that a clear voluntary assurance would bind even two people who share no practice of promising. My own suspicion — which I'd like tested rather than assumed — is that the two sides are answering different questions, one about a social institution and one about a moral relation, and that once that is separated out the genuine disagreement is narrower than it looks and turns on two things in particular: the direction of the obligation (that it is owed to the promisee, who can release the promisor unilaterally) and whether the promisee's power of release can be explained by the practice alone. I want the joint statement that the two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, to use as the framing document for the session.

So please draft that document. It should open with a numbered list of claims both sides can accept — about what a promise is, about what stands in need of explanation (why a certain kind of utterance changes what is owed, and why the resulting obligation runs to a particular person), about what the dispute is not about, and about which stock arguments both sides regard as failures. Then a disagreements table in which each crux gets a row: the crux stated neutrally, the formulation of each side's position that that side would accept as its own wording, the case, argument or consideration both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Please include, at minimum, rows on (i) whether an obligation of the promissory kind can exist without any practice, (ii) whether the practice view can account for the obligation being owed to the promisee rather than to the community, (iii) whether the promisee's unilateral power of release, and the promisor's lack of a unilateral power of cancellation, are to be explained conventionally or morally, and (iv) whether promising is best understood as the exercise of a normative power. If you judge that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why; say what each side would count as a genuine reason to abandon its own position; and say whether there is anything both sides agree would actually move the debate.

A few cases I'd like the two sides to be made to address, since I don't want the document to turn on the usual examples. First, two people from communities with entirely different institutions of undertaking improvise a form of assurance between themselves, understood by both as binding and used by neither before. Second, a community's binding form is a spoken formula accompanied by a clasped hand; a newcomer who has learnt the practice from a faulty description treats the formula alone as binding, and someone who knows the real rule utters the formula but omits the clasp, intending nothing by it, while the newcomer takes him seriously — which facts fix what the practice requires? Third, a practice in which undertakings are made to the group and only the group can release: what, if anything, is owed to the individual who was assured? Fourth, the asymmetry already mentioned — the promisee can release, the promisor cannot unilaterally cancel. Don't supply references or a reading list; I'll add those, so please refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular works. Write in British English, and tell me how confident you are in the agreed claims and in your verdict on which disagreements are verbal, singling out the single point in the document you are least sure of.
