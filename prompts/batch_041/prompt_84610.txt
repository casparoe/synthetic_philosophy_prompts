I am a second-year philosophy undergraduate, and a friend of mine — a physicist who believes that any decision worth taking seriously can be settled by expected value — and I have been meeting once a week to argue about ethics, and we keep returning to a problem I can feel but cannot state cleanly. He calls it the ledger problem: the thought that once you take the consequentialist's question seriously, the consequences that matter are the ones furthest out and least traceable, so the whole business of deliberating well becomes impossible, and yet we go on deliberating as though it were not. I am not sure whether this is a fatal objection to consequentialism or merely a confusion about what a consequentialist is supposed to be doing, and I would like you to look at the verdicts I actually have and tell me what principle, if any, generates them.

I am confident that giving £100 to a charity that distributes insecticide-treated bed nets is a good act. I am not at all confident about giving £100 to an organisation trying to reduce the long-run risks of advanced artificial intelligence: it might be enormously better, it might achieve nothing whatever, and I cannot get myself to a verdict at all. I do not think my choice of which city to take a job in — a choice that will determine who I meet, whether I have children, and much else — is morally arbitrary, even though I cannot trace a single consequence beyond a few years. Recycling one bottle feels like a small good even though its effects are untraceable. Voting feels like a genuine moral act even though my causal contribution is negligible and the long-run consequences of the election are unknowable. And when my friend says that cluelessness about the far future puts the bed nets donation and the artificial intelligence donation on a par, and the bed nets donation and a £100 dinner on a par, I want to resist both claims — but I notice that I resist them in different ways, and I cannot say why. The pair I genuinely cannot reconcile is this: I hold that an act is to be judged by its actual consequences, whatever they turn out to be; and yet if a time-traveller told me that my bed nets donation had, centuries later, contributed to something terrible, I would not conclude that it had been wrong to make it — I would say that no one could have known. If the same time-traveller told me that the dinner had led to the same terrible thing, I would say the dinner was just a dinner. So an unforeseen catastrophe attaches to neither act, exactly as my friend predicted; but I still think the bed nets act was better than the dinner, and that the artificial intelligence donation is not on a par with the bed nets act in the way he claims.

Is there a principle that gets all of this — perhaps one that restricts which consequences count towards an act's evaluation, or one that evaluates acts relative to the evidence available at the time, or one that demands only that an act be robust across the plausible scenarios? If a single principle can save every verdict, state it precisely, tell me what work each clause is doing, and name the case that forces each clause. If it cannot, tell me which of my verdicts I should abandon, and what the resulting position looks like once I have abandoned it. Please also say what your preferred principle predicts about two cases I have not mentioned: adopting a child, and a trivial act — leaving a light on, say — that I later learn set off an unforeseeable chain of events. I am not looking for a reading list or a survey of the literature; I want the principle, the cases it answers, and your reasoning about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
