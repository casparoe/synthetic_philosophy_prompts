I'm a novelist, and I've been reading in Confucian role ethics while drafting a book about a man whose job has swallowed his entire identity, so I want to test the novel I keep returning to against the philosophy rather than the other way round. Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the lens of Confucian role ethics, and specifically through the strong version associated with Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, on which a person is not an individual who happens to occupy roles but is constituted by the roles he lives, so that failing a role is not a breach of duty but a failure to be anyone in particular.

Two things I want pressed hard. First, Stevens's dignity. Is it the achievement of a role lived to maximum effect, or is it a Western, Stoic-Bradleyan substitute for role-constitution -- my station and its duties, but without the relational web that would make the station a self? Darlington Hall is full of ritual forms, the butler's manner, the silver, the schedule of the day, while the relations underneath them are dying; does li without a living community still constitute a person, or does it merely preserve a shape? Second, remonstrance. The Classic of Filial Piety includes a chapter in which Confucius rebukes the idea that a filial son simply obeys; on that reading, Stevens's failure to confront Lord Darlington over the dismissal of the housemaids, and his decision to keep serving through the Ribbentrop meeting, are not loyalty but a failure of the very role he claims to inhabit.

Make the reading faithful in both directions: say where role ethics illuminates scenes I may have misread, and where the novel resists the framework -- where Stevens's case suggests that role ethics needs something it may not supply, or where his self-erasure looks less like Confucian relationality than like its inversion. End with one sentence beginning Summary: stating what, if anything, the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot.
