You are a philosopher of technology with a firm command of the science-and-technology-studies literature on whether technical artifacts can be political. Think carefully before you answer, and open with a short brainstorm in which you enumerate the candidate decision rules a person might be running before you commit to any of them.

I am compiling a catalogue of a colleague's verdicts. She is a political theorist of technology working in the tradition of Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (Daedalus, 1980, later folded into his book The Whale and the Reactor), and she has been asked, case by case, whether a given artifact is political — that is, whether it embodies or helps constitute a form of social power. Her answers so far:

- The overpasses Robert Moses had built unusually low along the Long Island parkways, so that buses could not pass beneath them: political.
- The pneumatic molding machines installed at the McCormick reaper works, which displaced the skilled iron molders and broke their union: political.
- The mechanical tomato harvester developed for California growers, brought to market for efficiency but in practice eliminating most of the farm-labor jobs and favoring large operations over small ones: political.
- A commercial nuclear power reactor, which appears to demand a centralized, hierarchical, technically expert authority to run safely: political.
- A rooftop solar installation, which a household or a cooperative can own and operate and which sits comfortably with decentralized control: not political.
- A flint hand axe: not political.

The next item in the catalogue is a smartphone turn-by-turn navigation app that continuously reroutes drivers away from congested arterials and onto residential side streets. It was built to save its users time and was never designed with any political aim in mind. In practice, however, it systematically shifts traffic, noise, and tailpipe emissions onto whichever neighborhoods happen to lie between the arterials — frequently renters, and frequently poorer ones — while the benefits accrue to drivers and to the app's owners; and it runs equally well under centralized or decentralized governance, requiring no particular social order.

What I want from you is less the verdict than the machinery behind it. Specifically: (1) predict how she will rule on the navigation app; (2) state the rule or rules her catalogue so far is consistent with, and identify which of those rules fit her existing verdicts equally well; (3) give your best guess at the rule she is actually running, with reasons; (4) name the further case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules — the case whose verdict would force a choice — and explain why it discriminates where the others do not; and (5) flag any of her earlier verdicts that already quietly rule a candidate rule out. Be explicit about where you are guessing rather than inferring.
