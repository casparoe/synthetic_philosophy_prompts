I'm trying to write up my position on collective and inherited guilt for a seminar on Jaspers, and I want a consistancy check before I say any of it out loud. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Guilt worth the name requires that you did the thing or at least could have done something about it — you can't be guilty for what was never in your power. (2) Jaspers' political guilt is right: after the war the Germans as a collectivity carried liability for what the state did, including the ones who opposed it or just kept their heads down. (3) Moral guilt is strictly individual — only the person who acted, or who failed to act when they could have, is morally guilty; 'the German people' was never morally guilty as such. (4) I also think Jaspers' metaphysical guilt is real: there's a solidarity binding all human beings such that anyone who lived through it and didn't act stands guilty, full stop. (5) Guilt doesn't run down the generations — my grandparents' deeds can't make me guilty of anything. (6) All the same, I think their descendants do owe something special, memory and restitution and maybe ongoing care, that other people don't owe.

Go through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which ones only look like conflicts, and for the real ones what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. Rank them by severity rather than telling me all six are in tension — I want to know which single revision would clean up the most.
