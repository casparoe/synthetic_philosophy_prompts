I'm in a small reading group that's a weird philosophy-of-mind / AI crossover (thursday nights, four of us, one of whom is a retired roboticist who keeps derailing us into hardware stories), and we've spent the last month on the frame problem and gotten absolutely nowhere. What's maddening is that we all know the same material. Roughly two camps. Camp one says the frame problem is basically a solved technical annoyance: the original issue was that in a logic-based planner you have to write down, for every action, axioms saying everything the action *doesn't* change, and that explodes; once you have nonmonotonic logic, defaults, circumscription, successor-state axioms and so on, you can handle it, and sure there was the Yale shooting problem and some other wrinkles, but those are normal growing pains of a formal theory, and no one hand-writes frame axioms in a real planning system anymore. Camp two says all of that only addresses the version of the problem that a human has already carved up in advance: someone had to decide which predicates, which objects, which actions, which features of the world count, and the system will cheerfully ignore that its gripper is on fire or that the block is sitting in a puddle of gasoline, because nothing in the axiomatization tells it to care. That, they say, is the real problem, and it's about relevance, not about nonmonotonic inference. Then someone pipes up that the deep version isn't really about logic at all but about cognitive architecture (Fodor and Dennett both made a meal of it, I think), and the formalists say that's just a different problem that got stuck with the same name.

We keep replaying the same exchange: camp two produces a case where the system does something stupid in a situation the modeler didn't anticipate, camp one says give me the extra axiom and I'll encode it, camp two says that is precisely the point, and we go around again. Nobody budges. My own suspicion, and I want you to treat this as a hypothesis you're free to shoot down rather than something to agree with, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one name: one is asking whether we can build a formal system that does what we want inside a domain we've already specified, the other is asking what makes a piece of information relevant to a piece of reasoning and how a finite agent gets that right without being told, so the fight is really about what would even count as a solution. But I can also see a reading where there's a genuine hidden disagreement about facts, e.g. whether humans actually solve the relevance problem or just appear to, or whether any of the planning results tell us anything about cognition at all.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really, and is my hypothesis right? I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and please don't hand me a reading list, I can't get at most of those journals anyway. If it's verbal, tell me which word is doing the work. If it's really empirical, say what the experiment or measurement would be and whether anyone could run it. If it's two questions under one name, name both and say whether either is tractable, or whether one of them is just a bad question we should stop asking. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here, a distinction, a theorem, a working system, or nothing? I'd rather be told the impasse is my own fault for lumping things together than get a both-sides-are-right answer, so be concrete about which claims from each camp you're leaning on when you say it.
