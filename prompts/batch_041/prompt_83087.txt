My flatmate and I have been going round in circles about whether the numbers count, ever since I read a summary of Taurek's argument that if you can save one stranger or five strangers you should just toss a coin, because the five don't have any claim that's stronger than the one's — five seperate claims, sure, but none of them individually weightier than the single one. I think that's mad in the plain case. But then I started writing down my own gut verdicts and they don't obviously hang together, so I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, actually captures them. Here's what I think:

(a) One stranger on the left, five on the right, everyone will die if I don't act, and I can only get to one side: save the five.
(b) Same setup, except the five will only each lose a leg and the one will die: save the one.
(c) Same as (a), but the one is my brother: save my brother, and I don't even feel a pang about it.
(d) Same as (a), but saving the five means doing something that will foreseeably kill the one (he's tied to the track, I'd be diverting the train onto him): I don't do it, though I'm noticeably less sure here.
(e) One person will die, or a million people will each get a splitting headache for an hour: save the one. I know the headaches add up to something enormous on paper; I don't care.
(f) One against two, all strangers, all equally placed: save the two.
(g) One against one, all strangers: toss a coin; there's no fact of the matter about who I ought to save.

And the case I keep flip-flopping on is one death against a thousand people each going permanently blind. Some days the thousand wins, some days the one does.

My tentative principle is this: when the options are otherwise symmetrical — same kind of harm, same severity, no special relationship, no one at fault — the bigger number wins; but the moment the harms come apart I stop counting altogether and just save whoever's worse off. I can hear how that sounds like a cliff: a hair's difference in how bad the five's harm is flips me from "five wins" to "one wins", and I don't know if I can defend that.

So: is there a principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that gets all seven of those verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which clause each verdict is doing the work for, and say what it implies for cases I haven't put to you — for instance, saving one person for certain versus a 40% chance of saving five; or a choice where the sets overlap (I can save Alice and Bob, or Alice and three others); or one human life against a thousand dogs. If nothing saves every verdict, say which one I should give up and why. And be straight with me about the weak points in your own reasoning: what would actually change your mind, and if you end up defending something like "numbers count, weighted by how bad each person's fate is", tell me what would have to be true for you to drop it.
