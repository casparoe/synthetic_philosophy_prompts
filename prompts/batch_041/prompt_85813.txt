I'm a philosopher of mind three weeks out from a cognitive science seminar jointly hosted by my department and the vision lab across the road, and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence. The thesis I'll be presenting is that perceptual experience is cognitively penetrated, but never in the sense the literature usually intends. I hold that the familiar dispute — whether a belief, desire, or concept alters what one sees, or only how one sees it, or only what one is disposed to say about it — is an artefact of conflating content with what I call salience. On my account, cognition does not inject content into perception; it re-weights which aspects of an already-represented scene are vivid, insistent, and difficult to ignore. I distinguish occurrent penetration (a standing belief altering a single percept, online) from dispositional penetration (slow retuning of the visual system's weighting through training, habit, or expertise), and I argue that only the dispositional kind survives scrutiny. My central methodological proposal is a re-description test: if a subject can be brought to describe the same scene differently with no change in the percept, then what has changed is salience rather than content; a genuine occurrent content penetration would have to survive that test, and I claim nothing in the literature does. My positive evidence is deliberately indirect — the same stimulus with the same low-level signature gets reported differently by differently trained observers while adaptation and aftereffects stay put, so whatever moves is downstream of the encoding and upstream of the report. I should say that I expect to be filleted, since the last visiting speaker was kept on his feet for twenty minutes about whether his dependent measure was even perceptual.

The room will be roughly twenty people. A clear majority are psychophysicists and attention researchers from the vision lab: impatient with philosophy, but willing to grant any claim that arrives with a measurable signature; they think of perception as a set of mechanisms with timescales, and they are allergic to talk of experience. Two of them work squarely in the encapsulation tradition associated with Fodor and Pylyshyn and treat penetration claims as guilty until proven innocent. Then there are three or four philosophers of perception, representationalists who will say that my content/salience distinction is either trivial or incoherent — that if salience is part of how a scene looks, it is content, and if it is not, it is not perception at all. One computational neuroscientist who works on predictive processing will tell me the question dissolves, because precision-weighting simply is salience and cognition simply is the prior. And there will be at least one attention researcher who insists I have done nothing but redescribe attention with a new label.

Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes and what to hold. Then tell me the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the hour, what will lose me the room if I say it, and finally the question I am hoping nobody asks.
