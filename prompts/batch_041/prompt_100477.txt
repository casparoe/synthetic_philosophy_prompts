I am assembling a printed keepsake for the last meeting of a seminar on self-reference and consciousness, and the closing piece is to be a poem on Hofstadter's careenium. For any reader who does not already carry the image, it needs setting out: picture a frictionless billiard table strewn with countless tiny magnetic marbles, the simms, which careen about, collide, and sometimes clump into larger, slower clusters, the simmballs, that persist and hold the record of what has happened on the table. The table can be described in two ways — from below, as nothing but simms pushing simms, and from above, as simmballs with meanings that shove one another and seem to steer the simms in turn. The question the poem should hold open is whether the symbolic level does any pushing of its own, or is only a convenient description laid over a substrate that shoves itself.

Write a sestina that makes this question at once its subject and its form. Choose six end-words from the careenium's own vocabulary, and let their fixed rotation stand for the mechanical substrate — the rule that turns the words over and over whether or not anyone means anything by them. Let the sense that accumulates across the stanzas stand for the simmball level, so that the reader is put in the position of someone who cannot tell whether the meaning chose the words or the words merely produced the meaning. The envoy should turn back on the poem's opening line, so that the form enacts the strange loop by which the upper and lower levels fold into one another. Leave the question undecided: I want the tension exactly where the argument leaves it, resolved neither into a cheerful reductionism nor into a mystical top-down causation. It should be accurate enough that a reader who knows Hofstadter would recognize the whole picture — the simms, the clumping, the two descriptions, the loop — and self-contained enough that a reader who has never heard of the careenium still finds it a good poem.
