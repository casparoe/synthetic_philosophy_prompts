For a reading group I run with a few graduate students in economics and philosophy, we have been working through the socialist calculation debate, and I have come to suspect that most of our disagreements are not about the conclusion but about which question each of us takes the debate to be answering. What I want from you is not a summary of the debate but a classification instrument: a decision tree that takes a reader from the bare commitment that a planned economy would have to allocate capital goods without market prices for the means of production and leads them, node by node, to a named position. Please build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names — first the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located within that space.

Each internal node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and I want the branching to turn on distinctions that are usually run together. In particular: whether the alleged failure is one of economic calculation in the narrow sense (no prices for the means of production, hence no unit of account for comparing alternative uses of capital) or of the discovery and transmission of dispersed, partly tacit knowledge about particular circumstances; whether the obstacle is epistemic or motivational; whether the claim is impossibility in principle or comparative difficulty in practice; whether the relevant comparison is between idealised markets and idealised planning or between actually existing markets and actually existing planning; and whether the argument concerns static allocative efficiency or dynamic innovation and the discovery of new ends and means. I want the leaves to include, at minimum, the narrow Misesian impossibility thesis, Hayek's knowledge argument, the entrepreneurial-discovery argument associated with Kirzner, the Lange–Lerner trial-and-error market-socialist response, Lange's competitive-solution variant, a modern computational-planning position that concedes the epistemic problem but claims it is answered by machine learning and near-unlimited computation, and a deflationary position on which the two sides were never answering the same question. Where the tree cannot separate two of these, say so rather than papering over it, and where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether you take it to be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two further constraints. First, keep the instrument usable by someone who knows only the outline of the debate: no leaf should depend on having memorised a particular paper, and the node phrasings must carry their own weight. Second, I am especially interested in whether the modern computational position collapses the tree at a specific node — if the knowledge problem is the real objection, does better computation answer it, or does it relocate the problem rather than dissolve it? Build that into the branching rather than appending it as an afterthought, and close with a short note on which single node does the most sorting work.
