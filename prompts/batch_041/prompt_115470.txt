I am a retired field ecologist—forty years spent studying cooperative breeding in colonial birds—who has drifted into philosophy of biology and game theory since retiring. Next month I am giving a seminar at a joint workshop on the evolution of cooperation, and I want to prepare for the question period, not just for my talk. My thesis is this: the standard iterated prisoner's dilemma, with its single discount factor and its assumption of a common clock, is a special case that has been mistaken for the general form. What I have been calling braided reciprocity treats cooperative life as a population of asynchronous, overlapping, partially observed dyadic interactions, in which the relevant shadow of the future is not one number but a vector of three quantities: the probability of re-encounter, the probability of recognition, and the probability that a defection becomes known to third parties. On this model, cooperation can be stable even when pairwise re-encounter is unlikely, provided recognition and publicity are robust; and it can unravel even when re-encounter is likely, if recognition is noisy or reputational leakage is asymmetric. I use this to argue that kin selection, reciprocal altruism, and group selection are not rival explanations but limit cases of a single braided process. The audience will be roughly a dozen economists, mostly game theorists and behavioral economists who regard the Folk Theorem as the central result and who will suspect I am reinventing repeated games with extra steps; several philosophers of biology, one of whom is a persistent critic of adaptationist just-so stories, who will want to know what counts as altruism and whether I am smuggling group selection back in; and two or three mathematical biologists who will ask whether the model is anything more than a simulation in search of a theorem. Prepare my Q&A sheet: I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience—not the ones I would get from an evolutionary psychology crowd—ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would put it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, which objection I should not try to win but only survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
