Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether you can come to know that your eyes are working by using your eyes, and I would like the argument set out as a dialogue between the two of us. We are both philosophy graduate students, so neither of us needs the background explained; what I want is to see the disagreement sharpened, because I came away convinced that we were each sliding between several different claims and that the real crux is narrower than either of us made it.

Here is the shape of it. I hold what I would call the liberal line: when I look at a ripe tomato in good light and it looks red to me, I am justified in believing that it is red, and I do not need to have already justified the claim that my colour vision is reliable in order to have that justification. Perception gives me immediate justification for beliefs about my surroundings — not infallible justification, and not justification that survives every counter-consideration, but justification all the same. My friend holds the conservative line: a belief produced by a faculty cannot be justified unless I have antecedent, independent justification for thinking the faculty reliable. His charge is that my position licenses what he calls easy knowledge. On my view, he says, I can reason as follows: the tomato looks red to me, so it is red; my colour vision has just delivered a correct verdict; my colour vision is therefore reliable at least in this instance; and I may now rely on it in general, including in the cases where I would otherwise have doubted it. He says this uses the deliverances of a faculty to certify the very faculty that produced them, that it is circular in a way that cannot yield knowledge, and that it makes the difference between a reliable and an unreliable faculty invisible from the inside — a person whose colour vision was systematically unreliable could run the same argument with the same confidence and end up just as convinced that their eyes were fine. I say the alternative is worse. If justification always requires antecedent evidence that the faculty is reliable, then no perceptual belief is justified for anyone who has not already checked all of their faculties; the demand generates a regress; and the only ways out are scepticism or a stopping point that is just as arbitrary as the one I am accused of taking. He says I am confusing the claim that a requirement is hard to meet with the claim that it should not be imposed, and that the regress is just the structure of justification rather than a reductio of his view.

We also got tangled up over the move from ordinary knowledge claims to the falsity of scepticism. I said that I know I have hands, that if I know I have hands then I know there is an external world, and that the sceptical hypothesis is therefore the thing that has to give way. He said that this only has force if I am already entitled to my perceptual beliefs, which is precisely what is at issue, and that my confidence in the premise is doing work that an argument ought to be doing. It struck me afterwards that we kept circling an old shape of problem: whether one begins with particular claims to knowledge and extracts a criterion from them, or begins with a criterion and admits as knowledge only what satisfies it, and whether either order can be non-circular.

I would like the two of us written out in dialogue — one character holding the liberal or dogmatist line, the other holding the conservative line — with each responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past each other. Both of us should be competent: I do not want my friend reduced to repeating that I am being circular, and I do not want my own character to win by attributing to him an impossible demand that no one has ever held. Have the dialogue press on the following, and let the characters actually reach different verdicts on them rather than converging on a bland synthesis. First, whether the bootstrapping reasoning — using particular perceptual beliefs to establish the reliability of perception — is viciously circular, benignly circular, or not circular at all but merely uninformative. Second, whether the problem of easy knowledge is a genuine objection or simply a description of how most of our knowledge is in fact acquired. Third, what exactly the conservative requirement demands: that I have justification for believing my faculties reliable, or merely that they be reliable, or that I be able to defend the claim when challenged — and whether the stronger demand can be met by anyone. Fourth, whether the disagreement is really about the nature of justification, about the nature of knowledge, or about when one may legitimately rely on a belief in further reasoning. Fifth, whether there is a stable middle position — for instance that perception yields defeasible immediate justification, so that particular perceptual beliefs are justified without antecedent evidence of reliability, while the general claim that perception is reliable cannot itself be known by that route.

Please also have them address at least one case where the two views predict differently, so that the debate does not stay at the level of abstract principles — for instance a person whose colour vision is in fact reliable but who has strong evidence that it is not, or a person whose faculty is reliable only in some conditions and who has no way of telling which conditions those are. End not with a reconciliation but with a short passage in which each of us says what he takes the other to be committed to, what he thinks the other has got right, and what would have to be shown for either position to be established, and say plainly which of the disputed claims the whole argument turns on. Aim for something long enough to develop the arguments properly, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. No citations, no appeals to what any philosopher has written, and no name-dropping: I want the reasoning itself, in the mouths of the two of us.
