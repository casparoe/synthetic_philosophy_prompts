Two philosophers of mathematics have spent years talking past each other about Skolem's paradox, and their colleagues have asked them to produce the joint statement that a genuine adversarial collaboration would yield. The first is a relativist: she holds that the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem shows that no first-order axiomatization of set theory can pin down the intended universe, that every consistent such theory has a countable model, and that, consequently, whether a set is countable is relative to a model, with no absolute, model-independent fact of the matter. The second is an absolutist: he holds that the paradox is a benign artifact of first-order semantics, that the countable model simply fails to contain the bijection and the subsets that would witness uncountability, and that within the intended interpretation cardinality is as absolute as anything else in mathematics. Neither party doubts the theorem; they disagree about what it shows.

Draft the document both could sign. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — about the theorem itself, about what a model does and does not represent, about the intended interpretation of set theory, and about which of the parties' advertised conclusions the theorem does and does not support. Each claim must be one the other side could not reasonably deny, and none may be a truism that costs either side anything to concede.

2. The precise residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its position. In particular, isolate the disagreement over whether the missing-bijection explanation genuinely resolves the paradox or merely restates it while presupposing the absolute notion of subset it was meant to secure, and the disagreement over whether the relativity of countability is best classified as semantic, epistemic, or metaphysical.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to revise its position, ordered by how much would turn on them.

4. For each crux, a concrete argument, construction, or case that both sides agree bears on it — for example, a consideration about second-order or full semantics, about the availability and legitimacy of an intended interpretation, about whether the relevant models may be taken to be transitive or well-founded, or about how the paradox should be stated in the first place — followed by each side's prediction, recorded in advance, about how that consideration comes out.

Finally, state which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal, and what substantive disagreement would remain once the terminology were fixed. Write the document in a register both parties could put their names to, and keep every agreed claim and every statement of disagreement non-question-begging: if a sentence would strike one party as already conceding the other's conclusion, rewrite it.
