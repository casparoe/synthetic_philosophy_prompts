I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on decision theory under infinitary prospects, and I want a rating exercise that is not answerable by reciting the standard St. Petersburg discussion. Please work only from the three games defined below; no outside references are needed.

Game A. A fair coin is tossed until the first heads. If heads first appears on toss n, the player receives (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars, where a negative amount means the player pays that sum. The probability of outcome n is 2^(−n). The terms of the expected-value series are therefore (−1)^(n−1)/n.

Game B. Same probability structure and same payoff magnitudes as A, but the signs are assigned in blocks of doubling length: the first two outcomes are positive, the next four negative, the next eight positive, and so on.

Game C. Same probability structure, but the payoff on outcome n is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n², so the terms of the expected-value series are (−1)^(n−1)/n².

Now rate the following six responses to these games as accounts of what a rational agent should do when facing them:

R1. Restrict expected-value maximization to prospects with finitely many outcomes or absolutely convergent expectation series; A, B, and C fall outside its scope, and it is silent on them.

R2. Retain expected-value maximization but fix the order of summation to the natural temporal order of the coin flips.

R3. When expectation is undefined or unstable, defer to the agent's preferences over the payoff distributions themselves, for example via expected utility with a bounded utility function, or via dominance reasoning where it applies.

R4. Require any acceptable decision rule to be invariant under reordering of the outcome set, on the grounds that reordering does not change the physical gamble; consequently no natural order may be privileged.

R5. Assign each gamble a set of admissible values rather than a single value, and choose among gambles by a rule defined on such sets, such as maximin or maximax over the set.

R6. Treat the failure as representational: the objects of choice are probability distributions, not series, and any rule that cannot be stated over distributions alone is misformulated.

Deliverables. (a) A ranking of R1–R6 from most to least adequate as a response to these three games, with a score between 0 and 1 for each. (b) For each response, one or two sentences of justification, and an explicit statement of which of A, B, and C it handles best and which worst. (c) An explicit note on whether any response's position in the ranking changes if you consider only A, only B, or only C, and if so how. (d) If you think a response missing from the list is better than several of those present, state it and say where it would rank.

Close by stating how confident you are in the ranking you have produced and identifying the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
