I am a real-time media engineer. My working life is spent on live video pipelines: frame-rate conversion and slow-motion interpolation, motion-compensated prediction, the buffers that hold material before anything downstream is allowed to see it, and the telemetry logs that timestamp events as they travel through. I am fluent in that vocabulary and in very little else. I know the difference between capture time and presentation time, and between decode order and display order; I know that a bidirectionally predicted frame cannot be reconstructed until frames that will be displayed after it have already arrived; I know that a frame can sit in a staging buffer and be re-emitted later, and that a log can be amended after the fact so that the record no longer matches what was originally written. I have no philosophical training, and I do not want a reading list; I want you to work with the phenomenon and the two explanations below exactly as I have stated them.

The phenomenon. Two small shapes are flashed in succession on a screen, a short interval apart and in different places: a blue dot high in the frame, then a red dot lower down, or the reverse. At the right spacing and timing the viewer does not report two dots. The viewer reports a single dot that travels from the first position to the second and changes colour partway along its path, and reports the colour change as falling in the middle of the motion, that is, at a moment before the second dot had been presented at all. The report appears to describe an experience of the first dot's motion that already contains information from the second flash.

The two explanations. The first holds that the viewer did have an early experience, keyed to the first flash alone, and that this experience was subsequently rewritten: what was stored as the record of what had been seen was replaced, so that the viewer now remembers a colour change that was not there to be seen at the time. The second holds that no such experience ever occurred. On this story the perceptual system held the first stimulus, waited for the second, integrated the two, and then delivered a single experience representing a colour change as happening earlier than the moment at which the experience was delivered. The two stories are usually labelled Orwellian and Stalinesque, and the labels are self-explanatory.

Now please do the following. Restate the phenomenon and both explanations in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, using the concepts I actually have: capture and presentation timestamps, decode order against display order, prediction from later frames, interpolated frames that were never sampled from anything, staging buffers against committed output, retroactive amendments to the log, and the latency budget. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the puzzle map cleanly onto something my vocabulary already says; which parts I can express only by distorting either the puzzle or my vocabulary; and which parts have no counterpart in it at all.

I want the residue treated as the substance of the answer rather than as a footnote. Three things I especially want settled. First, does my vocabulary preserve the difference between the two explanations, or does it quietly collapse them into one? Second, my framework insists on a single moment at which a frame is presented, and on there being a source signal — a camera — whose output can be compared against any reconstruction. Does that insistence illuminate the puzzle, or does it import something the phenomenon may not contain? Third, my instinct on reading all this is that it is a buffering question: instrument the pipeline, find out where the frame was held and what sat in the buffer at each stage, and the answer falls out. Tell me whether that instinct is right, or whether it is precisely the thing the puzzle is built to embarrass.

Do not smuggle the engineering assumptions in and call the result a translation, and do not assume the puzzle has a canonical resolution that I ought to be told about instead of the residue. If the translation fails, I want to know precisely where, and whether the failure is a defect in my vocabulary or evidence that the distinction the philosophers are drawing was never as sharp as it sounded.
