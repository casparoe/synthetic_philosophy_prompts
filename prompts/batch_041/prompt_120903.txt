A colleague in my machine-learning reading group summarized inverse reinforcement learning for me as follows: "Reinforcement learning trains an agent by giving it rewards; inverse reinforcement learning runs the tape backwards. You watch an expert — a human driver, say — and from their behavior you recover the reward function they were optimizing. The premise is that behavior is a window onto hidden preferences: if the driver keeps taking the scenic route, then scenery must be carrying positive reward for them. And because an agent's optimal policy is determined by its reward function, enough demonstrations pin that reward function down uniquely — which is why IRL is the tool of choice for aligning AI with human values: rather than guessing what people want, we can read it off what they do. The remaining difficulty is essentially computational; recovering the reward is expensive, but with enough data and compute it can be done exactly."

This sounds too tidy to me, but he is the one who has actually read the papers. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on IRL would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply false. Where a claim is wrong, I want the corrected version, not just a note that it is more complicated — and I am especially interested in whether the value-alignment conclusion still goes through once the errors are fixed. No citations needed; I want the conceptual audit.
