I'm a science journalist -- I cover AI and cognitive science for a mid-sized outlet -- and I'm on our editorial standards commitee. We have a fight brewing over a piece that's supposed to run Friday. The reporter, who's good, says she's found a new interpretability result: a team trained a linear probe on a model's internal activations and it decodes a concept they're calling safety-relevant intent with 95% accuracy. She wants the headline to say the model has an internal representation of that intent. But the paper also reports that when they ablated the direction the probe uses, the model's behavior barely changed. Another lab, using a different technique, found a different direction that decodes the same concept just as well. The reporter says the probe's accuracy is enough to call it a representation; the standards committee's worry is that we're just reading our own categories into the model's geometry, and that a probe can decode information the model never uses. One member keeps saying that if the probe can decode it but the model doesn't use it, it's more like a correlation in the weights than a representation. Half the committee wants to spike the story or bury it; the other half says that's overcautious and we should run it with the representational language. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it: recommend whether we run the piece, and if so, what language we should allow in the headline and lede, with a concrete deadline for the reporter. Engage the strongest version of the losing side -- especially the point that if we refuse to say the model represents anything, we might be unable to describe what interpretability research actually found. I don't need a lit review or citations, just the memo.
