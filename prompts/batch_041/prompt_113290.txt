I am preparing a proposal for my qualifying paper in philosophy of science, roughly 8,000 words to be written over a single semester, and I would like you to draft the proposal itself: the question, the case for its being genuinely open, the method, what would count as success and as failure, and an honest assessment of scope. The subject is Malthus, and the half-formed idea I want worked up is this.

Malthus's principle of population claimed that population tends to increase geometrically while subsistence increases at best arithmetically, so that any improvement in the condition of the poor is eaten up by rising numbers and wages fall back to bare subsistence, with famine, disease and war doing the correcting when restraint does not. In later editions he gave greater weight to the preventive check of delayed marriage, and Darwin and Wallace both reported the book as an influence on the idea of a struggle for existence. The forecast that is usually attributed to the theory — that the industrialising countries would be driven back to subsistence — did not come about, and no one treats the case as a clean refutation. Economic historians who fit the model to pre-industrial data find that it describes that epoch rather well; the escape from it is then explained by factors, the Industrial Revolution and the demographic transition among them, that the theory neither forbade nor anticipated. So we have a prediction that is false as a dated forecast, apparently true as a description of the regime it was formulated in, and then re-employed as a premise in a different domain, evolutionary biology, where its truth is not in question.

I want the proposal to pose a question with a determinate answer rather than a general meditation on underdetermination. The question I am after is this: under what conditions, if any, may a long-run prediction that has missed its date be treated as deferred rather than refuted, and does the Malthusian case satisfy those conditions?

To make that tractable I want to use a distinction from my notes that I am not yet sure survives scrutiny, and I want the proposal to argue for or against it rather than assume it. Call a prediction a clock claim if it asserts that something will happen by or around a date, and a spring claim if it asserts that a mechanism is loaded, so that its manifestation is currently suppressed by factors the theory can name and whose removal would release it. The suspicion to be tested is that every rescue of a late prediction is a covert conversion of a clock claim into a spring claim, and that the interesting philosophical work is in saying when that conversion is legitimate and when it is a euphemism for unfalsifiability.

The proposal should also register that more than one kind of rescue is in play here, and should say whether these are variants of one move or distinct moves with distinct epistemic standing. I can see at least four: adding an auxiliary hypothesis, as with the enlarged role given to moral restraint; softening the claim into a ceteris paribus tendency; shifting the target from a prediction about a particular country to a claim about a mechanism that operates wherever nothing blocks it; and exporting the claim to another domain, as the Darwinian use of the struggle for existence does. For each I want an admissibility test, and my tentative test, which I would like defended or demolished rather than repeated back to me, is that a rescue is legitimate only if it transfers the risk rather than cancelling it: it must specify a state of affairs that would count as the mechanism having been absent or discharged, and that state of affairs must be one we could in principle have observed.

On the existing positions: I know roughly where the terrain lies — Popperian falsification and its difficulties with theories that are rescued by auxiliaries; the Duhem-Quine point that no hypothesis meets experience alone; Lakatos on progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts and on the difference between a research programme that predicts novel facts and one that only absorbs them; the various attempts to define novelty of prediction; and the instrumentalist line that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant so long as its predictions are useful. Name these positions and the arguments that go with them as you need them, but I will supply the references myself, so do not give me a bibliography or a reading list. What I need from the proposal is the claim that these positions do not jointly settle my question — that the Malthus case falls between them in a way that is worth a paper — and an account of why a conceptual rather than a purely historical treatment is the right one.

For the method I would like something that could actually be carried out in a semester: a reconstruction of the Malthusian core as a model, separating the parts that function as dated forecasts from the parts that function as mechanism claims; an inventory of the rescues that were in fact offered, ordered by the four kinds above; the application of the admissibility tests to each; and then a small comparison set as a robustness check, so that the criteria are not simply a restatement of my antecedent view about Malthus. Candidates for the comparison set are a case where deferred vindication is widely accepted, such as Hubbert's curve for oil production, and a case where the rescue is widely rejected, such as the resource-scarcity forecasts that Ehrlich wagered on against Simon in 1980 and lost in 1990. Two cases at most, and say which to drop if the paper is running long.

I would like the proposal to be candid about failure. It fails if the admissibility tests turn out to be a description of the Malthusian verdict rather than a test of it; it fails if the comparison cases can only be brought into line by adjusting the criteria after the fact; and it fails if the clock and spring distinction collapses because any theory whatsoever can be read as a spring claim, in which case the paper should say so and stop rather than manufacture a distinction to save itself. It should also say what a positive result would look like in a sentence or two, and which single section I should write first.
