Regulators and comparable institutions routinely announce a regime of surprise. A tax authority, an anti-doping body, or a workplace-safety inspector declares publicly that it will carry out exactly one unannounced visit during a stated period -- a quarter, a year -- while withholding the date. The announcement is meant to be believed; the visit is meant to catch the target off guard. A target raised on the standard rationalist diet of backward-induction and common-knowledge arguments can, however, run the familiar induction: the visit cannot fall on the final day, since by then the window would have narrowed to a single possibility and the visit would be expected; exclude that day and the same reasoning excludes the day before it, and so on to the conclusion that no genuinely surprising visit is possible -- so there is nothing to fear and nothing worth preparing for. The practitioners plainly believe the surprise is real, and it often is.

I want an account of what this practice presupposes. What theory of practical rationality, and of knowledge, does the announced-surprise regime commit its designers to? Does it presuppose that the target is not a perfect backward inductor, so that the surprise rests on the target's bounded rationality, or can it be made coherent even for a fully rational target -- for instance by treating surprise as a matter of low credence rather than logical exclusion, or by denying that the regulator's announcement is common knowledge in the sense the induction requires? Which resolutions of the surprise-exam paradox would license the practice as it stands, and which would force it to be redesigned -- say, as an explicitly probabilistic audit in which each day carries a stated chance? Note too the tension built into the design: the regime needs the possibility of inspection to be common knowledge, since deterrence requires the target to know it might be visited, while the occurrence must not be known; I would like to know how the practice resolves that trade-off, and whether it resolves it consistently.

Be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. I suspect several theories of knowledge, and several models of the target, would each be compatible with the same inspection schedule, and I want to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed picture with a fully rational, common-knowledge model of the target, what exactly would have to change about how the institution announces and conducts its inspections?
