Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on accuracy-first epistemology — the idea that the only thing that makes a credence good is how close it is to the truth, and that you grade credences with scoring rules. (Quick gloss, in case it saves you trouble: a rule is proper if it rewards you for reporting the credence you actually have rather than some other one, and strictly proper if it uniquely does; epistemic utility theory is the general machinery of scoring whole credence-functions by how much accuracy they'd be expected to yield.) The bit I find genuinely usable is how much you can squeeze out of that one starting point. Accuracy-dominance arguments get you probabilism, and there's a tidy result that the Brier score decomposes into a calibration term, a resolution term and a base-rate/uncertainty term — which means a forecaster can be perfectly calibrated and still add nothing, since calibration is only the reliability part and says nothing about whether you're discriminating hard cases from easy ones. There's also a gap I keep circling: accuracy-first seems to have nothing to say about where your priors come from. It'll call you irrational for being incoherent or for failing to conditionalise, but it has no opinion on whether you start at 50% or 2% on a brand new claim, so it tells you how to stay consistent, not what to believe. And I've never seen a satisfying accuracy-first treatment of what you should do when you get evidence your just bad at this — say you learn your hit rate on questions of this kind is basically chance. Do you conditionalise on that like any other evidence, or is something else supposed to happen?

What I'd like from you: the most important implications of this framework for how I actually run things. Context: I run the forecasting club at my local rationalist meetup, ~30 people, a yearly tournament of a few hundred binary questions, and I'm the one who sets the scoring and writes the wrap-up posts. I keep noticing that the choice of rule isn't innocent — the log score punishes a confident miss infinitely harshly and the Brier score doesn't, so people behave differently depending on which I pick, and the same forecaster can top the table under one rule and sit mid-table under another. So give me the practical implications for the scoring itself, for what I tell members to aim at, and for what the wrap-up posts should say about who did well and why. Then tell me which of those implications are robust across proper scoring rules and which are just artefacts of one rule rather than another, and which ones I should ignore as philosophical housekeeping with no practical bite.
