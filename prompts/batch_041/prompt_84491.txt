A friend of mine is a philosopher of language working in the speech-act tradition. Her verdicts on a series of cases: a sincere promise is a promise; the same words spoken by an actor on stage are not a promise but a parasitic use of one; a promise made with no intention of keeping it is still a promise, though an insincere one; a judge's sentencing is a valid verdict, while the same words said in jest by a spectator are not; and a promise made to a hearer who does not understand the language is still a promise. The new case: a company's customer-service chatbot, acting under a corporate policy that authorizes it to make refund commitments, tells a customer that it promises to refund her, and she relies on it. What will my friend say — is it a promise? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog? And what further case would distinguish the rules she might be running — the rule that a speech act requires a conventional procedure, the rule that it requires the speaker's intention, and the rule that it requires the hearer's uptake? Give me your best guess and the reasons for it.
