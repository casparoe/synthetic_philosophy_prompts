Write a dialogue between two professionals who disagree about whether the Scandinavian default of trusting public institutions or the American default of suspecting them is the more reasonable attitude toward government. Cast the trusting side as a Norwegian civil servant who spent several years seconded to an American agency, and the suspicious side as an American civil-liberties lawyer who has lived and worked in Norway. Both are competent and well informed; neither is a caricature. The trusting character must not be naive, and the suspicious character must not be a cynic who assumes officials are venal by nature. Foreclose the cheap moves from the outset: neither character may win a point by asserting that the other simply does not understand how the other country works.

The dialogue must satisfy each of the following.

1. The argument must run on mechanism rather than base rates. Neither character may rest a case on a country's standing in international rankings of corruption, trust, or government effectiveness; if rankings are mentioned at all, the other character must get to say what such an index actually measures and what it leaves out.

2. Each character must press the symmetry claim: trust and suspicion are partly self-confirming policies rather than neutral beliefs about the world. Trust economizes on monitoring, which preserves discretion, and discretion is where both good judgment and betrayal live. Suspicion sustains monitoring, and monitored people behave like people who know they are being monitored — compliant, guarded, and skilled at managing the metric. Let the characters work out whether this symmetry is genuinely symmetric, or whether one side breaks it, and on what grounds.

3. Both must confront the fact that the two errors fall on different people. Misplaced trust is usually paid for by the truster or spread thinly across a public; misplaced suspicion is paid for by the person suspected, often someone with the least capacity to absorb the cost. The dialogue must ask outright whether the question of which attitude is more reasonable is even well posed before one has said whose losses count. One character should argue that it is not well posed; the other should resist that, and give a reason rather than a shrug.

4. The Norwegian should use the practice of publishing individual tax assessments — anyone may look up anyone else's declared income, though the lookup is registered and the person looked up can, on request, learn who looked — to argue that the real contrast is not trust versus suspicion but trust in persons versus trust in transparent systems. The American should reply that universal transparency is institutionalized suspicion that only appears benign because it is symmetrical, and should press whether transparency can genuinely substitute for trust or only for one particular kind of vigilance. Keep institutional detail at the level of general practice: no statutes, no named agencies, no sources.

5. Each character should ground at least one claim in a concrete case both would accept as real — a public-health directive, a tax authority, a police force, a bank rescue, whichever fits — but the dialogue must contain no citations, no named books or authors, and no appeals to authorities the reader is expected to recognize.

6. This must be an exchange, not two monologues. Use short turns, direct replies to the point just made, at least two moments where a character concedes something real and then relocates the disagreement rather than dropping it, and at least one moment where a character anticipates an objection before the other can raise it.

7. Do not resolve the dialogue with a handshake, and do not resolve it by declaring that each attitude is reasonable in its own context. End with the crux identified, a judgment about which position the exchange leaves better supported, and a statement of what kind of evidence would move that judgment — described in general terms only.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, with minimal stage directions: the argument should carry the scene.
