I'm writing a feature on semantic externalism, and for the centerpiece I need a thought experiment built to order rather than one more trip to Twin Earth. Here is the specification, and the aim is to make the case do something the familiar examples cannot. Two speakers must be molecule-for-molecule identical, in identical internal states, with no stipulation that their inner lives differ. The environmental difference between them must not be a difference in what their shared term picks out: in both settings it refers to the same substance, so a straightforward causal or chemical externalism returns the same referent for each. Despite that, the case must give a serious externalist grounds for holding that the two speakers mean something different, and the difference has to flow from something other than the referent — from the division of linguistic labor, from the counterfactual profile of the term, from the pattern of its past use, or from whatever else you can defend. I want the case to make plain which of those relations is carrying the weight. Keep it realistic and to a single paragraph. Then walk through what each of three positions says and why: a Putnam-style causal externalist, a Burge-style social externalist, and a two-factor theorist who accepts wide content but wants narrow content to bear the psychological load. Tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing, what becomes of the verdicts if I strip it out, and how an internalist would attack the case to argue that the apparent difference in meaning is an illusion. The case is meant to show that externalism about reference and externalism about meaning can come apart, so a scenario that merely stipulates the divergence rather than generating it from the theories will not do. Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would set it out in a letter.
