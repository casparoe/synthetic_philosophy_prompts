I'm putting together the questions for a student-run interview series our phil of bio reading group does, and next month's guest is a philosopher of biology who's spent his career in the functions debate — the etiological / selected-effects camp versus the Cummins-style causal-role camp, the whole argument over whether a trait's function is fixed by what it was selected for or by what it contributes to a system's capacities, plus all the usual torture cases (the first heart, the swamp duplicate, the heart that also happens to make noise, artifacts, junk DNA). Except he's got his own twist, which is what makes him interesting and also a nightmare to interview: his line, which he's been workshopping in the book he'll never finish, is that the whole dispute rests on a false presupposition, because function isn't a binary property a trait either has or lacks. On his view it's a matter of degree, and the degree is just how strongly some downstream effect of the trait feeds back to keep the trait around — and selection is only one kind of stabilizing loop, so developmental canalization, cultural transmission, even a researcher's own interest in the trait can count as stabilizers. Once you see that, he says, the artifact cases and the novel-trait cases people use to torment the etiological account aren't counterexamples at all, they're just low on the gradient. And he's got this move he pulls in every Q&A where you press him on a hard case and he says, in effect, "you're asking me where the line is, and the answer is theirs no line, there's a gradient" — I've watched him do it three times in one session and get away with it every time.

What I want from you is nine or ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of that position, with the follow-up for the answer he's most likely to give, and where the likely answer is the gradient deflection the follow-up has to close that exit off rather than nod and move on. I need at least one question each on: (a) the no-history cases — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a heart that assembled by accident and was never selected for anything, a brand-new trait in its very first generation, the first heart in a lineage — where I want him to have to say whether the gradient bottoms out at zero and what that does to the claim that it's a heart at all; (b) his extension of stabilizing loops to artifacts and cultural traits, since I suspect he's quietly importing the designer's intentions back in through the phrase "cultural transmission" and I want to make him either own that or give it up; and (c) what the degree is supposed to be a degree of — what units the gradient is measured in, and what work the word "function" still does in biology if it's graded, because in genomics and in medicine people need a yes/no and a malfunction verdict and I want to know whether he thinks that's just a convenience or whether his view can't underwrite it. End with one question that forces him to say what would actually move him off the graded view — something that could come out a certain way and change his mind — and one about teleosemantics, since his account is supposed to be the kind of thing that explains how a state can be about something and can misrepresent it, and I want to know whether "half about" is a thing he's willing to say out loud or whether the gradient story stops at the door of representation. Casual tone is fine, he's a good sport, and if you think one of my three must-haves is actually a dead end and something else would press him harder, say so and swap it.
