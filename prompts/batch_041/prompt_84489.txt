I teach a course on the historicity of the Trojan War and want to use Wolfgang Petersen's *Troy* (2004) as a test case for the question of when a legend becomes history. The film is a patchwork: it compresses a decade into a few weeks, and it takes the sack of the city not from the *Iliad*, which ends with Hector's funeral, but from the *Odyssey*, the *Aeneid* and the *Metamorphoses*, while its best-remembered detail, the vulnerable heel, is a late accretion absent from the *Iliad* altogether — yet the whole is offered as a reconstruction of what happened in the late Bronze Age. Read it against a distinction I have been working with: subtractive demythologising, which deletes the gods and the marvels and keeps the narrative intact, versus reconstructive demythologising, which asks what conditions would have produced such a story in the first place — a palace economy under strain, a raid for plunder and women, an archive that may mention the Greeks and a city that may be Troy. Which of the two operations does the film actually perform, can it do both at once, and does it understand the difference? Point to the scenes where its implicit epistemology of the distant past holds up — where motive, contingency and logistics take over the explanatory work that divine causation once did — and to those where it merely swaps one unfalsifiable story for another. Close by saying what the film dramatises about the passage from legend to history that a methodological argument alone cannot, and where the film resists the reading you have given it.
