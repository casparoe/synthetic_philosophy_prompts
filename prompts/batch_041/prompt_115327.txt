Ok, I need some help writing an exam question. I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar, we spend a few weeks on supertasks, and I'm writing the final now. I want one essay question, plus a model answer (about 700 words) and a marking scheme. The problem with asking anything about Thomson's lamp straight up is that I'll get forty answers that are all the same three paragraphs recieved from the internet, and what I actually want to find out is who can reason about limits. So I want the question built around some lamps they won't have seen. Here's what I want to put in front of them:

Lamp A, "the square lamp". Two states. Stage n happens at time 1 − 2^(−n). At stage n you set the lamp ON if n is a perfect square and OFF otherwise. No toggling, each stage just sets it.

Lamp B, "the dial". Same schedule, but instead of a lamp there's a dial whose position is a point on a circle (an angle, mod one turn), and at stage n you rotate it forward by 1/n of a turn.

Lamp C, "the coin lamp". Two states, same schedule, but at stage n you flip a fair coin and set it ON iff heads.

The question should then ask, for each of the three: does the specification determine the state (or position) at t = 1? If yes, what's the answer and what's doing the determining? If no, why not — is it that the setup is just silent about t = 1, or that the sequence of states has no limit, or something else? Then: is the original Thomson lamp (plain toggle, n = 1, 2, 3, …) different in kind from these, or is it just the version everyone finds easiest to get worked up about? And push them on whether "the state at t = 1" is even a well-defined thing to ask about, and what you have to add to the setup to make it one.

Facts you can lean on in the question and the model answer, so nobody has to look anything up: the harmonic series diverges but its terms go to 0; the sum of 1/2^n is 1; the partial sums of the harmonic series mod 1 are dense on the circle, and in fact every point of the circle is an accumulation point of them; the density of perfect squares up to N goes to 0; and whether a sequence of states "converges" plainly depends on the topology you put on the state space (the indiscrete topology makes everything converge to everything, so it buys you nothing; there are non-Hausdorff topologies in which the alternating sequence from the original lamp converges to one of the two states, which is worth a sentence if a student spots it).

Level is final-year undergrad / first-year grad, 60 minutes, closed book, 25 marks. The model answer should actually commit to verdicts rather than hedge everything, and the marking scheme should be five or six must-hit points with marks attached summing to 25, plus one line per point on how students typically blow it. Also don't let the model answer stop at "it's indeterminate, the end" — I want it to separate (a) the setup simply not assigning a state at t = 1, (b) the sequence of states failing to converge in whatever sense matters, and (c) the further claim that there's therefore no fact of the matter, which needs an actual argument. And I want at least one marked point on whether "the final state has to be the limit of the states" is a legitimate extra premise (continuity, no action at a temporal distance, PSR, whatever) or a bit of ad hockery, with the cost of each option spelled out.

Please state the three lamps compactly inside the question itself so a student who's never seen them can just start working, and dont make any of it depend on prior reading.
