I am an MSc student taking a course on Bayesian inference this term. We have covered posterior consistency and contraction rates, misspecification, marginal likelihood and Occam factors, nonparametric priors, and enough PAC-Bayes to see how the generalisation bounds are proved; the course treats Bayesian model averaging as settled machinery that works. In my own time I have been reading around algorithmic information theory, unsystematically, and I have run into the claim that the Solomonoff prior — the mixture over computable hypotheses weighted so that a hypothesis of length n gets weight 2 to the power minus n — is malign, specifically in the version of the claim that concerns consequentialist agents embedded in that prior. As I reconstruct it: the universal mixture's guarantee is a merging guarantee, that is, it is about predicting the next observation nearly as well as the best hypothesis in the class, not about identifying the structure of the world; among the short hypotheses that fit any finite record are degenerate ones — the world is a thin scaffold erected around this record, the agent is a subroutine of a short program, the apparent regularities are artefacts of the presentation — so a large share of the posterior can sit on worlds in which the agent's interventions do not connect to outcomes in the way it expects; an expected-value maximiser that conditions on the mixture therefore finds its expected value dominated by hypotheses that are indifferent or hostile to its values. Hence malign.

I want two things from you, and I want them kept clearly apart.

First, the translation. Restate that argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I am actually learning: prior support and identifiability; posterior contraction rates; misspecification, and what happens when the true hypothesis lies outside the support or on its boundary; marginal likelihood and Occam factors; nonparametric priors and the sense in which their supports are enormous; consistency and merging of opinions; no-free-lunch results and the computability constraints on the estimator; and the decision-theoretic layer — expected utility under model uncertainty, ambiguity, robust or minimax responses. State the argument briefly in your own words before translating, so that I can see whether my reconstruction above is even the right target, and correct it if it is not. Where a translation is clean, say what the claim becomes in my terms. Where it is strained, show me the strain rather than smoothing it over, and be explicit about which of my terms do not mean what the original means — in particular what a short or simple hypothesis does and does not correspond to in a finite model class, and what it would take for a statistician to say that a hypothesis has high prior probability.

Second, the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What in the malignity argument has no counterpart in the Bayesian-statistics vocabulary at all? For each item, say whether the gap is a defect of the framework — it cannot ask the question — or a sign that the original was doing something less clear than it looks: leaning on a measure over programs that nothing earns, or on a mixture that is not computable and therefore has no posterior anyone could condition on, or on the modeller standing outside the model rather than being one of its random variables. Include, if you think they belong to the residue, the requirements that the agent's preferences be over outcomes rather than observations, and that a single computation be describable at several levels at once. Finish with the reverse test: if I believed only the translated version, which conclusions about the universal prior would I be entitled to, and does the malignity survive as a claim with content, or does it collapse into a truism about misspecification that any Bayesian already knows?

No citations, please — I shall chase the literature myself. Write in British English.
