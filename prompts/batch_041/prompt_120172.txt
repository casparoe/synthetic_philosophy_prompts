I am in a graduate seminar on consequentialism, and we have spent the last several weeks circling a single objection: that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. The objection, as it gets pressed in the room, has this shape. Utilitarianism tells us to maximize the total or the average of well-being, and in doing so it treats the many lives over which the sum is taken as so many containers of satisfaction — as though the comparison to be made were the comparison a single person makes between two of her own futures. So the theory licenses sacrificing one person's interest for a larger gain elsewhere, and that, the objection runs, is to fail to see that persons are not departments of one another. The other side of the room is unmoved, and their replies have become predictable. One reply is that this is not an argument but a picture: it asserts that this is not how we ought to think about persons, and then treats the picture as a premise. A second is that the objection proves too much, since any morality — including one that forbids interpersonal aggregation — must weigh a person's lesser interest now against her own greater interest later, and nobody has stated a principle that licenses the intrapersonal trade-off while forbidding the interpersonal one; the asymmetry is simply assumed rather than argued for. A third is that the separateness being invoked is a piece of metaphysics, and that once one stops conceiving of persons as sharply bounded, deeply separate units the objection loses its grip — to which the first side answers that if separateness goes, so does the special authority each of us has over her own life, and that this is a reductio.

What frustrates me is that after months of this nobody has moved, and I genuinely cannot tell whether we are disagreeing about something or merely talking past one another. My hypothesis — which I would like tested rather than adopted — is that the real fault line is not about aggregation at all, but about what the objection takes the unit of moral concern to be: the first side treats persons as the units, whose boundaries constrain what may be done, while the second treats interests or experiences as the units and persons as the sites at which they occur, so that separateness is not a shared premise but a conclusion the first side is smuggling in. Diagnose the impasse for me. Is it substantive, verbal, or two different questions travelling under one name? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work — for instance whether some aggregation rule can be shown to satisfy a plausible constraint that sum-ranking violates, or whether the intrapersonal/interpersonal asymmetry can be derived rather than stipulated? And tell me concretely what would count as progress here: a distinction, an axiom, a theorem, a counterexample, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed — or, if my hypothesis is right, why no amount of further argument should be expected to move anyone. I want the diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on whether utilitarianism is true.
