Ok, Kavka's toxin puzzle, but I want numbers. Setup: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial in front of you; drink it and you're painfully ill for a day, nothing lasting. If at midnight tonight you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon, a million dollars lands in your account before the drinking window even opens, and you're completely free to change your mind afterwards. Kavka's line is that you can't form the intention, because you know you wont drink it — once the money's banked the toxin buys you nothing. Commitment-style replies (Gauthier-ish) say you can intend it, and that the intention then constrains what you can rationally do the next day.

Give me your credence that a fully informed, ideally rational agent in exactly that setup walks away with the million, and decompose it. A number and one line for each: (a) that intentions are the sort of state that can be formed for reasons independent of the intended act's own payoff; (b) that knowing full well you'd renege is compatible with genuinely having the intention; (c) how much of the answer is hostage to which theory of intention and rationality you're running — causal/functional vs commitment-based; (d) that the described state is psychologically or architecturally possible at all, rather than an incoherent thing to demand of an agent. Then the combination, and which term your number is most sensitive to.

Then split the number in two: ideal theory permits it, versus an actual human (or an actual system like you) could really pull it off — and say where the gap lives.

One variant I think is the interesting seam: instead of intention, the billionaire pays if at midnight a scan shows you're more than 90% confident that you'll drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. Same freedom to change your mind. Credence for that version too, and if it differs, tell me exactly what intentions can do here that beliefs cant. Finally: which sub-question you're least sure of, and what would move it most.
