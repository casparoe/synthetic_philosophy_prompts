I retired three years ago after forty years in soil chemistry — the last twenty of them mostly arguing with people about whether a pH reading means anything without a calibration you trust — and since then I've been doing the reading I never had time for. I've gotten latley into epistemology, and specifically into the family of cases where you use a thing to check itself: you glance at the petrol gauge, believe the tank's a quarter full, do that a hundred times, and then conclude the gauge is reliable. My question is whether the "too easy" feeling people report about that conclusion is a genuine epistemic defect or just an aesthetic reaction to tidy reasoning, and I've decided I can't settle it by reading more variations on the gas tank. I want a case built to order. That's what the case is for, so please keep it in mind — if you find yourself designing something whose whole interest is that the agent might be wrong, you've built a different case than the one I'm asking for.

So, here's the commission. Build a bootstrapping case that meets all of these, and I'll be strict about them. (1) The bootstrapped conclusion — "this thing I'm relying on is reliable" — has to be true, and true because the thing really is reliable. No broken instruments, no deception, no brains in vats, no evil demons; I don't want the case to be interesting only because the agent could be wrong. (2) The reasoning has to be valid, and each premise has to be one the agent is entitled to by the lights of whichever theory ends up licensing the conclusion — no smuggling the verdict in by stipulating that she "just knows" something or that the premise is basic unless you show why the theory grants that. (3) Ordinary setting: a home, a garden, a shed, a car, a walk; a consumer instrument or a pair of senses; something my neighbour could plausibly own. Here's a seed if it helps, though feel free to bin it or tell me why it won't work: said neighbour has one of those phone apps that listens to bat calls and names the species, and he now trusts it completely — I've never been able to pin down what's wrong, if anything, with how he got there. (4) There must be exactly one feature of the case doing the work of making the bootstrapped belief feel like cheating to an internalist, and it must be a feature I can delete in a single sentence to produce a second version of the case in which the same form of reasoning is uncontroversially fine for everybody. Give me both versions, one paragraph each, and make sure the two differ in nothing else.

Then do the checks. Give me the verdicts and the route to each — for a plain process reliabilist, for a dogmatist who thinks your senses and memory can justify without any prior evidence that they're reliable, and for an evidentialist internalist who thinks you can't be justified by reasoning that takes for granted the very thing under test — and show me where in the case each verdict comes from rather than just asserting it. Tell me which feature is doing the work, and what exactly happens when I delete it: does the case collapse back into the petrol gauge in new clothes, or does something survive? If it collapses, say so plainly; I'd rather know than have it dressed up. Then, as honestly as you can, tell me how a reliabilist would attack the case if they wanted to keep their theory and lose mine — what they'd say is a cheat or a stipulation. And since I'm a beginner who is probably asking for something that can't be had: finish by telling me which single consideration would change your mind about the verdict you've given, and where in your own reasoning you'd put your money on it having gone wrong.
