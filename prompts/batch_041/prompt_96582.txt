I'm putting together a session on Dennett's multiple drafts model and the Cartesian theatre picture he wants us to give up, and I need a worked example rather than the usual hand-wave. So: build me a thought experiment to order.

The target is a case where a Cartesian theatre theorist is forced to say when, and where, a bit of content became conscious, while the multiple drafts person says that question is malformed rather than merely hard. Just so we're on the same page about the second view: no finish line anywhere in the system, content getting revised by parallel processes, and the sense of one tidy stream of consciousness being a retrospective construction rather than a readout.

Constraints:

- Ordinary human perception. No brains in vats, no split-brain ops, no quantum anything, no invented neuro-gadgets. Whatever the person is doing has to be something you could actually do on a Tuesday afternoon.
- The two views must agree about everything observable — every report, every reaction time, every twitch. So the disagreement has to be about whether there's a fact over and above what she says and does, not about competing behavioural predictions. Don't cheat by having the multiple drafts view predict something the theatre view can't accommodate.
- Make the content something with meaning in it — a word, a pun, an instruction, a half-finished sentence — not a flash or a beep. Half-understood is the interesting case.
- The theatre theorist's answer has to be a live option, not daft. I dont want a strawman; I want them to be able to point at a moment or a place and have it sound reasonable.
- She gives a confident report, by the way. She isn't dithering, and the question is whether that confidence marks anything.
- Crucially, the multiple drafts verdict must fall out of a structural feature of the case — something like the content being revised rather than accumulated, or there being no single point where the streams converge — and not out of vagueness in the words conscious or moment. So name that feature, then toggle it: tell me what happens to the case if you flip that one feature and change nothing else. If flipping it makes the theatre theorist plainly right, say so. If the case survives the flip, then the feature wasn't doing the work and you've built the wrong case.
- And I want her own report to be part of the mess: it should be produced without waiting for the content to settle, so it's one of the drafts rather than a readout of the winner. I think that's the bit that actually makes this work, but convince me.
- Not a warmed-over Libet-style readiness potential thing. That one's been done to death.

Then, after the scenario:

- Walk through what each view says about it, step by step, in plain language.
- Say which single feature is doing the work, and why that feature and not some other.
- Say how a defender of the theatre picture would attack the case — their strongest reply, not a silly one — and whether that reply succeeds.
- And tell me what cheating would look like here: what would a version of this case look like that simply stipulates the conclusion instead of earning it?
