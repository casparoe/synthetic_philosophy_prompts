I'm helping put together a small grant call — call it three years, enough for maybe four or five people, working on the theory side of bounded rationality (no lab, no new experiments, just conceptual and formal work). The topic we've settled on is satisficing: the whole family of ideas around "good enough" stopping rules, aspiration levels that shift as you search, and the claim that agents don't maximize but settle. My background is in econ and game theory, and I keep running into a wall that I suspect is actualy the interesting part. When someone says an agent "satisficed," I can never tell whether that's (a) a description of a heuristic that happens to be cheaper than maximizing, (b) a normative claim that this is what a rational agent with limited resources should do, or (c) a redescription of maximizing where the objective function has quietly been expanded to include the cost of deliberating. And then a further thing bugs me: to satisfice you need a threshold, but where does the threshold come from? If you set it by asking what's good enough given your goals and your deliberation budget, you seem to be back to optimizing something; if you just pick it, the theory looks arbitrary. I've also heard people say satisficing needs a "small world" — a fixed menu of options and consequences — and I don't see how it's supposed to work when the option set is open-ended and you can invent new alternatives as you go.

What I want from you is an agenda. Which open questions in this area are most worth three years of four or five people's time, and in what order? I'm less interested in a survey than in the reasoning: for each question, why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people doing conceptual and formal work rather than experiments, and whether settling it would actually change anything downstream — in decision theory, in how we model firms and organizations, in how we think about resource-bounded intelligence. If some of the questions people usually list here aren't worth it, either because they're pseudo-questions or because the answer wouldn't matter much, say so and say why. And tell me what argument or result over the next couple of years would move a question up or down your list.

One thing I'd ask you to be careful about: don't just tell me the field is contested and leave it there. I want your actual ranking, with the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and neglectedness made explicit, and a clear sense of which single question most of the others implicitly depend on. If you think the framing I gave above is itself confused — that the three readings of "satisficing" aren't really competitors, or that the threshold problem dissolves once you see something I'm missing — I'd rather hear that than a tidy list.
