I chair the selection panel of a mid-sized foundation that makes a single flagship grant each year. The award is indivisible, and our published guidelines commit us to funding the most promising of the shortlisted proposals. This year we shortlisted two, and they scored identically on all four criteria we published. The tie is not the product of agreement: different panelists reached the same totals for different reasons, and we never fixed the relative weights of the criteria, so I am genuinely unsure whether the tie is a fact about the two proposals or an artifact of how we added up our own judgments.

Two constraints force a decision by 15 December. If the funds are not committed by then they revert to the endowment, which is worse for everything the foundation exists to do; and our guidelines promise each finalist a short written statement of the panel's reasons for its decision. Our bylaws say only that the chair may resolve a tie, provided the resolution and the grounds for it are recorded in the minutes. I am that chair. I could break the tie by fiat at our 5 December meeting, but I would rather the panel adopt and record a defensible basis than have me supply one, so I want the memo to be the document we vote on.

Four positions on the panel need to be dealt with rather than smoothed over. One member, who reads more philosophy than is good for her, distinguishes choosing from picking: choosing is done for a reason, picking is what you do when there is nothing to choose between, and the fact that you had a reason to pick something is not thereby a reason for the thing picked. On her view a coin toss is honest and an invented reason is a lie. A second holds that the two proposals are not equal but on a par: he accepts that slightly improving either would not make it better than the other, and concludes that they are neither equal nor unequal in merit, which he says changes what a tie-breaker has to accomplish. A third wants to split the award, though it is indivisible and carries a residency that only one organization can hold. A fourth wants to reopen the rubric and add a fifth criterion, which the others call gerrymandering and he calls finishing a job we left undone. And the old donkey between two identical bales at least had the excuse of not seeing the third option, starvation; ours is plainly on the table, and it is worse than either grant.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the panel: roughly 800 to 1,000 words, formal in tone, with the recommendation stated in the first paragraph and a concrete course of action with dates that can be executed before 5 December. It should give the reasoning, say what the panel should be prepared to put in writing to the finalist who is not chosen, address whether our tie is a fact about the proposals or about our process and whether that distinction should change the recommendation, and state the strongest case against your recommendation fairly enough that the panelist who holds it would recognize it as her own. Do not write a survey of the philosophical literature on the subject; write the document we can vote on.
