I'm taking a seminar on the history and philosophy of science, and I keep running into a tension I cannot resolve. On the one hand, we are taught that major discoveries are often made independently and at roughly the same time—the calculus (Newton and Leibniz), natural selection (Darwin and Wallace), the telephone, the theory of evolution—so that if one person had not got there, someone else almost certainly would have. On the other hand, our institutions treat priority as enormously important. Patent offices in most jurisdictions award rights to the first applicant rather than the first actual inventor, so an independent inventor who arrived earlier but delayed filing, or who used the invention secretly within a firm, can lose the legal right to a later applicant who filed first. Scientific prizes and eponymy likewise attach a discovery to a small number of names—there is even a wry principle to the effect that no scientific discovery is named after the person who actually discovered it—and priority disputes are treated as serious moral matters rather than as administrative bookkeeping. I want to understand what philosophical commitments this cluster of practices presupposes. Does it presuppose that discoveries are the acts of individual minds rather than inevitable products of a social process? That credit is a deserved reward for intellectual labor? That the identity of the discoverer matters for truth, progress, or justice—or only for incentives? And are these commitments consistent with the fact of multiple discovery, or does the practice merely fit them loosely?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely make convenient, and say where they are philosophically underdetermined—that is, where several rival theories of discovery, credit, and desert would license the same rules. Then consider a counterfactual: if we took simultaneous invention seriously as the normal case rather than a coincidence, what would have to change in patent law, scientific credit, and the way we teach the history of ideas? Would we abolish priority rights, replace individual names with collective or anonymous attribution, or keep the practices for incentive reasons while abandoning the moral language of desert? I am especially interested in whether the current arrangement can be defended without assuming a heroic, individualist picture of discovery, and what a coherent alternative would look like in practice.
