Okay, bit of an odd one. I read Asimov's Foundation books when I was a teenager and they properly rewired my brain, and I've just come back to them after going down a rabbit hole on cliodynamics — Turchin's stuff, secular cycles, elite overproduction, popular immiseration, the claim that you can put history on a graph and see the instability coming. I work as a software engineer, so maybe that's why the idea appeals and also why it bugs me: it definately feels like somebody claiming they can predict a system by modelling the aggregate and ignoring the individual, and my instinct as an engineer is that you don't get away with that unless the individual genuinely doesn't matter.

So what I want is a proper philosophical reading of the Foundation trilogy through the question of whether history can be quantified and predicted. Not a plot summary, I know the story. I want to know what theory of history Asimov is actually presupposing, and whether the books believe it. Psychohistory only works on huge populations, and only so long as nobody knows what it's predicting — which, incidentally, is a problem Popper had a name for, the Oedipus effect I think, where the prediction changes the thing predicted. Then the plan gets wrecked by the Mule, one single unpredictable individual, and later quietly patched up by the Second Foundation, which is basically a load of statisticians with a secret maintenance budget. So the whole drama of the series is driven by exactly the stuff the theory treats as noise. Is that a flaw in the books, or is Asimov dramatising a real objection to macrohistory — that the aggregate regularities might be genuine but all the interesting bits are the deviations, and you only spot them afterwards?

Concretely, I'd like to see: where the books and the actual cliodynamics line up and where Asimov cheats; what a philosopher of history would say about the Seldon Plan as a model (does it assume history has laws, or just trends?); what the time-vault scenes are doing dramatically, since Seldon's recorded messages keep announcing that the plan is fine and then get overtaken by events; and whether the Mule is a genuine counterexample to statistical history or just a rare event that any probabilistic model can absorb. I've also always wondered whether Asimov and Tolstoy are even disagreeing — War and Peace seems to make the opposite move, that the great men are irrelevant and it's the tide that matters, yet both end up saying the individual gets swallowed by the aggregate. And if you think the books add something to this argument that a philosophy paper wouldn't, or if they're just a nice illustration of a debate that's been had better elsewhere, say so plainly — I'd rather have an honest verdict than a forced reading. I've got no formal philosophy background either, so if you use jargon, define it in passing.

No need for citations or a reading list, I can look things up myself. And could you write it in British English, please — I work with a London team now and the spelling is starting to rub off on me anyway. Cheers.
