I have no training in philosophy or law. I've just spent a few years worrying at the question of what makes consent valid, and I'd like to get the question itself straight before I read anything about it, so what I want from you is not a theory but the specification a theory would have to satisfy. Please write me the list of requirements that any adequate account of valid consent has to meet.

The cases I keep turning over, and that I'd want the list built around, are these. A mechanic sells me a car and says nothing about a defect he knows about, though he would have answered honestly if I'd asked; I want to know whether my agreement is as void as if he'd lied outright, and whether withholding and lying really come to the same thing. My landlord offers me money to leave early, and because I'm broke I take it; I can't tell whether that's coercion or just a good offer, and I notice the answer seems to depend entirely on what I was entitled to before he spoke, which nobody ever specifies. I sign a hospital form while sedated and in pain and later deny I meant it; the opposite also happens, where someone understood perfectly at the time and only regrets it afterwards. Someone consents to a procedure in general but not to the student who actually performs it, and I don't know whether that's partial consent or no consent. A relative with dementia agrees to something during a lucid hour and has no memory of it the next day, and I can't decide whether the later forgetting retroactively matters. And a salesman deceives me about something I genuinely don't care about, or about something I care about that wouldn't have changed my decision either way, and I don't know whether that leaves my consent intact.

So the requirements should cover which cases the account must get right; which distinctions it must not collapse, such as between consent and mere compliance, between a threat and a hard bargain, between withholding and lying, between being unable to consent and merely consenting oddly, and between whether consent is present at all and how far it reaches; and which plain facts about the world it cannot contradict, among them that people often agree to what harms them, that agreement can be real without being cheerful, and that hospitals, employers and courts have to be able to act on consent without reading anyone's mind. For each requirement, tell me the case or consideration that puts it on the list. Then say which of the requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the preference of one way of thinking about consent rather than another, and flag any pair or group that I should suspect cannot be satisfied together, because that is the part I most want to see; I already half-suspect that demanding consent be a fact about a person's inner state and demanding that it be checkable from the outside will turn out to be in tension, and I'd like to know if I'm right and what it costs to give up either one. Finally, tell me which single requirement, if I took it seriously, would rule out the most otherwise plausible accounts.

Write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you'd write a long letter. Don't give me a reading list or name any authors or books; describe positions in plain terms if you need to refer to them, and assume I can't look anything up.
