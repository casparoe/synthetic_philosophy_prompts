I keep a private ledger of my verdicts on local conventions — the small rules a place enforces without ever writing them down — and I would like to know whether a single principle, or a small family of them, generates the pattern. I record the cases as they actually strike me.

**Tipping.** At a sit-down restaurant in a country where the wage structure assumes a tip, leaving nothing is not merely rude but wrong; I feel it as an obligation, and I would think worse of a visitor who skipped it on the grounds that people do not tip at home. At a counter-service café in the same country, where the staff are paid hourly and the payment tablet merely offers twenty per cent as a default, leaving nothing is entirely fine; I feel no obligation, only a faint friction at the screen. In a country where service staff are salaried and an offered tip is read as pity, or as a suggestion that the server is servile, I think a visitor should not tip, and should not be thought stingy for declining — there the local convention binds and my home convention does not. But in a resort town of a country whose residents do not tip, where the seasonal staff live on tourist tips, I think a visitor should tip even though the residents do not. That last case sits badly with the one before it, and I have never worked out why.

**Queueing.** Joining a friend who is holding a place for me in a line of two, where the person behind loses nothing, is fine. Slipping into a friend's position near the front of a long line is not fine, even though the extra delay is spread so thin across everyone behind me that no one could name it.

**Escalators.** In a city where everyone stands on the right and the left lane is left free for walkers, standing in the left lane to finish a conversation is mildly wrong: it costs a stranger a few seconds and I know it. In a city where no one walks and everyone stands where they like, the same act is not wrong at all. The physical act, and the cost to others, look identical; only the local regularity differs.

**Jaywalking.** Crossing an empty street at two in the morning against the signal, in a city where pedestrians do this constantly and no traffic is in sight, is fine. The same crossing in a city where pedestrians wait scrupulously is not fine, even though no one is harmed — the driver who slows for me, and the people on the corner, are meant to read it as my deciding the rule is for them and not for me. Yet my verdict flips back if my six-year-old nephew is with me: then it is not fine even in the first city, because he is learning what we do.

**Shoes.** In a home where removing shoes is the practice and the guests do it, keeping mine on is wrong. In a home where the hosts have twice told me not to bother and no one else removes theirs, keeping mine on is fine. Where the hosts wave me through but their elderly parent plainly expects it, I remove them, and would think badly of myself if I did not.

**A limit.** In a country where a small payment to the clerk is the ordinary way to get a permit processed, and where declining will leave my papers untouched for months, I do not think I should pay. Not because the sum is small or the harm diffuse — I think the convention simply does not reach this case, and I want to know what feature it lacks.

A tentative principle I have been carrying: a local convention binds me exactly when someone would be made worse off, in a way they could reasonably have relied on, by my breach; otherwise it is a courtesy, and courtesies bind only where the breach would be read as contempt rather than as ignorance.

That principle seems to get the queue, the escalator, and the shoes. It gets the sit-down restaurant if I count the server's reliance on the tip as the relevant reliance. It struggles with the scrupulous-city jaywalk, where no one is made worse off in the reliance sense and yet I still think the crossing is wrong. It struggles with the resort town, where the local convention says do not tip and the local reliance says do. And it seems to get the bribe backwards, since the clerk does rely on the payment and my refusal does make him worse off.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of them if one will not do, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not listed. For each clause, the case that forces it. Its verdicts on cases I have not considered — in particular, conventions whose content is arbitrary but whose observance is near-universal, and conventions that belong to a subgroup I am not part of but am standing in front of. And an honest statement of which of the verdicts above the principle cannot save, together with your recommendation about which verdict I should surrender rather than complicate the principle further.
