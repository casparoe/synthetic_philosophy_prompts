Two of my friends got into it at a party about pile-ons and I ended up in the middle of it with nothing to say. Can you referee it move by move and tell me who's right about what?

Background: a guy in their extended circle — neither of them knows him well — said something shitty on a podcast years ago, it resurfaced, and inside a week he lost a speaking slot, a book deal, and a job offer. Alot of the people going after him weren't the ones he'd wronged, and one of them kept saying it wasn't punishment, just consequences, that nobody owes him a stage. That's what set Sam and Ravi off.

Sam's position: there's a real difference between four things you can do to someone after they do something bad. (a) Close a door — stop offering them something you were never obliged to offer. (b) Set a fence — keep others away because you think they'll do it again. (c) Charge a price — make them pay for what they did. (d) Hand down a verdict — declare them guilty and say they deserve it. He says (a) and (b) need no court, no proportionality and no term limit, because they aren't about desert at all. (c) and (d) need all three. And he thinks most pile-ons are (d) wearing the costume of (a), and that the nobody-owes-him-a-stage line is the costume.

Ravi's counter: he says costume talk is fine in a seminar, but the guy is equally ruined either way, so the distinction has no moral content where it counts. What actually happened is a thousand people each did something individually trivial and the sum was catastrophic — and because no one person did anything big, nobody can be held to proportionality and nobody feels responsible. He says that's not a bug, it's the engine, and calling it a fence doesn't change it. He also thinks you-can't-punish-him-you're-not-a-court is a dodge, because it's a punishment in effect whether or not you've got jurisdiction.

Sam: then who's the punisher? If it's the aggregate, aggregates don't deliberate or intend and can't be blamed. If it's each individual, each act really is trivial. He says there's a gap here, and that Ravi wants the moral force of the word punishment without the accountability that normally comes with it.

Ravi: we already do this with pollution. One bucket in the river is fine, all the buckets together are not, and we don't let anyone off with my bucket was fine. Predictability is enough — if you know piling on gets people fired you can't hide behind your one tweet. And he says (b) is doing sneaky work: a fence is meant to keep people safe, but the crowd is fencing off someone who already stopped, so it's a fence with a punishment inside.

That's where they stopped. Go through it move by move — not just who wins overall. Which of Sam's four things actually needs what, and which of the two of them is right about where the moral weight sits? Is Ravi right that the bucket case transfers, or does the analogy break somewhere? Is Sam right that there's an accountability gap, and if so is it a reason to stop doing anything or just a reason to be uneasy? And is there something they're both missing about what the guy's reputation even is — whether there's a baseline you can be said to have damaged in the first place, and whether anyone could tell from the outside if a closed door is really a verdict in disguise.
