I am a professor of early modern history who has, for the past four years, also served as volunteer director of the small county museum in the town where I grew up. This Saturday I must give the opening remarks at the members' preview of our new permanent exhibition, which is built entirely around a single week in the spring of 1911: a mill fire, a failed rescue, and a photograph of the crowd outside the gates that has since become the emblem of the town. The exhibition is deliberately old-fashioned in form — a wall of portraits of named individuals, a day-by-day timeline, a vitrine of charred objects, a single surviving ledger opened to the fatal page — because that is the shape of thing our donors funded, and several of the lenders are the great-grandchildren of people visible in the photograph, who will be sitting in the front row. My difficulty is that my own scholarly convictions run the other way. I have spent a career on the slow variables: the draining of the marsh, the drift of the grain trade, the rainfall record, the soils, the turnpike and then the railway. On my view the fire is not really the thing that happened; it is a brief agitation on the surface of processes that were already a century old, and the courage and the villainy in the photograph are, as explanations, close to beside the point. I cannot say that plainly at a members' preview — it would insult the lenders, misrepresent what the exhibition actually shows, and the county paper will quote me. But I cannot give the standard speech either, because I do not believe a word of it, and there are two academic historians on our board who will hear the flattery.

Please write the remarks: six or seven minutes, roughly 900 to 1,100 words, composed as a speech rather than an essay, so that it is speakable, has a clear shape a listener can follow without notes, and is something a person could actually stand up and deliver in this room. Take the role of an experienced speechwriter who is also thoroughly at home in the philosophy and methodology of history; think carefully, before you begin, about what this occasion permits and what it forbids, and about where the genuine tension lies, rather than dissolving it with the usual truce — events matter, but so do structures. The speech has to do more than gesture at the longue durée: the slow variables should do real explanatory work on the fire, and the speech should decide, and defend in passing, what an event and an individual can legitimately mean for someone who thinks as I do. The hard part is that none of this may sound like an argument with the audience. The lenders should leave feeling their ancestors were honored rather than demoted; the donors should feel the exhibition they paid for has been taken seriously rather than patronized; and the two historians should be unable to catch me in an inconsistency. Treat the portrait wall, the timeline, and the photograph as constraints rather than props: if the form of the exhibition is irreducibly event-shaped, the speech has to say something honest about why we keep building them that way, and what they are for. Give me a short note first — three or four sentences, no more — on how you have decided to manage the tension, and then the remarks themselves.
