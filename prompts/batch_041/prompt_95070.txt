I host a small philosophy podcast, and the guest for our next episode works on practical rationality; for the last several years she has been developing a deflationary line about money-pump arguments. The target is the familiar family of arguments in which an agent with some defect — intransitive preferences, or preferences that shift across time — can be walked through a sequence of trades that returns her to her starting point minus some money, from which it is concluded that she violates a requirement of rationality, often a distinctively diachronic one. Her contention, as I understand it, is that these arguments run two things together. Exploitability — the fact that a third party can, without deceiving her, arrange a sequence of choices that leaves her worse off by her own lights — is a property of an option set together with an agent's dispositions to choose. Irrationality is a defect in the attitudes themselves. A money pump, on her view, is at best a vivid display of the first, and wherever it does bear on the second it exposes a defect already present at a time (an inconsistent or incomplete preference state), so that it never earns its keep as an argument for a genuinely diachronic requirement. She concedes that agents can be "unstable in a way that matters," but locates the instability in the choices rather than in the requirements.

Draft eight to ten questions that press her on the substance rather than inviting her to restate the view, with at least one question each on: whether every pump traces back to a defect at a single time, the hard case being an agent who is perfectly consistent at each time but whose preferences cycle over time, so that the sequence itself seems to be the only locus of the problem; whether exploitability really can be detached from irrationality, given that the pumper needs no false beliefs and the whole sequence can unfold in an afternoon, before the agent has any chance to learn; the sequential-choice trichotomy she must be leaning on — the agent modelled as myopic, sophisticated, or resolute — and whether resolute choice is even available to someone who cannot bind herself and whose preferences genuinely change; and what her view implies for the standard arguments that treat susceptibility to a diachronic Dutch book as a reason to conditionalize, and for the everyday practice of precommitment, which on her account would come out merely prudent rather than rational. Give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and skip the biographical warm-up.

I would rather have a handful of questions that land than ten that do not, so for each question say how confident you are that it presses a defender of this line rather than inviting a stock reply, and name the consideration that would make you withdraw or rewrite it — a distinction she may already be drawing, or a case she may already have handled elsewhere. Say too where your reconstruction of her position is most likely to be wrong, and what I should listen for in her answers that would tell me it is.
