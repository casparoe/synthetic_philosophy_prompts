Last winter I began a year-long course of physical rehabilitation after a spinal injury, and I have been taken aback by how little my settled judgment governs what I actually do: I know, in a way that feels entirely sincere, that the daily exercises matter, and I still find myself bargaining with myself at nine in the evening and losing. A topic I once treated as an exam question has become something I think about every day. I also run a small interview series for a philosophy-and-economics reading group, and our next guest is a theorist who defends the view that weakness of will is best understood as the strategic interaction among successive motivational states, each of which discounts future rewards hyperbolically—the battle-of-the-selves picture treated as more than a metaphor, with commitment devices as its practical upshot.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view. They should press where the literature actually presses, and for each one I want a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I need at least one question on each of the following, in whatever order serves the interview:

- Whether the hyperbolic-discounting apparatus explains akrasia or dissolves it. If at each moment the agent simply prefers what she prefers, where does the acting-against-one's-own-better-judgment go? Press on whether the model captures synchronic akrasia—acting against a judgment held at the very moment of action—and not only the diachronic preference reversals it was built to fit.

- Whether the shape of the discount curve is doing any real work. Is a hyperbolic curve actually required, or are the same data compatible with exponential discounting plus something else—uncertainty about whether the later reward arrives at all, subadditive discounting over intervals, or a discontinuity between now and not-now that is not a matter of degree? The follow-up should force him to say what would count as evidence that the curve itself, rather than the framing, is the explanatory engine.

- The multiple-selves, planner-versus-doer framing: is it a literal claim about agency or a useful fiction? And does treating the person as a team of temporally indexed agents make weakness of will either impossible (no single agent is defeated) or, worse, non-culpable (there is no one agent to blame)? The follow-up should draw out what he thinks remains of responsibility on his own account.

- The classical contrast: Socrates' denial that anyone acts against what he judges best, and Aristotle's insistence that the phenomenon is real and that the akratic's knowledge is present only in a way. Ask whether the discounting model sides with Socrates, with Aristotle, or with neither, and whether it can distinguish the case where a settled judgment is overridden by a present temptation from the case where the temptation corrupts the judgment itself, so that at the moment of action the agent no longer judges the better course to be better.

- The practical and normative question: on his view, is the person who leans on commitment devices—precommitment, penalties, locking money away, enlisting others—exercising a virtue, or merely routing around a defect? The follow-up should make him commit on whether successful self-management is an achievement of agency or evidence that agency is fragmented.

- The empirical standing of the willpower picture. The willpower-as-depletable-muscle story and the ego-depletion findings it rested on have taken a beating in replication, while the delay-discounting findings look more robust. Ask which parts of his account he would surrender if the willpower-as-muscle evidence evaporated entirely, and what would remain.

If they sharpen the interview, add two or three questions beyond that list—for instance, one that makes him say what would change his mind, and one that presses on whether the account can be squared with ordinary first-person deliberation, given that the deliberating I is supposed to be merely one of the selves in the bargain. The register should be that of a well-prepared but non-hostile interlocutor; no questions that merely invite a summary of the view, and none that require him to cite literature.
