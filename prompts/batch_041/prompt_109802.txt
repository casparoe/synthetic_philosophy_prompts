I'm assembling a long-form piece for an ideas-and-technology column on the strange afterlife of John Dewey's experimentalism — the way his vocabulary of inquiry, fallibilism, and democracy-as-a-way-of-life has migrated into the idiom of civic-tech firms and governance consultancies. For the centerpiece I want a parody written entirely straight: the launch packet for a fictional firm's flagship product, as it would actually be circulated to clients and reporters. The firm is called Civic Continuum; the product is Great Community OS, marketed as an end-to-end platform for what its brochure calls continuous democratic improvement. Please draft the packet at roughly 800 to 900 words, comprising a press release announcing the launch and a short FAQ for prospective municipal clients, in the deadpan voice of a well-funded communications team that believes every word. Suggested modules you may use or replace: a Situation Room that detects indeterminate situations in a municipality before they become contentious; a Public Assembly (beta) that convenes affected stakeholders; a Warranted Assertibility Dashboard that scores policy hypotheses as they accumulate evidence; and a Habit Loop that nudges residents toward civic habits between elections. A sidebar of sample dashboard metrics would be welcome.

The humor has to come from getting Dewey right, not from generic corporate absurdity, and it should be legible to a reader who has never opened Dewey while being recognizably fair to someone who has. Let the packet quietly take positions on the real difficulties: who is authorized to name a situation as problematic in the first place, and whether a client who pays for the diagnosis can be the same public that suffers the consequences; what distinguishes experimenting on a public from a public inquiring, given that Dewey's method requires the inquirers to be able to revise the hypothesis rather than merely receive its results; whether inquiry that terminates in a metric or a deliverable has stopped being inquiry at all; what fallibilism amounts to when the cost of a failed experiment falls on residents rather than on the firm; whether ends as well as means are subject to experimental revision, or whether the platform quietly treats the goals as fixed inputs; and whether the whole enterprise reinstates the expert commission that Walter Lippmann proposed and Dewey spent The Public and Its Problems (1927) resisting, now with a friendlier interface and a subscription tier. The satire should also register the uncomfortable possibility that Dewey invited this — that treating democracy as experimental, inquiry as problem-solving, and the public as something that must be organized and reorganized may make his thought unusually available to exactly this kind of appropriation — so the reader should not be able to settle comfortably on betrayal or on fulfillment.

Keep the irony sustained and unwinking: no authorial nudges, no character who voices the critique on the reader's behalf, and no jokes that require knowing the secondary literature. Do not put any words in Dewey's mouth and do not fabricate quotations from him or from any real text; where you need his formulations, paraphrase them plainly and unattributed, or let the fictional executives garble them in their own words. Where the packet cites evidence, let it cite the kind of evidence such a firm would actually have — pilot programs, satisfaction scores, retention rates, an advisory board of former mayors — rather than inventing scholarly findings. Dewey's term warranted assertibility, from Logic: The Theory of Inquiry (1938), may be used as a product feature name without quotation marks.
