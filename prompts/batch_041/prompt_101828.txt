A colleague of mine who works on the ethics of deception has spent the past term working through cases with our graduate seminar, and I have been trying to work out the rule she is actually applying. Here are her verdicts as I recorded them.

First, a physician who wants to spare a patient several anxious weeks tells her that a biopsy came back entirely clear when in fact it showed a lesion that will need monitoring; he wants her to believe there is nothing to watch. My colleague called this wrong.

Second, a homeowner asked by a prospective buyer whether the house has any problems answers that the boiler was serviced last winter, which is true, while knowing the house has serious damp, and he wants the buyer to conclude that the house is in good repair. She called this wrong too, and said it was no better than a lie.

Third, a hotel clerk asked by a journalist whether a particular person is registered at the hotel replies that she cannot comment on guests. She called this permissible.

Fourth, a clinician asked by an insurance adjuster to confirm a patient's diagnoses lists the ones already on file and passes over a sensitive condition that the insurer has no right to and whose disclosure could harm the patient; she makes no false statement and does not mean the adjuster to draw any particular conclusion about what she has left out. She called this permissible as well.

Now here is a case I have not yet put to her. At a family gathering, a relative who has no claim on the information asks my cousin whether he and his partner are trying for a child. They are; he tells her that they have not really given it much thought, because he does not want the subject raised. He means her to believe that they are not trying. The relative has no entitlement to the truth here, and no one is harmed by what he says.

I would like you to predict how my colleague will judge this new case; to set out the several rules that fit her four earlier verdicts equally well, for instance that deception is wrong only when it is intended to produce a false belief in the hearer, or only when it violates a claim to the truth that the hearer actually holds, or only when it causes wrongful harm, or only when it exploits the hearer's reasonable reliance on being told the truth; to say what each of these rules predicts about the new case; to give your own best guess about her verdict, with the reasons that lead you to prefer one rule over the others; and to name the further case you would put to her in order to decide among the rules that this new case leaves standing. I care less about the verdict than about the structure: which rule is doing the work, which cases would expose it, and what each of the rival rules would have to say about the case I have described.
