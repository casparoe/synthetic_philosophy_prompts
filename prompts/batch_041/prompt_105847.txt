For some years I have been in the habit of consulting a close friend of mine — a philosopher whose work concerns where a mind stops and the rest of the world begins — about the various external props on which my writing depends, and I have come to treat her rulings as a settled body of case law. Her verdicts, as I recall them, are these. The large index-card box in which I keep the threads of my plots and the facts of my characters, kept in a private shorthand of my own devising, sitting open on my desk, and consulted perhaps fifty times a day, she counts as part of my memory — as genuinely mine as anything I recall unaided. The address book on my telephone, which has never once been wrong, she counts likewise. My wife, who has an extraordinary memory for my appointments and whom I ring whenever I cannot remember whether I am free on Thursday, she does not count: my wife is another person, not an extension of me. My wristwatch, consulted a dozen times a day for the time, she does not count. Nor do my reading glasses, without which I can barely read at all. These verdicts were given over several years, in no particular order, and I have never managed to extract from her a single principle that covers them all.

Now I have begun to work in a manner that troubles me, and I should like you to reason out, in her spirit, what she would make of it. Beside my manuscript I keep open a piece of software that reads the half-sentence I have just written and offers the next few sentences in what it has learned to call my style; I have come to rely on it, I accept perhaps a third of what it proposes, and when the window is closed I find I can barely get going at all. What would my friend say of it — is it, in her sense, part of my mind, or merely a tool? Set out the rule, or the several rules, that best account for the verdicts she has already given, and say which of them you take to be doing the real work; if more than one rule fits her record equally well, say so plainly, and tell me what each of them predicts about my software, together with your own best guess and the reasons for it. Then name the further case — some other device, or some alteration to my present arrangement — that would most sharply distinguish those rules from one another, and explain why it would be decisive. Please give your answer in British English.
