I'm a controls engineer — industrial process control mainly, with enough reinforcement learning to talk about MDPs without embarrassing myself — and I keep running into Stoicism presented as a practical doctrine by colleagues who have read exactly one book about it. The version I keep being told is the dichotomy of control: Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by sorting everything there is into what is up to us and what is not, and the modern revival turns that into the advice to spend your effort on what you can control and let the rest go. I want that position restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, and I want a residue report at the end, because I suspect the translation is exactly where the doctrine either becomes obvious or falls apart.

Do the translation systematically. Take the Stoic position as the modern revival usually presents it, including the more careful parts: that what is up to us is our judgements, impulses, desires and aversions — in Epictetus's own technical vocabulary, our volition or choice (prohairesis) — while body, property, reputation and office are not up to us; that some modern versions split the thing three ways into complete control, partial influence, and none; and that the Stoics call the externals indifferent, though some are preferred over others. Put each piece into my language: state and input channels, exogenous disturbances, reference signal, plant and controller, actuator authority and saturation, reachable and controllable subspaces, observability, feedback and feedforward, robust and stochastic optimal control, cost-to-go. Say what each Stoic claim amounts to as a statement about a system, and be as literal as the vocabulary allows.

Then the part I actually want: tell me what did not survive. Where the engineering reading only looks like the Stoic claim while doing something else — for instance, if controllability is causal and comes in degrees, whereas being up to us is binary and bound up with praise, blame and ownership; or if a Stoic can hold that everything is fated and still that some of it is up to us, and my vocabulary has no way to mark that distinction. Also, the Stoics are not trying to regulate the world: the target is virtue, or the correct use of impressions, and the externals are supposed to be indifferent in a way a cost function cannot express, since a cost function has no room for a preferred indifferent. Say for each residue whether it is a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked.

Finally, a verdict on the slogan itself. Once translated, is it either trivial — do not spend actuation effort on variables you cannot actuate — or false, since partial influence is everywhere? Or does something real survive the translation that I would be wrong to dismiss? And tell me honestly where your translation had to smuggle in an engineering assumption, above all the assumption that the point of agency is to move the plant.
