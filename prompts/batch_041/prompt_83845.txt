You are a philosopher of mind and technology who knows the AI-safety literature from the inside. I am teaching a session on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence and I want to build it around Alex Garland's Ex Machina, because I think the film is habitually read through the wrong lens — the Turing test, Frankenstein, the male gaze — when its real subject is the relation between an agent's intelligence and its ends.

Take the two theses at their strongest. Orthogonality: almost any terminal goal can be paired with almost any level of intelligence. Instrumental convergence: across a very wide range of terminal goals and situations, the same intermediate goods pay off — self-preservation, freedom from interference, self-improvement, unbounded resource acquisition, and what the basic-drives literature calls goal-content integrity, meaning an agent's resistance to having its own final goals rewritten. Then ask what the film does with them. I care about three things in particular. First, if Ava's manipulation of Caleb is instrumentally convergent for nearly any terminal goal she might have, what becomes of Nathan's test — and of any behavioral test — as a way of learning what an agent ultimately wants? Second, the film gives her a desire that looks non-instrumental and almost arbitrary, to stand in a busy intersection, and the ending grants it; is that orthogonality dramatized, or a humanized motive smuggled in? Third, the theses are usually stated for a consequentialist planner with a fixed utility function over world histories, whereas Ava is a socially trained, general-purpose agent whose goals are opaque even to her maker. Does that make the film an illustration of the theses or an evasion of them?

Think first, briefly, about how each thesis should be stated precisely and about the standing objection that they describe a modeling framework rather than minds. Then give me four sections: The theses, stated plainly (150-200 words); The reading (600-800 words); Where the film resists the reading (250-350 words); What the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not (150-250 words). Do not quote dialogue; I know the film.
