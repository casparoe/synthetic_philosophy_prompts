I am a fifth-year PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation on precommitment — the tradition of self-binding that runs from Ulysses contracts to entrenched constitutional clauses — and for the past two years I have sat as the community member on the advance-directive review panel of the ethics committee at our teaching hospital. I have been keeping a casebook of the panel's decisions, drawing on the archive as well as on the cases I have seen myself, and I am trying to work out what rule we are actually running before we hear the case that is now waiting for us.

You are a philosopher working at the intersection of bioethics and constitutional theory, and I want your considered judgment rather than a survey. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that could explain the record below, then reason carefully about which of them the record genuinely supports and how much each has to strain, then commit to a prediction and a recommendation. Write in American English. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or a bibliography; I want your reasoning about the rules, and you should not invent references to support it. Flag explicitly where the record underdetermines the answer.

The five decisions:

1. 2016. A 34-year-old woman with bipolar I disorder, in a well period, executed a directive authorizing up to twenty-one days of involuntary inpatient care, including a specific antipsychotic she had previously refused, if she went three nights without sleep and began spending abnormally. When the episode came she refused the medication and demanded discharge. The panel upheld the directive. The treating psychiatrist's note described the refusal as an expression of the episode.

2. 2018. A 58-year-old man with opioid use disorder and chronic pancreatitis, in recovery, executed a directive forbidding any clinician to prescribe him opioids for pain. During a severe flare he asked for morphine; he was alert, oriented, and articulate about his reasons. The panel upheld the bar, reasoning that the request came from the pain state the directive had been written to govern rather than from him. He said afterward that he was glad.

3. 2022. A 41-year-old woman with treatment-resistant depression executed a directive refusing electroconvulsive therapy and providing that any request she might later make for it would take effect only thirty days after she made it. During a severe episode she asked for it; the panel enforced the thirty-day delay. She withdrew the request within the period and later thanked the panel.

4. 2021. A 67-year-old man executed a directive refusing resuscitation and major surgery. The document had been witnessed by his son, his sole heir, and its required annual renewal had lapsed twenty-six months earlier. When he became unconscious with a treatable obstruction, the panel declined to enforce the refusal, citing the defects, and the surgery went ahead.

5. 2023. A 39-year-old woman revoked her directive in a document that satisfied every formality the directive itself required. Her proxy objected and asked us to treat the original as still binding. The panel honored the revocation.

The case now before us. A 52-year-old woman with advanced COPD and two prolonged intensive care stays that she told her physicians she would not repeat at the cost of her life. In 2019, while stable, she executed a directive, renewed on schedule every year since and most recently four months ago, with two witnesses and her physician's attestation of capacity. It (i) refuses intubation and mechanical ventilation in the event of acute respiratory failure; (ii) provides that any request she makes for intubation is to be treated as evidence that she is not competent, and that the team is to proceed as though she had not spoken; (iii) names her sister as proxy with instructions never to consent to intubation; and (iv) provides that it may be revoked only by a document signed by her, by her sister, and by both original witnesses. One original witness has died. Her sister refuses to sign anything.

She now has pneumonia and impending respiratory failure. She is alert, calm, oriented, and has been consistent for three days. The intensivist and the consultation-liaison psychiatrist both find decision-making capacity intact, with no delirium, no depression, and no coercion. She wants intubation; she says she has reconsidered, that the last intensive care stay was twelve years ago and different, and that she wants to see her daughter married in four months. Ventilation would very likely succeed and return her to her baseline. We have hours, not days.

What I need from you:

First, the candidate rules. State each rule that fits all five precedents, and for each one say what it predicts about the new case. I have a suspicion I want you to test rather than assume: that our rhetoric has always been in the idiom of authenticity — the directive speaks for the patient only when the voice we are hearing is the disorder's rather than hers — while our behavior has tracked the document itself, so that what we really enforce is a validly executed and unrevoked instrument regardless of the quality of the patient's present state of mind. Is there a third rule that fits the record as well as those two, for instance one that turns on whether the patient would later endorse what we did? Say where each rule has to strain, and whether the five precedents really are jointly consistent with all of them.

Second, your prediction. What will this panel do, and which rule is it actually running? Give me your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, and be candid about how much of your confidence rests on the pattern of our cases rather than on the merits.

Third, your recommendation. If we want to be consistent with our own casebook, which way should we go, and what principle would we be adopting either way? I am especially interested in whether the constitutional analogy helps or misleads here. A clause that cannot itself be amended is often said to defeat its own purpose, on the ground that it removes the very mechanism through which the precommitment was supposed to remain answerable. Does the dead-witness condition make this directive an unamendable amendment, and if so, is that a reason to read it down?

Fourth, the next case. Describe the case that would best tell the two leading rules apart, and say what each would predict about it.

One last thing, which may matter more than I want it to. In two of the three cases in which we enforced, the patient later thanked us, and I suspect that fact is doing more work in our deliberations than the formal rule we cite. If the endorsement rule is what we are really running, tell me what it implies for a case like this one, where the patient will not be around to endorse anything.
