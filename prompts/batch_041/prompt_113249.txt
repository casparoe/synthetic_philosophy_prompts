I serve on the grants committee of a small family foundation, and at our last meeting two other members, neither of them me, got into an extended argument that we never resolved. I was taking the minutes and have no stake in how it comes out. The application at issue came from a meals-on-wheels program asking us to fund a part-time coordinator whose entire job is to recruit and schedule the volunteer drivers who deliver the meals. Our governing rule states that funds "may be used for direct services to clients, and may not be used for administration or overhead."

Dana said the rule settles it: a coordinator's salary is administration, the rule forbids administration, so we must decline. She said she was going by what the sentence says, not by what we might wish it said.

Marcus said we had funded exactly this before -- a volunteer coordinator at a food bank, three years ago -- and the board recorded it as direct service. He said the rule means what our practice has made it mean, and that the member ignoring the history is the one not following the rule.

Dana said history cannot do that work. Our past decisions are a finite list, and any finite list of decisions is compatible with indefinitely many rules -- including one that counts the food bank coordinator as direct service and this one as overhead. So "how we have always done it" does not pick out an answer; it only feels as though it does because we have stopped looking for the alternatives.

Marcus said that argument proves too much, because a written rule is also finite: any text has to be read against some history of readings. If a finite history cannot determine the rule, the text cannot either, so her move undercuts her own opening. He added that it also implies a new staffer can never misunderstand the rule, only differ from us -- but we correct new staffers because they are wrong, and we would be talking nonsense if we meant only that they disagreed.

Dana said she would grant the symmetry. What follows, she said, is not that the rule is clear but that nothing determines this case; we will settle it by a vote, and that is no scandal -- it is simply what committees do. Calling the winning outcome "what the rule required" is a manner of speaking.

Marcus said that if correctness is just the vote, then the member who loses is making no mistake, only losing. But we treat the dissenting member as having misread the rule, and we say of ourselves that we were wrong on past decisions and have since corrected ourselves. "We were wrong" cannot mean "we were outvoted."

I have read Kripke's skeptical reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following remarks, and this felt to me like that argument happening in a committee room, with both sides getting hold of something real. Please take the role of a philosopher of language who works on meaning and rule-following, think the exchange through carefully before committing to anything, and begin by stating what you take each side's strongest version of its position to be. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or correct but for the wrong reason, and justify each rating briefly. Then step back: what do the rule-following considerations actually license here -- does either side have a point the other never answers, what are both of them missing, and does the case look different once we separate what determines the rule's content from how the committee should decide? I do not need references; I want the arguments worked through. If my assumption that there is a fact of the matter to adjudicate is itself confused, or if the question cannot be settled without my choosing between competing readings of Kripke, say so plainly rather than producing a tidy verdict, and flag any step where you are going beyond what the arguments actually establish.
