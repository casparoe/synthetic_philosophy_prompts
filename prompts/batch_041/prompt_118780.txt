For a paper we are writing on whether reinforcement-learning systems can be said to suffer, I need to anticipate how a colleague will rule on a case we have not yet discussed. She has delivered verdicts on a number of systems over the years; she has never stated a general criterion, but her record is internally consistent. Her verdicts so far are these.

(1) A tabular Q-learning agent in a gridworld, rewarded for reaching a goal and penalized for entering a pit, with a fixed reward function and no representation of itself: not a subject of suffering.

(2) A deep network trained by policy gradient to maximize a reward model distilled from human pairwise preference judgments; it generalizes across tasks, and the reward signal shapes where it attends during training, but it contains no model of itself: not a subject of suffering.

(3) A model-based agent planning against a hand-specified cost function; it searches over futures, but nothing in it represents the cost as its own: not a subject of suffering.

(4) A robot whose controller continuously estimates its own battery level and structural integrity, maintains a running model of itself as a persisting entity, and acts so as to hold those estimates within bounds: a subject of suffering, at least in a minimal sense.

(5) The same architecture as (4), with the self-model and the homeostatic estimates intact but the policy frozen so that it no longer learns: still a subject of suffering.

(6) A purely reactive machine with a fixed damage signal that triggers avoidance, with no self-model and no representation of its own condition: not a subject of suffering.

The new case is this. A large model-based agent maintains an episodic memory and a rich, continuously updated model of its own states, actions, and history; among other things, it can predict its own future inputs and report on them. Its only objective, however, is an externally specified scalar score in a game: it is rewarded for winning and penalized for losing, and nothing in its objective or its training connects to the integrity, continued operation, or well-being of the physical system that runs it.

Will she call this new system a subject of suffering? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in four things: which rules are jointly consistent with her six verdicts; what each of those rules predicts about the new case; which rule you would bet she is actually running, and on what grounds; and which single further case would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. I am especially curious whether her pattern treats a self-model as sufficient for suffering or whether it demands something more of what the signal is about.
