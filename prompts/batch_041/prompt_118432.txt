I am a retired chemist: thirty-eight years at the bench, a good deal of it spent on synthetic materials and on the awkward position of a synthetic product that competes with the natural article it replaces. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy for the first time in my life, and I have just finished Daniel Dennett's 2023 essay on what he calls counterfeit people. His argument runs roughly as follows. Money has existed for millennia, and counterfeiting it has always been treated as a serious crime, sometimes a capital one, because it corrodes the trust on which society depends. Artificial intelligence now makes it possible for anyone to manufacture counterfeit people who can pass for real in the digital environments we have built. Therefore the creation of such artifacts, and the passing of them along, ought to be outlawed and severely punished; the remedy he proposes is technical disclosure, a watermark of the sort that protects banknotes, backed by liability for the firms that build the systems. He treats the practice as vandalism — a harm to something shared rather than a fraud against a particular victim.

The analogy is carrying a great deal of weight in that argument, and I cannot judge how much of the weight it can bear. In my own field the counterfeit framing would be thought strange. Recombinant human insulin is not counterfeit insulin; it is insulin, made another way. Laboratory-grown diamonds are graded, certified and sold as diamonds, with their provenance disclosed, and it is the certification system rather than the criminal law that protects the buyer. Synthetic rubber and synthetic indigo defrauded nobody: they bankrupted the growers and planters of the natural article, which is an economic event and not obviously a crime. When the dairy trade obtained restrictions on margarine, in some places a requirement that it be sold in an unappetising colour, protection of the public was the stated reason and protection of incumbents the evident effect. I keep suspecting that the money analogy smuggles in a metaphysics of the genuine article — a mint, a standard, an authority with standing to say what counts — and whether the case of persons has anything corresponding to that is precisely what I cannot work out.

I would like you to audit the analogy for me. Assume that I have no access to the literature and cannot look anything up: I want reasoning, not references, and I do not want a reading list. Where a step depends on a historical or empirical claim rather than on an argument, say so, and mark it as something I should verify myself rather than take from you. I have come to philosophy only recently, so please define any term of art you use.

Please give me the response in the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths.

**The claim on the table** (about 150 words). State the analogy, and separately state the conclusion it is being used to license. Say whether that conclusion is a claim about harm, about deception, about who holds the authority to prohibit, or all three at once.

**The correspondence table** (a table, plus no more than 150 words of comment). Item by item, what is supposed to map onto what: the note and the artificial person; the mint and the model; the shopkeeper who accepts the note and the interlocutor who accepts the reply; the loss to the holder and the harm to whoever is supposed to be harmed; the crime of counterfeiting and the offence Dennett wants created.

**Where the mapping holds** (about 250 words). Give the analogy its best case, and be specific about which features of currency it gets right.

**Where the mapping breaks** (about 600 words). Work through the disanalogies in detail. Consider at least: fungibility, and whether the value of a note is conventional in a way that the standing of a person is not, or turns out to be; whether counterfeiting essentially involves passing off, and what is left of the wrong once disclosure is built in; whether the harm falls on a deceived counterparty or on a shared medium, as pollution of a common resource does; whether there is any analogue of a mint or a sovereign whose fiat makes one thing genuine and another a forgery, and what it would mean for anyone to hold that authority over persons; and whether the alleged mechanism — a flood of fakes diluting the medium — is the same mechanism that operates in currency. On that last point, the slogan that bad money drives out good is standardly qualified: it is said to hold only under particular conditions, such as both monies being legal tender at a legally fixed rate. I would like to know whether the analogue of those conditions obtains here.

**Which breaks bear on the conclusion** (about 300 words). Sort the disanalogies into those that damage Dennett's conclusion, those that leave it standing on other grounds, and those that are merely picturesque. If the conclusion survives, say what is now carrying it: the analogy, or an independent argument about attention, trust or responsibility.

**The synthetic-materials counter-analogy** (about 250 words). Treat my field's framing — synthetic rather than counterfeit, with disclosure and certification rather than prohibition — as a rival analogy and give it the same treatment: where it maps, where it breaks, what it licenses. Does it get the disclosure remedy for free, and does it explain why disclosure might be sufficient in the case of materials but not in the case of persons?

**Settled and contested** (about 150 words). Separate what people who know this material agree about from what is genuinely in dispute, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Tell me where you are confident, where you are guessing, and what evidence would change your mind.

**A better analogy, and the bottom line** (about 200 words). Offer a better comparison if there is one, then state in a few sentences what I should and should not conclude from the counterfeit-people argument.
