I am a retired experimental physicist, and after several years of reading philosophy on my own I have enrolled part-time in a graduate seminar on medieval Islamic thought. The seminar requires a research proposal of about 1,500 words for a term paper of roughly 8,000 words, to be written over a single semester. I would like you to write that proposal from the half-formed idea below. I will add the references and secondary literature myself, so please include no bibliography or citation apparatus, and do not feel obliged to name particular modern commentators, since I can look those up.

The received picture I keep encountering is this: in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī argues that the connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect is not necessary — fire does not burn cotton by any power or necessity of its own, and the burning is instead created by God at each moment. This is called occasionalism, and it is usually presented as the Islamic anticipation of Hume's attack on necessary connection, sometimes with the further claim that it explains why the natural sciences withered in the Islamic world while they later flourished in Europe.

My suspicion, which I cannot yet defend, is that this picture fuses several claims the text does not clearly separate. At least four look separable to me: (i) the causal connection is not logically necessary; (ii) it cannot be demonstrated, or we cannot know it to be necessary; (iii) the fire has no efficacy of its own, so that created things are not genuine causes at all; and (iv) God is the sole agent. My impression is that al-Ghazālī is plainly committed to something like (i) and (ii) — his argument being defensive, aimed at showing that the philosophers cannot prove the necessity and that its denial is not impossible — whereas occasionalism, a label I gather is of modern coinage and applied retrospectively from the seventeenth-century debates, imports (iii) and (iv) as well. I am equally suspicious of the Hume comparison: Hume's target is the very idea of necessary connection, and his replacement for it is a psychological mechanism, custom in the observer, whereas al-Ghazālī's regularities seem meant as a fact about how God acts rather than about how we come to expect things. The two customs appear to be doing different work.

Please sharpen this into a question with a determinate answer and a method that could actually settle it. Include: a precise statement of the question, in a form that lets me say at the end whether the answer is yes or no; an account of why the existing treatments leave it open, including why the argument's defensive or dialectical character makes the metaphysical reading underdetermined; the method, in the order I would actually proceed across the semester — how I would construct a taxonomy of the senses of necessity, which passages I would read closely and against what context, which comparison texts within al-Ghazālī's own corpus (his later, more straightforwardly Ashʿarite theological writing, for instance) would test the reading, and how I would weigh the rival reading on which he is a thoroughgoing occasionalist; a defence of why this approach beats the obvious alternative of simply cataloguing what commentators have said; what a genuine result would look like and what would count as the project failing, since I would rather be told the ways it could turn out empty than have them hidden; and a realistic scope for one semester. As I read no Arabic and will work from translations, please say candidly how far that limitation should constrain the ambition of the project and where it is most likely to bite.
