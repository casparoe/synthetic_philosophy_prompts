I teach a 300-level political philosophy seminar, 16 students, one 90-minute session, and we're on the transitional justice part of the course (South Africa, Argentina, Rwanda). Last year I did the obvious thing — split the room into a prosecute team and an amnesty team and let them debate — and it died, because everyone already knew which side their supposed to take and nobody had to feel anything. What I want them to feel instead is the shape of the bargain. The commission's only real currency is the perpetrator's own disclosure, so what it buys with impunity is a kind of information a trial can't produce — a defendant who pleads not guilty generates a finding, not a confession. And the people who pay for it are the families, who don't get a vote on the price. Worse, in the South African case the deal was already cut by the negotiators before anyone asked the victims anything: amnesty was the outgoing government's price for handing over power, and (if I'm remembering right) when victims' groups took it to the Constitutional Court they lost. So the commission's job isn't really to make the trade, it's to make an already-made trade legitimate. I want the class to walk into that, not be told it.

So: design me a runnable 90-minute activity — roles, rules, materials I can print, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. Things I care about. No lecture up front; they shouldn't hear the phrase transitional justice until the debrief. I want the disclosure itself to be something they have to decide whether to believe — full disclosure is the condition for amnesty and nobody in the room can verify it, so give me at least one applicant whose account is self-serving in a way the participants will fight about, and make them live with whatever they decide. I'd also like the small-fish/architects asymmetry to surface if you can manage it: my sense from the readings is that the operatives came forward and the people who gave the orders mostly didnt, and I want them to notice that a process like this can only buy what's actually for sale. And the families need real leverage, not a speaking slot — the state shouldn't be able to hide behind 'the commission decided'. If a two-round structure (decide, then find out who you are, then decide again) earns its keep, use it; if a straight deliberation with the right roles does the work better, do that instead. Don't be cute for its own sake.

Then give me the debrief, tying specific moments in the activity to the actual issues — the truth-for-impunity trade, who has standing to forgive, blanket versus individualized amnesty, restorative versus retributive framings, why trials and commissions produce different kinds of knowledge — plus a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. My guess is it can't reproduce the decades-later aftermath, or the fact that the stakes were real for real people; tell me what else I'm papering over. And if the whole thing is a bad idea for a 90-minute class, say so and tell me what to do instead.
