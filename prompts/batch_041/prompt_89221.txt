Can you explain what's actually going on with Quine's take on moral values? I get the rough naturalist story — no moral facts over and above the empirical/scientific ones, moral judgments aren't true in the way that a claim about a brick on a table is true, and he leans on a comparison between normative inquiry and engineering. What I want is the conceptual mechanics, not the slogan.

Specifically: how is the engineering analogy supposed to work when engineering norms are relative to a goal that's just assumed (the bridge should stand, the drug should cure), while in morality the goal itself is what's in dispute? Does Quine have a way out of that, or does he just accept that moral ends are given by our psychology and social training and that all the normativity left is instrumental? And how does his holism and indeterminacy of translation interact with this — if moral terms are learned as socially conditioned verbal dispositions, whats left of the idea that two people disagreeing about abortion are actually disagreeing about anything, rather than just making different noises with different practical upshots?

Please separate what's settled in the secondary literature from what's genuinely contested, and don't smooth over the places where Quine is ambiguous or where people who know him well disagree about what he was entitled to.
