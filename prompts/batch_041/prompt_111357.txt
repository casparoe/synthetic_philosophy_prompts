I've been reading alot of philosophy on the side for a couple years (no classes, just podcasts and encyclopedia articles), and I wrote up a short essay on the whole "knowledge depends on stakes" thing. Can you give me comments? I'd like to know (a) whether I'm describing the two camps correctly, (b) whether the distinction I draw near the end is actually doing any work or if I've just relabeled something, and (c) what the single strongest objection to my whole line is. Be harsh if you want, I'd rather find out now then later. Please write in American English.

Here's the essay:

Everyone's heard some version of the bank case: you "know" the bank is open on Saturday when nothing much rides on it, but when missing the deposit would be a disaster you suddenly "don't know" anymore, even though your evidence hasn't changed. Contextualists say the word "knows" itself shifts meaning with the conversational context. The pragmatic-encroachment crowd says no, the word means the same thing, it's just that what you actually know really does depend on what's at stake for you. I think both are onto something, but I think they're both lumping two different things under "stakes," and once you pull them apart the whole debate looks different.

The two things are: (1) how bad is it if I'm wrong, and (2) how hard is it to undo if I'm wrong. In software these come apart all the time. A database migration on staging: I'll say I know it's fine, and nobody objects, because if it isn't fine I restore from a snapshot in ten minutes. Same migration on prod, same evidence, same confidence, and I'll say I don't know that it's fine, and I mean it, it's not just a hedge. What changed wasn't really the badness (staging being wrong is annoying, prod being wrong is bad, sure, but it's not ten times worse) — what changed is that prod is a one-way door. So my hypothesis: the intuitive pull of pragmatic encroachment is driven mostly by reversibility, not by cost. Contextualists can say fine, whatever, reversibility just feeds into the context. But if it's reversibility doing the work, then "stakes" is the wrong word for it, and the people who insist something non-evidential is in there are right — it's just options, not stakes.

The obvious objection to me: you can't have a one-way door without some cost sitting behind it. Reversibility only matters because getting it wrong hurts. So maybe I'm just redescribing cost with extra steps. I don't have a good reply yet, except that I think you can hold the cost fixed and vary the reversibility, and intuitions about whether you "know" still move.

Second thing I'm unsure about: is this even a debate about knowledge, or a debate about when it's OK to say "I know"? Those come apart for me. I'll happily say "I know it's fine" about staging while privately admitting I'm not certain, and I don't think I'm lying, so maybe I'm a contextualist about the word but not about the mental state. Not sure that's even coherent.

So: comments on accuracy, on whether the reversibility thing is real or a redescription, and on what I'm missing?
