I'm in a reading group that's half philosophy grad students and half people who actually write historical fiction and screenplays (one of them has a biopic in development, which is part of why this got heated), and we keep re-fighting the same fight about dramatizing real dead people -- inventing scenes they never lived, putting dialogue in their mouths, merging two real people into one character, that kind of thing.

The writers' line is: it's a dramatization, nobody signs up to be a character, and the dead can't be embarrassed because there's no one left to be embarrassed. The philosophers' line is: you dont get to fabricate a real person's life and call it art, the dead still have a stake in how they're remembered, and some of them spent their last years trying to control exactly this.

We've been going at it for months and nobody has budged, and it feels like the two sides aren't even answering the same question. My suspicion -- tell me if its wrong -- is that the real disagreement is buried one level down, and is partly metaphysical (can the dead be harmed at all, when there's no subject left to bear the harm?) and partly a values question about whose interests the story is allowed to trade away. There's a version of this where the whole fight is really about harm versus wronging: the philosophers keep reaching for 'wronged' and the writers keep answering about 'harmed', and those two can come apart -- you can wrong someone without harming them, and harm someone without wronging them. If that's whats happening, then alot of the shouting is noise.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute about the words 'harm' and 'respect', a genuinely substantive disagreement, two groups answering different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Take the writers' "no subject, no harm" claim seriously and work out what actually follows for the other side if it's true. Then tell me what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a test case, a reframing -- or whether I should just accept that this one is permanent.

Two sub-cases I want you to hold onto, because they pull in opposite directions: (a) the biopic invents something that makes the person look worse than they were, and (b) it invents something that makes them look better, or that they themselves would obviously have wanted. And then the case where the borrowed person has no surviving family or community to complain, versus the case where a whole living group treats the misrepresentation as an insult to them. Does the shape of the disagreement change across those, or is it the same disagreement wearing different clothes?
