I'm a third-year student taking a course on epistemic logic and its applications, and I've run into a mismatch between two things I've been taught that I can't resolve on my own. I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of knowledge that one of my course's practices presupposes, and then tell me whether that theory is coherent.

The first half of the course was standard possible-worlds epistemology. We modeled knowledge as truth in all accessible worlds, and we spent time on the fact that these models make every agent logically omniscient: an agent knows every logical consequence of what it knows, instantly and at no cost. This was presented as a defect of the framework, or at best as an idealization we tolerate, and we looked at a few patches for it. The puzzles we used, like the muddy children and the coordinated attack, get all their difficulty from information flow precisely because nobody in them ever fails to draw an inference.

The second half of the course is about formal verification of security protocols, and there the practice is different in a way I find striking. The standard move is to reason about a symbolic adversary, usually called the Dolev-Yao adversary after the paper that introduced it. This adversary is deliberately given enormous inferential power and deliberately denied computational power. From the messages it has overheard, it can derive every consequence: it can pair and unpair, decrypt with any key it holds, re-encrypt under keys it knows, apply functions, and combine the results in any way it likes, with no limit on time or effort. But it cannot invert a hash, guess a key, or exploit anything about the bit strings underneath. So it knows everything that follows from what it has seen, and it can compute almost nothing. The field also says, without apparent embarrassment, that a principal knows a key when it can use that key, which sounds like a statement about capability rather than about closure.

What I want from you is the implicit epistemology of this practice. Specifically:

1. Is the deductive closure of the adversary a descriptive claim about knowledge, an idealization like a frictionless plane, or a normative claim about what a rational inquirer ought to conclude from what it has? Which of these readings does the surrounding practice actually commit to, and which does it merely fit with?

2. Why the asymmetry? Inference is idealized away; computation is the very thing the model refuses to idealize. Is there a principled distinction between inferring and computing that justifies that line, or is the line drawn simply because inference is not what the field is studying while computation is? If it is the latter, is the practice committed to any view about knowledge at all, or is it silent?

3. What does the practice rule out? It seems to exclude any account on which inference takes time, can fail, is defeasible, requires awareness of the concepts involved, or requires a subject that believes something. It also seems to exclude the possibility that the adversary's epistemic state could be inconsistent or incomplete in a way that matters.

4. Is the same word being used in two incompatible senses within the practice, given that knowing a key is glossed as being able to compute with it, while knowing a message is glossed as having everything derivable from it? Does that internal tension matter for the soundness of the idealization, or is it just a terminological convenience?

5. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Which rival theories of knowledge would license exactly this modeling choice? In particular, could a defender say the model is not about knowledge at all but about an information state, or about what an omniscient observer could derive from the transcript, in which case the epistemic vocabulary is a misnomer rather than a commitment? If so, what is lost and what is gained by calling it knowledge?

6. If we replaced the idealization with a resource-bounded one, what would have to change? I'm interested in what happens to the definitions of secrecy and authentication, to what a security proof is a proof of, and to the relation between symbolic and computational models. Would the guarantees become weaker, or would they change shape rather than strength?

I'd like the answer to reconstruct the implicit theory rather than survey the literature, to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, to say where several theories would do the same work, and to end with a verdict on whether the practice is coherent as it stands. I'd also like to know whether the objection to logical omniscience in the first half of the course and the tolerance of it in the second half are in genuine conflict, or whether they are objections to different things, so that someone who accepts the security practice can still reject omniscient models of belief.
