I am a junior at a high school with a fairly serious philosophy club, and our faculty advisor has asked me to prepare the moderator's questions for the keynote guest at our spring symposium. She is a philosopher who has spent her career arguing, in the spirit of David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, that there is no duty to remember: that collective memory has no intrinsic moral value, and that communities are frequently better off, both morally and politically, letting past atrocities recede. She is careful to insist that she is not recommending oblivion and that her case is pragmatic rather than absolutist, but the conclusion she draws is that commemorative practices, truth commissions, and memory-based curricula must justify themselves entirely by their downstream effects. Our club has now spent two meetings arguing about her and we keep going in circles, largely because the standard objections seem to come from the other side of the debate — Avishai Margalit's Ethics of Memory, with its distinction between thick relations, the communities of memory whose members owe one another remembrance, and thin relations, the merely moral community of all human beings — and I do not know how to turn that reading into questions that actually corner her rather than letting her restate her thesis.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and include at least one question on each of the following: (a) whether her argument depends on a sharp distinction between history and memory, where memory is understood as the present-serving use of the past, and what becomes of her case if that distinction is blurred; (b) whether she accepts the thick/thin distinction and then denies that even thick relations generate a duty to remember, or rejects the distinction outright; (c) the transitional-justice cases — the Spanish pacto del olvido, the South African amnesty, the Chilean settlement — and how she separates a society that chooses to stop relitigating its past from a society whose perpetrators have successfully imposed silence on their victims; (d) whether her thesis concerns forgetting as a psychological event or only public commemoration, and what she says to the objection that forgetting is not something one can decide to do, so that a policy of forgetting is really a policy of suppressing other people's memory; (e) whether forgetting and forgiving can be compatible, given that forgiveness appears to require that the wrong not simply be erased; and (f) whether anything is owed to the dead that is not owed to the living, since that seems to be the place where her consequentialism is asked to do the most work. I would also like a closing sequence of three questions that makes her standard deflection — that she is only denying memory's intrinsic value, not praising oblivion — progressively harder to use, ending with a question about what, on her view, would make a particular act of remembrance wrong when it is wrong. For every question, please indicate what she is likely to concede and what she is likely to dispute, so that I can press her rather than simply hand her the floor.
