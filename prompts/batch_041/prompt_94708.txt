I've come around to the view that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (often called his utilitarian theorem) settles the basic form of social evaluation: once individual and social preferences both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society endorses what every individual unanimously prefers, social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. On this view the utilitarian structure of social evaluation is not a moral premise we choose but a constraint that rationality and respect for individual preferences impose on us. Tell me what would count against it. Press on the soft spots without assuming they are fatal: the weights in the sum are left free, and von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are fixed only up to positive affine transformation, so does the theorem constrain social evaluation at all, or is it equally hospitable to prioritarianism and to many views its critics would call non-utilitarian? Does the result lean on an assumption about the form of social preference — that it is a function of individual utilities — such that the theorem is really a conditional about representation rather than an argument for utilitarianism? What becomes of it when people disagree in their beliefs rather than merely in their tastes, when apparent agreement is manufactured by opposed beliefs canceling opposed tastes, and when the ex ante and ex post standpoints pull apart? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, separate the ones the theorem's defenders would themselves concede from the ones they would dismiss as objections to a moral gloss rather than to the theorem, and say honestly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a vice here or simply what a theorem of this kind looks like. I would be most moved by a case in which the premises hold and the conclusion is plainly unacceptable, or by a demonstration that the conclusion is empty. Answer in American English.
