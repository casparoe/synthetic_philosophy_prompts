I'm writing a seminar paper on Borges's Funes the Memorious and I want to read it through the frame problem -- not the narrow AI version (how do you write down that everything an action doesn't change stays unchanged) but the philosophical one: any finite mind has to work out which of its beliefs a new bit of information bears on and which it can safely ignore, and there's no general procedure for that. Funes looks like the limit case of failing it. He remembers everything, can barely sleep because he can't stop seeing the moulding on the houses around him, can't do general ideas -- my hunch is the storys moral is that thinking just is generalizing, leaving differences out -- and his system of enumeration gives every number its own name, which the narrator points out is the opposite of a system at all. So is this a dramatization of the frame problem, or of some adjacent failure (of abstraction, of induction) that the frame problem literature has quietly annexed? And which side does the story land on: that the need to ignore is a limitation you'd engineer away with more memory and better search, or that the ignoring is what thought is? Come down on one side with reasons instead of laying out the options, and tell me straight whether the fact that Funes's world doesn't change and he isn't updating anything kills the analogy or sharpens it. Also don't smooth over the narrator's half-admiration for him, and say what the story sees that the technical debate can't, if anything.
