I've been going back and forth with a friend about insects for like two years and neither of us has moved an inch, and I want you to help me figure out what we're actually disagreeing about, because I've started to think it isn't insects.

Her side goes: there are supposedly something like ten quintillion insects alive at any moment (I might be off by an order of magnitude, the point stands). Nobody knows if any of them feel anything. But say the odds are one in a thousand, or one in ten thousand. Times the tiny odds by the stupidly large number and you get a mountain of suffering bigger than every human problem put together. So eating, gardening, spraying, leaving the porch light on all night — all of it has to be weighed against numbers that size. Her line is that if you won't do the arithmetic you're just flinching, and flinching isn't an argument.

My side goes: those odds aren't a fact about insects, there more like a fact about how confused we are, and multiplying our confusion by ten quintillion doesn't produce a quantity, it produces a scary sounding number. If we genuinely don't know whether it's one in a thousand or one in a trillion, the calculation looks less like a result and more like a machine for laundering ignorance into confidence. I also notice I stop caring about the numbers as soon as someone asks me what I'm supposed to *do* about it, which she says is me changing the subject.

Here's my theory, and I want you to tell me if it's any good. I think the fight isn't about probabilities and isn't really about insects either. I think it's about whether being sentient is a yes/no switch or a matter of degree. If it's a switch, then "a 0.1% chance that this ant is sentient" is a weird thing to say — it either is or it isn't, and the percentage is just our ignorance — and then multiplying ignorance by a headcount looks like a category mistake. But if it's a degree, then a little bit of sentience times a huge number of insects is a perfectly sensible sum, and I'm the one being irrational by refusing it. Is that the real fault line? Or is it something else — different standards for what counts as evidence (does she need proof of a capacity, or is the bare possibility enough), different gut feelings about whether a small chance of something enormous can outweigh a near-certain something small, or just two people answering different questions under one heading, hers being "what does the math say" and mine being "what can I actually change"?

And if you can, tell me what would count as progress here. Would some lab finding about insect brains settle it, or would we just relitigate whether that finding was the right thing to look at? Is there an argument that shows the whole expected-value move is broken when the stakes are huge and the odds are tiny, or does that just pick a side? Would the honest answer be that the question needs to be replaced with a better one? I've noticed the same shape of stall shows up in arguments about future generations and about microscopic chances of enormous disasters, so if you think this is the same disagreement wearing different clothes, say so and say why — and if you think no progress should be expected, I'd rather hear that than a fake way out.
