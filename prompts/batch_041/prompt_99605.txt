Lately I have been reading everything I can find about split-brain patients, because my sister is scheduled to undergo a corpus callosotomy for epilepsy that medication no longer controls, and I have realized that I cannot say clearly what I think the operation would be doing to her: creating a second mind, revealing one that was already there, or merely degrading something that admits of degrees. I would like a decision tree that takes me from the raw patterns in the commissurotomy cases to a named position on the unity of consciousness.

Make each internal node a question I could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and phrase it over some concrete feature of the evidence: whether the hemispheres can be shown to harbor conflicting intentions or only disconnected access; whether the patient's own reports should be taken at face value; whether unity is one property or a conjunction of several kinds, such as the binding of features into objects, a shared spatial field, a common temporal window, a single subject of experience, and first-person access to one's own states; and whether disunity is produced by the operation or merely exposed by it. Let the leaves be positions, named where a standard name exists and otherwise described in a sentence. Include at least these: one subject with two streams of access; two subjects; one subject whose unity is diminished in degree rather than kind; a partial-unity or spectrum view; one mind that is not one agent, so that unity of consciousness and unity of agency come apart; and the deflationary view that unity names no deep fact about minds. Flag any leaves the tree cannot separate, and mark the answer-combinations no one occupies, with a sentence on whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep it to eight or ten leaves.
