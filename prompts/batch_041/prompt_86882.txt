Our studio is building a generative tool that proposes new letterforms within a given style, and our bottleneck is a judgment call: when the tool emits a shape, what decides whether it is still an 'a'? My colleagues have put forward the following candidate criteria for letter identity, and we need to rank them before we encode any of them as hard constraints:

- Topological: the glyph must enclose exactly one counter, as nearly all printed 'a's do.
- Genealogical: the glyph must descend by an unbroken chain of handwriting and type from the historical ancestor of our 'a'.
- Ductus-based: the glyph must be writable in the same number and order of strokes as the 'a' in the model hand.
- Differential: the glyph's identity is fixed only by its distinctness from the other letters of the same alphabet.
- Ecological: the glyph is an 'a' just in case readers of the target script, encountering it in real words, do not stumble.
- Constructive: the glyph must be derivable from the style's own generative grammar of strokes and transformations.

Please rank these six from most to least central to letter identity, assign each a weight between 0 and 1 (weights summing to 1) reflecting how much of the burden it should bear in our system, and say in a sentence or two why each lands where it does. Then tell me which single criterion we could drop with the least damage, which one we could not drop at all, and which pair stands in the sharpest tension. I care less about what is historically true of the letter than about what our system should treat as constitutive of it, so make the trade-offs between the criteria explicit. Answer in American English.
