I'm a software engineer who ended up managing a platform team at a mid-size company, and I keep ending up in the same meeting. We've always got two or three long-running things on the books — a payments rewrite we kicked off about fourteen months ago, a homegrown search service that's been limping along, an internal tool that three engineers keep polishing — and every quarter we sit down to decide whether to keep funding them, and somebody says we can't quit now, look how much we've already put into it. The stakes aren't small: each of these is a few hundred thousand dollars a year and a couple of people's jobs, and I'm the one who has to walk into the VP's office and either defend the spend or kill the thing. I've read enough pop economics to know the standard line, that past spending is water under the bridge and you should only compare what it costs from here against what you get from here, but the blanket version of that has always bugged me. There seem to be real cases where the fact that you already spent the money does matter — it bought you skills, or a reputation for finishing what you start, or an option you'd lose by walking away — and I definately don't think everyone on my team is wrong every time they say something that sounds like the fallacy. I've got no formal philosophy or econ background, so I need this spelled out and turned into something we can actually run.

So here's what I want: a keep-going-or-kill procedure for in-flight projects. The questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a good answer to each, the point where the procedure just says kill and we stop, and the point where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because it's turned into a values question — like how much we owe the people who bet their careers on this — rather than a factual one. Be honest in the writeup: I want the version where sunk costs really are irrelevant stated as a special case with its conditions attached (something about who's doing the deciding, whether the spending bought anything durable, whether it changed what anyone else will do, whether it tied our own hands), not as the whole story, and I want the cases where honoring the past spending is actually defensible laid out next to the ones where it's just the fallacy in a costume. Also flag where the procedure will misfire and where a clever person could game it to keep their pet project alive, because I know exactly who on my team will try. Keep it to something two tech leads can run through in about an hour at a whiteboard, since we don't have an economist on staff and I'm not going to get one.

Then run it end to end on three cases and show your work, including where it gives an answer I don't like. First, the payments rewrite: the old system still works for now, but we just found out it cant meet a new compliance requirement, so some of those fourteen months were genuinely necessary and some of it was gold-plating we did because it was fun. Second, something I keep chewing on with a friend: they're four years into a long training program and want out, and everyone tells them that's the sunk cost fallacy talking, but the same four years have made them better at this than they'd be at anything else they could switch into now, and I honestly don't know if the standard line gets that one right. Third, the internal tool that three engineers have been polishing for a year and that about six people use; I'm pretty sure the answer is going to be kill it, and I want you to not soften that. Last thing: tell me what the procedure can't do. Specifically, is there a version of this where the honest answer is that a group of people who all invested together will basically never be able to run it on themselves, and if so, what do you do about that?
