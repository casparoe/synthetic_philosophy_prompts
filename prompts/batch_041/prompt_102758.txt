You are a philosopher of value theory and decision theory with expertise in population axiology, the aggregation of small goods, and the structure of the betterness relation. Think carefully before answering, and begin by laying out for yourself the considerations you take to be in play; then give me a structured answer.

Consider the following case, a familiar one in the literature. One person is tortured for fifty years. Separately, some finite number n of people each receive a single dust speck in the eye — an annoyance so trivial that each person forgets it within a minute. For any n, however large, we can ask which outcome is worse.

Define the Non-Archimedean thesis (NA) as follows: for every finite n, the torture is worse than the n dust specks. This is the claim that the disvalue of severe suffering is lexically prior to — of a different order than — any quantity of trivial suffering, in the way that an Archimedean value ordering denies. Define the Archimedean thesis (A) as the negation: there is some finite n for which the n dust specks are worse than the torture. Assume the two outcomes are comparable — neither is on a par with the other — and that worse than has a determinate answer here; if you think those assumptions are the real problem, say so.

My own credence in NA is about 0.7; a friend's is about 0.15. Give me your own credence, but the number matters less than the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-questions you think it actually turns on — for instance, whether the correct theory aggregates welfare across persons at all (versus being person-affecting or anti-aggregation), whether the value ordering is complete and comparable, whether a lexical or threshold structure is independently motivated, how much weight the intuition that no pile of trivial annoyances adds up to torture should get given its likely origin in scope neglect or in the small-n setting where it is plainly correct, whether the comparison is even well-defined for an n so large that no one could grasp the outcome, and whatever else you think belongs. Assign each sub-question a rough probability, give one line of justification for each, show how they combine into your number, and identify the term your number is most sensitive to.
