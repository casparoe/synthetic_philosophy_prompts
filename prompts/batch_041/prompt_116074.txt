I chair the Clinical Systems Accountability Committee of a twelve-hospital nonprofit system, and I need a written opinion in my name to circulate to the board before its November meeting.

The case. Since 2022 our hospitals have used a vendor-supplied model, embedded in the electronic record, that at two in the morning flags inpatients as stable enough for early discharge and displays a confidence percentage. The workflow asks the covering clinician to accept or override: accepting is a single click, overriding requires a free-text justification that a quality officer reviews monthly, and each clinician's override rate appears on her dashboard and in her annual review. Two months ago a 61-year-old patient with a rare hematologic condition was flagged at 0.94 confidence, discharged at nine the next morning after the covering nurse had seven minutes with the chart, readmitted thirty-six hours later in septic shock, and died. Four things have surfaced since. The model was trained on 2015-2019 data from our six wealthiest-county hospitals and validated only there, so its confidences are calibrated in a population that does not include this patient. The nurse's override rate was 2.1 percent, the lowest on her unit, and she had been commended for it; three colleagues have since said they stopped overriding because their justifications were treated as evidence of poor judgment. The vendor's contract caps its liability at fees paid and forbids us to publish performance data. And our general counsel reads the state's serious-adverse-event rule as requiring a report within seventy-two hours, which the vendor says would breach the contract. The nurses' union has asked to speak at the meeting.

What we must decide. The committee will recommend one rule to the board: (1) keep the present arrangement, in which accountability is settled case by case after the fact; (2) the proposal now on the table, under which every automated recommendation must have a designated responsible clinician, but a clinician whose displayed confidence was at least 0.90 and whose override rate for the preceding quarter fell below her unit's median is presumed not to bear individual responsibility, with the institution absorbing the loss and reporting the event; (3) strict individual accountability, with the accepting clinician presumptively responsible unless she can show that the recommendation was unsupported by the data actually before her; or (4) a no-fault institutional fund that compensates patients without assigning fault to any individual, paired with mandatory public reporting. You may recommend a variant of one of these, provided you say precisely what it changes.

What I need from you. A memo of roughly 1,200 words that recommends one course with a date by which the board should adopt it, states what should happen to this nurse and to the patient's family, says what we report and to whom, and names the principle we would be committing ourselves to along with the conditions under which we should revisit it. Three matters I want handled rather than gestured at. First, the strongest argument for whichever option you reject, put in a form its supporters would recognize as their own. Second, whether the proposal's reliance on a clinician's aggregate record rather than on what she did that morning can be defended at all, given that her record was itself produced by our dashboard and our review practices; if you think the clause is defensible, say what would have to be true of the dashboard for it to be. Third, whether the responsibility-gap framing that two members have adopted is doing any real work or is a redescription of an ordinary allocation problem, and whether capping the vendor's legal liability should have any bearing on where we say the moral weight lies.
