You are grading a take-home examination for an advanced undergraduate course on historical method. The question and a student's answer are below. I need a grade and a point-by-point justification.

Question: A historian proposes to write an account of a nineteenth-century industrial strike using only the company's payroll ledgers, police arrest records, and contemporaneous newspaper reports, deliberately excluding oral histories and union pamphlets. The historian claims that this method is objective because it relies on hard documentary evidence and avoids partisan narratives. Drawing on Ranke, Carr, and Hayden White, assess whether this claim to objectivity is defensible. In your answer, distinguish (a) the selection of sources, (b) the interpretation of evidence, and (c) the narrative form of the resulting history.

Student answer:
Ranke's ideal was to show the past as it actually was, and this requires the historian to give primary documents priority over later interpretations. The historian's proposal is Rankean insofar as it relies on contemporary records rather than retrospective accounts. Carr, however, argued that facts do not speak for themselves; they become historical facts only when a historian selects them for a purpose. So the proposal cannot be objective in the naive sense, because the choice to exclude union pamphlets already reflects a present-day judgment about what counts as evidence. White goes further: he holds that historians necessarily impose a literary plot on the past, and therefore no historical account can be true or false. Carr and White thus agree that objectivity is impossible. Still, the historian is right that contemporary records can correct romanticized accounts, so the account may be useful even if not objective.
