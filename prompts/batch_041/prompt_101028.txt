So I've been on a Marx kick lately — reread the 1844 Manuscripts, most of the first volume of Capital, and that "Results" draft that gets shoved in as an appendix — and I want to try something. Take the whole platform/gig labor scene and run it through Marx on exploitation and alienation. Not the cheap version where you just intone "capitalism exploits the workers." I want the actual mapping, and then where it breaks.

The episode I have in mind: Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, the fight over whether the drivers are employees or independent contractors — the Dynamex ruling, California's AB5, and then Prop 22 in 2020, where the companies spent over two hundred million bucks to write themselves an exemption (it passed with around 59%). Use that as the case.

Stuff I actually want you to deal with, because this is where the standard story gets weird:

- The drivers own the means of production. In Marx that's basically the whole line between a proletarian and a petty bourgeois — the worker is the person seperated from the means of production, with nothing to sell but their labor-power. But a ride-hail driver owns the car. By the letter of the theory these people shouldn't be proletarians at all, and yet it definately looks like exploitation and alienation. Does Marx have the machinery to explain why, or does the framework just fail here?

- Related: is the platform's cut even exploitation in the technical sense — surplus value skimmed off production — or is it rent? The platform doesn't make the ride; it owns the match, the market, the reputation system. I've seen people try to revive Marx's theory of rent (Capital vol. 3) for exactly this, and I can't tell if that's a rescue or a bailout. Also: the formal-vs-real-subsumption thing — formal subsumption being capital taking over a labor process it found lying around and pumping surplus out of it, real subsumption being capital remaking the process itself (machinery, scientific management, the works). Which one is the dispatch algorithm?

- Alienation. The four-part 1844 deal: from the product, from the labor process, from species-being, from other workers. Does gig work fit, or does it break in an interesting way? The driver picks her hours, owns her tools, and is managed by a rating system. Is that less alienated or more? Marx's core complaint was loss of control over the labor process; here the control is real but it's been outsourced to an app.

Then flip it around. What does this episode show about Marx? Does the gig economy confirm him — the extraction just got sneakier — or does it expose that his picture was welded to a particular industrial moment (the factory, the wage, the boss), so he can't quite see a mode of extraction that runs through owning the marketplace instead of the workplace? Be honest about which parts of the framework earn their keep here and which are just doing vibes.

No cites needed. Just the analysis, and a clear-eyed call on where the model breaks.
