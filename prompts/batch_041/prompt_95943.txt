I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on democratic theory, 18 students, and the session is on the idea that democratic outcomes express "the will of the people." Design a simulated assembly in which they first elect a governing platform and then vote the same issues one at a time, with the district preference sheets and voting rules arranged so that the platform winning the election is one a majority of participants rejects on every issue it contains — or, if you prefer, so that a majority ends up on the losing side of a majority of the issue votes. The idea should land through the rules, not through anything I say up front: they should run into the result, feel the pull of wanting to call one side the real voice of the people, and only then have it named. Give me the preference sheets and how to assign them, the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, a second round in which one participant is quietly allowed to manipulate the order in which issues are decided, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the underlying problem and separates the several things "the will of the people" might mean — the winning platform, the majority position on each issue, the output of a fair procedure. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: that I have engineered a case real electorates of this size would rarely produce, and that the deeper question of whether any collective will exists at all has to be raised by me rather than staged. Don't build anything on named paradoxes or citations; I want them to hit the structure before they hear a label for it.
