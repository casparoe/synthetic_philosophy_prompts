I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I've now worked at three companies that all do the same strange thing around incidents, and I cant work out what they actually believe about blame.

When we take prod down, we write a postmortem. The template forbids names in the timeline and forbids the phrase 'should have' — everything is a 'contributing factor'. Officially it's blameless, and the line is that we're fixing the system, not the person. But the same company runs a quarterly reliability review where causing more than a couple of sev-1s lands on your perf review, and it is not a friendly conversation. Meanwhile the security team runs phishing simulations and publishes a click-rate leaderboard by team, which is about as blameless as a dunk tank.

The part I really can't square: if I fat-finger a config at 3am in the middle of a firefight, it's basically a shrug, everyone's done it. If I make the identical mistake on a quiet Tuesday afternoon, calmly, it's treated as sloppiness. Same blast radius, oppisite reaction. And there's one rule I do like: if I break something and page it in myself right away, I'm more or less immune no matter how bad it was; if someone else finds it first, it's a much bigger deal. So sometimes fault is a count of outages, sometimes it's about whether you owned up, and sometimes it's officially nothing at all.

What I want out of this: what theory of responsibility does the bundle actually presuppose? Is 'blameless' here a claim that nobody was responsible — that the causes were all systemic — or just a claim that blaming is counterproductive? Those seem like really different claims and I think we say the first while quietly practicing the second. Which parts of the process strictly need some particular view of when a person is blameworthy, and which parts would survive under almost any view? Where is this just three incompatible ideas taped together, such that I could predict the next dumb argument we're going to have about it? And if you swapped the underlying view — say, if blame really tracks the state of mind behind the mistake rather than the damage done, or if nothing but the system ever matters — what specifically would have to change in our postmortem template, our reliability review, and the phishing leaderboard?
