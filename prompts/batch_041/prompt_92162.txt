I'm in a small reading group that has spent several sessions circling what people call the paradox of fiction — the fact that we seem to feel genuine sadness, fear, or grief for characters we know perfectly well do not exist. One member of the group, whose judgment I usually trust, has proposed a resolution that I can't decide whether to accept, and I'd like your honest credence that it is correct, decomposed rather than merely asserted.

Her proposal is this. The paradox only looks like a paradox, she says, if we assume that an emotion is fitting only when it is directed at something that exists and rests on beliefs that are true. Drop that assumption. On her view an emotion can be fitting relative to a presupposed world: it is true within the story that the character suffered and lost everything, and so sadness is the appropriate response to the story taken as a story. Emotions toward fiction are therefore genuine emotions with real fittingness conditions, just indexed to a stipulated world rather than to the actual one. She then goes further, and this is the part I doubt. She claims the same holds for our emotions toward real people: when I grieve for a friend, I am responding not to the friend as she is in herself but to the version of her that my partial information has constructed — a world of my own making that is no less stipulated than an author's. On this view there is no deep difference between grief for a character and grief for a real friend; only the source of the world differs.

Please give me one number for the credence that the first part is right — that the paradox is genuinely dissolved by relativizing fittingness to a presupposed world — and a separate number for the second, that the generalization to real emotions is sound. Then decompose each: how much rides on whether emotion requires belief at all; on whether fitting-relative-to-a-world is a coherent notion or merely a relabeling of the problem; on whether anything important is lost once emotion is detached from existence-beliefs, for instance its connection to action or the sense in which we can be mistaken about what we feel; and on whether the distinction between a stipulated world and a constructed one can bear the weight she puts on it. Tell me which single sub-question your two numbers are most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move them most.
