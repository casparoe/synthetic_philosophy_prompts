Our reading group has now spent four sessions on the Ellsberg setup — two urns, one with a known even split of red and black balls, one whose proportion is unknown, matched bets on red from each urn and matched bets on black from each — and we have reached the point where everyone understands everyone else's argument and no one's position has moved. The question that organizes the group is not whether the familiar pattern of choices occurs; it plainly does, and we can reproduce it at the table. The question is whether that pattern is rational.

Two camps have formed. Camp A holds that a subjective probability is simply a degree of belief, and that a degree of belief is whatever it is: if I decline to bet on the unknown urn at odds I accept on the known urn, that does not show that I lack a credence, only that my credence is a complicated psychological fact rather than a gap in the world. On this view, treating unknown probability as a distinct state of affairs is a category mistake, and the sure-thing principle is not an optional extra but the expression of a truism — a choice better in each of two exhaustive eventualities is better. If ambiguity aversion is rational at all, it must be re-described as a higher-order risk attitude: a second-order distribution over first-order probabilities which, if its mean is one half, gives the two urns the same expected value and leaves any residual preference to be explained by the agent's treatment of the two-stage structure rather than by anything about the urns.

Camp B holds that representing the agent with a single additive probability forces a determinate credence where there is none. The right object is a set of probabilities, or a capacity; the preference for the known urn is a preference over the spread of that set, not over any of its members. On this view the sure-thing principle is precisely the axiom that begs the question, and the pattern of choices is not an error but the manifestation of an attitude toward uncertainty as such — an attitude no bet can expose as inconsistent, because it ranks acts, and acts need not be ranked by rankings of outcome probabilities.

I have a hypothesis about what is happening and I would like it tested rather than endorsed. The hypothesis is that the two camps are not disagreeing about the rationality of the choices but about what the words probability and preference are being used to mean, and that each camp's argument is valid under its own reading. Camp A reads a probability as a summary of the agent's betting dispositions, on which reading an agent who refuses the symmetric bets merely has non-additive dispositions, and calling those dispositions irrational is a further step that still needs an argument. Camp B reads a probability as an estimate of a mind-independent chance-like quantity, on which reading the absence of a determinate estimate is a fact about the world's determinacy rather than about the agent. If that is the diagnosis, the debate is largely verbal, and its persistence is explained by the fact that the word does real work in both readings and neither camp will surrender it.

I want three things.

First, is the hypothesis right, wrong, or half-right? Specifically, is there a residual substantive disagreement left once the verbal dispute is set aside — something the camps would still disagree about even after agreeing on a stipulation for the two words?

Second, test the diagnosis against a case we built to try to break it. Suppose the unknown urn's composition was itself fixed by a publicly known fair coin: heads, seventy red and thirty black; tails, thirty red and seventy black. Everyone now agrees the urns differ, and everyone agrees a bet on red from the second urn has a win probability of one half. Camp B still prefers the first urn; Camp A says the preference is now plainly a preference about variance — ordinary risk — and that Camp B has thereby conceded the reduction. Camp B replies that conceding this case is risk does not touch the general case, because in the general case there is no coin to point to and no second-order distribution to invoke except the one in dispute. Is this case decisive, a red herring, or evidence that the camps were never arguing about the same class of cases? Note also that many people who avoid the ambiguous urn for a gain are willing to take it when the same bets are framed as losses; tell me whether that asymmetry indicates a taste or a framing effect, and whether it bears on the diagnosis.

Third, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? I do not want a survey of models. I want to know which of the following would move the dispute, and why: a representation theorem forcing one side's reading of probability to be the only one supporting a given decision rule; an experiment showing that ambiguity attitudes are unstable enough to look like framing rather than taste; a demonstration that the ambiguity-averse pattern is exploitable by a sequence of bets each of which the agent accepts — and, if so, whether exploitability is a criterion of rationality or only a pragmatic defect; or an argument that the question is ill-posed because the same odds is not well defined across the two urns. If none of these would move it, say so and explain precisely what the impasse consists in.
