I'm a novelist, third book, and I've written myself into a corner I actually want to stay in. The new one is set in an invented country — I've got the geography and the dialect and the half-finished seaside hotel, what I don't have is the thing the whole plot turns on: my protagonist ends up one of seven commissioners on an amnesty body after a civil war, and she has to vote on individual petitions. Not in the abstract. Case by case, file by file, and I need the reader to believe the votes. Right now they read like whatever the chapter needs them to be, which is death for a book like this.

So here's what I'm asking. Build me a decision procedure she can actually run on a petition. I've read around the obvious stuff — the conditional amnesty in South Africa where you got immunity in exchange for full disclosure, the Argentine amnesties that got unpicked years later and put everyone back in court, the gacaca courts in Rwanda where confessing early bought you a lighter sentence — but what I take from all that is a pile of history, not something a person can sit down with at 11pm over a bad photocopy. What I want is the machinery underneath: what amnesty is actually being traded for, in what currency, and who can't be offered it at all. I know there's a real tension here, that a body like this is usually illegal under the international-law consensus against impunity for mass atrocities, so a procedure that pretends otherwise is fantasy. I'd rather have the honest version, where the illegality is one of the things the commissioner has to weigh, than a clean flowchart.

Things I need it to handle. Petitions from people who gave orders and petitions from people who were nineteen and holding a rifle because the alternative was being shot themselves — is that one procedure or two? The disclosure condition, and what counts as complete disclosure when the only person who knows where the bodies are is the man who put them there. Whether victims get to say anything, and what weight that carries. Whether the commission is really deciding anything, or ratifying a deal that was already made by the politicians who set it up — I think the book is partly about that and I don't want the procedure to hide it.

Then run it on three cases, in this much detail, and please include one where it comes out the way I don't want, where the procedure says yes to someone I find repellent or no to someone I'm rooting for. First: a mid-level commander who ordered the killing of a village, forty-odd people, but who is the last living person who knows where the second grave is, and whose testimony would also implicate two men who are now in the government. Second: the conscript, one killing, under threat, and he's been trying to talk about it for a decade. Third: something you pick that stress-tests the procedure, ideally a case where full disclosure is on offer but the crime is the sort that people will not forgive, and where the political situation means saying no could end the peace.

Be straight with me if the premise is confused — if there's no such thing as a non-arbitrary procedure here and the whole thing is a value judgment wearing a lab coat, say that plainly instead of handing me a tidy checklist, and tell me what I should have my character do instead. Same if you think I'm importing assumptions from the real cases that don't survive being moved into a fictional one. I'd rather find out now than after the copyedit.

Format it like this, with these section headings in this order:

1. **What the procedure is actually trading** — about 400 words on the underlying logic, including what the amnesty is exchanged for and who is disqualified in principle.
2. **The steps, in order** — the procedure itself, numbered, roughly 900 words. For each step, what a sufficient answer looks like and what the commissioner writes down.
3. **Where it stops and a person decides** — about 400 words on the questions the procedure can't settle, and what the commissioner is being asked to weigh when it hands over.
4. **Three petitions, run through it** — about 900 words, one subsection per case, showing the steps and the verdict, including the one I won't like.
5. **What this can't do** — about 250 words. No citations needed anywhere, I'm not writing an essay.
