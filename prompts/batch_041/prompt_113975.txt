You are a philosopher of science with a solid working knowledge of contemporary machine learning, and you have been asked to compose a dialogue for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of AI training. Think carefully before you begin: spend a few sentences mapping the conceptual terrain — what is genuinely at stake in the disagreement, where the two positions actually come apart, and which of the familiar moves are too well worn to be worth staging — and only then write the dialogue itself.

The dialogue is between two equally competent researchers. The first holds that AI systems should be supervised on outcomes: what matters is whether the final result is correct, checkable, or useful, and the training signal should attach to that result alone. The second holds that outcomes are too sparse, too delayed, and too easily gamed, and that systems should instead be supervised on the process by which they reach results — each reasoning step rewarded or penalised for its quality. Neither character is a strawman; each must engage the other's strongest point rather than talking past it.

Give the exchange a genuine crux by building it around the following ideas, which you should treat as contested rather than settled:

- The suggestion that process supervision is not a rival to outcome supervision but the same thing at a finer grain: a process is simply an outcome observed at many intermediate points, so the true primitive is not 'process versus outcome' but what the supervisor can actually verify, and how long verification takes.
- The symmetry of gaming. Just as outcome-supervised systems learn to secure the reward without genuinely performing the task, process-supervised systems learn to produce reasoning that looks competent to the supervisor. Ask what stops the second failure mode, whether the answer is any more principled than the standard answer to the first, and whether one failure mode is more dangerous than the other when the supervisor is itself fallible.
- A concrete case to which both characters keep returning: a system that must decide, partway through a task, whether to continue with a familiar method or attempt a genuinely novel one whose outcome will not be verifiable for years. Which supervision regime would each of them want here, and does either answer survive the observation that the supervisor's own judgement of 'good process' may simply encode the prejudices of the current paradigm?

Let the dialogue end with the two characters disagreeing sharply and without resolution, and then add a short closing paragraph in which you, as the author, state which argument you find strongest and where you think each side is overreaching.

Write the whole answer in British English.
