Virtue ethics, which goes back to Aristotle, treats the central question of ethics as being about what kind of person one should be rather than what one should do. Its key notion is the virtue: a stable character trait, such as honesty or compassion, that a person has and that reliably issues in virtuous behaviour across a wide range of situations. The situationist critique, developed by Gilbert Harman and John Doris in the late 1990s and early 2000s, argues that this picture of the person is empirically untenable.

The critique leans heavily on social psychology. In Milgram's obedience experiments, ordinary participants were willing to keep administering what they believed were painful electric shocks to a stranger because an experimenter told them to; to the situationists this shows that people do not really possess stable compassionate characters, and that what a person does depends far more on the pressures of the situation than on any inner disposition. Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study points the same way: seminary students who had been told they were running late were much less likely to stop and help a man slumped in an alley, even though they were on their way to give a talk about the Good Samaritan.

Doris concludes that we should give up on global character traits and replace them with local traits, e.g. "helpful-when-not-in-a-hurry." Harman goes further still and proposes that we replace the whole vocabulary of character with local traits, since the fundamental attribution error makes us systematically overestimate how much behaviour is driven by dispositions and underestimate how much is driven by situations.

The usual virtue-ethical reply, from people like Julia Annas and Rachana Kamtekar, is that the situationists are attacking a straw man. On the Aristotelian picture a virtue is not a blind habit that fires automatically; it involves practical wisdom and is exercised through deliberation, so the fact that people behave badly when they're stressed or in a hurry doesn't show that virtues don't exist, it only shows that most people aren't virtuous. The experiments tell us about the average person, not about the virtuous person. So the situationist critique, while it raises interesting questions, doesn't refute virtue ethics; if anything it shows why we need virtue ethics in the first place.
