I run a short module on the causal exclusion argument for a mixed group of philosophy and cognitive-neuroscience doctoral students. They arrive knowing the standard positions in the philosophy of mind at the level of a good undergraduate survey — functionalism, type and token identity, non-reductive physicalism, the general shape of the mind-body problem — but none of them has actually worked through the exclusion argument, and their default attitude is that the whole debate is a verbal dispute that will dissolve once everyone agrees on what a cause is. I do not want the argument explained to me; I want a plan for teaching it.

First, the order in which the components have to be introduced so that the conclusion arrives as something forced rather than merely announced. My own inventory of load-bearing pieces is the supervenience claim, the causal closure or completeness of the physical, the irreducibility of the mental, and the exclusion principle itself; tell me whether that is the right set, what has to be in place before each piece can do its work, and where resistance is likely to arise before the argument has even been properly grasped.

Second, the one example that should carry the entire module. Say what features it must have and what it must not have, whether the familiar neuron-firing case is adequate or a liability, and how the same example can be carried from the opening session to the close without going stale.

Third, the misconceptions I should expect, with the precise point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off. Assume at least these: that the argument shows mental events do not exist; that overdetermination is unobjectionable because we tolerate it elsewhere; that supervenience is itself a causal relation; that the closure premise presupposes determinism; that downward causation is a distinct solution rather than a restatement of the problem; and that a sound exclusion argument tells against physicalism as such rather than specifically against the non-reductive variety. Add any further misconceptions you think this particular audience will generate.

Fourth, what I should deliberately omit at this level and openly flag as omitted. Finally, one question I can put to the group at the end that would show whether they have understood why the non-reductive physicalist in particular is trapped, as opposed to merely being able to recite the steps.
