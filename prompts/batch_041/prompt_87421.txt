I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have spent two weeks losing an argument to a colleague who has some. Our team is three years and a substantial budget into a system that we now have reason to think will never pay for itself. My instinct is to keep going a while longer; his view is that my instinct is the sunk cost fallacy wearing a nicer suit. When I asked him to state his case properly instead of naming the fallacy at me, he wrote out the following, and I have not been able to find the error since.

(1) A sunk cost is a resource already spent that no present decision can recover or alter.
(2) A rational decision ranks the available options by the expected value of what will follow from them.
(3) What follows from an option lies wholly in that option's future, and a sunk cost lies wholly in its past.
(4) Therefore a sunk cost can never enter the comparison by which the options are ranked.
(5) Therefore any decision that is moved by a sunk cost is moved by something that cannot bear on it.
(6) Honoring sunk costs is always irrational; the only legitimate question in our meeting is which option has the better future.

Every step looks to me like a definition or a tautology, and yet the conclusion is larger than the disagreement we were actually having. Read at face value, (6) also tells me that a promise I made last month should carry no weight with me now, since my having made it is as unrecoverable as the money we have spent; that the ten years I put into learning my trade are no reason at all to stay in it; and, more generally, that any decision procedure which takes account of its own history is irrational by construction. I am prepared to be told that I am being sentimental about the promise and the ten years. I am not prepared to be told that history-sensitivity is itself the error, which is what (6) appears to say.

I would most like to keep (4). I am not trying to rescue the fallacy, and I think my colleague is right that the thought that we have come too far to stop is usually a bad argument. What I want is the diagnosis: which step should I give up, why did that step look as though it needed no defense until someone tried to give one, and what does giving it up cost me elsewhere? I do not need names or a reading list. I need to know where the argument breaks, and whether the answer leaves the fallacy intact or forces me to abandon something I would rather keep.
