I'm reporting on how the word "barbarism" gets deployed in contemporary arguments about human rights and cultural difference, and I've gone back to Montaigne's essay on the cannibals — routinely described as the founding document of cultural relativism, the moment when a European conceded that we have no standing to judge another society. The essay resists that description. Montaigne argues that we call a practice barbarous only because it is not our own; he plainly admires much of what he reports about the Brazilians he knew, one of whom lived in his household for years; and yet he writes of their killing and eating of captives, "I am not sorry that we notice the barbarous horror of such acts, but I am heartily sorry that, judging their faults rightly, we should be so blind to our own." He then ranks the two kinds of cruelty, holding that our manner of putting a still-feeling body to the rack and the fire is the worse of the two — which is itself a judgment, and a judgment implies a standard against which both practices can be measured. So in the same essay that supposedly teaches the impossibility of cross-cultural judgment, he judges, and he judges by a standard he treats as universal. Should I write this up as a straightforward inconsistency, or am I misunderstanding what the essay is actually claiming?
