Read Sophie's Choice — Styron's novel, or the film if you know that better — through the debate about incommensurable values and hard choices (Raz, Williams, Chang and co.). The standard move is that Sophie faces two incommensurable goods, so there's no right answer and all that's left is regret. I don't think thats right: her options aren't two goods, they're two evils arranged by someone else, and she isn't weighing anything, she's being tortured — so does calling it a hard choice in the philosophers' sense flatter the case, or does the theory need a case like this to see what it's actually for? Two things I'd like sorted: (1) whether the novel itself marks a difference between a choice that's hard because the values can't be ranked and one that's hard because both options have been made unbearable by a third party, and which of the two it treats as worse for the person; (2) what moral remainder looks like when the agent was coerced — is Sophie's guilt the ordinary kind, or is Styron showing that we go on holding someone responsible even when there was nothing else she could have done? Say too where the novel resists the reading, and whether Stingo's late, second-hand narration is making a point about whether such a choice can be told at all, or is just scaffolding. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble.
