I'm the pharmacist who chairs the antibiotic policy group at our district hospital -- about 90 beds, we revise the first-line choices maybe twice a year, and whatever we pick has to be signed off by the medical superintendent, so I can't just do whatever the loudest consultant wants. Lately I've been reading a bit of classical Indian epistemology on the side (a friend got me into it, mostly the Nyāya stuff about the pramāṇas -- the accepted ways you can actually come to know something: perception, inference, comparison/analogy, and testimony from a reliable speaker) and honestly it describes my job better than any of the EBM handbooks do. Because look at what I actually recieve. A visiting ID consultant tells me over tea that we should switch to drug X. Our own lab sends me a sensitivity table. A national guideline written for a completely different region says Y. A drug rep says Z is 'basically the same' as something we already use. And I have to weigh these against each other with no library and patchy internet.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a piece of incoming evidence, written so that a colleague with no philosophy at all could follow it. Roughly: first work out which kind of pramāṇa the evidence is claiming to be, then what has to be true for that kind to deliver knowledge rather than just a confident feeling -- for perception the contact and the condition of the instrument, for inference that the connection holds generally and no case breaks it, for analogy that the resemblance is the relevant kind, for testimony something about the speaker's competence and honesty and about whether the claim survives contact with the other three. Then the checks in order, where it stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person, and where it says 'this isn't a knowledge question at all, it's a values question' (cost, equity, who bears the risk). Include a step for the bit I found hardest: on this view a true belief isn't automatically knowledge, and being sure isn't the same as being entitled to be sure, so the procedure needs a 'what would confirm this' step and should say what to do when confirmation isn't available. And please be honest about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three of my actual cases and one I'll probably hate. (1) the consultant's tea-break recommendation; (2) our own lab's sensitivity data, which everyone here treats as the gold standard precisely because we generated it ourselves; (3) the rep's 'basically the same' claim; (4) the national guideline. Then tell me what the procedure can't do -- where I'm going to have to just decide and live with it. Keep it under a page and a half if you can, I need to hand it out at the comittee meeting on Thursday.
