I've been a graduate student in philosophy for a few years now, and one thing I've done to keep myself honest is keep a running document of my gut verdicts on trade-offs between pleasure and pain, long before I had any theory that could house them. I'm hoping you can look at the list and tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work — and where I've been inconsistent. Here's what I've written down. A dull two-out-of-ten ache for an afternoon versus a really good dessert: I'd take the dessert, no hesitation; a small pleasure clearly outweighs a small pain, and they feel like they're on the same scale. Ten thousand people each stubbing a toe versus one person spending a week with a badly sprained ankle: I'd rather the one person take the sprain and spare the ten thousand, which suggests that lots of small pains can add up to outweigh a bigger-but-still-modest one. But then: an hour of the worst torture I can imagine versus any number of people — a thousand, a billion — getting an hour of the most intense pleasure: I would not trade, and it doesn't feel like a close call or like a failure of imagination; the pleasure just doesn't seem to be the right kind of thing to weigh against that pain. And a minute of pain I'd call genuinely severe — worse than the sprain, but not torture — to spare a million people a bad migraine: I want to say no, that's not a permissible trade, even though by the logic of my sprain case I should be willing to aggregate the migraines until they outweigh the minute. Somewhere I seem to be drawing a line: below it, pleasures and pains are commensurable and add up; above it, no amount of the other side buys you anything.

Two further data points complicate this. First, a million people each with a mild pain that individually sits below my line — say a stubbed toe — still feels like something I can trade against other below-the-line goods, even though the total suffering is obviously enormous; so my line seems to be about how bad any one episode is, not about the total. Second, when I imagine a life containing a great deal of intense pleasure but also a single episode of line-crossing suffering, I find I don't think the pleasure justifies it, which suggests the priority holds inside a single life and not just across people. My tentative principle is something like: there is an intensity threshold such that pains below it are commensurable with pleasures and with one another on a single scale, and can be aggregated and traded off, while pains at or above it have lexical priority — they can't be outweighed by any amount of below-threshold pleasure or pain, and can only be traded against other above-threshold pains.

I'd like you to test that against all of the above, tell me which of my verdicts it fails to capture and which of those I should give up, and state the repaired principle (or a small set of competing candidates) precisely enough that I can see exactly which clause each case is forcing on me. In particular I'd like to know what it says about two cases I haven't listed: a choice between one line-crossing pain and a billion below-the-line ones, and a choice between a line-crossing pain and a slightly worse line-crossing pain — whether the second forces a second, higher threshold. And I should confess the obvious: I can't locate the line, and small moves in intensity near where I think it is don't flip my verdict, yet I insist the line is real rather than a useful fiction. Please tell me whether that's coherent, and whether the whole structure survives once you press on it.
