Copycat is a program Hofstadter built with his student Melanie Mitchell back in the 80s at Indiana, and its whole job is solving little letter puzzles like "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?" Hofstadter's big idea is that thinking just IS analogy-making, and analogy-making is a kind of perception — you "see" a situation one way or another, and the seeing is the hard part, not the reasoning that comes afterward. Copycat does the seeing with a bunch of little independent agents called codelets that all run at the same time and compete to build descriptions of the letters, and whichever description ends up winning is what decides the answer. Everything the program knows lives in a network of concepts he calls the slipnet, and the trick is that concepts there aren't rigid — the program can "slip" one, like seeing a "b" as "the successor of a" instead of "the second letter," and that slippage is what lets it find different analogies in the same puzzle. There's also a "temperature" knob that makes it give different answers on different runs, and that's where the creativity comes from. Basically the whole thing is a parallel terraced scan, meaning it tries out every possible description at once and just keeps whichever one fits best. And the point of the exercise is that AI people who build systems out of rigid hand-coded categories are wasting their time, since real thinking is fluid. Oh, and it learns — every time it solves a puzzle it adds new concepts to the slipnet, which is how it gets good at puzzles it wasn't built for. He ended by saying that since it only ever does letter puzzles and can't do anything else, Copycat is basically just a toy, which shows the whole approach is a dead end.
