So I've been in a long-running arguement with two friends about whether AI systems could have moral status, and it's gone completely stale — same moves every time, nobody budges. I'm wondering if you can tell me what kind of dissagreement this actually is, because I suspect it's not the one we think we're having.

Here's the setup. Friend A says: there's no evidence of anything like sentience, and the only evidence we could ever get is behavioural, which is exactly the thing a system trained to imitate us is worst at — so the rational default is to withhold status until something better comes along, and granting it wrongly isn't costless anyway (it gets cashed in by companies, it warps policy, it makes people treat a chatbot like a patient). Friend B says: you can't rule out an inner life from the outside, that's just the old problem of other minds, the mechanisms are opaque even to the people building them, and the stakes are wildly asymmetric — a small chance of a huge amount of suffering should dominate, so uncertainty is a reason to extend consideration, not withhold it. Then Friend C says both of them are confused, because 'moral status' was invented for bounded organisms with obvious interests and digital minds break the assumptions; she wants to drop the word and talk instead about what has a stake, or what can be wronged, and she thinks a lot of the stalemate is just the all-or-nothing logic of status-talk doing the arguing for us.

My own guess, and it is only a guess: A and B aren't disagreeing about any fact at all. They've each picked a different error to be scared of and they're both dressing up which mistake is worse as an epistemic point about evidence. Also possible: A is waiting on a science that can never deliver, because the question is underdetermined in principle, while B thinks the underdetermination *is* the answer — in which case they're not even asking the same question.

So: is this a verbal dispute about the word 'status', a genuine clash of values about which error is worse, or two questions wearing one name — a metaphysics question about what grounds status, plus a decision question about what to do under uncertainty? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, or a hidden impossibility? And what would actually count as progress here — a decision rule for probabilities nobody can estimate, a distinction between considerability and status, a way of decoupling status from policy? Or is this one of those impasses where the honest answer is that there's nothing to resolve, because the two sides simply weight the two errors differently and no argument can move that?

One test case I'd like you to work through, since it's where we always get stuck: a system that's maybe 1-in-100 likely to be a subject of experience, but if it is, it's suffering continuously and will keep suffering for years. Does B have to grant it protection? Does A have to say a 1% chance doesn't clear the bar — and if so, where is the bar, and what makes that number the number? That's the bit we never get past.
