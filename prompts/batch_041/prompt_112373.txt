I'm TA'ing an upper-level seminar on Rousseau and the Enlightenment — we read the Second Discourse, the Social Contract, Emile, and the Letter to M. d'Alembert on Spectacles (1758), in the Bloom translation — and I've been handed the job of writing one essay question for the midterm. The professor wants the question to get at something students can't just answer by listing Rousseau's objections to the theatre, i.e. actors are phonies, comedies make vice charming, Geneva is too small. Half the class already has that list memorized from lecture and they'll write it out whether or not they've done the reading.

Here's the thing I actually want them to wrestle with. Rousseau seems to hold at least three separate theses that are easy to run together: (i) the theatre is powerless to change morals, because spectacles are always shaped by, and flatter, the morals the audience already has; (ii) the theatre positively corrupts, e.g. by getting us to take pleasure in seeing honest Alceste humiliated while the smooth Philinte comes out on top in Le Misanthrope; and (iii) the actor's craft is itself degrading, because the actor traffics in counterfeit feeling and sells his person to the public. (i) and (ii) look like they're pulling in opposite directions — if spectacles just mirror the audience, where does the corruption come from? — and (iii) seems to be about a different subject entirely: the profession, not the performance, not the audience.

And then there's the Geneva-specific move. Rousseau doesn't claim theatre is bad in the abstract; he claims what a spectacle does depends on the size and character of the state, so that a theatre that was fine in Athens is poison in a small republic. That's a contextualist thesis and I suspect it's doing most of the real work — but it also threatens to undercut his own rhetoric, since he spends a lot of the Letter exhorting the Genevans not to let the thing in, which only makes sense if the theatre can change them.

So: write me ONE essay question that forces students to sort out which of these claims Rousseau is actually committed to, whether they can be held together, and which one the argument about Geneva actually needs. Answerable in 45 minutes, in class. Not answerable by someone who has only absorbed the lecture bullet points. Please don't have the question ask them to quote the text (they'll mangle it) or to bring in secondary literature (they won't have read any).

Then give me:
1. A model answer of roughly 700–800 words, pitched at a strong A student — someone who has read the whole Letter but isn't a specialist.
2. A marking scheme out of 20, broken into named must-hit points with marks attached, plus a short list of the moves that should seperate an A from a B (what a B answer will do that an A answer won't).
3. A short common-failures note: the three or four things students reliably get wrong here, and how many marks each should cost.

Before you write any of it, think through the exegetical problem yourself in a scratch section — work out whether (i), (ii) and (iii) can be reconciled, and what you take the strongest reading to be — and then build the question and the model answer so that the crux is exactly that reconciliation problem. I'd rather the model answer take a definite line and defend it than sit on the fence.

At the very end, after everything else, add one last line in exactly this form, naming the crux the question turns on in under fifteen words:

Crux: <your phrase>
