I'm in a first-year seminar on ancient philosophy and we spent two whole weeks on Zeno of Elea, which felt like overkill until it started bothering me in the shower. The standard story is that the paradoxes (the runner who can never even start, Achilles and the tortoise, the arrow that's at rest at each instant, the stadium, plus the weird one about a single grain of millet falling in silence) were basically a defense of Parmenides: everything is one unchanging thing, and motion and plurality are illusions. And then the modern answer is just calculus — the series converges, the limit exists, velocity is the derivative of position, done. But that feels like it answers the letter of the argument while missing whatever Zeno was actually up to. I want you to treat Zeno as a philosopher with a real project, not as a guy who didn't know about limits, and tell me what he'd make of a specific modern idea.

Here's the idea. Suppose spacetime is fundamentally discrete: there's a minimum length and a minimum duration, and the universe updates in discrete ticks, like a giant Game of Life. A particle occupies one cell on one tick and an adjacent cell on the next. During a tick, nothing at all is true about where it is — no trajectory, no in-between position, no fact of the matter about how it got from A to B. Some physicists like this because it gets rid of actual infinities and singularities; some philosophers say it's a disaster because then motion is just a list of rest states, so the arrow never actually moves, and others say it's fine because motion was never anything more than occupying different places at different times anyway. My roommate thinks Zeno would love this, my TA thinks he'd hate it.

So: would Zeno see the tick universe as a vindication (finally, no infinite divisibility to worry about) or as his own arrow paradox come back in a lab coat? If it's the second, is there anything he could say in its defense, or does he just lose? And more broadly — do you think he'd even accept the calculus reply as a reply to him, or was his real target never motion but the coherence of divisibility and plurality themselves? What would genuinely surprise him about the way we still argue over these?
