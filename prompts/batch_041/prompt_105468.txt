I'm in a graduate seminar on care ethics, and this week we read the opening of the sixth part of Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments alongside some contemporary care ethicists, and I've gotten stuck on the first paragraph. Here is the passage:

"The preservation and healthful state of the body seem to be the objects which Nature first recommends to the care of every individual. The appetites of hunger and thirst, the agreeable or disagreeable sensations of pleasure and pain, of heat and cold, etc. may be considered as lessons delivered by the voice of Nature herself, directing him what he ought to chuse, and what he ought to avoid, for this purpose."

That is from the sixth edition of 1790, and the spelling is Smith's. What I can't get past is how the word "care" is being used here. My whole seminar has been treating care as something that arises in relations between people — the attentiveness and responsiveness one person owes to another who is dependent on her — and the standard objections to care ethics turn on whether that kind of care can be a moral ideal at all, rather than a parochial habit we happen to have. Smith seems to say the opposite of all that: care begins at home, quite literally in one's own body, and it is "Nature" that directs us there first, as though self-care were the primitive case and care for others a later and weaker extension. But then he calls the appetites "lessons delivered by the voice of Nature herself," which sounds less like a psychological observation than a claim about what we ought to do, and "recommends" is a strange verb — weaker than "commands," stronger than "inclines." I can't tell whether Smith is making a descriptive claim about the causal order in which concern develops in a person, a normative claim about the order in which our duties rank, or a providential claim about how a benevolent orderer arranged things for our good. I'm told the same part of the book goes on to work out how our benevolence fans out from ourselves to family, to neighbours, to country, to humanity at large, and I suspect the answer to my question lies in how that fanning-out is supposed to work — but I keep reading the first sentence as if it had already settled the question, and I don't think it does.
