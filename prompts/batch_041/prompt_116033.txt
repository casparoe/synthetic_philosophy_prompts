ok, here's my attempt at a coherent position on lying vs misleading, and I'd like a sanity check on it before I commit to it in writing. (1) What makes deception wrong, when it is wrong, is that it bypasses the other person's rational agency — instead of giving them reasons you reach in and install a beleif. (2) That's why I think lying and merely misleading are morally on par: the difference between them is a fact about semantics, about what your sentence literally said versus what you hoped they'd infer, and I don't see how a fact about semantics could carry moral weight on its own. A lawyer who says something literally true in order to plant a false belief is as bad as one who says something false. (3) I've started calling bald-faced lies "dare-you lies" — the politician who says he never saw the memo when the whole room knows he did — and I think these are the most contemptible kind of dishonesty there is. The whole point of them isn't to fool you, it's to dare you to do something about it. (4) I also think what's distinctively wrong with lying is that it breaks the assertion norm, the standing convention that when someone tells you something they're putting their credibility behind it. Misleading exploits that convention without breaking it, which strikes me as a different and sneakier kind of wrong. (5) Withholding the truth isn't deceiving, though — you don't owe everyone everything, and staying silent doesn't install anything in anybody's head. (6) And I'll admit that misleading is often worse in practice, since the person did the inferring themselves, so the false belief ends up stickier and harder to walk back.

So: which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each real one? Order them by severity, tell me what would have to give in each case, and then tell me the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most — I'd rather fix one thing than four.
