My old advisor has a running list of verdicts on whether famous legends "really happened," and I can never reverse-engineer the rule she's using. Her calls so far:

- Trojan War: there was almost certainly some fighting at the site of Troy in the late Bronze Age — the place shows up in Hittite records, and there's a destruction layer around 1180 — but the war the Iliad tells (the coalition, the ten years, Helen) is fiction. She still calls the war historical, which drives the classicists in our department up the wall.
- King Arthur: hard no. The earliest references show up centuries later and they're already legend-shaped, and every candidate for a real Arthur gets reconstructed out of those same late texts.
- Exodus: no. No trace of the migration or the conquest the story requires, no Egyptian mention of it, and the narrative is best explained as an origin myth. She'll grant that some small group might have come out of Egypt, but she says that isn't the Exodus, and treating it as the Exodus is like calling the Trojan War historical because people fought near Troy.
- Romulus: no, even though Rome obviously existed.
- Boudicca: yes, real person, real revolt, even though all our accounts are Roman and written decades after the fact.
- Socrates: yes, obviously, even though he's known to us mainly through students with agendas and a comic poet who mocked him.

She said once in a seminar that the question isnt whether something happened, its whether the smallest thing you can actually defend is still the thing the story is about. I'm pretty sure that's doing a lot of work in her Troy and Exodus calls. But then I don't see why it rules Arthur out, since "a real war leader who won a battle against the Saxons" seems like it's still the thing the Arthur stories are about, and she says no anyway.

Next week's case is the historical Buddha: was there a real teacher behind the tradition, given that the earliest texts are centuries later, they come from the community that venerated him, and nobody outside that community mentions him for a long time (Ashoka's inscriptions seem to be the first outside evidence, as far as I know)?

What I want: the rule or rules that fit everything above, at least one rival rule that fits her calls just as well, what each one predicts about the Buddha, your own best guess and the reasons for it, and what further case she and I should argue about to tell the rules apart. If the honest answer is that she's just inconsistent, tell me that.
