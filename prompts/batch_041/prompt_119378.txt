My roommates and I got into this at dinner last week and it's still bugging me. It came out of a real thing that happened: our neighbor Yolanda went away for a month and asked our roommate Dev to water her plants, which were mostly her late mother's, and he said sure. Then he booked a last-minute trip for the same stretch, turned his phone off, and came home to a windowsill full of dead ficus. Dev feels terrible. But the actual argument was between my friend Marcus and Dev's friend Priya, and it went about six rounds and neither of them would move. Here's my best reconstruction:

(1) Marcus: Dev didn't *cause* anything. Nothing he did made those plants die. What killed them was no water reaching them, which is just the soil drying out and the roots failing. An omission isn't an event — it's a hole in the world — and you can't stand in a causal relation to a hole. And if not-watering counts as a cause, then everyone who also didn't water those plants caused them to die: me, you, the mailman, the whole city. That's not a theory of causation, that's a theory where everything causes everything.

(2) Priya: So the lifeguard who watches someone drown and doesn't move didn't cause the drowning? Causation is difference-making. Had Dev watered, the plants would be alive. Done. And we say this kind of thing outside moral contexts all the time — a mechanic says the car died because the fuel pump didn't engage, a doctor says the patient arrested because nobody gave the drug in time.

(3) Marcus: The mechanic is exactly where you're cheating. "The pump didn't engage" is shorthand for something positive: a relay failed, a wire corroded. There's always a *something* underneath the omission, and the something is the real cause. The omission is a way of talking, not a thing in the world. And notice what actually picks out Dev's omission when we blame him — a norm. He gets blamed and the mailman doesn't, not because his non-watering has different causal powers, but because he promised. Change the promise and you change the "cause" without changing one physical fact. That's the tell that we're doing blame, not causation.

(4) Priya: Then "the soil dried out" isn't the cause either, because that's also a description chosen for our interests — the sun did it, the potting mix did it, the dry air did it. If interest-relativity disqualifies something from being a cause, you've disqualified every cause anyone ever names. And the two descriptions aren't rivals anyway; they're the same structure at different grains. Also your positive story never terminates: the relay failed, fine — what made the failure reach the plants? Somewhere down the line there's a spark that didn't happen, and no pile of positive events upstream makes the absence go away.

(5) Marcus: Your chain doesn't bottom out either, and that's the problem. If absences are causes, there are infinitely many of them for every event — every absence of every preventer is a cause. And the doing/allowing thing isn't *only* a norm. There's something real it's tracking even if nobody can draw the line, and if omissions were causes in the same sense as actions, that whole asymmetry would be a pure illusion.

(6) Priya: The asymmetry is real, but it doesn't show omissions aren't causes — it could be that they're causes of a different kind, or weaker ones, or only when there's a duty. And Dev didn't merely fail to act: he agreed to act and then arranged his life so acting became impossible. If you can cause an outcome by arranging your own inability to prevent it, the omission is downstream of a positive act, and the "no event" complaint falls away — unless you want to say the trip booking killed the plants, which sounds even weirder.
