I am a graduate student in philosophy of language, and I have spent the better part of a term on a paper about indicative conditionals and the triviality results. The section I reproduce below is the part I am least confident about. A colleague in my reading group told me, rather curtly, that I have helped myself to a philosophical conclusion which the technical result does not license, and although I suspect he is being uncharitable, I have not been able to shake the worry that he has put his finger on something. I would be grateful for a careful set of comments on the draft: where the reasoning is sound, where it is merely gestured at, and above all whether the transition from the formal result to the moral I draw from it can be defended or is, as he says, a sleight of hand.

The draft, minus its examples, runs roughly as follows. The received view is that the triviality results show that the probability of an indicative conditional cannot be identified with the corresponding conditional probability. I want to argue for a sharper and more diagnostic reading. Reconstruct the argument as follows. Assume, first, that indicative conditionals express propositions and that the ordinary probability calculus therefore applies to them, so that a conditional can be negated, conjoined with other sentences, and embedded as the consequent of a further conditional. Assume, second, that for any propositions A and C, and for any credence function we might hold, the probability of the conditional 'if A then C' is the probability of C given A, whenever the conditioning proposition has positive probability. Now take any three propositions A, C and D with the relevant conditional probabilities well defined, and consider the conditional whose antecedent is A and whose consequent is the conditional 'if C then D'. By the second assumption its probability is the probability of D given A and C. Consider next the conditional whose antecedent is the conjunction of A and C and whose consequent is D; by the second assumption its probability is the same. So the nested conditional and the flat one must have the same probability. But the nested conditional is a proposition like any other, so the calculus permits us to split its probability according to whether D holds: the case in which D holds contributes exactly the probability of D given A, because under the supposition of D the inner conditional is certain, while the case in which D fails contributes nothing, because under the supposition of not-D the inner conditional is certainly false. Both of those steps apply the equation once more, now to the credence function obtained by conditionalising on D, respectively on its negation. Equating the two routes yields the result that the probability of D given A and C equals the probability of D given A; and since C and D were arbitrary, taking C to be D and A to be a tautology forces every proposition to have probability either zero or one. The equation, then, is satisfiable only by a degenerate, all-or-nothing credence function, which is exactly the triviality. The moral I want to draw is that the results do not by themselves refute truth-conditional accounts of the indicative. What they refute is a package: the propositional treatment of conditionals together with the assumption that the equation holds for every credence function, including those we obtain by conditionalising on an arbitrary proposition. The suppositionalist can shrug, since she never claimed that a conditional sitting in the consequent position of another conditional carries a probability of its own. The truth-conditional theorist who wants to keep the equation can restrict it to conditionals whose consequents contain no conditional, treating the embedded cases as derived rather than primitive. My claim is that this second route is not the ad hoc fudge it is usually taken to be, because the restricted principle is precisely what one should expect if the equation is a rule for assessing an unembedded assertion rather than a constraint on the semantics of the connective.

Concretely, I should like to know three things. First, whether the reconstruction is correct as it stands, and in particular whether the step I have flagged — that the equation must hold for conditionalised credence functions as well as for the original one — is genuinely the load-bearing assumption, or whether some subtler assumption is doing the work while I congratulate myself on having found the culprit. Second, whether the move I attribute to the truth-conditional theorist, namely restricting the equation to conditionals whose consequents contain no conditional, is principled or merely a way of dodging the result; if conditionals are propositions, it is not obvious why the probability of one should depend on the grammatical company it keeps. Third, whether the suppositionalist can really shrug, given that conditionals do after all turn up inside the antecedents and consequents of other sentences. Please be blunt about which of these I have got wrong, and say plainly which single step in the argument you would abandon first. I do not need suggestions for further reading, and I would rather you did not tell me that the literature is divided; I want your own assessment, argued for rather than asserted. Please write your comments in American English.
