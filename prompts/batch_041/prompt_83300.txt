I'm a novelist — third book out in the spring, which is neither here nor there except that it's the reason I'm bothering you — and I've got myself into a proper tangle over it. There's a woman in the book, Nadia, who is uncannily right about people: she meets someone, gets a bad feeling, and nine times out of ten she's right, but she can never say why, and nothing she's doing looks like reasoning. My editor adores her. My brother-in-law, who teaches philosophy, says she's just a lucky guesser and that whatever she's got is worth no more than the bloke down the pub who's right by accident. Which dragged me back to the Meno problem — why is knowledge supposed to be better than a true belief that just happens to be true? I get the Daedalus statue bit (I think that's the Meno, though it might be the Theaetetus, I can never seperate them): true beliefs scarper unless you tether them down. What I don't follow is why the tethered one is supposed to be better if both of them get you where you're going — and if the only thing that's finally good about a belief is that it's true, then it's hard to see how bolting anything on top, justification or reliability or pedigree, adds a penny of value. I'm told that's called the swamping problem. Fine, but I want the whole map, not the name of the swamp.

So: could you build me a decision tree? Something I could print at the back of the book, or use in a piece I'm doing for a literary magazine — the reader starts from the fact that Nadia and the lucky bloke are behaviourally indistinguishable (same belief, same confidence, same outcomes, no practical difference whatsoever), and each branch is a question that walks them down to a named position on what, if anything, makes knowledge worth more than true belief. Build it out of the logic of the question rather than out of alot of -isms, and phrase every branch as something my readers could answer without knowing where it leads: is there a difference in the state itself, or only in how the state got produced? If there's a difference, is the extra bit valuable in its own right, or only because it stops the belief from running off? If it's only instrumentally valuable, valuable for what — acting, asserting, being credited as the one who got it right? And is knowledge even the right thing to be pricing here, or is the real good something more like understanding, in which case Nadia's problem dissolves and I've wasted my afternoon? I'd like these to come out somewhere as leaves, named or marked as unnamed if nobody holds them: reliability is the whole story and knowledge earns nothing extra; the knower deserves credit and that's the distinctive good; knowledge is valuable because it licenses action and lets you stop deliberating; understanding, not knowledge, is where the final value lives; and the deflationary line that the question is malformed.

Where your tree can't separate two of those — I suspect the first and the last are hard to tell apart by cases — say so rather than fudging it, and if some combination of answers turns out to be a position nobody has ever bothered to hold, tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. One test case I do want in there, because it's Nadia with better hardware: a gadget that delivers perfectly true beliefs with no reasons attached at all. No more than a dozen branch questions, please, and no reading lists — I've got a book to finish.
