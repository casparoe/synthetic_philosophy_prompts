Alright, I need an outside opinion on something and I swear it's not a joke, even though it sounds like one.

Three weeks ago I got a letter, no return address, no letterhead. The gist: a group of researchers builds detailed simulations of individual people and can predict, roughly 4 times out of 5, what a given person will do in a specific situation. Before I ever laid eyes on the letter, they say they ran their simulation of me and then flipped a fair coin. If the coin came up tails, they would have quietly put $40,000 into my bank account — on the condition that the simulated me, who has exactly the information I have right now and no more, would hand over $500 on getting this letter. It came up heads. So they're asking me for $500, payable to a PO box. If I don't send it, nothing happens to me, ever. They add that they don't need the money, it funds the next round of letters, and they publish aggregate results.

My girlfriend thinks I'm being an idiot for even hesitating. Her line: the coin already landed, the $40k was either going to happen or it wasn't, and my mailing a check now can't reach back and change a coin that already came down. The only thing my choice affects is whether I'm $500 poorer. I think she's right about the causation bit, and honestly I'm not sure I disagree with the conclusion. But I can't shake the thought that the way they pick who gets the tails-branch money is "people whose decision procedure pays up in the heads case" — and if that's how it works, then the people who get the $40k in the tails case are exactly the people who, in my shoes, send the check. She says that's just me dressing up "I want $40,000" as a principle. Maybe it is.

So: do I send the money? And give me the actual reasoning, not vibes — I want to be able to explain to her why, whichever way it goes.

Things I keep wondering about and can't tell if they matter:

- I'm maybe 70% confident the whole simulation claim is even true. Two people I know say they got surprise deposits from these people with a note explaining the experiment, which is either great evidence or a very wierd prank.
- At $50 I'd just send it. At $20,000 I'd obviously keep my money. Is there a principled place to draw that line, or is it just nerve?
- What if the letter said the coin gets flipped after they read my reply — everything else the same? My gut says that shouldn't change the answer, but it also feels like it does, and I don't know which gut to trust.
- What if, before any of this, I'd been offered the chance to sign up in advance for a standing policy — "I pay every letter of this form" — then been made to forget I signed up, and then a letter arrived? Is that a different question from the one I'm facing now, or the same one in disguise?

And if the answer is don't pay, tell me exactly where my reasoning goes wrong, because right now I genuinely can't tell if I'm being clever or just gullible.
