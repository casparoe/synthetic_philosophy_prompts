Im trying to write a long piece on fakes and I keep hitting the same wall. Every few months there's a fresh story — a forged Old Master, a 3D-printed sculpture that's indistinguishable from the one in the museum, some AI that's been trained to turn out 'new' Vermeers — and the same two camps line up and talk past each other. Camp one: aesthetic value lives in the experience. If a copy gives you exactly the experience the original gives you, the copy is aesthetically just as good, and the only thing wrong with a forgery is the fraud, which is a moral and legal matter rather than an aesthetic one. Camp two: no — the original has something the copy can't have, namely that this particular person, at this particular moment, made this particular thing, and the achievement is part of what we're admiring. Nobody's moved in decades. My hunch, and I want you to tell me if its wrong, is that this isn't really a disagreement about fakes at all. It's a disagreement about what the object of aesthetic appraisal is: the surface as experienced, or the historically situated act of making. If that's right, what kind of disagreement does that make it — a verbal one (are they just using 'aesthetic' to mean different things?), a metaphysical one (do aesthetic properties include relational ones, like being made by so-and-so in a particular year?), or two different questions wearing one name, one about the value of the encounter with a thing and one about the value of the work? And what would count as progress — a distinction, a counterexample, or should I stop expecting a winner and just report that these are two research programmes? Does it change anything if the copy is made by the original artist's own hand, or by a machine that's learned the style, so the causal history gets strange? Also say whether any of this bears on museums that hand back the original and keep a replica, because I might need that bit.
