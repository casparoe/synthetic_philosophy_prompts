A carmaker I've been following wants to ship an autonomous car with a dial the owner sets once — prioritise me / neutral / prioritise others — which shifts whose safety the car favours when a crash is unavoidable. The national ethics body has come back and said no: the car has to run one fixed setting for everyone, picked by the maker and signed off by the regulator. It's allowed to minimise total expected harm, but it may not use personal features like age, sex or health as inputs. The company's counter is that the dial would actually save lives overall, because people who'd otherwise never buy an autonomous car would buy one if they could set it to protect themselves.

I want to know what that ruling is really committed to. What view of morality does it presuppose — that ethics is one thing for everyone and can't be a user preference, that fairness is ex ante and rule-based rather than case-by-case, that there's a fact of the matter about the right setting? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and then be honest about which bits the ruling strictly needs versus which bits it just happens to fit. Could you defend the per-driver dial on the same footing, or does the ban on personal features quietly rule it out too? And where is the whole thing underdetermined — how many rival theories would license the same rule? Finish by telling me what would have to change in the ruling if we swapped the presupposed view for a straight expected-harm one, and then again if we swapped it for a contractualist one. British English please, and don't send me off to read anything — I want the argument.
