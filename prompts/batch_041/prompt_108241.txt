I recently came across the following characterization of epiphenomenalism in an encyclopedia-style overview of the mind–body problem, and I would like a careful audit of it before I rely on it. The passage reads:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experience is a by-product of the brain. Physical processes cause our experiences, but our experiences cause nothing in turn: consciousness is a shadow cast by the nervous system, and it does no work in the production of behavior. The position is usually traced to T. H. Huxley, who held that animals are conscious automata and compared consciousness to a locomotive's whistle — a noise the engine produces but which contributes nothing to its motion. The standard objection is that the doctrine is self-refuting. If every thought and utterance is produced by physical processes alone, then the epiphenomenalist's own belief that epiphenomenalism is true is produced by a brain state rather than by argument or evidence; such a belief could not be justified, and the view therefore destroys the only grounds anyone could have for accepting it. It follows, moreover, that no one could ever know anything about their own experiences, since knowledge of a thing requires that the thing make a difference to the knower. Defenders today generally concede the force of this objection and retreat to a weaker claim: that only the qualitative or felt aspects of experience are epiphenomenal, while beliefs, desires, and intentions remain causally efficacious. But that is interactionism in disguise, since it grants that some mental states produce effects. Epiphenomenalism therefore survives chiefly as a threat — it is where non-reductive physicalists are said to end up once they grant that physics is causally closed."

Please go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of mind working on consciousness would accept as accurate, what would make them wince (right in spirit but misleading in a way that matters), and what is simply wrong. I am not looking for citations; I want the substance. I am inclined to think the objection has some force without refuting anything, but I do not trust that instinct.

Four further questions, which are the ones I actually care most about:

1. The passage calls the central objection a demonstration that the doctrine is "self-refuting." Is that the right diagnosis? The objection as stated moves from "the belief is caused by a brain state" to "the belief is not caused by argument or evidence" — is that a fair inference? And is the real target of the objection the justification of our judgments about experience, their content, or their reliability? Those look like three different complaints to me, and I want to know which is strongest and whether epiphenomenalism faces all three. A friend calls this the paradox of phenomenal judgment; I would like to know whether that label is apt and what exactly the paradox is supposed to be.

2. Suppose the epiphenomenalist grants that the physical state which causes the pain also causes the judgment "I am in pain." Does that answer the objection by explaining why our phenomenal judgments are reliable, or does it explain only why we make the judgments while leaving it unexplained why those judgments are about the experiences at all? Which of the replies open to the epiphenomenalist — direct acquaintance with one's own experiences, the idea that the content of a phenomenal judgment is fixed by the experience even if the judgment is not caused by it, or something else — addresses which horn of the problem, and is any of them available without abandoning epiphenomenalism?

3. A friend tells me the objection proves too much: the same premise — that one can know only what makes a causal difference to one — would bar us from knowing anything about the intrinsic nature of physical things, since we know those only through their effects. Is that a fair reductio, or does the disanalogy matter?

4. The last two sentences use "epiphenomenalism" for what look like two different theses. Is the restricted view described there — only the felt aspects of experience lack causal power, while beliefs and desires keep theirs — the same position as the one non-reductive physicalists are said to be driven to by the causal-exclusion argument? And is the charge that the restricted view is "interactionism in disguise" defensible, given that interactionism requires mental states to have effects as mental?

Please close with a single paragraph giving your verdict: what the passage gets right, what the one most important correction is, and whether its closing claim follows from anything true in the rest of it. Answer in American English.
