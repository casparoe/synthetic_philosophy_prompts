A colleague of mine, whom I will call R., has spent a career defending hedonism about well-being, and in seminars she rules briskly on which states count as pleasures. I have kept a record. The taste of cold water when you are parched: a pleasure. The moment a long headache finally lets go, leaving behind nothing but the absence of pain: a pleasure. The click of understanding when a proof at last goes through, an episode with no sensory content she can point to: a pleasure. Aesthetic chills during a favorite passage of music: a pleasure. The bittersweet ache of remembering someone you have lost, which she insists is a pleasure of a kind and not merely a relief: a pleasure. The pain a masochist seeks out and calls delicious: a pleasure, and the fact that it is pain is no objection. The glee of taking pleasure in someone else's misfortune: a pleasure, an ugly one. And a long-term user of a drug who reports that she wants it desperately, obtains it, and no longer enjoys it at all: not a pleasure — R. is adamant that wanting and enjoying are different things, and that only one of them makes a state a pleasure.

She has never given me a general criterion. When I press her, she says she trusts her ear.

I am writing on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and I want to build an objection around a case she has not seen. Before I commit to it, I need to know what she will say, and, more importantly, what her past rulings commit her to. The case: a patient — the neurological details do not matter, only the profile — who, while a certain stimulus is applied, reports in the moment that the state is intensely delicious. She says, unprompted, that it feels wonderful, and every vocal and facial marker of delight is present. But she will not lift a finger to obtain it again. She does not want it, does not miss it, and does not work for it; the episode leaves no trace on her subsequent conduct, and nothing she does is strengthened or changed by it. On later reflection she says she is glad it happened and would not have missed it, though she still has no desire for another. Call it the case of the unfollowed delight.

Will R. call it a pleasure? Give me the two or three criteria that fit her earlier rulings equally well, say what each predicts about the unfollowed delight, and tell me which of them she is most likely running, with your reasons. Say, too, whether her ruling on the drug user already settles anything about how liking and wanting must come apart in her scheme. Then name the further case, one that neither she nor I have yet put to her, that would best force the surviving criteria apart, and say what each would say about it. If her rulings genuinely underdetermine the answer, say that instead, and tell me what the indeterminacy shows about the heterogeneity problem: whether it reveals that pleasures share no common core, or merely that my colleague has never been made to state hers. Argue from the cases; I am not interested in who has defended which view.
