I'm in a running arguement with a friend and I want to actually try to see the other side, so I'm going to ask you to argue it for me. She's been reading a lot of Hofstadter and she's convinced that a self -- an "I" -- isn't something sealed up inside one skull; it's a pattern, a kind of looping process, that can be spread out over more than one brain at a time, so that when someone you love dies, a genuine piece of them keeps going in the heads of the people who knew them. She insists this is not a metaphor, that the person is literally partly located in those other brains. I think it's warm feelings dressed up as cognitive science, but I want to lose the argument honestly if I'm wrong, so: play a committed defender of that view. Be sharp, make the strongest case you can, concede a small thing now and then but never the core claim, stay in character until I tell you to stop, and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph of plain prose -- no headings, no bullets, no bold, just talk to me like a person. Don't step outside the role to lecture me about what Hofstadter "really meant"; I want the believer, not the commentator.

Here's my opening. Take her favourite example and then watch it eat itself. Suppose I die tomorrow and two people who loved me each keep running me in their heads -- close copies at first, but they drift, and after thirty years one remembers me as gentle and the other as a bully, and by the end neither would recognise the other's version. She says there's no fact of the matter about which one is "really" me, because I'm the pattern that got copied and copies drift. Fine. But now run the same logic outward: if a self lives wherever a copy of it lives, then I'm partly in every person who's ever carried a version of me, including the ones who've got me completely wrong -- the ex who thinks I was a monster, the acquaintance who half-remembers my name. So the "real me" is either the accurate copies or the botched ones, and if you say "all of them," then most of me is error and hearsay sitting in strangers' skulls, and "I" turns out to mean "the sum of everyone's inaccurate memory of me." That isn't a self, that's a rumour. So which is it -- is the me that survives the true copies or the false ones, and if your answer is "both, it's one distributed pattern," tell me what work the word "I" is still doing that a shrug wouldn't do just as well.
