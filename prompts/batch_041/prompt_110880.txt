Write a short story in British English, of roughly 1,500 words, set in a near-future public office that adjudicates applications to conceive. The office decides every case using Benatar's asymmetry alone: pain's presence is bad, pleasure's presence is good, pain's absence is good even where no one exists to enjoy that good, and pleasure's absence is bad only where someone is deprived by it. Tell the story from the point of view of a long-serving assessor who has spent a career applying those four clauses, and who is now required to rule on a case the asymmetry was not built for: whether an already existing person's life should continue. Let the story dramatise, rather than announce, the gap between the question the asymmetry answers and the question the office has been handed, and let the assessor's habit of reading each case off the four clauses push her towards a conclusion she cannot bring herself to sign. Resist explaining the philosophy in dialogue; let the office's forms, its ledger vocabulary of goods foregone and unclaimed absences, and the pressure of the particular case carry the idea. Leave the case unresolved, but not evasively so.
