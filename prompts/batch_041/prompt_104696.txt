I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I want to be sure I am not fooling myself about a belief I have drifted into. The position I hold is this: the Church-Turing thesis is a substantive claim rather than a definition or a bookkeeping convention. That is, "effectively calculable" picks out a real class of procedures -- ones a finite agent could carry out step by step, given unlimited time and scratch space -- and the claim that this class coincides exactly with what Turing machines can compute is a genuine discovery about the limits of procedure, not something we stipulated into truth by choosing our definitions. I also hold the stronger, physical version: no process in the physical world can compute anything outside that class, so physics never hands us a way around the halting problem.

What would count against this? I would like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and I would like the two halves kept apart, because I suspect they have very different exposure. For the mathematical or conceptual half: what would it take to show the thesis is false rather than merely under-specified, and is the familiar reply -- that it cannot be refuted because "effective" is an informal notion -- a real defense or a way of rendering the claim unfalsifiable? For the physical half: what would a genuine counterexample look like? I keep hearing about hypercomputation and about analog or real-valued machines, and my instinct is that every such proposal either smuggles in an oracle, assumes infinite precision that no physical quantity actually carries, or secures only a bounded speedup. I would like to know whether that instinct is correct, and what the strongest surviving case against me actually is.

A colleague has pressed me with an argument I have not been able to answer cleanly: if a physical system genuinely stores an infinite-precision real quantity -- say, a voltage that encodes a full real number -- then comparing that quantity against a threshold looks like it decides an undecidable problem in finite time, which would be a physical counterexample to my view. Tell me whether the argument works, and if it fails, exactly where it fails: whether the flaw is physical (no such precision is realizable in principle), formal (the comparison is not the computation it appears to be), or conceptual (the idealization has already assumed the answer). I would also like to know what defenders of my position would themselves concede as counting against them, as distinct from what only critics say. And give me an honest verdict on whether the position risks anything at all, or whether it has been qualified until nothing could count against it -- and if so, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what a thesis about an informal notion has to look like.
