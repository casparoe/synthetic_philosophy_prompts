I am a PhD candidate in political theory, currently on a placement with the policy planning staff of a foreign ministry. My desk receives proposals for humanitarian intervention and regime change at a rate of roughly two or three a quarter, usually under time pressure and with incomplete intelligence; the recommendations we produce go to the minister, who signs off, and in some cases they shape a decision that cannot be reversed once forces are committed. I have been trying to develop a decision procedure that a generalist analyst, not a just war theorist, could run in a few hours on a new proposal. I am calling it the burden-shifting screen, though the name is not important. Its core commitments are these: first, it treats the population the intervention is meant to protect and the population that will bear its collateral risks as two separate ledgers, and it does not allow gains on one ledger to cancel losses on the other without a separate, named authorization; second, it requires a counterfactual baseline, comparing the no-intervention trajectory with the intervention trajectory over a fixed horizon, and it stops if the intervention is not superior at the lower bound of the uncertainty range; third, it imposes a higher threshold and a named exit authority on any intervention that creates facts on the ground not reversible within five years, including regime change, partition, and long-term occupation; fourth, it requires a last-resort test that names a specific, time-stamped diplomatic offer or path that has been rejected or is unavailable, rather than a general claim that diplomacy has been tried; fifth, it requires a proper-authority test that distinguishes UN Security Council authorization, regional authorization, coalition authorization, and unilateral action, and says what the procedure does when authorization is contested; and sixth, it requires a post-intervention accountability test naming who will be responsible for reconstruction, transitional justice, and withdrawal, with the procedure stopping if no actor is named.

I want you to turn this into a procedure my desk can actually run. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and where it will misfire or be gamed. It has to be usable by someone with a generalist background, in a few hours, with no ability to commission new research. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. You may assume familiarity with the standard just war criteria and the responsibility to protect framework; use them only where they earn their place, and do not give me a literature review, a reading list, or citations. Then run it on three cases: the 1999 Kosovo intervention, the 2003 Iraq invasion, and the 2011 Libya intervention. You may assume I know the basic chronology of each case; do not spend space recounting it. For each, say what the procedure returns, what facts it needs that are contested or missing, and whether the return depends on hindsight. I am personally inclined to think the procedure should license Kosovo and block Iraq; if it blocks Kosovo or licenses Iraq, I need to see that clearly rather than have it softened. I am less sure about Libya, and I suspect that is the case where the procedure will either do real work or reveal its limits. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and which of the six commitments above is doing the most work and which is mostly decoration.
