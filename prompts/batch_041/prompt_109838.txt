I am opening the session on the School of Names in my classical Chinese philosophy seminar with a poem read aloud, and I would like you to write it. The text we will be reading that day is the White Horse Dialogue from the Gongsun Longzi, in which two unnamed speakers — an advocate who maintains that a white horse is not a horse, and an objector who takes this to be nonsense — argue the proposition back and forth without either of them leaving the field. I want the poem to be a dialogue in verse: two voices only, twenty-four to thirty-two lines, unrhymed, in lines short enough to be spoken aloud, English throughout (no characters, no transliteration needed).

Three things should do structural work rather than being merely mentioned.

First, the proposition is the refrain. It should return at least three times in exactly the same words, and the poem should make its sense shift under it without altering a syllable — the advocate means that a white horse is not identical with horse, does not answer to the same demand that horse answers to; the objector hears the claim that no white horse belongs among horses. Please do not let the poem hang on the absence of articles in classical Chinese; that is the explanation students reach for first, and it is not the interesting one.

Second, let the two speakers be distinguishable by their relation to language before the argument is named: the advocate speaks in definitions (what a term designates), the objector in existences and quantities (what there is, what cannot be denied). The objector's objection — that if there is a white horse one cannot say there is no horse — should be the strongest form of it, not a straw man.

Third, the advocate's two supports should both appear, and be recognizable as such to someone who knows the text: that horse designates shape while white designates color, and the test of what will satisfy a request, namely that yellow and black horses will answer to horse but not to white horse.

End the poem by giving the objector's opening question back verbatim as the last line, so that it closes on the question it opened with and leaves the reader to judge whether the advocate has drawn a genuine distinction or merely traded on one. Leave the hardness-and-whiteness material out; this poem is for the white horse chapter alone. It should be good enough to read aloud to a room that has not done the reading, and accurate enough that a specialist in the disputers would not wince.
