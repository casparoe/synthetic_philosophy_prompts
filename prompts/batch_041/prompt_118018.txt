I am taking a philosophy of physics course this term and need to produce a short research proposal — roughly 2,500 words, framed as one semester's worth of work — for a project I would like to pursue afterwards. Please develop the following half-formed idea into that proposal.

The standard story is that the thermodynamic arrow of time is a consequence of the universe's having begun in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate: given time-symmetric microdynamics and some probability measure over microstates, entropy is overwhelmingly likely to increase away from that end. What I cannot get past is that this appears to explain an entropy gradient and very little else, whereas the asymmetries that structure our lives are asymmetries of information and agency: we have records of one direction and not the other, we can intervene in one direction and not the other, and causation, deliberation, and regret all point one way. I want to ask whether those further asymmetries can be derived from the entropic one together with a low-entropy boundary condition, or whether every candidate derivation quietly imports a premise that is already temporally asymmetric — for instance a notion of one state's carrying information about another, or of a microstate's counting as typical, that is defined only relative to an observer already oriented in time. My hypothesis, which I would like the proposal to test rather than assume, is that no such derivation succeeds without a premise of that kind, and that the philosophically interesting question is which premise it must be and why.

Please turn this into a proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer, including what would count as a successful derivation and what would count as a counterexample to one; explain why the usual treatments leave it open rather than settling it; set out the method and say why that method rather than the obvious alternatives — I imagine writing the boundary condition and the probability measure in deliberately temporally neutral vocabulary, then locating precisely where asymmetry enters the argument, and checking the diagnosis against simple models such as a gas expanding in a box, a spin system subjected to a reversing pulse, and a toy system that forms records of its own earlier states; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project's failing, bearing in mind that I would accept an argument that the question is ill-posed as a genuine result; and give a realistic scope for a single semester, including an honest account of what would make it too large. Please do not supply a bibliography or citations — I will add those myself, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular works.
