So my team runs the ethics module in the onboarding for our city's behavioural insights unit -- the group that reorders the cafeteria line and moves the opt-out box on the pension form -- and the director wants to open with something funny instead of yet another slide on liberty versus welfare. So: write me the thing itself, a deadpan parody onboarding handbook / FAQ for a fictional Office of Choice Architecture, the kind of packet a new hire actually gets handed on day one, numbered sections, around 700 words. The running joke should be that everyone in the office sincerely believes the standard libertarian-paternalist defence -- we only ever rearrange options you already had, nothing was taken away, the opt-out is right there if you want it -- while the handbook keeps cheerfully describing practices that quietly make that defence false. Get the machinery right: the difference between changing what's on someone's menu and changing the order of the menu; why being transparent and easy to ignore is supposed to be the load-bearing condition; the split between helping people reach their own ends and deciding those ends for them, with the handbook assuming the first while doing the second; the bit where some person somewhere still has to pick the default and nobodys quite sure who; and the fact that the office manufactures the very friction it complains about. I'd love a System 1 versus System 2 bit treated like two departments locked in a turf war, one invented senior Nudge Officer quoted being soothingly reassuring, and a dissenting memo from an invented junior staffer who keeps asking who the nudges don't work on -- both of those people made up, and please don't cite real studies or researchers, I want the thing to stand on its own.

Two details I specifically want in there, since they're our actual running internal arguments: the Cafeteria Pilot, where the salad got moved to eye level and the fries to the far end, and half the office still insists nothing was taken away; and a metric called the Autonomy Preservation Score that gets calculated every quarter and has never once appeared in a report. Land the real objections -- who actually sets the defaults, what happens to the people who can't find the basement, whether letting people pick their own ends survives contact with someone who never finds out they were nudged -- but don't state them outright, don't wink at the reader, and don't explain the joke at the end. Start directly with the handbook: no title page, no preface, no framing paragraph about what you're doing.
