I have no philosophical training, but for some months I have been turning over a view of moral responsibility that I cannot quite leave alone, and I would like to use it to read a novel. P. F. Strawson, as I understand him, argued that what really anchors our practice of holding people responsible is not some metaphysical fact about whether their wills were free, but a whole fabric of attitudes we cannot help having toward one another — resentment, gratitude, indignation, forgiveness, hurt, love — the attitudes of someone who is participating in a relationship rather than observing it. He set these against what he called the objective attitude, the stance we take when we treat a person as something to be managed, studied, or worked around rather than as a fellow participant: the way we might regard a small child, someone gravely ill, or, at the limit, a case to be handled rather than an interlocutor to be answered. His claim, if I have it right, is that we could not give up the participating attitudes wholesale and remain the creatures we are; the objective attitude is something we can adopt, but only locally, and as a general policy toward everyone it would be barely intelligible as a human life.

I want to read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through this idea, and I would like your help doing it carefully rather than as a mere illustration. Stevens, the butler, seems to me to have organized his entire existence around declining the participant's stance. Toward his employer, Lord Darlington, he refuses to resent or condemn even after the man's politics turn poisonous; toward Miss Kenton he will not acknowledge love or grief; toward his own dying father he is almost clinical; and toward himself — the part I care about most — he never seems to resent, forgive, or even pity, but only to manage himself in the name of dignity. Strawson, as I remember him, is writing mainly about our attitudes toward other people, and largely assumes we cannot help having them. Stevens looks like a possible counterexample: a life that is, on its surface, a sustained attempt to occupy the objective attitude toward everyone, himself included.

What I want from you is a reading of the novel in terms of this idea, faithful in both directions. Where does the distinction between the participating and objective attitudes genuinely illuminate a scene or a choice — the conference scenes and Darlington's guests, the episode of Miss Kenton's letter, the night of the father's death, the final bantering on the pier? Where does the novel resist the reading — is Stevens really an exception to Strawson, or does his dignity turn out to be a way of participating after all, a set of reactive attitudes in disguise? And please take seriously the question Strawson might put back to me: whether the attitudes he describes can be refused the way Stevens tries to refuse them, or whether the novel in the end confirms him, given that the feeling leaks out anyway. Tell me which scenes a reader should return to, and finish by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that an argument alone could not — in particular whether it shows something about the objective attitude directed at oneself that Strawson's account does not quite have room for. I do not need citations or a reading list; I would rather have the reading itself.
