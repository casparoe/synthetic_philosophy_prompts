I am the research director of a mid-sized grantmaking foundation. We award roughly forty grants a year, between fifty and four hundred thousand dollars each, in maternal and child health, family planning, nutrition, and support for people with disabilities, in two low-income countries. Two program officers screen every proposal; the board approves all of them at its quarterly meetings; an officer who misapplies a funding rule in either direction is answerable to the board, and our annual report is read closely by donors who take the rules seriously. Last spring the board adopted, by a single vote, a funding principle it calls the asymmetry. The minute reads: where a proposal would determine whether a person exists, the suffering that person would undergo counts against the proposal and the happiness that person would enjoy does not count for it; where a proposal affects people who would exist in any case, their suffering and their happiness both count in the ordinary way. The minute does not say whether the board takes this to be a claim about what we owe anyone or a claim about what is good, and the board has now asked me to turn it into the instrument our officers actually use — something an officer with no philosophical training can run in half an hour, reaching the same verdict as the other officer on the same proposal, and showing work the board can audit.

Write that instrument. Specifically:

1. An ordered procedure: the questions to be asked in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what evidence we should accept from field partners (who mostly supply program data and plausible projections rather than trials), and what an officer records at each step so that the reasoning can be reviewed.
2. The conditions under which the procedure returns a no, and the point at which it must stop and hand the decision to the board because what remains is a judgment about values rather than a fact about the world.
3. A rule for proposals that both spare suffering and change who exists, since most of ours do, and a substitute for the comparison we ordinarily use — the same beneficiaries, better or worse off — which is unavailable when the proposal determines who the beneficiaries are.
4. A rule for the many cases in which we cannot get a number.
5. An explicit list of the steps that are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and what the officer is really deciding at each one.
6. A plain statement of what the instrument cannot do and the kinds of proposal on which it will misfire.

Then run it on three proposals, giving the verdict and the reasoning for each:

- A district-wide expansion of contraceptive access, projected to avert several hundred births a year and also to reduce maternal deaths;
- A neonatal unit that will save the lives of children who would otherwise die in infancy, many of whom will go on to experience illness, hardship, and grief as well as ordinary goods;
- A co-funding request for fertility treatment, which the board's principle was probably not written with in mind and which I happen to support.

At least one of these should come out in a way I do not like, and I would rather you say so plainly than soften the procedure to avoid it. In particular, tell me whether a consistent application of the principle makes saving a life look worse than preventing a birth, and whether it commits us to preferring a smaller population to a larger and happier one; if it does, say whether that follows from the principle itself or from our way of counting. Say also whether the two readings of the board's minute — the reading on which the happiness of new people is not owed to them, and the reading on which it is not good at all — yield different verdicts on any of the three, and what sentence the board would have to add to its minute to remove the ambiguity. Where the minute's phrase does not count for it is doing real work, say whether you are treating it as zero, as weightless, or as incomparable, and what turns on the choice.

I do not need sources, citations, or further reading; I need the instrument and the worked examples.
