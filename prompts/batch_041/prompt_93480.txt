Act as a philosopher who specializes in practical rationality and decision theory. I'm trying to work out what I actually think about "rational irrationality" — the kind of case where it seems right to have some policy or state of mind even though, if you just look at the act on its own, it looks dumb — and I want you to help me find the principle behind my gut verdicts, then tell me which of them I should probably give up.

Here's where I keep landing.

The hitchhiker. I'm stranded, and the only way the driver will take me is if I promise to pay him a thousand bucks once we reach town. I think making that promise is fine, and I think I should pay if he asks, unenforceable as it is. But suppose I knew he'd forget and never ask — I don't think I'd pay, and I also don't think promising was wrong. That pair feels slightly inconsistant and I can't shake it.

Deterrence. I think a country can rationally threaten retaliation it would never actually carry out, and that cultivating real anger is part of what makes the threat credible — the rage isn't pretend. Yet if the moment actually came and everyone could still think straight, launching would be insane.

Toxin. I'd drink a day of agony for a million dollars and I'd take that deal. But I only get the money if I genuinely intend to drink it tonight, and I can't make myself intend something I know I'll bail on once the cash is safe. I don't think I'm being irrational there.

The interview. I tell a company I'll stay at least three years, because that's the only way they'll hire me, and I might well leave in eighteen months. This one I go back and forth on.

Is there one principle that gets all of these — maybe maximize the expected value of a policy or disposition rather than of the act, or "be the sort of person for whom the good outcome is actually reachable"? What does it say about the interview case, and if nothing fits, which verdict do I drop? Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a bit before you settle on an answer.
