So my friend and I got into it for like two hours last night about logical omniscience and I want to actually get clear on it instead of just trading intuitions at each other.

Here's the position I ended up defending, and honestly I'm not sure how much of it I even believe: that logical omniscience isn't a real problem for the logic of belief and knowledge, it's an artifact of modeling belief as a set of sentences that's closed under logical consequence, and once you stop doing that the problem mostly dissolves. My dumbest version of the argument was the disjunction one — you believe P, are you therefore committed to believing "P or Q" for literally any Q? Like, I believe it's raining, do I thereby believe it's raining or that the moon is made of cheese? No. Nobody does. So that looks like a counterexample but I said it isn't one, it just shows that "belief" in the formal models isn't the same object as the psychological state, and we shouldn't expect a logic to capture the state — closure is a feature of the *idealized* notion of belief, which is the only one worth formalizing anyway. I also threw in this thing I half-remember about belief being more like a map than a list of sentences: a map can be totally accurate without literally containing every fact about the territory, so "believes everything that follows" might be a kind of category error rather than a claim about a superhuman agent.

My friend said I'm just relabeling the problem and calling it solved. So I want the actual defeaters — what would count against this, not the vague worry that it feels like a dodge. Like: are there cases where the closure is doing real explanatory work, where I can't wave it off as "oh that's just the idealized notion"? Does the map picture have its own closure problem — since a map that represents the terrain is itself a thing with logical consequences, and if it's accurate doesn't it have to represent those too? What would an agent actually have to *do* to demonstrate it believes a consequence it can't derive — is there a test here, or is "tacit belief" just unfalsifiable? And the weedy stuff: does it matter that P entails P-and-(Q-or-not-Q) for every Q, so a triviality-padding trick gets you from any belief to infinitely many? What about conjunction — if you know a thousand separate things, do you know their giant conjunction, and if I say no, what broke?

Be honest with me about whether my position has quietly become unfalsifiable — if every apparent counterexample just gets reclassified as "not the idealized notion," is that a real move or a rescue?
