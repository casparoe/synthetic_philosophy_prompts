Gould and Lewontin's spandrels are usually invoked for a single slogan — that not every trait is an adaptation — but the force of the example seems to depend on something narrower than mere byproduct status: given a dome resting on pendentives, the spandrels cannot fail to exist, and the decoration later placed in them is a consequence of that geometry rather than a solution to an independent problem. Explain what work that unavoidability is doing in the argument, and then the difficulty it leaves behind: if a spandrel is defined by the absence of selection, what would evidence for a byproduct explanation look like, as opposed to evidence that one particular adaptationist story is unsupported? Say how the later notion of exaptation — a byproduct subsequently co-opted and refined for a use — complicates the contrast, and whether the appeal to developmental or architectural constraint is a genuine alternative to adaptationism or instead a constraint that selection can work inside.
