I cover science and ideas for a weekly, and I am drafting a long piece on responsibility and inequality. Before I file it, I want to know whether my own position holds together, because the readers who will write in about it know this territory far better than I do. Here is where I have landed.

1. What people end up with should track what they choose, not what they were handed. Inequalities that trace to unchosen circumstance — the body you were born into, the family, the decade, the market you happened to enter — are unjust and ought to be corrected. Inequalities that trace to choices, including choices about risk, are not unjust in themselves, even when the resulting gap is enormous.

2. Nobody should fall below a decent floor. The floor is unconditional: it does not matter whether you gambled, squandered, or turned down help. You get housing, medical care, and enough to live on, and you get it without anyone auditing your character.

3. The point of equality is standing rather than distribution. What is objectionable about inequality is that it lets some people command others, buy their way out of shared obligations, and treat fellow citizens as supplicants. A society of equal standing could tolerate a wide spread of incomes; a society with a narrow spread could still be a hierarchy.

4. I am convinced by the charge that making aid conditional on a verdict about your choices is humiliating, and that a state which asks the unfortunate to prove they did not deserve their misfortune has already got the moral relationship wrong.

5. In a piece last year I argued that someone who knowingly takes a long-odds gamble — remortgaging the house to open a restaurant, skipping insurance, quitting a secure job to day-trade — has no claim on the rest of us when the gamble fails. I stand by that.

6. I have also been floating a scheme I call the endowment: every citizen receives a real, spendable sum at eighteen, and after that the state never makes good a loss on it, beyond the floor in (2). Whatever you do with your endowment is your business, and the record of what you did is nobody's business but yours.

I suspect at least one of these has to give, and I would rather find out from you than from the letters page. Which of the six are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what that revision would cost me. If a single change would clear up more than one of them, tell me which one and why. And if the set turns out to be consistent after all, tell me what I am failing to see that makes me think otherwise.
