I am designing a ninety-minute seminar on psychiatric classification for a deliberately mixed audience: eight second-year psychiatry residents and six philosophy graduate students. No one will have read anything in advance, so the plan must not assume, recommend, or cite any reading; I want the whole session carried by material we generate in the room. The residents arrive with a professional stake in the categories being usable and real; the philosophers arrive braced for a social-construction argument and prepared to find it dull. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation.

The target idea is the looping effect: that a classification of people can alter the people classified, and that the alteration then bears back on the classification. I want it taught in a form more specific than the slogan, and I have drafted an apparatus for that which I want you to interrogate rather than accept. First, three levels at which the loop can run: the person (the category supplies a new vocabulary for one's own experience), the population (who presents, how often, and with what story), and the instrument (the criteria, checklists, and interview schedules that decide what counts as a symptom). Second, bidirectionality: a downward loop, in which the category reshapes the classified, and an upward loop, in which the classified and their advocates reshape the category. Third, a carry-away tool: a short list of features that make a category loop-prone, which I take to include criteria that are publicly readable, a name that can be worn as an identity, an intervention whose success changes who still meets the criteria, and the absence of an independent marker that would anchor the category's extension. Tell me where that list is wrong or incomplete, and whether the three-levels-and-two-directions scaffolding earns its complexity in ninety minutes or should be cut back.

Then give me the plan itself, addressed in this order:

1. The sequence. What has to be in place before the claim is even stateable as more than a slogan, and what depends on what. I am tempted to open with the idea of kinds that can read their own classification, and to earn it later. Tell me whether that is the right first move or whether it front-loads a metaphor the rest of the session then has to pay for.

2. The carrying example. I have been considering taijin kyofusho: a category used in Japan for a fear of offending or harming others through one's appearance or body — blushing, gaze, imagined offensive odor — where the sufferer's concern is directed at the harm done to other people rather than at embarrassment to oneself, and for which a treatment tradition was developed in Japan aiming at acceptance of the anxiety rather than its removal. I want to know what an example must have and lack to carry this lesson, whether this one is the right choice or whether something closer to the residents' own clinical week would be better, and specifically what it must lack — I suspect a loaded political history of the kind that would pull the discussion into the discipline's most famous diagnostic deletions, a media-contagion story, and a biomarker.

3. The misconceptions. I expect at least these: that admitting looping effects shows the categories are arbitrary and the suffering unreal; that the looping effect is just labeling theory, deviance amplification, the label manufacturing the disorder; and that looping is a historical event that happened once, to other people, elsewhere. What have I missed? For each, name the point in the sequence where it must be headed off and what the corrective has to consist of at that moment — the shape of the move, not a script.

4. The check for understanding. One question to ask at the end that would separate a group which has grasped the claim from one that has merely learned to say that the kinds are interactive. I want it to require applying the loop to a category that does not look controversial.

5. What to leave out, said out loud, and where in the sequence I should say so — including the separate question of whether taijin kyofusho and the Western social anxiety category are the same condition, which I want parked rather than resolved, and the point at which the residents' professional stake and the philosophers' expectations each get addressed.
