I'm putting together a handout for the first meeting of a reading group I'm running on scientific realism, and the topic that keeps derailing us is verisimilitude -- the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another even though neither is true. I want to open with a taxonomy rather than a reading, because last time we burned forty minutes arguing past each other over whether the whole notion even makes sense, and it turned out half of us meant different things by closer to the truth. So build me a decision tree of the positions: every branch node should be a question someone could answer without already knowing which label it leads to, and every leaf should be a named position (or unnamed, if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order they should split the tree: first, whether truthlikeness is a real property that false theories can have in degrees at all, or whether the whole project of defining it is a mistake and the word just does pragmatic work -- flag that as the biggest fork, since if someone lands on no, the rest of the tree doesn't apply to them. Then, on the yes side: does being closer to the truth require having no false consequences, or can a theory that still asserts some falsehoods count as closer? Does the account measure closeness by content (more true content, less false content) or by likeness or distance to the truth, i.e. some metric over the space of possibilities? And within the distance camp: is closeness the average distance from the truth, the distance to the nearest point where the theory is right, or some weighted matter of which questions the theory gets right? There's also the question of whether the account only works for quantitative theories -- the kind whose claims plot as regions in a parameter space -- or is meant to cover qualitative ones too, and the nasty one about whether closeness is relative to the language or conceptual scheme you happen to describe the world in, so that the same pair of theories can flip order when you redescribe them.

For leaves I definitely want: the original content-based proposal (Popper's); the repair that just drops the false-content requirement; a distance or likeness approach (Tichy-style, and if you can pull them apart, the min-sum and average-distance variants -- I think those are Niiniluoto and Oddie but don't quote me on that); the relevant-consequence line (Schurz and Weingartner); the nomic or structuralist one where you compare theories by their sets of possible worlds or models rather than by their consequences (Kuipers); the restricted view that truthlikeness is only definable for quantitative theories; and the deflationary view that we don't need a definition at all. Use these three probes as the concrete cases, so the tree sorts people by what they say about cases and not by which name they like: (a) two false estimates of a physical constant, one off by a little and one that names a disjunction of two values, one of which is right; (b) two false theories of planetary motion where one is closer in its predictions but also asserts an extra false regularity the other never mentions; (c) the same pair of theories redescribed in a coarser vocabulary, where the content-based ranking flips. Where the tree can't separate two positions -- I suspect the min-sum and average variants collapse on some of these -- say so rather than forcing a split. And note any combination of answers that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
