I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy preparing a two-page proposal for a departmental summer research grant, which I hope will yield a standalone paper and eventually a dissertation chapter. My area is moral epistemology, and over a term of reading for a seminar I have grown convinced that the long-running dispute about moral testimony is being prosecuted as a single question when it is really two. The familiar framing asks, roughly, whether hearing another person assert that some act is wrong can give the hearer moral knowledge, or justified belief, that it is wrong — a question about the transmission of epistemic warrant from speaker to hearer. I want to argue that a second question is being smuggled in under the same heading: whether a hearer who acts on such testimony can be the author of that action in the sense required for it to count as an exercise of her own moral agency — the sense in which the deed reflects on her, and she is credited or blamed for it, rather than reflecting on the person she deferred to. Call these the transmission question and the authorship question. My working hunch is that they can come apart in both directions. One might be an optimist about transmission — grant that testimony reliably transmits moral knowledge — while still holding that acting on it is a form of agential outsourcing, so that the action is not properly the hearer's own. Conversely, one might be a pessimist about transmission — deny that testimony ever transmits moral knowledge — while thinking authorship is entirely untouched, because authorship concerns the shape of one's practical reasoning rather than the pedigree of one's beliefs. If that is right, a striking number of the standard positions turn out to be underdetermined by the arguments offered for them, and the debate may need to be redivided along these lines before either half can be settled.

I want to be careful not to overstate the novelty, and I would rather the proposal present this as a diagnostic hypothesis to be tested than as a discovery. What I would like from you is a proposal that states the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, explains why the existing positions in the literature do not already settle it (in particular, why an optimist and a pessimist about transmission can each occupy either side of the authorship issue), and lays out the approach I would actually take. My inclination is a conceptual-and-case-based method rather than anything empirical: construct matched pairs of cases in which the epistemic credentials of a hearer's belief are held fixed while the structure of her practical reasoning varies, and see whether our judgments about authorship, credit, and blame track the epistemic features or come apart from them. I would also want the proposal to say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — for instance, rather than a straight argument for or against moral deference, or a survey of intuitions about particular cases — and to identify the point at which the two questions might turn out to be more entangled than my hypothesis allows, since that would be the most interesting way for me to be wrong.

Please include a realistic month-by-month plan for a ten-week summer, a clear statement of what a successful result would look like (I take it a genuine result would be either a stable dissociation between the two questions or a demonstration that the alleged dissociation collapses), an honest account of what would count as the project failing, and a candid assessment of whether this is too much for a summer or should be trimmed to a single question. Do not bother with citations or a literature review by reference — I will add those myself, and I would rather the space go to the framing and the method. Write it as a proposal document I could submit, in a register appropriate for a funding panel of philosophers, and keep it to two pages.
