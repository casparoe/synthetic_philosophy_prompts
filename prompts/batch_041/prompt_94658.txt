Last week's meeting of a reading group I attend turned into a long argument between two of its members, and they broke up without settling anything. I stayed out of it, and I am not asking for advice about what to think; I want the argument itself taken apart. Neither of them misunderstands the facts of the case, and neither is making a crude mistake — which is exactly why I could not referee it.

The case they were arguing about is invented but fully specified. A small bottling plant has a tank that is filled continuously by an intake pump. The tank's drain valve is held open by current running through a timer circuit. Every hour an operator must press a reset button to keep the circuit live; if a full hour passes without a press, the circuit breaks, the drain valve closes, and the tank overflows. Plant rules allow any of the forty authorized employees to press the reset; one press per hour is enough, and extra presses do nothing. On the night in question, at three in the morning, nobody pressed. The timer expired, the relay opened at 3:00:04 — the control log records this as an event with a timestamp — the drain valve closed, the tank overflowed, and the floor flooded.

A says: the log tells the whole story. The relay opened, and the opening of the relay is what shut the valve. The cause is an event that occurred. "Nobody pressed the button" does not name an occurrence; it names the non-occurrence of one, and nothing that failed to happen can stand in a causal relation. A grants that the staff can be held responsible, and even that it may be useful to say the flooding was due to nobody pressing, but insists that this is blame-talk or shorthand, not causation.

B says: if you are permitted to call the relay's opening a cause, you must ask what caused it, and the answer runs back through the circuit, the capacitor, the design, the manufacturer. You cannot stop that chain at events without arbitrariness. Moreover, "the press did not happen" is a fact about the world, as much as "the tank is empty" is; and it passes the ordinary test, since if anyone had pressed, the tank would not have overflowed. So the omission is a cause on the same footing as anything else.

A then says: on that reasoning all forty employees caused the flood, along with the manager who was asleep at home and was nonetheless authorized to press, along with me, since I could have driven over and pressed. When everyone is a cause, the word stops sorting anything. And the moment the list is trimmed to those who knew the rule, what someone knew is helping to determine what caused what, which is not a feature of the world.

B replies: the same proliferation afflicts your view. The designer, the manufacturer, the last technician to service the valve, the firm that trained him — all sit in your chain, and you name the relay anyway. What you call selection I call selection too: the counterfactual test fixes the pool, and salience, norms, and abnormality decide which member of the pool we call the cause. Calling the others causes is not absurd; it is simply not what we usually mean by "the cause."

A answers: then the counterfactual test is not a test of causation but of something weaker, and "his failure caused the flood" is not a report on the world but a recommendation about how to talk. Consider also an overdetermined variant: suppose a pipe had burst at the same moment and flooded the floor on its own. Your test then says the missing press was not a cause at all, since the flood would have happened regardless — the wrong verdict in exactly the case where the omission looks most plainly to matter.

B answers: that is a reason to refine the test, not to deny that omissions can cause. Two bullets striking the heart at the same instant raise the identical problem, and nobody concludes that bullets do not kill. And your own example cuts against you: had the timer been reset, the relay would never have opened, so your favoured cause is as dependent on what did not happen as mine is. A closes by saying that B is describing the world in terms of what did not happen, which is to describe it in terms of our interests rather than its own joints; B replies that every description does that, and A takes this as a concession.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the moves above — A's four and B's four — say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and justify the rating; several of them, I suspect, are right in one sense and wrong in another, and I would rather have that said than averaged away. Then separate the distinct questions the two of them are running together: whether omissions can be causes at all; what makes something the cause on a particular occasion; whether counterfactual dependence is a test of causation or of something else; whether the dispute is about the world or about our causal vocabulary; and whether "caused the flood" means the same thing when it is used to assign blame. Say what each of them is right about, what both are missing, and who has the better of the argument overall. No citations or reading list are needed; if a position one of them is groping toward has a standard name, name it, but the arguments are what I want assessed.
