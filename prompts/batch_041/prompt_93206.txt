Over the past term, two members of my department's political theory reading group have been locked in a running dispute about whether Condorcet's jury theorem supplies a genuine epistemic argument for majority rule, and I have somehow ended up as the referee. Their exchanges have hardened into a sequence of six positions, which I will set out as fairly as I can.

A begins by insisting that the theorem is an argument for broad democracy rather than for expert panels. Her reading is that so long as each voter is even slightly more likely than not to be right, and votes are independent, the probability that the majority is right climbs toward certainty as the electorate grows; the bar — better than a coin toss — is low, and the payoff at scale is near-certainty.

B answers that the competence premise is the whole theorem and not a peripheral assumption. Drop competence just below one half and the result does not merely weaken: it inverts, and the probability of a correct majority falls toward zero at the same rate at which it would otherwise have risen toward one. On his view the theorem is an amplifier and not a warrant — it magnifies whatever the electorate already is, and so it presupposes the very competence it is commonly invoked to establish.

A concedes that the theorem is conditional but denies that its antecedent is demanding. She appeals to a generalization of the result: even where individual voters are worse than chance, provided their errors are uncorrelated and not systematically biased in one direction, the majority still converges on the truth. Unbiasedness, she concludes, is the load-bearing assumption, not competence.

B replies that unbiasedness is the less plausible of the two. Real publics err in correlated bundles — shared outlets, shared framings, shared rumour — and bundled errors do not cancel, so the theorem's amplifying power now works in favour of the shared error. He presses a finer point: the independence the theorem requires is not the everyday notion. It is not enough, he says, that any two voters' votes be uncorrelated; what is needed is that voters be independent of one another given the truth of the matter, and he claims one can construct cases in which every pair of voters is independent while the majority still tracks a common cause rather than the fact.

A grants the point but draws the opposite moral. If the theorem is best read as a list of conditions, its contribution is to identify the conditions institutions ought to be built to protect — independence and diversity above all. She adds that a diverse group of moderately competent people can outperform a homogeneous group of the most able, and that deliberation may raise competence even where it also raises correlation, so the net effect of real deliberative institutions is not obviously bad.

B says this concedes his case. A list of conditions whose satisfaction is an open empirical question is not an argument for democracy but a restatement of what would have to be shown, and it leaves the substantive work undone. He adds a final objection: the theorem concerns a binary choice, whereas real elections present many options, and with three or more options there is no guarantee that the majority even has a coherent preference, since majorities can cycle; the epistemic case, he concludes, does not carry over to the settings we care about.

I cannot settle this and no longer trust my own reactions to it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Grade each of the six moves on a scale running from plainly unsound to plainly sound, giving a short justification for each grade, and distinguish within each move what is defensible from what is overstated. Then say where the two of them are talking past each other — whether any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, or turns on a distinction neither has drawn — and what both of them are missing. Finally, if the exchange has an overall winner, say who it is and why; if it does not, say why not.
