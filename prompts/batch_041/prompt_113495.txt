I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on practical rationality — eighteen students, mostly philosophy majors, two economists, and three who read decision theory on blogs in high school and are therefore certain they already understand the money pump, which is part of my difficulty. What I want from the session is not the standard observation that intransitive preferences can be exploited; it is the diachronic shape of the thing: a sequence of choices, each of which is beyond reproach at the moment it is made, that leaves the chooser strictly worse off by her own lights, and a conclusion that turns out to be conditional on a premise nobody says out loud — that the chooser cannot see the sequence coming, or cannot bind herself against it. By the end they should have felt, in their hands, that (a) the cost is created by the sequence rather than by any single step, (b) an agent who anticipates the whole thing simply refuses to trade, so the argument shows that intransitivity is expensive for the naive rather than that it is irrational as such, and (c) whether there is any requirement of diachronic coherence over and above getting it right at each moment is exactly the question the game makes urgent and cannot settle. Nobody should hear the words money pump, intransitivity, or diachronic rationality before the debrief.

Design the activity under these constraints. The cycles have to come out of the participants' own choices rather than from a role card that stipulates a taste ranking — I don't want the objection that this isn't really their preference to be available — so give me a way of eliciting pairwise preferences over three or four options that reliably manufactures cycles in a decent fraction of the room, plus a fallback for the people it doesn't catch. Run the trading in at least three conditions: one sequential, each offer appearing only after the previous one has been accepted; one in which the entire sequence is announced before anyone moves; and one in which a participant may pay a small amount to lock her holdings in advance, so that the resolute-choice escape gets discovered rather than described. Put a handful of students in the role of brokers and one in the role of recorder: I want the room split between people being pumped and people doing the pumping, and I want the brokers to report afterwards whether their mark ever refused, and what the refusal cost her. The currency should be points redeemable for one small real prize at the end of the hour — no money changes hands, and the fees must be large enough to hurt relative to that prize, or the whole thing will feel like a toy. I also half-remember an argument that the pump does not need a sequence at all: that you can charge someone for taking an option off the table, on the grounds that a person who cannot rank three options will pay to be guaranteed the best of two. I don't trust my memory of it, so have the debrief say what the game does and does not show about that version, and whether the diachronic framing survives it. Skip the reading list and the citations; I'll supply the secondary literature myself.

Give me the answer as: a Setup section, about 150 words, covering group, room, materials, and what I do in the first five minutes; a The game section, about 500 words, with rules, roles, the elicitation procedure, the payoff scheme, and exact numbers; a Run of play section, about 150 words, as a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; a Debrief section, about 400 words, with the questions I put to the room and the mapping from what happened to the argument, including the strongest version of the response that exploitability simply is not something one needs to care about, and of the view that diachronic rationality is nothing but synchronic rationality applied at each moment; a What will go wrong section, about 250 words, on the predictable failure modes and how to handle each in real time; and a The ceiling section, about 200 words, on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply by talking — including whether the cycles it produces are artifacts of my elicitation rather than genuine intransitive preference, and why that matters for the argument I am teaching.
