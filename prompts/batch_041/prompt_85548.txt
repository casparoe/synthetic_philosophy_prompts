At the university counseling center where I supervise trainees, self-deception has become our most overused and least examined phrase: trainees reach for it whenever a client will not accept a recommendation, and I have no way to tell when they are right. Write the decision procedure we should actually run when a client's stated belief looks motivated — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the judgment back to the clinician. It has to be usable by a trainee without a philosophy background, fit inside a supervision hour, and produce something I can sign off on. Because self-deception is itself contested — on one account it requires a person to hold and disbelieve the same proposition at once, on another it requires only a single belief plus a motivated refusal to look at the evidence — every step should say which account it presupposes, and no step may smuggle in the divided-mind reading by instructing the clinician to uncover what the client already knows. The procedure must also separate self-deception from honest error, weakness of will, strategic impression management, and reasonable optimism. Then run it on three cases: a client who insists his drinking is under control, a client who denies that her marriage is ending, and a client who refuses to accept a terminal prognosis. At least one of them must come out in a way the clinician will not like, and you should close by saying what the procedure cannot decide and where the remaining step is a value judgment rather than a finding.
