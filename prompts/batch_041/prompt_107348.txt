I read a lot of decision-theory blogging, and for a couple of years now I have been tracking the published verdicts of a friend who writes on the same beat — partly out of interest, and partly because I have been saving up one problem to spring on him. His record so far is this.

In the predictor case, where a being who has never once been wrong about you has already put a million dollars in the opaque box if it expected you to take only that box, and nothing if it expected you to take both, he takes only the opaque box — while cheerfully granting that the contents are already fixed and that taking both boxes would be strictly better in whichever world he is actually in. In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma played against a perfect copy of himself, he cooperates. In the poison case, where a paymaster will hand you a large sum at midnight if and only if you can sincerely intend, at that moment, to swallow a painful but harmless poison the next day, he says you take the money and then decline the poison. And on a case I put to him myself — a stranger, decades before your birth, paid a fixed sum into an account for you if and only if they predicted that you would one day give a tenth of your income to famine relief, and the question is whether, on reaching the age at which you could give, you should give — he says you should give, again while agreeing that the deposit is a done deal either way.

He has also written, more than once, that it can be rational to be irrational: that a person may be better off for carrying a standing disposition whose every single act-by-act exercise is against her interest, and that it is a mistake to evaluate such a disposition one decision at a time.

Now the case I actually want to put to him, which I have not yet shown him. You are stranded in the desert with no water and not long to live. A driver pulls up. He is the sort of person who will take you to town only if it is in his own interest to do so, and he has no wish to chase you for money afterward; so before he lets you in, he asks whether you can honestly say that you will hand him a thousand dollars from an ATM once you reach town. He is exceptionally good at reading faces, and he will take you only if he believes you. Once you are in town, nothing you can do will put you back in the desert, and a thousand dollars is a thousand dollars. Will my friend say the traveler survives — that he can answer the driver honestly and then hand over the money — or that he dies out there?

I care less about the verdict than about the following. Give me at least two distinct rules that fit everything in his record above, and say what each one predicts for the hitchhiker. Tell me which you would bet on, and why — including whether anything in his record already breaks the tie. Then name the further case that would separate those rules most cleanly, and say what each rule predicts there.
