I'm a retired chemist — thirty years of it — and since I stopped I've been reading moral philosophy, mostly on how moral norms actually change. Here's a case I keep circling and I want you to really work it.

Take the collapse of dueling in Britain, roughly the 1790s to the 1850s. It was illegal the whole time, denounced from pulpits and by Bentham and the Edinburgh reviewers for decades, and none of that stopped it. What seems to have stopped it is that it became dishonorable to duel, or maybe that the sort of people who dueled stopped being the sort of people who mattered. Now run that through three stories. The interest story, in the Williams mode: some material or class interest made the practice costly, and the losers couldn't hold it up anymore. The argument story: the moral case was always there and eventually it got through, or the arguments got better. The honor story, Appiah's: the practice died when the code of honor that sustained it turned against it, so the mechanism is shame and standing, not argument or profit.

What I want is what each story predicts here, which features each handles and which it fumbles, and then your own verdict. Does the honor story do any seperate work, or is it interest wearing a costume — reputation is an interest, after all — or argument in disguise, since the arguments are what changed what counted as honorable? Push it the other way too: if honor is the engine, it's an engine that ran the practice for two centuries before it ran it down, so what does that say about whether honor is a mechanism of moral progress or just an amplifier that takes whatever content it's handed? And is dueling awkward for the Williams thesis precisely because there's no obvious pile of money in it? Commit to a position, don't give me a survey of the literature, and tell me where your reading feels shaky.
