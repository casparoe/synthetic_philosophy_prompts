I have been arguing with a friend for months about a pair of one-shot games, and I have ended up on the superrational side without ever being sure whether I can defend it. Here is the pair, restated so we are talking about the same thing. In the first game, an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram, charges reversed, before noon the next day, that person will receive a billion dollars; if he receives two or more telegrams, or none at all, nobody gets anything; and the twenty are forbidden to communicate or collude. The answer I accept is that you send the telegram with probability one in twenty: not with certainty, since twenty certain senders win nothing, and not never, since then nobody wins anything. In the second game, a magazine announces a lottery in which entering once means mailing a postcard with a 1 on it, entering many times means mailing a postcard with the larger number you choose, and the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to the submitter of one randomly chosen entry. The answer I accept here is the analogous one: roll a die with as many sides as you believe there are serious entrants, and mail a single entry only if it comes up one. What I believe, stated as the thing I want tested, is this: in a one-shot symmetric game with no communication, where each player knows the others are the sort of reasoner who will reach the same conclusion from the same problem, the right choice is the one that maximizes expected payoff on the supposition that everyone who reasons as you do makes the same choice, and this is a requirement of practical rationality rather than a convention, a heuristic for the friendly, or a piece of advice that lapses once the game is real and the stakes are mine. My friend says this is magical thinking, that I am smuggling a causal connection between my own reasoning and the reasoning of nineteen strangers; I cannot tell whether the objection is decisive or is merely the standard external complaint that any non-causal rationality will attract.

I want a map of this position's exposure rather than a defense of it. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward, and for each item say what would have to be true for it to be a genuine threat rather than a debating point, and whether a committed defender would concede it or dismiss it and on what grounds. I care especially about five things. First, whether the prescription in the twenty-person game sits badly with the intuition that grounds the whole view: the grounding thought is that identical reasoners reach the same answer, yet the recommended play has you and nineteen others doing different things nineteen times out of twenty, so either the grounding thought is not doing the work I think it is, or it is doing work that a mixed strategy cannot carry. Second, whether my position is a rival to ordinary decision theory at all, or is just expected-utility maximization under a particular and questionable prior about which agents count as reasoning like me, and what becomes of it if that reference class is ambiguous: do I include the nineteen others, everyone who read the same article, or anyone anywhere who would accept the same advice, and does the answer change when I cannot say? Third, whether the view has anything determinate to say once the players stop being identical, for instance when one of them is known to be impulsive, or when stakes differ across players, or when I know that one other person will send regardless, and whether the silence or the verdict in those cases is a defect or merely a boundary. Fourth, what my position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether any of those predictions have been or could be tested, since I would rather know it is risky than be told it is safe. Fifth, whether the position has been quietly insulated by qualifications, and if so whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or simply what any normative view must do. Say too what a shared random device would change, given that twenty reasoners who could jointly designate a single sender would do strictly better than the play I am recommending, and whether that makes my answer a stopping point or merely a way station.

The thing that would move me most is a demonstration that the twenty-person answer is an artifact of the symmetry of the case rather than something derivable from a general rule, or that the view answers confidently only where its grounding intuition is idle and goes silent or arbitrary exactly where it is asked to do real work. I would also be moved by a showing that the position cannot be distinguished from a rival I already reject, whether that rival is evidential decision theory or a team-reasoning ideal that presupposes a shared precommitment device I do not have. I am not interested in reading lists, citations, or who said what; I want the arguments worked out, with the weakest links named as such.
