Title: Repro steps or it didn't happen

Every company I've worked at has some version of the same ritual. Something breaks for a user or a support person or a designer, they file a ticket describing it, and the first engineering response is a request for a minimal reproduction. No repro, no investigation. I used to think this was just triage, and I still think the cost argument behind it is real — you can't debug a vibe, and an hour spent chasing an unreproducible report is an hour not spent on something else.

But I've started to think the repro requirement is doing a second job that nobody admits to. It's a credibility filter wearing the costume of a cost filter.

Here's the distinction I want to defend. When someone reports a problem and they don't have my vocabulary, I can discount them in two different ways. Call the first one explanation-discounting: I accept that something happened to them, but I don't accept their theory of why. They say the app forgot their address, I translate that into a stale cache keyed on session ID and go looking there. That seems fine to me. Their observation is data; their mechanism is a guess.

Call the second one observation-discounting: I don't accept that anything happened at all, or I accept it only provisionally, pending proof. They say the app forgot their address, and I hear that they probably fat-fingered it and don't want to say so. Nothing gets investigated until they produce a video.

My claim is that the repro requirement is a machine for sliding from the first to the second, and that the slide is almost invisible from the inside because the policy sounds neutral. Everyone needs a repro is technically true, but the engineer filing a bug doesn't need one, because we already believe the engineer. The requirement is only ever enforced downward. So a policy that looks like it's about evidence is actually a policy about whose reports start out as evidence and whose reports start out as claims.

Second claim, and this is the one I'm least sure about: part of this isn't about anyone's attitude at all. There's a vocabulary gap that's structural. If the product has no word for the thing that's going wrong — no way to say the state got out of sync across tabs, in the interface or in the ticket form — then the person experiencing it genuinely can't describe it in terms I'll recognize, and they'll describe it in terms that sound like user error. So even a perfectly fair-minded engineer, with no bias whatsoever, ends up with a filtered intake. The unfairness is baked into the vocabulary, not into the person.

Objection I keep running into: this is just inefficiency, not injustice. Maybe. But I don't think injustice requires a villain, and I notice that the people whose reports get filtered are not randomly distributed across the org. Support, design, junior QA, and basically anyone outside engineering. That pattern is what makes me think it's not just noise.

What I want from you:

1. Is the explanation-discount / observation-discount distinction actually a real distinction, or am I dressing up "sometimes you need more info" in fancy clothes?
2. Is the claim that the repro requirement is only ever enforced downward true, or am I pattern-matching off three companies?
3. The vocabulary-gap section — is it doing any work, or is it decoration on the first claim? And if it is a separate thing, is it a seperate *kind* of thing, or the same phenomenon at a different scale?
4. Where does this overreach? Specifically, does the move from "this pattern is bad" to "this is an injustice" survive contact with someone who actually thinks about this stuff, or does it need a lot more argument than I've given it?
5. Is there a standard name for what I'm describing in parts 1 and 3? I have a feeling there is and that I'm about to badly reinvent a wheel. I'd rather be told the name and told where I'm getting it wrong than keep going.

And honestly, at the bottom of all of it: is any of this interesting, or is it the kind of thing that reads as obvious the moment you say it out loud?
