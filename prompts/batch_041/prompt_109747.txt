I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of artificial intelligence, and we have been spending an unusual amount of time on the section about extra-sensory perception in Turing's 1950 paper. Most of my students treat that section as a quaint aside: Turing is willing to grant telepathy for the sake of argument, worries that telepathy would break the imitation game, and then proposes that the game be conducted in a room shielded from telepathy. I want a rigorous argument that this section is in fact the most philosophically revealing moment in the paper, because it exposes a conditionality in Turing's operationalism that the standard objections do not. Please argue for the following position: the imitation game cannot serve as a self-sufficient operational definition of intelligence, since it sorts for intelligence only if we antecedently know that the communication channel is free of paranormal leakage; the shielded room is therefore not a minor technical fix but an external boundary condition that the test itself cannot certify. In making the case, explain why the theological, mathematical, consciousness, and disability objections do not force the same concession, and say what would follow for the test if telepathy were real but irregular.

Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
