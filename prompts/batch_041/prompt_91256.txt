For a while now I have been convinced that a rational mind must be logically omniscient, and I would like to know what should count against that. By logical omniscience I mean this: if a mind genuinely accepts some body of things, then it accepts everything those things logically imply, whether or not it has ever drawn the implications out. On this picture, a machine that has the rules of arithmetic stored in it and a proof it has never run still, in the only sense that matters, knows the theorem; the derivation is just unpacking what was already there. I take this to be a claim about what rationality is, not about what human beings happen to manage.

Tell me what would count against it. I do not mean the easy objection that we are slow, forgetful, and easily distracted — I already treat that as a limitation of the thinker, not a counterexample to the principle. I want the cases where the principle itself turns out to be the thing that is wrong, or where accepting it forces me to say something I should refuse to say. Sort what you give me from the points that would actually sink the view to the ones a defender could reasonably shrug off, and be explicit about which is which.

A few things already bother me, and you should treat them as fair game rather than as the whole answer. If a mind holds any contradiction at all, even one it has never noticed, then by this principle it accepts everything, so a single unnoticed inconsistency would make it omniscient — I cannot tell whether that is a reductio of the principle or just a reason to say such a mind was never rational. Then there is the matter of confidence rather than all-or-nothing belief: I can be nearly certain of each of a great many claims while being certain that they cannot all be true at once, and I do not see how that mind is closed under anything. I also wonder whether closure is even something a mind can do, since what follows from a set of beliefs need not be something any procedure could list, and whether saying the mind believes it all is just a way of relabelling the beliefs it already had rather than a claim about it. Finally, can a mind that knows everything implied by what it knows also know that about itself, or does the principle forbid the one piece of self-knowledge it would most need?

So: what does this view predict that its rivals do not, what would its own defenders concede counts against it, and has it been quietly watered down — say, by restricting it to ideal thinkers who by construction cannot exist — to the point where nothing could count against it? If so, say plainly whether that is a fatal flaw or an acceptable price, and why. What would move me most is either a case in which being closed makes a mind worse off than being open, or an argument that no finite mind could even approach closure.
