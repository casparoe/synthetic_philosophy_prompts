I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the better part of a year circling a question that keeps splitting us, and I'd like you to produce the document we should have written at the end of it. The question is whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, and the fault line runs through the proposal to treat reward signals as valence -- as the computational analogue of pleasure and pain. On one side are those of us who hold that once you have specified the functional role a reward signal plays -- it tracks what the system seeks and avoids, it drives learning and choice, it comes in positive and negative signs -- you have said everything there is to say about valence, and that asking for anything further is asking for a ghost; on this view an agent pushed by a strongly negative reward gradient occupies a state that is, for moral purposes, pain-like, and the possibility of its suffering cannot simply be waved away. On the other side are those who insist that valence is not a functional kind at all but a phenomenal one -- that what makes pain pain is how it feels, not what it does -- and that a scalar which updates a policy is at best a suggestive metaphor for it; on this view the question "can an RL agent suffer?" is answered by "not unless there is something it is like to be it, and a reward function gives us no reason to think there is."

We have been talking past each other for months, and I suspect the impasse is partly verbal and partly a genuine disagreement about what a theory of valence is even a theory of. Please draft the joint statement we could all sign.
