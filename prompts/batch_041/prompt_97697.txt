I am the modal reference editor for a shared-world fiction archive that publishes stories set in the same invented universe but in different branches: some branches are meant to be the same history with a different turn, others are meant to be full counterpart worlds with no shared individuals. My editorial assistants screen roughly twenty submissions a month, and each submission may contain identity claims — that a name in the story picks out the same entity as a name in the archive, or that a natural-kind term in the story picks out the same substance, or that two descriptions are co-referential in that branch. We have no in-house philosopher, decisions have to be made in under half an hour, and they have to be defensible to authors who know the archive's canon but not the metaphysics. The archive has also learned the hard way that a term can be rigid within a branch but not across possible worlds, or vice versa, and my assistants keep conflating those two questions. I need you to turn the theory of rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori into a decision procedure my assistants can actually run.

Write the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, what answer stops the procedure with a no, and what answer sends the case to me because it turns on a judgment call rather than a fact. The procedure must handle at least these three recurring disputes: (1) a submission claims that in its branch the morning star and the evening star are two different bodies, not one; (2) a submission claims that in its branch the substance everyone calls water is not H2O but a different compound with the same observable behavior; (3) a submission claims that in its branch the person everyone calls Napoleon is a different individual, born to the same parents and living the same life, but not the same person. It also has to decide, for each case, whether the claim is being offered as a necessary a posteriori truth within the branch, as a contingent authorial stipulation, or as a category mistake. And it has to say, at the point where the distinction matters, whether the question is rigidity within a branch or rigidity across possible worlds.

Then run the procedure on all three cases and give me a verdict for each. Include at least one verdict I will not like — for instance, if the procedure forces me to reclassify a flagship story from same-world continuation to counterpart world, or to reject a star author's premise — and defend that verdict rather than softening it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which disputes it will always leave to me, which distinctions it loses in the simplification, and where an assistant is likely to mistake a value judgment for a factual test.

I want a committed answer, not a survey. Choose one ordering of the questions and one set of stopping rules, defend why that ordering is the right one, and give definite verdicts on the cases. If you think a step is a judgment call, say so and say whose judgment it should be. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to the literature; I need a procedure I can hand to my assistants.
