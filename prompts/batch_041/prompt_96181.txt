I have been trying to settle what I think about copies, forgeries and originals, and my verdicts keep arranging themselves into two patterns that will not lie down together. I should like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts on them.

1. A forger paints a canvas that no available test can distinguish from a lost Vermeer. Shown to me as a Vermeer, it moves me exactly as the genuine article would; my experience of it is not diminished in the slightest. Yet I do not think it is worth a fraction of what the Vermeer would fetch, and I do not think that is only a fact about the market. Something has been lost.

2. The same canvas, exposed, and hung as a modern forgery after Vermeer. My experience of it changes — I now look at it as a feat of skill — but I find it genuinely interesting, and I would not call it worthless. It seems to have become a different work rather than a debased copy of the first.

3. A museum's plaster cast of a Greek bronze whose original is long lost. I value it highly, as art, and I do not feel in it the loss I feel in case 1, although no one supposes that the sculptor's hand ever touched it.

4. A paperback of a novel, or a digital copy of its text. I think the work is wholly present, and that the first edition's additional value is the value of an object, not of the work.

5. A student company dancing a canonical dance work, faithfully. I take it to be the same work, performed. But I would pay far more to see the original cast, and I do not think the difference is mere spectacle: something of the work is better realised in them.

6. A photographer's print, made by the photographer, and a later print struck from the same negative by a skilled technician, visually indistinguishable from it. I think the later print is a lesser work, though I cannot say what it lacks.

7. A photograph taken digitally. The file on the photographer's laptop and a perfect copy of it seem to me of exactly equal worth; the notion of the 'original' has no grip here, and I would not cross the road for the original file.

8. A letter in a famous hand, and a facsimile of it. The facsimile tells me everything the letter tells me, and I still think the letter is worth vastly more, and rightly so.

9. An artist re-photographs another artist's photograph without alteration and exhibits the result as their own work. I think the second is a genuine work and not merely a copy: the making of it and the claiming of it as one's own seem to have produced something.

My own guess is that value attaches to the act that produced the object rather than to the object's appearance, and that the act is individuated by its history — so that the value is the value of a certain contact between this object and that act. That seems to get 1, 6, 8 and 9 right. But it then obliges me to say that the file in 7 is worth more than its copies, and that the cast in 3 and the paperback in 4 are worth less than what they reproduce, and I believe none of that. The opposite principle — that value attaches to the perceptible pattern, and that the history of the particular token is irrelevant to the work — gets 3, 4 and 7 right and overturns 1, 6 and 8.

What I want is one of two things. Either a single principle, stated precisely, that yields all nine verdicts — I will accept complexity, provided each clause is doing identifiable work and you tell me which verdict forces it — or a demonstration that no such principle exists, together with a recommendation as to which verdict I should abandon and why the surviving principle is worth that cost. In either case, say what your principle implies for at least two further cases of your own devising in this area, preferably ones where its verdict is not obvious, and state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
