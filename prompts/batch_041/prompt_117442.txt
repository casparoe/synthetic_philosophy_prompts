I am a retired geophysicist. For thirty-odd years my trade was the inversion of surface measurements for structure I could never see directly — travel times and waveforms in, a velocity model out — and much of my thinking since has been about what it means to call such a model right when the data never pin it down uniquely. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, mostly the parts adjacent to what people in machine learning call alignment, and I have run into a debate that looks from where I sit like a version of my old trade conducted without the vocabulary of my old trade. I would like you to carry it across.

The debate, as I understand it, concerns whether an agent's preferences can be inferred from its behaviour. One camp holds that if you model the agent as approximately optimal with respect to some reward function — choosing actions that tend to produce high reward — then observations of its choices are evidence about that reward, and recovering the reward, or enough of it to build a system that does what the agent wants, is a tractable problem. Another camp holds that the inference is underdetermined in ways that more data of the same kind will not repair: that the optimality assumption, the choice of which reward functions one is willing to consider, and the notion of a preference itself are all doing unearned work, so that behaviour is compatible with so many preference structures that talk of recovering the agent's preferences describes the modeller's choices rather than discovering a fact about the agent. A third position, which may be the practical one, concedes the underdetermination and says the goal was never the true reward but a policy the human finds acceptable, judged case by case. I gather that some of the non-uniqueness is already catalogued — transformations of a reward that provably leave the induced choices unchanged — and that the live disagreements are about what remains once those are set aside. I would like to know what that remainder looks like to someone who thinks in my terms.

I know the vocabulary of my own field well: forward model, inverse problem, ill-posedness, identifiability, null space, regularization, resolution, uncertainty quantification, model misspecification, observational equivalence, and the difference between an estimate and the thing estimated. I know the machine-learning side only from reading. I can follow reward functions, policies, and Markov decision processes well enough to read a paper, but I have no craft in the subject, and I have no philosophy training at all. My instinct on first reading was that most of the alarm was a rediscovery of the fact that inverse problems do not have unique solutions. I want to know whether that instinct survives contact with the details, and I do not want the translation to succeed by quietly redefining their terms until they land on mine.

Please write the response in the following sections, in this order.

1. Bottom line — no more than 120 words, before anything else.
2. The dictionary — 200 to 300 words. A working mapping in both directions between their terms and mine, flagging each entry where the correspondence is loose rather than exact.
3. The debate, restated — 700 to 900 words. The three positions above, set out as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, with the arguments each camp would actually make. Where a move turns out to be a familiar one in inverse theory under another name, say so plainly and name the familiar move.
4. What carried across cleanly — 200 to 300 words.
5. The residue — 500 to 700 words. Itemise what did not survive, distinguishing three cases: claims my vocabulary cannot express at all; claims it expresses only by distortion; claims it expresses better than the original did. I am particularly interested in whether a normative assumption, such as the assumption that the agent is doing what it should, functions as a forward model in the relevant sense, and in whether my field's notion of model error has any room for the case where the model is a claim about what the agent ought to do; in whether it matters that the target of the inference can read the inference and respond to it; in whether there is a fact of the matter about a preference for a situation the agent has never faced; and in the status of a person's own report of what they want when it conflicts with what they do, given that in my field two disagreeing data streams about one latent quantity mean noise or a wrong forward model and nothing more exotic.
6. Reading the residue — 300 to 400 words. For each item, whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked, and whether my instinct that this is an ordinary ill-posed inverse problem is right or is a parochial reaction of a man who has spent his life in one trade.

I am not asking for reading recommendations or an account of who said what; I want the argument carried across and an honest account of the loss. Pitch it at someone who knows inverse problems cold and philosophy barely at all.
