Hey — I run a small reading group that's half rationalist-blog people, half lapsed analytic phil grad students, and we just spent two weeks on Goodman's grue. Problem is everybody walks in already knowing the punchline ("green is entrenched, grue isn't") so the discussion never actually goes anywhere. I want a take-home question that forces them to run the machinery on cases they haven't memorized.

Here's the setup I want to hand them. Three predicates on emeralds, all of which agree with "green" on every emerald anyone has ever looked at:

(a) grue, the classic — green if first examined before 2100, blue if first examined after.
(b) meridigrue — green if the sample is found north of the equator, blue if south of it.
(c) retrogrue — blue if first examined before 2020, green if first examined after.

Then the question is: for (b) and (c), does the standard entrenchment answer give the right verdict, and does either case show that the riddle isn't really about time / isn't really about weird predicates at all? I want them to notice that one of these is a live empirical question you could go test this afternoon and the other one quietly isn't a paradox at all, and to say what that difference does and doesn't buy them. And I want at least one of the must-hit points to be them confronting the circularity charge against entrenchment head-on rather than waving at it.

So please write me, in this order and roughly these lengths:

- The exam question, ~100 words, written to the students (no preamble, just the prompt I'd paste into the take-home).
- Model answer, ~550 words, the kind of thing that should get full marks.
- Marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each, 20 total, each point stated as the specific move the answer has to make (not "mentions entrenchment" — the actual claim).
- Where they go wrong: one line per must-hit point naming the specific mistake that loses those marks.

Level: upper-level, students have read Goodman once. Don't ask for citations or reading lists, they can't look anything up.
