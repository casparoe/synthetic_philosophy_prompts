I am a retired process-safety engineer — thirty-one years in the chemical industry, including a long stint on an industrial tribunal — and I have spent the first two years of my retirement reading for an MA in philosophy. My module convenor, who has discovered this, has asked me to draft one of the questions for this year's paper on Blame, Hypocrisy and Standing, and I should like you to serve as my examiner-in-reserve. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher who has examined in moral responsibility for many years, think carefully about where the difficulty of a good question ought to lie, and begin by sketching for yourself what a genuinely discriminating answer would have to do before you write anything down. The students have had the ordinary diet of material on the non-hypocrisy condition, on the difference between blaming someone and holding them responsible, and on excuses and justifications; I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting that diet back at me. I want one that forces them to commit themselves on what standing is for.

I have therefore invented a case, set in a research institute, on which the question is to turn. Dr Hart, a senior researcher, was several years ago found to have fabricated results in two papers; she was sanctioned, has been candid about it since, and now sits on the institute's research-integrity panel. Dr Neale, a colleague of similar standing, has just been found to have fabricated a figure in a grant application. At a laboratory meeting Hart publicly rebukes Neale, calls the conduct inexcusable and says she should resign. Four features of the case are to be pressed: first, Neale's fabrication was done under precisely the career pressure that Hart herself cites as having driven her own; second, Neale maintains, not implausibly, that she took Hart's earlier conduct, and the mildness of its reception, as showing how things are done here; third, the person most harmed by Neale's fabrication is a doctoral student whose unpublished results she used without acknowledgement, and Hart presents her rebuke as speaking for him; fourth, Hart has nothing to gain and is plainly not acting from self-interest. I want the paper to introduce, in its own words, two rival accounts of what standing is — call them the gatekeeping thesis, on which standing is a condition on the very act of blaming, so that a blamer who lacks it has not blamed at all, and the uptake thesis, on which standing concerns only whether the person blamed has reason to take the rebuke to heart — and to require candidates to adjudicate between them using the case. Candidates will have seventy-five minutes, may bring no texts, and are not expected to cite anything; please make that explicit on the paper. Structure the question in three parts: part (a), ten marks, on whether Hart has standing and on what turns on each of the four features; part (b), nine marks, on the adjudication between the two accounts and on what would count as evidence for each; part (c), six marks, on whether it matters that Hart's own wrongdoing was excused or excusable rather than justified, and what that implies for the tu quoque. Twenty-five marks in total.

What I need from you is, first, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the case, the parts, the marks and the time allowance; second, a model answer of about seven hundred words, written as a first-class candidate would write it and leaning on no named author or text; and third, a marking scheme that sets out the must-hit moves for each part with the marks attached to each, followed in every case by a single line on how candidates characteristically lose those marks, and closing with two or three sentences on what separates a first from an upper second. Please write the whole of it — question, model answer, marking scheme and your own commentary — in British English, in spelling and in idiom, and use no quotations or citations anywhere, since neither I nor the candidates will have a library to hand.
