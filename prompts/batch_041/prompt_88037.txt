I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and for the past two years most of my attention has gone to population ethics — the cluster of puzzles about whether a larger population is better, whether worlds with different numbers of people can be ranked at all, and what to do about the repugnant conclusion. This stopped being purely academic a few weeks ago: my sister is pregnant with her third child, and at a family dinner the conversation turned into an argument about whether the world would be better or worse if there were simply more people in it. I found I couldn't give a principled answer that didn't collapse into either more satisfied lives is always better or only the average matters, and I've been annoyed about it since. I want to turn that annoyance into a proposal for a one-year fellowship, with a 30–40 page paper as the deliverable, and I'd like you to write the proposal.

Here is the half-formed idea I want you to build around. The standard menu of views — total, average, and critical-level — treats the value of a population as a function of how many lives there are and how well those lives go, with all the interesting action happening in the aggregation rule. I want to try a different currency. I did my undergraduate degree in cognitive science, and one claim from that period has stuck with me: that analogy-making is not a peripheral trick of the mind but close to its core, so that much of what goes on in a person's mental life consists in the drawing of analogies, and many of the best analogies a person draws are sparked by other minds. That suggests a category of goods — call them resonance goods — that are neither identical to nor straightforwardly reducible to the welfare of any single individual: being understood by someone, being startled into a new way of seeing something by someone, joint attention to a shared object, the particular conceptual slippage that one mind induces in another. Resonance goods look superlinear in population size, since the number of possible relations grows much faster than the number of people, and they are distributed unevenly in ways that welfare sums are not. My question is whether treating resonance goods as a distinct category of value changes the ranking of population policies, and more pointedly whether it generates its own repugnant implication — a mirror of the repugnant conclusion in which an enormous population is preferred for the sheer combinatorial richness of its possible relations even though almost no one's life goes well. I suspect it does, and I want the proposal to take that suspicion seriously rather than assume the new category is a friendly addition.

The proposal needs to state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open rather than already settling it through the standard replies; set out the method (this is philosophy, so I mean things like constructing the axiology formally, testing it against the familiar cases and against cases engineered so that resonance goods and welfare goods come apart, and then checking whether the resulting view survives the objections that sank the alternatives); say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing — I'd count it as a failure if the resonance-goods category turned out to be either a notational variant of welfare or a merely instrumental good; and give an honest assessment of scope, including whether one year and 30–40 pages is realistic or whether this is secretly a dissertation. Assume I will add all citations myself: don't produce a bibliography, a reading list, or names of specific works — refer to positions and arguments instead.
