I run a fortnightly working group that mixes philosophy-of-science students with people from a computational-neuroscience lab: fourteen regulars, one hundred minutes, one seminar room, a whiteboard, no computers, and I want the session to be a game rather than a discussion. What I want them to feel in their hands is that flagging an anomaly and deciding that two things are the same mechanism are one decision made twice — that you cannot say a system has gone wrong until you have settled what would count as the same system, and that the criteria people reach for (the same parts in the same arrangement; the same responses to intervention; the same input-output profile) are not equivalent and pull apart precisely in the interesting cases: a rewired system that behaves identically, a system that behaves differently with every part intact, a system whose failure is masked by a redundant or degenerate path, a system where what one description treats as a single part is a whole sub-circuit under a finer grain. I would like the rules arranged so that participants commit to a criterion in writing before they see the evidence, and then spend the session discovering what the commitment costs them. No reading list or citations needed — I want the runnable activity, plus your debrief notes.

The shape I have in mind, though you should redesign it freely: two or three teams of maintenance crews each hold a paper implementation of the same small machine — a board of six to eight nodes with directed links, chips or tokens for values, and a step rule that propagates values each round — and each crew's board is wired differently from the others while being behaviourally identical on every input the crews can reach. One crew's single node should secretly be a two-node sub-circuit in another crew's board, and at least one board should contain two routes to the same effect so that intervening on either alone changes nothing. Crews may run their machine and apply interventions (cut a link, pin a node to a value, add a load) but may not inspect each other's boards; they see only a shared log of readings, some of which deviate at an intermediate node while the output is unchanged, others only at the output, others nowhere until you intervene. Each crew files a written anomaly report with a threshold and a criterion of sameness stated up front, and the scoring should be arranged so that no single criterion is uniformly best, with a trivial prize so the stakes are real but silly. I expect they will end up arguing about identity rather than about the data, which is the point.

Then the debrief: map each disputed case onto a criterion of mechanism identity and onto the kind of explanatory question that makes that criterion the right one, and show them how the same dispute recurs when people compare two trained networks with matching behaviour and different internal circuits, or two organisms that reach the same function by different routes. Be frank with me about what the activity cannot reproduce — real mechanisms have timing, continuous dynamics, and nested levels of constitution that a discrete board flattens, and my boards are finite and fully observable, so the underdetermination I want them to feel is engineered by hiding the wiring; once the boards are shown, the puzzle collapses into bookkeeping. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect at least one crew to spend half the session reverse-engineering the other board rather than filing reports, and at least one participant to insist it is all semantics), how to time-box it, and what the facilitator has to supply verbally because the activity cannot stage it.
