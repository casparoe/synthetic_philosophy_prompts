I am the consulting historian for a new permanent gallery at a natural history museum, and over the next five months I have to write or approve roughly 120 object labels, a dozen audio stops, and an eight-minute film script. The gallery's thesis is that non-human things — pathogens, climate, volcanoes, pests, soils — have been actors in human history, not scenery. That thesis is currently getting me into trouble from two directions at once. A reviewer from the history department objects that my drafts are environmental determinism with better production values, and that we are reviving a tradition whose worst versions ranked peoples by climate. A reviewer from the earth sciences side objects that when we hedge, we bury the actual physical evidence behind a fog of agency-talk about emperors and merchants, and end up telling the same old story in which only people do anything.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my label writers can actually run — people with good general educations and no graduate training in history, working about ten minutes per label, under a hard sixty-word limit, at a reading level a fourteen-year-old can follow, with no footnotes and no citations. Each label has to survive review by the lead curator, by an external historian, and — for the sections on plague and on the conquest of the Americas — by a community advisory panel. The procedure should return one of a small fixed set of verdicts, and for each verdict it should say what wording that verdict licenses in sixty words. It needs to contain, at minimum:

1. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than evidence — and which human being that is.
2. A rule about grammar, since I have noticed that agency gets smuggled in by syntax: when a non-human factor may be the subject of the sentence, and when it may appear only as a modifier, a date, or a background clause.
3. A rule for the four-way case where the physical evidence is settled but the historiography is not, and the reverse.
4. A screen that separates a claim about how a non-human factor shaped the terms in which things happened from a claim that it explains why one people's history diverged from another's.
5. Something to do about the fact that "the mosquito," "the volcano," and "the plague" are shorthand for long chains of engineering, settlement, trade, and finance — so that naming the natural thing as the actor sometimes hides the human decisions that made it decisive.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the labels it produces: the volcanic dust veils of 536–541 and the Justinianic plague that followed; the 1815 Tambora eruption and the harvest failures of the following year; and the malaria epidemic and famine in Egypt in 1942, which I have been quoting in the form of Timothy Mitchell's argument that the mosquito did more killing there than the battle everyone remembers. I expect one of the three to come out in a way I will not like, and I want that one flagged and argued rather than softened.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and where a writer who follows it will still produce something a good historian would object to. Do not send me a reading list; I need the procedure, not the bibliography.
