I am assembling a handout for the opening meeting of a graduate seminar on moral worth and acting for the right reasons, and I would like that handout to take the form of a decision tree rather than a survey of positions. Please write the tree.

Begin from the most general question that divides the field and proceed through questions a student could answer about concrete cases without already knowing which labelled position lies downstream. The organising question is what makes an action morally worthy in the distinctively moral sense, and I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from a roll-call of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space.

The following distinctions must do real branching work, in whatever order you find most perspicuous: whether moral worth is a property of actions, of agents and their motives, or of neither; whether the agent's operative motive must be the very feature that makes the action right, or whether it suffices that she act from the thought of duty or take herself to be acting rightly; whether an occurrent motive is required or a standing disposition will do; whether worth admits of degrees; whether culpable ignorance defeats it; and whether the action must actually be right, or a well-motivated but mistaken agent can act worthily.

Each leaf should carry the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want these leaves to appear: the strict acting-from-duty view; a quality-of-will view on which acting for the right reasons suffices without the thought of duty; a view on which the agent's operative reason must coincide with the right-making reason; a character-expression view; a view on which worth attaches to acts or to the agent's causal contribution and not to motive at all; a hybrid on which a good motive is necessary but not sufficient; and a sceptical leaf on which the notion should be abandoned.

Use these cases as the tests at the branch nodes: a bystander who rescues from spontaneous compassion; a donor who gives to an effective charity chiefly for the pleasure of feeling superior; a soldier who does what is required from drilled obedience; one person who acts rightly for an irrelevant but benign reason and another who acts rightly for a bad reason that happens to be the very consideration that makes the act right; someone who refuses to join a wrongdoing out of cowardice and a distaste for scenes, which I have been calling the reluctant accomplice; and a well-meaning but factually mistaken agent whose act is objectively wrong.

I should also like the tree to locate a position I have been developing, which I call the competence view: that moral worth tracks the exercise of a reliable practical competence, neither the agent's motive nor the coincidence of motive with right-making features. Say which existing leaf it sits nearest to and exactly where it comes apart.

Note any combinations of answers that no one occupies, and diagnose briefly whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate rather than papering over the gap. Keep the whole thing to roughly eight to ten leaves.

For each leaf, state how confident you are that the position as you have characterised it is one that anyone actually holds, and close by naming the single placement in the tree you are least sure of and why.
