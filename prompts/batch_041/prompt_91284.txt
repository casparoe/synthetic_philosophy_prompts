I'm a science journalist and I'm about two thousand words into a piece about whether a book could be conscious — you know the old thought experiment where they keep Einstein's brain alive and you sit down and have a conversation with it. A philosopher I interviewed told me flatly that no book could ever be conscious, no matter how clever the book is. I said, come on, that can't be right, and now I actually have to defend it, and my defense has a conclusion I can't swallow. Here's the argument the way I'd put it in the draft:

(1) What makes a system conscious is the information processing it does, not the stuff its made of. Software, not hardware.

(2) Information processing is defined by the relations among a system's states. It's an abstract pattern, and the same pattern can be carried by any number of diffrent physical set-ups.

(3) And you can always describe a physical thing as carrying out a given pattern. Take a book, or a rock, or the solar system: match its successive configurations to the states of the pattern, in order, so that the transitions come out right. Nothing stops you, and nobody insists the match be a natural one.

(4) So a rock carries out whatever computation Einstein's brain carries out when it's thinking. So does a book — and if you like, a book with a page for every question and its answer, so that flipping to the right page hands you the reply.

(5) If (1) is true, the rock and the book are conscious.

(6) But they obviously aren't. Nothing is happening in there.

So (1) is false, consciousness must depend on the material after all, and the one thing I was sure of is the thing I have to give up. Which step do I drop instead, why did it look so good at the time, and what does dropping it cost me? And I need to know where the book lands, because my whole piece is that the book could be conscious and a rock obviously can't be — if your fix saves me from the rock by throwing out the book too, I'd rather hear that now. I'd take (1) over (5) any day, so if there's a way to keep it, show me, but give me the bill. (I think the view I'm defending is called functionalism, if that helps.)
