ok, I have to hand in a short position paper for a seminar on explanation in the social sciences next week and I want to check my views on this stuff actually hang together before I commit them to paper. Heres roughly where I've landed:

1. Ideas and values can be genuine causes in history. Weber's basic move in the Protestant Ethic strikes me as right — the ethic wasn't just decoration on the rise of capitalism, it did work.
2. I'm a methodological individualist. Every social explanation has to cash out in facts about individual people: what they believed, wanted, were disposed to do. No group minds, no "society decided".
3. That said, selection-style explanations look perfectly legitimate to me. You can explain why an institution persists by showing that it out-competes the alternatives, even if nobody involved intended that or saw it coming.
4. Recovering the norm an agent was following, and thereby making the action intelligible, is a complete explanation in its own right. It doesn't need to be topped up with a causal claim about the norm having a grip on them.
5. The is-ought gap is real. No set of purely descriptive premises is going to entail a normative conclusion.
6. I read Weber as doing something fundamentally interpretive — the payoff is making the Calvinist's conduct make sense from the inside, not slotting it under a law.
7. When I say X caused Y in this territory I mean the counterfactual thing: without X, Y wouldn't have happened, or would have gone differently. Causal talk that can't survive a counterfactual test isn't causal talk.
8. And a concrete verdict: if the same ethic turned up in two otherwise similar regions and only one of them took off economically, I'd still want to call the ethic a cause of what happened in the one that took off. Causes don't have to be unique to the outcome they explain, as far as I can tell.
