I'm a senior at a public high school, and last summer I took an online course on AI safety; the instructors have since asked a few of us to write one exam question each for the cohort's mock final, and I've been assigned the topic of low-impact AI. My classmates are sharp, but they sit the exam without internet access and the course has no textbook, so the question has to stand entirely on its own: it can't assume familiarity with any particular paper, and it shouldn't reward anyone for having memorized a slogan. What I want to test is whether they can reason about the following puzzle instead of reciting the phrase "side effects." As I understand it from the course, the puzzle runs like this. We want an agent to accomplish its assigned objective while leaving the rest of the world alone, and the natural way to formalize leaving the world alone is to compare the world after the agent acts with the world as it would have been had the agent not acted, then penalize the difference. But the difference requires some measure of how far apart two world states are, and every measure I can think of fails in one of two ways. A physical measure, such as counting atoms displaced or pixels changed, ranks a breeze stirring a million leaves as a greater change than one irreversible harm, so it plainly isn't what we mean. A measure that weights each change by how much it matters to someone is exactly the value specification we were trying to avoid needing in the first place. There are further wrinkles I'd like the question to force them to confront. A world with no agent in it is not the same as a world containing an idle agent, and other agents are acting in the baseline too, so "doing nothing" may not be a well-defined counterfactual at all. An agent that does nothing scores perfectly on the impact measure alone, which means the measure can only ever function as a penalty term beside the goal, and the trade-off between the two then does real work that nobody has specified. And any measure that effectively says preserve the world as it is seems to smuggle in a substantive preference for the status quo, so that an agent would decline to intervene in something bad precisely because intervening would be high-impact. I have also read the suggestion that impact might be defined not as change but as what remains reachable, an agent being low-impact to the extent that it doesn't foreclose options previously open to anyone, and I want the question to make students say what that buys and what it still presupposes.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty-five marks. The question should be answerable in about fifty minutes of writing, and it should require students to do at least the following: state the dilemma clearly; take seriously two candidate definitions of impact, one physical or state-space-based and one option- or preference-based; say for each what it presupposes about value; and reach a defended verdict on whether a value-free definition of side-effect avoidance is possible, rather than ending on a hedge. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, assign each a mark value, and, for each one, describe in a sentence or two the specific way students typically miss it, so that a strong answer is clearly distinguishable from one that merely says impact measures are hard because values are hard. Please write everything in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables, the way one would set it out in a letter to a colleague, with the marks described in the running text.
