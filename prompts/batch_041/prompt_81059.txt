I am currently taking a course on rational choice and its applications, and I have been keeping a list of cases that I cannot fit under a single heading. The pattern is this: someone is made worse off by coming to know something that is true, freely available, and directly relevant to a decision they face. None of my cases turns on the news being unpleasant, and none turns on the information being difficult to obtain. In one, the customers of an insurer all learn their own risk categories; the pooling that had made coverage affordable comes apart, and everyone ends up worse off than they were in mutual ignorance, although no one has been misled. In a second, a person would rather not learn something about their partner, because they know that once they know it they will feel bound to leave, and leaving is worse for them by their own lights. In a third, part of what a person cares about is what they believe — about their own health, say — so that a more accurate belief can leave them worse off even when it leads them to do nothing differently.

I have been told, as though it were a general result, that information cannot have negative value: that knowing more never leaves you worse off, at least before the fact and on average. My cases look like counterexamples, but I suspect the claim holds within limits I was never told about, and that each of my cases crosses a different one.

What I want is not a label but a boundary. Is there a name for what I have been describing, and is it one phenomenon or a family of them? For each case, which condition of the general claim does it violate, and are the cases genuinely independent of one another? I would also like to know what my cases are not: how they differ from the familiar point that information is often costly to acquire, and from the familiar observation that people sometimes avoid news they expect to be bad. A name by itself would settle none of this for me; I need the edge of the concept.
