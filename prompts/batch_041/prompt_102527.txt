I teach a small undergraduate seminar, and for the past two terms I have been opening each session with an informal poll: a short vignette, a yes/no question, a show of hands. I have accumulated a pattern I cannot place in the literature, and I would like to know whether it already has a name or whether it needs one.

The pattern has three strands, and I cannot tell whether they are one phenomenon or three.

First: I describe an agent who is certain that his action will produce a side effect he neither wants nor cares about, and the side effect is a harm. My students overwhelmingly say he knew what he was doing, brought it about on purpose, and is answerable for it. When I change only the side effect — same action, same indifference, same certainty — so that it is a benefit, the same students hedge: it was a lucky byproduct, he did not really do it, it would be strange to give him credit. I take this to be the familiar asymmetry about bad versus good side effects, so far nothing new.

Second, and this is where I lose my footing: the asymmetry seems to disappear when I substitute a non-moral valence of comparable magnitude. If the foreseen side effect is that a room will be left ugly, or that a pleasant smell will be released, the students treat the pair symmetrically — no one withholds the "he did it" ascription in the ugly case and no one grants it in the pleasant case. So the asymmetry appears to track something about the moral standing of the side effect specifically, rather than about its being good or bad in general. Is that a distinction anyone has drawn?

Third: I tried to strip out indifference, supposing that must be the driver. I now tell them the agent actively wants the side effect, but only as a bonus, never as a reason for acting. With a wanted benefit they still resist saying he did it on purpose; with a wanted harm they still say he did. The agent's desires seem to make no difference — only the moral valence of the side effect does.

What I want is not a label but a carving. Is the whole thing just the well-known bad-side-effect-as-intentional finding under another description, or is at least one strand a distinct pattern that deserves its own name? If it is distinct, or unnamed, name it and define it, and tell me what falls inside its boundary and what falls outside. I am especially interested in how it differs from three nearby ideas I can only half-articulate: that foreseen side effects are not intended (the means/ends distinction), that we judge agents by outcomes they could not control (moral luck), and that knowing an outcome was coming makes us read intention into it after the fact (hindsight). I also want to know whether the aesthetic-symmetry observation in the second strand is a genuine prediction of any account or merely an artifact of my vignettes.

Please give me your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "nearest_concepts": an array of objects, each with "name" (the established concept, if you use one), "what_it_captures" (the core idea in a sentence or two), and "where_the_pattern_diverges" (how my described pattern departs from it, or "no divergence" if it is a match).
- "verdict": one of "same_as_existing", "distinct_subpattern", or "unnamed".
- "proposed_name": a string — the existing name if the verdict is "same_as_existing", otherwise a name you coin.
- "definition": a one-paragraph definition, phrased so that it would let a reader decide of a fresh case whether it is an instance.
- "boundary_cases": an array of objects, each with "case" (a short vignette or description), "is_instance" (true or false), and "why" (one sentence).
- "distinguishing_features": an array of strings, each a feature that separates this pattern from one of the nearby ideas named above.
- "open_questions": an array of strings — what my vignettes would need to test to settle the points you are unsure about.
