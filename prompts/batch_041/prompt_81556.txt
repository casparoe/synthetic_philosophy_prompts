I'm applying for a small departmental grant — one semester, one paper-length deliverable — and need a short research proposal (roughly 700 words) for a conceptual project I've been circling for a while. The topic is the cluster of norms that get lumped together as attitudes toward success: the Law of Jante, the tall poppy syndrome, and the American Dream. The usual framing treats the first two as a single leveling impulse aimed at the successful and the third as its opposite. My half-formed suspicion is that this framing is wrong in a way that matters, and that three distinctions do the work: (i) whether a norm is addressed to the person who is rising or to the people around her, (ii) whether it constrains what she may think of herself or what standing she may hold, and (iii) whether the standing at issue is comparative (a gap between persons) or non-comparative (an earned desert). On that reading the Law of Jante is chiefly a constraint on self-assessment and so need not reduce anyone's standing; tall-poppy cutting is other-directed and genuinely comparative; and the American Dream is not the contradictory of either but shares their premise that standing is earned and therefore always answerable. If that is right, the ethical question shifts from whether envy-driven leveling is unjust to whether the successful have a claim not to be answerable in this way, and from whom such a claim could be owed.

Please turn this into a proposal with the question stated so that it has a determinate answer, an account of why the standard pro-success versus anti-success framing leaves it open, and the method — I imagine a small battery of cases (a village where nobody is elevated but nobody is put down; a case where the successful person's conceit is real and public; a case where cutting someone down is the only available remedy against an unjust hierarchy) used to test whether the three distinctions carve the positions cleanly or collapse into one another. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and be honest about whether this fits a semester. No citations — I'll add those myself.

End with a single sentence, on a final line beginning "Summary:", stating the proposal's central question and the one result that would justify funding it.
