Here's the episode as I've got it: for something like two thousand years people treated the parallel postulate as something that had to be true, and a geometry where it fails as not just false but impossible. Some of them definately tried to prove it by assuming the opposite and hunting for a contradiction, and when they got strange results they took the strangeness itself as evidence they'd found the contradiction. Then in the 1800s Lobachevsky and Bolyai (and Gauss, who sat on it) built geometries where the postulate fails, and later people showed those are consistent if ordinary geometry is -- you can model them inside Euclid, basically.

Now run that through the actual thesis, that conceivability is a guide to possibility. Not the slogan. The real claim: if you can conceive of a scenario, that gives you reason to think it's possible. I want it stage by stage -- what was supposed to be conceivable, by whom, and whether the episode confirms the thesis or just shows everyone was wrong about what they could conceive. And this is the part my mate kept hammering: apparently philosophers split "prima facie" conceivability (you just don't spot a contradiction) from "ideal" conceivability (you've ruled out every hidden contradiction, which nobody ever actually does). Does that rescue the thesis here, or does it make it something you can never check, so it does no work?

The other direction is the bit I actually care about: what does this episode show about the thesis? Because my gut says it's the strongest case against it, not for it. The thing that settled it wasn't anyone finally managing to conceive of it clearly -- it was a consistency proof, and a consistency proof only says "this is possible if that other thing is possible." So did conceivability do any work at all, or did the geometry just inherit its possibility from Euclid?
