I am taking a seminar on material constitution, and I have been stuck on an argument that a classmate constructed. I understand the usual distinction between identity and constitution, but I cannot see exactly where the argument below goes wrong, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a literature review. Suppose a sculptor makes a statue, S, out of a lump of clay, L. (1) S and L are not identical, because L existed before S was made, and L would survive being rolled into a ball while S would not. (2) If S and L are not identical, then there are two objects in the same place at the same time. (3) If there are two objects in the same place at the same time, then any statement that there is exactly one object here is false. (4) But when I say there is exactly one object on the pedestal, I am speaking truly. (5) Therefore S and L are identical. (6) If S and L are identical, then S has every property that L has. (7) L can survive being rolled into a ball, but S cannot. (8) Contradiction. Therefore either S or L does not exist, and by generalization no ordinary composite object exists.

I would like to keep (1), (4), and (7) if I can. Which step should I reject, and why did it seem so plausible? What does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is that counting is sortal-relative, explain exactly how that blocks the move from (2) to (3) without making (4) ambiguous in a way that undermines ordinary counting. If the answer is that modal properties do not transfer under identity, explain how that is compatible with the indiscernibility of identicals. If the answer is that constitution is not identity but also not a second object, explain what that relation is and why it does not simply rename the problem. I have also heard that the deeper issue is that S's modal properties would be brute or ungrounded if S and L are distinct but coincident, but I cannot see how that is anything more than the property-transfer problem in different terminology. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want a clear conceptual map of the options and their costs.
