For a final paper in my philosophy of mind course, I'm using Mary Shelley's Frankenstein as a test case for the four grades of creature that Dennett distinguishes: Darwinian creatures with fixed responses, Skinnerian creatures that learn by reinforcement, Popperian creatures that can run a hypothesis through an internal model and let the wrong ones die in their stead, and Gregorian creatures whose behaviour is shaped by mind-tools absorbed from their culture — a grade named after the psychologist Richard Gregory. My working thesis is that the creature climbs the whole ladder: he starts as a Darwinian organism reacting to cold, hunger, and fire; becomes Skinnerian during his months of trial and error around the De Laceys; turns Popperian once he can predict how the villagers will react before he acts; and becomes Gregorian once he has language and has read Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Werter. I'd like you to test that reading rather than endorse it. Two things bother me. First, the order does not quite hold: he reasons hypothetically about his own origin, and about whether he is a monster, well before he can read, which suggests Shelley makes him Popperian before he has the mind-tools that are supposed to make it possible. Second, if he really is a Gregorian creature, the framework predicts that the mind-tools should make him more competent among people, and they do the opposite. My guess is that the books hand him tools that no community ever validated for him, so he inherits the products of cultural learning without the transmission that corrects them — but I want to know whether that is a genuine gap in the picture or just a Gothic accident. Please say which scenes carry the argument, and where the novel resists the framework rather than illustrating it; I don't want a reading that merely dresses the taxonomy in Gothic scenery.
