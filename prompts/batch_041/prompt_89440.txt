I teach a grad seminar on the metaphysics of chance and I keep butting heads with a colleague down the hall who runs the propensity interpretation. His move — and he deploys it every single time a student worries about single-case chances — is that a propensity is just a disposition: chance is to a coin toss what solubility is to a sugar cube. Solubility, he says, is a perfectly respectable, real, objective property of the cube; nobody thinks it's a fact about our minds or our evidence, and it belongs to the individual cube rather than to the class of sugar cubes. So if chance is a disposition in that same sense, then (a) chances are real and objective rather than credences or long-run frequencies, and (b) single-case chances come out just fine, which is exactly what the frequentist can't give you. It's a tidy little argument and my students find it convincing, which bugs me, because I suspect the tidiness is doing all the work.

Can you lay out the mapping for me — what plays the role of the soluble stuff, the solvent, the stimulus conditions, the manifestation, the categorical basis — and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks? I'm especially interested in which features of solubility get quietly imported and just aren't there on the chance side. The ones I half-remember involve the manifestation of a disposition being itself non-chancy, dispositions having some kind of categorical base, and the whole stimulus/manifestation distinction getting slippery once the chance setup already contains the trial. And then the payoff: do the two conclusions he wants — realism about chance and the legitimacy of single-case chance — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the realism riding on the analogy the whole time? Does he at least get to keep the anti-frequentist point even if the rest of it collapses?

Be as honest as you can about the limits of your own verdict here: tell me which further consideration would actually change your mind about whether the disposition analogy earns the realist conclusion, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be shaky. I'd rather have a hedged answer that flags its own weak joint than a confident one that papers over it.
