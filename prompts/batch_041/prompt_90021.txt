I am trying to get clear on a knot in Kant's practical philosophy, and I would like help with the structure of the position rather than a summary of it. The following is my own paraphrase, not a quotation, of the passage I keep returning to in his moral writings on our relations to non-human animals: he maintains that animals are not self-conscious, that they exist only as means to an end, and that we have no duties to them; that we nonetheless have genuine duties regarding them, which are indirect duties to humanity; and that these duties are real because cruelty to animals dulls the shared feeling of sympathy that is the sensible condition of our duty to other human beings, whereas kindness towards them strengthens it. He adds that an animal's service and fidelity, though nothing is owed to the animal itself, is analogous to something owed to a person, and he does not say plainly whether that analogy is illustrative or does work in the argument. I am aware that this compresses claims from texts of different periods and that the wording is mine, so please reason from the content rather than from the exact phrasing.

My difficulty is this. I can reconstruct the dulling argument as an empirical one: cruelty now makes cruelty later more likely, so refraining is prudent. But Kant seems to want the requirement to be necessary and not merely prudential, and an empirical generalisation about habituation cannot deliver that. If instead the argument is transcendental, the claim must be that the capacity for moral feeling has conditions and that we are under a duty to preserve them; yet that looks like a duty concerning a state of ourselves, and it is not obvious that a duty to keep one's own sympathy intact can be a duty of virtue in his sense, which is a duty to adopt ends rather than a duty to manage one's sentiments. Nor can I see how the no-direct-duties claim survives the analogy claim: if animals are not persons but their service is the analogue of what persons are owed, is the analogue doing any independent work, or is it merely a way of saying that treating animals well is practice for treating persons well?

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Identify which strand in the position is load-bearing and which is ornament; say whether the dulling argument can be made out as a genuine duty of virtue, or whether it must fall back on prudence or on self-cultivation; and explain what the analogy to what persons are owed is doing if the denial of direct duties is retained. I would rather keep the denial of direct duties and make the indirect account strong than abandon it for a softer sentimentalism, but I want to know what that costs and where the account becomes unstable.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of what I have asked. Please write in British English.
