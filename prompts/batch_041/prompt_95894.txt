I run the country-risk desk at a mid-sized foundation that funds rule-of-law, independent-media, and electoral-integrity programs. Twice a year we assign each of roughly forty countries a status — stable, eroding, or backsliding — and that status drives seven-figure funding decisions and, increasingly, a sentence in our public materials. I have two to three days per country, no in-country staff, and no reading knowledge of most of the relevant languages. The board wants verdicts it can defend, the program officers want verdicts that don't embarrass us, and I have been handed a concept that everyone in the building uses and no one in the building defines.

Please write the decision procedure itself: a sequence of questions to run in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I don't need a literature review, a reading list, or citations — I need something I can run on a Monday.

The procedure has to survive the following, which is where I keep failing.

- Backsliding is a comparative concept, so every verdict smuggles in a baseline. Tell me how to choose one and what each choice costs me — a country's own earlier equilibrium, its constitutional text, its regional neighborhood, an ideal-type democracy. A historical baseline seems to make recovery invisible and to make long-standing authoritarianism look like decline; an idealized baseline seems to make nearly everyone a backslider.
- Slow erosion and sharp rupture look nothing alike in a single year's evidence. Each individual step — a court-packing statute, a media-ownership rule, a change to how elections are administered — can be defensible on its own, so at the time nothing is diagnostic; only the trajectory is, and a trajectory requires either a long time series or a theory of what the incumbent is building. I have neither. Tell me how the procedure handles this without becoming unfalsifiable in the moment.
- A procedure that checks only whether each step was legal will clear every case I actually care about, because the erosion is carried out through legal instruments and formal majorities.
- I still have to combine sub-dimensions — courts, media, elections, civil society, corruption — into one label, and the aggregation rule is a choice. Tell me what to do when one dimension is healthy and another has collapsed, and when a movement is small enough that it may be within the noise of expert coding.
- The verdict is itself a political act. Separate the diagnostic stage from the response stage so that evidence-gathering is not contaminated by what we intend to do about the country, and make the response stage declare its own value premises instead of hiding them in the scoring. Say who should sign off: me, the board, or a panel with no funding role.
- I want a red-team step. For every country I label, I want the strongest available case that the label is wrong, stated as a defender of the government would state it.

Then run the procedure start to finish on Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, including at least one country where it returns a verdict I won't like and one where it returns no verdict at all. Tell me what to do when the answer is "no verdict" and the funding decision cannot wait.

Finally, tell me which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, what the procedure cannot do, and whether it does anything to answer the objection that "backsliding" is a partisan label with a measurement apparatus attached — or whether it only relocates the politics into the thresholds. Write in American English.
