I sit on the professional-standards subcommittee of my hospital's ethics committee, and we vote next week on a proposed addition to the physician code of conduct. The draft lists roughly a dozen "aspirational commitments" — calling the family of every patient who dies unexpectedly before the shift ends, walking anxious patients personally down to imaging, phoning discharged patients at home the next day to see how they are doing — and it states explicitly that these are not enforceable and carry no disciplinary consequence. It was proposed by a group of attending physicians who already do most of these things and who want them "on the books" so that residents absorb the culture they are trying to build. A dissenting faction, led by one of our senior surgeons, wants the section struck outright. His argument is that the moment you write something into the code — even as aspiration — you have made it an expectation, that expectations migrate into evaluations, and that this turns what used to be generosity into a baseline against which people who are already stretched thin will be quietly judged. He keeps adding a point I find harder to brush off than I would like: that an act cannot really be admirable *as supererogatory* once the institution has publicly declared that everyone in your position ought to be aiming at it. One colleague has floated a middle path — keep the section, but append a sentence saying that nonperformance of these commitments may never be referenced in any evaluation, promotion, or disciplinary process, and is to be treated by the institution as invisible — and I am not sure that does the work he thinks it does.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before the vote: a clear recommendation (adopt as written, strike the section, or adopt with specific modifications), the reasoning behind it, and a real answer to the surgeon's objection rather than a restatement of our values. Please write that memo for me. I am a palliative-care physician, not a philosopher, so I need any conceptual apparatus spelled out in plain terms the committee can evaluate on its own. In particular, work through the standard machinery around supererogation — the idea that some acts are morally good without being morally required, that omitting them is not blameworthy even though performing them is praiseworthy, and the familiar puzzle that if an act is the best thing one could do, it seems odd to call it optional while still granting that one ought to do it — and use it to decide whether our "aspirational" section preserves or quietly destroys the very category it means to honor. Address head-on whether the surgeon is right that codification is self-undermining; whether the fairness and demandingness worry about overworked staff is a reason to strike the section or only a reason to write it differently; and whether an unenforceable-but-listed standard is a genuine third option or simply an obligation in disguise. Give the recommendation up front, keep the whole thing under about two pages, and write it in American English.
