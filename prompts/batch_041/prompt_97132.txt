I'm a features writer at a science-and-ideas magazine, at work on a long piece about what people call the mistake thesis: the claim, given its most familiar popular form by Jared Diamond and prepared for by Marshall Sahlins's idea of an original affluent society, that the turn to agriculture was a catastrophic wrong turn — that it purchased a larger population at the cost of worse health, longer hours, and the eventual arrival of hierarchy and the state. My editor is determined that we not simply march out Diamond and his usual critics again, so instead of a summary I want to run an actual exchange between two thinkers who have each pushed the argument into less-trafficked territory, and I'd like you to write that dialogue. Please make both of them fully competent: neither is a crank, neither is a foil, each can state the other's case in its strongest form, and — this matters most to me — each responds to the specific thing the other just said rather than talking past it or conceding too easily.

The first is a defender of forager affluence who has grown dissatisfied with the standard metrics. She will grant that farming fed far more people, but insists that "more people fed" is not self-evidently a good if the lives thereby produced are worse; and she has an unusual way of pressing the point. She argues that the right measure is neither calories nor lifespan nor even leisure hours but what she calls unforced time — the share of a life spent on activities the person would not have chosen — and she claims that by this measure early farmers lose decisively to foragers, and that the usual defenses of agriculture quietly assume the very hierarchy that farming made possible. The second is an archaeologist who accepts most of the empirical record, including the health declines and the early evidence of inequality, but rejects the framing outright. She holds that the mistake thesis smuggles in a counterfactual that never existed — once populations and climates had shifted there was no road not taken — and that even if farming lowered average welfare, it was justified by the sheer number of lives it made possible, a total-welfare argument she is prepared to defend at its uncomfortable edges, up to and including the implication that adding lives of meager quality can be a good. She also suspects the villain is not farming but the state.

Let them start from a concrete case rather than abstractions — a single excavated settlement, or a single generation at the transition, will do — and let the argument move: I want the affluence advocate to press the archaeologist on whether "more lives" is doing any real justificatory work or is just a byproduct she has dressed up as a value, and I want the archaeologist to press back on how unforced time is supposed to be measured, and on whether foragers' choices were ever as free as the phrase implies.
