I am preparing a short paper on a thought experiment I will call Aunt Hillary: a colony of ants, none of which understands anything, each following simple local rules, organized so that the colony as a whole tracks and responds to its surroundings in the manner of a person — it appears to anticipate, remember, and choose, and its behavior is best predicted by treating the colony as a single agent with beliefs and desires. A colleague maintains that Aunt Hillary has a mind; I am inclined to agree, yet I have reconstructed her reasoning in a form that appears to prove something I cannot accept, and I cannot find the faulty step. I want a diagnosis that (i) identifies the step or steps to reject and explains why they looked compelling; (ii) says what the colleague must add or concede in order to reach her conclusion; and (iii) makes clear what it would cost to render the disputed notion precise. Here is the argument.

(1) A system has a mind if and only if it realizes the appropriate functional organization: there is a profile — a specification of states and of transitions among them — and a correspondence mapping the system's states onto the profile's states, such that the system's transitions mirror the profile's transitions.

(2) Aunt Hillary realizes such a profile. Hold the colony's inputs and outputs fixed, read the colony's internal configurations as its states, and the transitions among them match the transitions of a mind. This is exactly why the intentional description of the colony is so predictive.

(3) Therefore Aunt Hillary has a mind.

(4) Yet the criterion in (1), so understood, is met by almost anything. Consider an ordinary rock. Over any interval it occupies a succession of microphysical states; and because we are free to choose the correspondence, we may assign the profile's states to those microphysical states so that the transition structure is preserved. Nothing in (1) forbids choosing the correspondence with the profile already in hand. So the rock realizes the profile.

(5) Therefore the rock has a mind.

(6) The rock does not have a mind.

I would like to keep (1) and (3) and reject (5), but (2) and (4) appear to invoke the same kind of correspondence, and I cannot see what distinguishes the one from the other. Tell me which of (1), (2), or the steps leading to (3) and to (4) I should abandon, why it seemed innocent, and what abandoning it costs — in particular, whether the colony can still be credited with a mind once the repair is made, and whether the same repair is available to computational accounts of the human mind or whether it threatens them. I would also like to know whether the answer turns on the colony's correspondence being natural in a way the rock's is not, and if so, what makes it natural, given that we chose the colony level of description ourselves.
