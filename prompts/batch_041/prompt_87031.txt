For two years a reading group I'm in has been circling the naturalism-versus-normativism debate about health and disease, and it has gone exactly nowhere. The naturalists hold that disease is a value-free biological fact — roughly, statistically subnormal species-typical functioning — so a diagnosis can be correct even if no one minds the condition and incorrect even if everyone abhors it; the normativists hold that calling something a disease is already an evaluative act, a judgment that the condition is bad or unwanted, so there is no neutral biological fact for the diagnosis to track; the hybrid views pair a factual component (a biological function is failing) with an evaluative one (that failure is harmful or undesired). Every argument is on the table and no one has budged.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three stacked together: a semantic one about how the word disease is used, a metaphysical one about whether biological function can be characterized without appeal to goals or interests, and a normative one about what medicine ought to treat. I don't want agreement, I want a diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath — for instance about whether the biological notion of function can be made evaluatively neutral — with the two sides answering different questions under a single heading? What would actually count as progress: a distinction, a decisive case, a demonstration that one side's central notion is incoherent, or an argument that health and disease are essentially contested concepts, so the impasse is the normal condition of the concept rather than a failure to be repaired? And could any concrete case settle it — a condition that is statistically typical yet harmful, or abnormal yet harmless, or one whose status changed the moment a treatment existed — or would each side simply read such cases its own way?
