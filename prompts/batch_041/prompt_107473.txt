My father died six months ago, and I keep noticing something I can't write off as mere grief. When I deliberate about certain things — how to handle his affairs, whether to take a risk he'd have weighed differently — I find I'm routing the decision through a working model of how he reasoned, and sometimes the model overrules my own inclination. I want a dialogue I can think with, between two equally competent people who disagree about whether anything of him survives in this.

One is a distributionist: if a self just is a self-referential pattern, then a pattern can be realized partly, and redundantly, across more than one brain, so his "I" may persist in attenuated form in me and in the others who modeled him. This is the line Hofstadter takes in his 2007 book I Am a Strange Loop, where he argues that a human "I" is distributed over numerous brains rather than confined to one. The other is an individuationist: a self requires a single, unified first-person perspective — one locus of experience — so what I carry is a third-person model, a simulation, not a subject, and nothing of his first-person life is there. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual points rather than restate their own.

Please don't let either of them simply recite the strange-loop picture. Make the exchange turn on the distinction I keep bumping into: whether a model of a person that plays the right causal role in someone else's deliberation counts as an instantiation of that person or only as a simulation of them, and what could possibly tell the two apart from the inside. Have them work through a case I've constructed: two people who model each other so thoroughly over decades that each one's self-loop has the other's model as a load-bearing part; when one dies, is there one loop that lost a substrate, or one loop plus a simulation? Then have the individuationist press that against the distributionist by asking what, if anything, it is like to be the surviving sub-loop — and have the distributionist try to answer without simply helping themselves to the survivor's own experience as the deceased's.

End with a short note in my voice, kept apart from the dialogue, separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: which moves in the dialogue are standard, which are live disagreements, and which are my own speculative constructions. Don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
