I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology who has been drafted into TAing an introductory ethics course, and one passage in the textbook I'm teaching from is now being quoted back at me by students with more confidence than I can match. It runs roughly: "Moral particularism, as the name suggests, is the view that morality is a matter of particular cases rather than general rules -- that there are no moral principles, and that judging is a matter of perceiving the details of a situation rather than applying anything to it. Its central insight is holism about reasons: whether a consideration counts in favour of an action depends on the other features of the case, which the particularist takes to show that nothing is ever a reason in itself. Generalism, by contrast, holds that morality is a system of general principles from which particular judgements can be deduced, so the debate is between those who think morality can be codified and those who think it cannot. Since the particularist's picture of context-sensitive judgement is now widely accepted, what remains in dispute is largely whether to call the resulting perceptiveness a kind of knowledge or a kind of skill. In any case the position cannot be right as it stands, since a morality with no general principles gives us no basis for criticising the practices of other cultures."

I would rather not correct my students into a different error, so I want an audit of that passage: which parts are accurate, which are harmless popularisation, which a specialist would call a misrepresentation and what the view actually says instead, and whether the closing claims -- that the surviving disagreement is largely terminological, and that the absence of principles leaves the position unable to criticise anything -- follow from anything true in it. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
