I've been drafting a short essay on liberty and I'd like your comments on it — specifically on whether I've got the positions right and whether the argument is actually interesting or just a relabeling of something already out there. Here's the gist.

Everyone teaches the three-way split: negative liberty (you're free to the extent nobody is actually interfering with you), positive liberty (you're free to the extent you're your own master, or living according to your real/true ends rather than your passing desires), and the republican view (you're free to the extent you aren't under anyone's arbitrary power, even if they happen to leave you alone). My claim is that these are really answers to two seperate questions that got lumped under one word. The first question is quantitative: how much interference do you actually face? The second is about standing: what is your position relative to whoever holds the power to interfere? Negative liberty answers the first. Republican liberty answers the second. And I want to argue that positive liberty is a misfit here — it isn't about other people at all, it's about the relation between your two selves, so putting it on the same axis as the other two was a category error that Berlin made and everyone copied.

The reason I think the standing question is the interesting one is that unfreedom looks dispositional rather than occurant. Consider a worker under an at-will employer who is in fact treated generously; a tenant whose landlord never raises the rent but could; someone on a platform whose terms of service can change at any time without their say. None of them are being interfered with right now, and the negative view says they're free, but intuitively something is off — they're free on sufferance. So I want to say freedom is partly a matter of what could happen to you and on whose say-so, not just what is happening.

From there I sketch a fourth category I call freedom as standing: you're free to the extent you can contest the rules that bind you — not just that no one is dominating you, but that the terms are answerable to you. A tenant with a lease and a tenants' union has standing; one without either doesn't, even under a kind landlord.

What I want from you: (1) is my characterization of Berlin fair, or does positive liberty actually have a social/relational reading I'm ignoring? (2) is my fourth category genuinely distinct from the republican idea of non-domination, or am I just reinventing it with a new name — and if it is distinct, what's the cleanest way to state the difference? (3) the obvious objection to my dispositional point is that a power nobody exercises isn't a constraint at all, and that I'm just dressing up a feeling of insecurity as a fact about freedom — how would you answer that, and is there a better example than the platform one? And (4) does the whole thing collapse if you say interference just is whatever makes the standing claim true, in which case I've only got one concept after all. Comments on accuracy first, then on whether it's worth finishing.
