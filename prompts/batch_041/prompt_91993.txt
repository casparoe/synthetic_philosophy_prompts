I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and for the past year I've been sitting in on a fortnightly working group run by a colleague of mine, Ines, who works on democratic theory and has spent most of her career on the boundary problem — the question of who gets to be part of the demos that authorises a collective decision in the first place. Part of the group's routine is that we bring Ines cases and she gives verdicts, and I've been keeping a list. For a binding municipal referendum in a mid-sized river city of about four hundred thousand, near a provincial border, her verdicts so far are: long-term resident non-citizens who have lived in the city for a decade and pay its taxes, in; citizens who emigrated thirty years ago and retain no property or family ties there, out; residents of the city currently serving sentences in the city's own jail, in; adults under full legal guardianship because of profound cognitive disability, in; children, including children of primary-school age, in; non-resident owners of property in the city, out; tourists and business travellers who happen to be in town on polling day, out; corporate entities, out. She is firm about all of these, and she refuses the deflationary move of saying the children and the guardianship cases are really about interests being represented by proxy — she insists those people are members of the demos in the same sense as anyone else.

Next month the group takes up a live case. The city is holding a binding referendum on a flood-control and water-storage scheme: a dam and reservoir on the river above the city, which would drown a valley in the neighbouring province, displacing roughly twelve thousand people, mostly farmers, and taking their land, in exchange for securing the city's water supply for a century. The vote binds only the city, and the neighbouring province has no veto. The twelve thousand are not residents of the city, hold no property there, are not subject to its bylaws, and live under another province's law; but if the referendum passes, their homes will not exist in five years. Ines has agreed to give a verdict on whether they belong in the demos for that vote, and I'd like to get ahead of her.

So: predict her verdict on the valley residents. I care much less about the verdict than about the machinery. I want to know which rules are still standing after her earlier verdicts, which of those rules are observationally equivalent on everything she has said so far, what each one predicts about the dam vote, and which of them I should bet she is actually running, with reasons. Then name the single further case — something I could actually put to her — that would do the most to separate the surviving rules, and say what each rule predicts about that case. If her record genuinely leaves the answer open, say so, and tell me what she would be committing herself to, as a matter of principle, by going each way. You need not cite anyone or point me toward literature; state the principles in plain terms and reconstruct them rather than naming them, since I want to see the machinery and not a set of labels.

Please lay the answer out in this order: a section headed "Verdict" (one or two sentences on what she will say about the valley residents); a section headed "Rules still standing" (roughly 350 words, one short paragraph per rule, each stating the rule and identifying which of her earlier verdicts keeps it alive and which rules out its rivals); a section headed "What each rule predicts about the dam vote" (a short table, one row per rule, with the prediction and a phrase of justification); a section headed "Which rule she is probably running, and why" (200 to 300 words); a section headed "The case that would settle it" (about 150 words, describing the case and what each rule predicts about it); and a closing section headed "What she would be committing to either way" (about 100 words).
