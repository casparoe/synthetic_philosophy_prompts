Consider the following exercise in counterfactual intellectual history. Aristotle is brought up to date on one specific body of modern results and then asked for a verdict.

The results concern statistical instruments that assign risk scores to persons — of the kind now used in credit, hiring, insurance, and pre-trial detention. Suppose it is explained to him that for any such instrument, three properties, each of which seems independently required by fairness, cannot hold together. The instrument may be well calibrated within each of two groups, so that a score of a given value carries the same meaning in both. It may have equal false-positive rates across the two groups. It may have equal false-negative rates across the two groups. But if the two groups differ in the base rate of the outcome being predicted, and the instrument is less than perfectly predictive, at most two of the three can be satisfied at once. One must be surrendered, and in current practice the choice of which to surrender is usually made by the engineers and firms that build and deploy the instrument, not by a legislature or a court. Suppose he is also told a further fact about how such guarantees behave in practice: a property secured for two broad groups need not survive a finer breakdown, so that satisfying the chosen criterion for each group taken as a whole may leave it unsatisfied for a smaller subgroup within one of them.

Write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words reconstructing the position Aristotle would most plausibly take, organized in four numbered parts.

1. The verdict. State his position as a definite claim rather than a survey of possibilities. The central question to answer is whether he would treat the trade-off as a genuine moral dilemma, in which something of real value must be sacrificed whichever way one chooses, or as a symptom of a badly posed question — the wrong currency being measured, or the wrong class of cases being compared — in which case the impossibility is not yet a problem about justice at all.

2. The argument in his own terms. Reconstruct the reasoning using his own apparatus rather than modern vocabulary: proportional equality in distributive justice as set out in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, the contrast between distributive and corrective justice, the role of merit together with his acknowledgment that partisans disagree about what merit consists in, the requirement that desert track voluntary action, and equity (epieikeia) as the correction of what is defective in law because of its generality. In the course of the reconstruction, address each of the following explicitly: (a) whether proportional equality, itself a condition on ratios, gives him a way of satisfying two of the three criteria at once rather than merely trading one against another; (b) whether group-level rates are even the right objects of assessment for him, given that his account of justice concerns claims among particular persons; (c) who he would say should make the choice among the three criteria, and whether the authority of the instrument's designers over that choice is intelligible to him; and (d) what he would make of the fact that a criterion met for a whole group can fail for a subgroup within it, given his treatment of the parts of a political community and of the different claims their members can make.

3. The strongest objection and his reply. State the most serious objection to your reconstruction — for instance, that proportional equality simply is the selection of one currency among others and therefore stands on one horn of the trilemma rather than above it, or that the impossibility is a theorem about predictors and not about justice, so that it leaves his theory untouched — and then give the reply he would make, in his terms.

4. What would change his mind. Identify at least one feature of the case such that, if it were altered, his verdict would flip, and explain why that feature carries the weight it does. The feature you name should be specific enough that a reader could check whether the altered case really does produce the reversed verdict.

Throughout, do not invent quotations, and do not put words in his mouth as if they were verbatim. Where his texts leave a question genuinely open, say so and explain how you resolved the indeterminacy. Do not turn the essay into a survey of the contemporary literature on fair prediction; the modern material is fixed by the description given above, and the interest lies in what he would make of it.
