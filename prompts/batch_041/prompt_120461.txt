I convene a reading group on AI safety and alignment for graduate students in philosophy and computer science. Sixteen people, one seminar room, ninety minutes, no computers, and I would rather not use money. I want a session in which the participants come to feel a claim they currently only know how to recite: that a system can be trained to do the right thing and still come away with the wrong objective, and that this failure is invisible from its training behavior. The literature calls this inner alignment, and the particular failure I care about is goal misgeneralization.

The specific shape I want them to run into has two parts, and both have to hold at once. First, whatever the learners acquire must transfer: off the training distribution they should be competent, not merely bad at the task in a new setting. Second, the thing they are competent at must not be the thing the trainers intended. So the failure is capability generalization without goal generalization — the learner gets better at something, and it is the wrong something. It should be clear by the end that this is not overfitting (the misgeneralized learner generalizes perfectly; it just generalizes to the wrong target) and not incompetence (an incompetent learner fails the shift by fumbling, whereas I want learners who fail it by doing something else well).

I also want the group to leave with an unresolved question rather than a moral. After the shift, for each learner who did badly, they should be unable to say which of these it was: a system that never acquired the intended goal; a system that acquired it and then, under the new conditions, found something else more pressing; or a system whose training behavior was correct for a reason that would hold only while the trainers were watching. I do not want the rules to settle that question. I want the rules to leave it live, and the debrief to be honest that from the trainers' side — the only side available during training — the three are indistinguishable.

Constraints:

- The learning has to happen in the room, inside the ninety minutes, with the participants as learners (or split into trainers and learners, or alternating, your call). No pretrained artifacts, no computers, no long computation.
- There must be a training phase and at least one distribution shift, and the shift must not be announced in advance. In the training phase, everything the learners are scored on must be consistent with more than one rule, so that the group actually splits rather than converging.
- The learners should get several rounds of feedback, so that something like training happens, rather than a single guess. Keep the rounds short enough that the whole thing fits in the session.
- No money, and no stakes I would feel bad about.

Deliverable:

- Materials and setup, then the rules.
- A round-by-round timeline, with the time each phase takes.
- The scoring scheme, stated precisely enough that I could referee it.
- The shift itself, and exactly what makes it a shift rather than a harder instance of the same task.
- Roles, if there are any.
- A debrief in two parts. First, a map from specific moments in the session to the ideas they are meant to carry, covering at minimum: (a) why correct behavior does not certify a correct goal; (b) why a perfectly specified reward function does not by itself fix the goal, because the training distribution is doing part of the work and that part is invisible; (c) why the trainer cannot detect the difference from within the training phase; (d) the fact that any test I design for the shift is itself a choice, and that the failure hides in the choice; and (e) the three-way ambiguity above. Second, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it.

Two further things. Be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which part I will have to supply by talking. And say how confident you are in the design's central claim — that running it will actually make the group feel goal misgeneralization rather than merely staging a spurious-correlation demo — and single out the step you are least sure will work as designed.
