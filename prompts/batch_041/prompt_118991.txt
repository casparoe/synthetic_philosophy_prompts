I'm closing a graduate seminar on the two inversions — Darwin's and Turing's — that between them make competence without comprehension thinkable: the thought that a system's outputs can be dependably right, and its parts well fitted to their conditions, without any grasp of why being present in the system or anywhere behind it. I want to end the last session with a poem read aloud, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. A sestina is already one of the two inversions: a fixed rule generates an order that no one needs to understand in order to execute, and the order is a consequence of the rule rather than of anyone's foresight about what would be good to say. So write me a sestina, under the following constraints.

Form. Thirty-nine lines: six stanzas of six lines and a three-line tornada, with all six end-words present in the tornada, three of them as line-endings and the rest inside the lines. Loose iambic pentameter; no rhyme. Choose the six end-words so that they can bear conceptual weight, and make at least one of them something other than a noun, so that the permutation forces it to change grammatical role as it recurs. At least one end-word should shift its sense across the poem the way a structure selected for one job comes back doing another, so that a reader sees the same word return as a different thing.

Content. At least two stanzas should be unmistakably Darwinian — fit explained by the retention of variation, no foresight anywhere in the process, and the reason for a trait's presence located in a history rather than in the trait itself, so that the competence on display is always competence with respect to the conditions that were and not the conditions that are. At least two should be unmistakably Turingian — correct outputs produced by a procedure whose correctness is opaque to whatever runs it, with whatever comprehension there is sitting outside, in a specification or a designer, and needing to be nowhere in the machine.

The two inversions are not one inversion, and the poem should not let them blur into a single slogan: in the first case no one ever comprehended; in the second, someone comprehended but it is not in the thing. Give that difference a stanza of its own, or the tornada. The tornada should also turn on the poem's own predicament: a sestina is an order generated by a rule, which makes it a fair model of the second inversion and a poor model of the first, because there is a poet. I would rather the closing lines concede this than smooth it over, and I would rather the concession be made in the poem's own voice than in the voice of a footnote.

Two prohibitions. No proper nouns — these two names included. The poem should render both inversions well enough that someone who knows the material recognizes each without being told whose it is. And no labels: avoid "algorithm," "emergence," "blind watchmaker," "ghost in the machine," and the phrase "competence without comprehension" itself. If you can manage a line that would work as an instruction — something executable — and still scans as verse, do.

Someone who has read the literature on this should find nothing to wince at, and someone who has not should be able to read it as a poem about a thing that works without knowing that it works.
