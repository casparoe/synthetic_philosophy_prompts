I've been in a Discord reading group that's slowly working through philosophy-of-probability stuff, and last week someone brought up the St. Petersburg game, which I already know cold — coin gets flipped until it lands heads, you win 2^n where n is the number of flips, expected value blows up to infinity, nobody would pay more than twenty bucks, diminishing marginal utility, done. But then she hit us with what she called the Pasadena game (or Pasadena paradox? something Pasadena) and it's been stuck in my head since. Same setup, except the payoff when the first heads comes on flip n is 2^n/n with the sign flipping every time, so the payoffs run +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, −32/3, and so on. The expected contribution of flip n works out to (−1)^(n−1)/n, so if you just add them up in the natural order you get 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯ = ln 2, about 69 cents, which is weirdly less than the single most likely outcome (+2, probability one half). But the series is only conditionally convergent: the positive terms sum to infinity and the negative terms sum to infinity, so the "expected value" is really infinity minus infinity, and you can rearrange the terms to get literally any real number you like, or drive it to +∞ or −∞. So the expected value of the Pasadena game isn't 69 cents, isn't infinite, it's just not defined — and apparently there's a whole fight in the literature about whether that's the game's fault or expected value's fault, and what a decision theory is even supposed to say to someone who's offered it.
