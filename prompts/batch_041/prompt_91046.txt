Me and a friend got into a stupidly long argument last night about whether you can actually learn anything about how to live from reading about other people's lives, and it ended with us both shouting about Amadeus of all things, so now I want a proper answer rather than his.

Here's the shape of it. Plutarch says at the start of his Life of Alexander that he isn't writing history but lives, and that a chance remark or a jest often shows you more of a man's character than the biggest siege or the bloodiest battle — he likens himself to a portrait painter who concentrates on the face and leaves the rest of the body to other people. So biography is meant to be a moral instrument: you read the life, you see virtue and vice actually operating, and you get better somehow. Then there's hagiography, which is that same impulse gone religious — the saint's life written so it can be imitated, with everything unedifying quietly filed off. And then there's the sceptic's line, which is my friend's, that you can't learn anything from an exemplar except how to feel bad about yourself.

Now read Amadeus (Forman, 1984, Shaffer adapting his own play) against all that, because I think it's doing something quite nasty to both traditions and I want to know if I'm right. Salieri is the biographer: he's telling the whole story to a priest in 1823, he's the only witness to most of what we see, and he's effectively writing his own life as a parallel life with Mozart's — that's the Plutarchan form exactly, except the comparison is being drawn by the man who loses it. Mozart barely appears as a person; he appears as evidence. Salieri reads the man off the manuscripts, off the fact that there are no corrections, and that is a Plutarchan inference, character from a small sign, except the small sign is a work of genius, so the method has been turned inside out. And the ending, where he's wheeled through the asylum and appoints himself the patron saint of the mediocrities and blesses them, is hagiography with the sign flipped: a saint for the people the genre was never for.
