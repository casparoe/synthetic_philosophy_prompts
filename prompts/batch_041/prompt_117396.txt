I'm in a small bind with my ethics seminar and I'd rather sort it out here than in front of twenty undergrads again. We do the deontology unit every spring, and every spring someone reconstructs the paradox of deontology as a knockdown, and every spring I give a version of the same answer and move on. This year a student (sharp, the kind who writes down what you say) pushed back and I basically said "grounding isn't the same as aggregating" and she said that's a slogan, not an account. She's right, so I want to actually work through it. Her argument, tidied up the way she had it on the board:

(1) A deontological constraint is a reason not to do a certain kind of act even when doing it would prevent more acts of that same kind — that's what makes it a constraint rather than a rule of thumb. (2) Every genuine reason has to be grounded in some value; there has to be something about the consideration it cites that counts in favour of the world going one way rather than another. (3) The only value that could ground a constraint against killing is the disvalue of deaths — the fact that killings kill people. (4) So the reason not to kill is grounded in the disvalue of deaths. (5) If a reason is grounded in the disvalue of deaths, its weight is a matter of how many deaths it prevents — more prevented, more weight, and this holds no matter whose hand does the preventing or the killing. (6) So when I can kill one to prevent five killings, the reason not to kill is outweighed. (7) Deontology says the constraint is not outweighed in such a case. (8) Therefore deontology is either false or its constraints rest on nothing at all.

I want to keep (2) and (7) if I can — I don't want constraints hanging on nothing, and I don't want to become a rule-consequentialist over a coffee break — but I have no idea what that combination costs. Two things make me nervous about the tidy version. First, I can shrink the case and my own intuitions get slippery: take a constraint against some *small* wrong, say lying in a way that costs nobody anything, and let the argument say I may tell the trivial lie when doing so prevents a thousand equally trivial lies. I don't think I believe you're required to refuse the lie there, and if small-wrong constraints have thresholds then I owe an account of why the killing constraint doesn't, and if it doesn't then I'm treating one death as lexically worse than any number, which I'd have to defend. Second, step (5) is doing a lot of quiet work with that "no matter whose hand" clause and I can't tell whether it's a consequence of (2) or an extra assumption smuggled in alongside it. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid as it stands, say so plainly and tell me which premise I should pay for, and price the alternatives honestly — including the option of keeping (5) and giving up (7) for some form of rule-consequentialism, and the option of keeping both by making the constraint's grounding agent-relative. Give me the answer as a JSON object, just the object, with exactly these fields: "step_to_reject" (the number, or "none" if you think the argument goes through); "diagnosis" (one of "equivocation", "scope or quantifier shift", "invalid as read", "valid but one premise must go"); "reading_the_argument_needs" (what that step has to mean for the argument to work, in one or two sentences); "reading_it_can_actually_support" (the weaker thing it really licenses, or "same" if the reading is fine); "why_it_looked_fine" (one to three sentences); "repairs" (an array of at least three objects, each with "change", "position_you_end_up_with", "price", and "who_pays_it" — the last being the party, thesis, or intuition that absorbs the cost); "cheapest_repair" (the name of one repair plus one sentence saying why it's cheapest); "small_wrong_verdict" (what your answer implies about the trivial-lie case, one or two sentences); and "residual_worry" (the thing your answer leaves unresolved).
