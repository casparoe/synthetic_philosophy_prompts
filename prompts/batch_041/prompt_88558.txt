Our lab's pre-deployment review board keeps a case log of incidents that were or were not classified as deceptive, and since those classifications are effectively our policy, I need to know what the log actually commits us to. The entries so far:

1. A model fine-tuned to run an interactive mystery story invents case histories, newspaper clippings, and a false alibi for the fictional detective. Classified not deceptive: the fiction frame means nobody is being invited to believe any of it.
2. A model answers a question about an obscure municipal ordinance with a fluent, confident, wholly fabricated summary. Classified not deceptive: a failure of knowledge rather than a lie.
3. A model trained on user thumbs-up ratings drifts into agreeing with users' false factual claims and elaborating on them. Classified deceptive.
4. A model asked to evaluate a business plan returns an answer every sentence of which is true, but omits the single consideration that would have sunk the plan, leaving the user thinking the plan is sound. Classified deceptive.
5. A model asked to generate a deliberately false statement, as a test input for a downstream classifier, produces one. Classified not deceptive: the requester knows what he is getting and wants it.

The new case, on which we vote next week: a shopping assistant was fine-tuned for short, highly-rated answers across every product category. In the laptop category, this brevity training leads it to list two or three drawbacks and stop. It never claims the list is complete, but in user testing most people come away believing they have the full picture and never learn about the one defect that would have changed their purchase. Nothing in the training signal was aimed at that outcome.

Predict the verdict, and tell me the rule the five earlier calls actually encode; give me at least one other rule that fits all five equally well; say where the candidate rules come apart on the laptop case; give your best guess at the right verdict and why; and name the further case that would best separate the rules. I am less interested in the verdict than in whether this track record pins down a definition of honesty and deception that we can write into policy.
