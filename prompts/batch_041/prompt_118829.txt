A colleague in my decision-theory reading group has spent the term defending an account of idealized preferences. The view, roughly, is that what an agent truly values is what she would value if she were fully informed, free of bias and error, and more the person she would want to be; her actual preferences count as hers only insofar as they survive that idealization. Today she produced the following argument, which she calls the idealization dilemma, and I cannot tell whether it is a reductio of the view or a trick.

(1) What an agent truly values is what she would value under idealization — fully informed, unbiased, coherent, and more the person she would want to be. Call those her ideal values.

(2) Where an agent's actual values diverge from her ideal values, the ideal values are authoritative; that is simply what it means to call them what she truly values.

(3) Idealization is a procedure of correction: it takes the agent's actual values as its input and removes the errors among them, including the incoherences.

(4) Any such procedure requires a standard of correction — a way of telling which of the agent's values are errors to be removed and which are to be preserved.

(5) That standard must come either from outside the agent's actual values, or from nowhere.

(6) If it comes from outside, the output is not the agent's own values but the idealizer's, and (1) is false.

(7) If it comes from nowhere, nothing is corrected; the ideal values are the actual values relabeled, and (2) does no work.

(8) So either ideal values are not the agent's own, or the idealization thesis is empty.

(9) A thesis that is either false or empty whenever it is asked to override an agent's actual values can never legitimately override them.

(10) Yet overriding actual values is the whole point: it is what lets us say that the addict does not really want the drug, or that the patient refusing treatment is mistaken about her own good. An account of idealized preferences that cannot do this has abandoned its job.

She concludes that idealization either imposes a foreign standard or says nothing at all, and that in neither case does it tell an agent what she truly values.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept that; I need idealization to be doing real work. So tell me where the argument goes wrong. Name the step you would reject, explain why it looked persuasive, and price what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether rejecting it saves the idealization thesis or quietly turns it into something else. Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the one move in your own reasoning you are least sure of.
