At a game night I wrote a two-player game on a napkin and asked what the row player should do. Rows are T, M, and B; columns are L, C, and R. The row player's points are: T pays 8 against L, 0 against C, 0 against R; M pays 3, 3, 4; B pays 0, 8, 0. The column player's points are: against T she gets 6, 0, 2 from L, C, and R; against M she gets 0, 6, 2; against B she gets 6, 0, 2. Both want to maximize their own points, and each can see the other's table. Two friends, Ade and Bo, argued about it for an hour, and I couldn't referee.

Ade: R is a bad column for her, and not just bad -- against any row she gets 3 from an even split of L and C, and only 2 from R, so a rational column player never picks R. Delete R, and M becomes a bad row for him: an even split of T and B pays him 4 against L and 4 against C, while M pays 3 against both. So a rational row player never picks M either. Keep going and the game unravels: in what is left, C is worse for her than L, and once only L remains, B is worse for him than T. So the answer to my napkin question is T.

Bo: You can't play an even split. He has to put something down, and there is a column where anything he puts down does worse than M -- against C, T pays him 0 and M pays 3. Your mixture beats M only on average, and it beats it only by making him choose, half the time, a move he would never choose on purpose.

Ade: A coin is how you act on an even split; it is an option like any other. And the mixture does not beat M merely on average: against L it pays 4 to M's 3, and against C it pays 4 to M's 3. Once R is deleted it is better in every case that can still arise.

Bo: Better in every case that can still arise -- but only because you deleted R, the one column where M pays 4 and your mixture pays 0. So the whole case against M is a claim about her, not about him: it assumes she does the deletion too. He is rational, but his being rational does not tell him that she is, or that she will reduce the game the way he does. If he has any doubt about her, M is his best move against that doubt, and insuring against another player's mistake is not itself a mistake.

I gather there is a name for what Ade was doing, but I don't know whether it is a sound way to reason about games or a sleight of hand. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about what, and why. In particular: is Ade's elimination procedure legitimate, and does Bo's objection to the mixture have any force? Finish with what the row player should do and what has to be true for that to be the right answer, and tell me what both of them are missing.
