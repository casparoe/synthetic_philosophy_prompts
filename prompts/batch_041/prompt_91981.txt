I have a set of verdicts about cases in which doing the rational thing and being the sort of person who does well seem to pull apart, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

A walker dying of thirst promises a driver a hundred dollars for the ride into town. Once he is in town the promise buys him nothing, and I do not think he is irrational if he refuses to pay; but I think the driver is sensible to refuse him the ride, and I think less of the walker for not paying, even though I could not demand payment of him.

A man is offered a fortune to intend now to swallow a foul-tasting poison tomorrow, the money paid at once and his mind left free to change. I think he should form the intention and then not drink the poison, and I do not feel that anything has gone wrong: no one has been cheated, and the sequence strikes me as clean. I cannot say why this feels so unlike the walker, since in both cases I form a plan whose last step I will have no reason to perform.

A state builds a machine that will launch its arsenal automatically if attacked. I think it should build it, and that if the machine fails while the attack is under way, no one should press the button by hand; yet the machine does not make the state's threats a bluff in the way the walker's promise may be one. By contrast, a man cultivates a temper for retaliating at a cost to himself, and once wronged finds that retaliation would cost him far more than it gains. Cultivating the temper seems to me rational; retaliating seems to me imprudent, self-indulgent, beneath him — but not irrational, and a man who at that moment weighs the costs has already lost what he was cultivating.

A novelist writes only when she believes her book is a masterpiece, so she arranges for a friend to praise her drafts. I think the arrangement is rational. I also think that if she arranges it in order to produce the belief, the belief will not take: she must not see the friend as a device. So the act is rational only if she does not understand why it is.

My brother borrows whenever he judges that I will lend. I want to become someone who refuses as a rule. But the rule would also make me refuse the one time he is desperate and I could easily help, and refusing then seems to me plainly wrong — and I still think I should acquire whatever would make me refuse the other times.

I have been assuming the usual division of labour: acts are to be judged by their expected consequences from the moment of acting, dispositions by their expected consequences from the moment of acquiring them. That gets the walker and the poison right, but it makes the retaliation irrational, which I resist, and it says nothing about the novelist. Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets all of these — stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause? Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should give up, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed, ideally one where its two halves come apart, so that I can test it.
