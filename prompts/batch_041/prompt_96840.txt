I'm giving the after-dinner speech at our local history society's annual dinner — sixty-odd people, mostly amateurs who've spent years in the parish records, plus two real academic historians we've invited as guests of honour — and the occasion is the launch of our book about the 1766 grain riot in the town. The whole reconstruction hangs on a memoir one man wrote in 1821, aged 71, about what he saw as a boy, plus one hostile paragraph in the next town's newspaper, plus a burnt layer the dig found under the car park. A retired professor wrote to the local paper last month saying we should just admit we dont know what happened, half the room quietly agrees with him, and he's coming to the dinner. What I want the speech to do is show that "this probably happened, and here's roughly why" is a real claim and not a hedge — and to do the actual reasoning out loud, in plain language, so the non-historians follow it: why a memoir written fifty years later isn't automatically worthless if we can say how the man could have known and what he stood to gain by lying; why a source that dislikes us conceding something is worth more than a friendly source asserting it; why two accounts agreeing isn't automatically two witnesses if both might be drawing on the same bit of pub gossip; and why the gap in the register only counts against us if we'd have expected a mention there anyway. It still has to be a celebration — thank the volunteers, sell a few copies — not a defence brief. Four or five minutes, warm, no citations, no jargon, and keep it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullets, no bold, nothing like that, just the way you'd write a letter to a friend.
