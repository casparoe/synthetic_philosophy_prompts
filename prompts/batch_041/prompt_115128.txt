I am a second-year PhD student in eastern Mediterranean archaeology, and for the past two years I have kept a running document of my advisor's verdicts on fringe and borderline claims. He runs our department's seminar on pseudoarchaeology, writes a regular column for a popular archaeology magazine, and is the person our press office calls whenever someone announces a lost civilization; he is also, by his own admission, occasionally too quick on the trigger, having once denounced a thoroughly mainstream colleague's paper as pseudoarchaeology at a public panel and had to walk it back. His rulings so far, as I have logged them: the literal-Atlantis literature is crankery and does not get seminar time; ancient-astronaut readings of Egyptian monuments are crankery; Fomenko's new chronology is crankery, and he insists this is not softened by the fact that its author is a serious mathematician with a real publication record, since chronology is not his field; Velikovsky's cosmic catastrophism is crankery; Great Pyramid pyramidology is crankery; the Kensington Runestone is a forgery, which he treats as a separate category belonging in a course on fraud rather than one on method; the authenticity literature on the Shroud of Turin is crankery, but the cloth itself is a perfectly respectable object of study as a medieval devotional artifact, so the ruling attaches to the claim and not the topic; Wegener's continental drift was legitimate science wrongly dismissed for decades; the Alvarez impact hypothesis for the end-Cretaceous extinction was legitimate from the start and was resisted largely because it was inconvenient; the pre-Clovis occupation of the Americas was legitimate, and the field's long refusal of the Monte Verde evidence he calls a scandal; the argument that Amazonia supported large engineered societies was legitimate and overturned an orthodoxy; the Solutrean hypothesis of Ice Age Europeans crossing the Atlantic was a real hypothesis that made risky predictions and has now been largely killed by ancient DNA, which he says makes it a different animal from Atlantis; and the Sphinx water-erosion argument he regards as wrong and badly motivated but still within the range of what a graduate student may write about.

Two new cases have landed in my lap, and I have to decide whether to take either of them on, so I need to know what he will say before I commit. The first is a two-hundred-page manuscript posted to a preprint server by a retired materials engineer, arguing that some fifty small incised stone plaques, catalogued in three museums for a century as decorated weights or gaming pieces, are the surviving fragments of an unattested writing system used somewhere in the eastern Mediterranean around 1800 BCE. His case turns on the incisions falling into roughly forty recurring sign types, a frequency distribution of the kind natural languages show, positional clustering of particular signs, and recurring sequences across plaques from different sites; he predicts that a group of plaques from a fourth site, never compared with the others, will display the same co-occurrence structure, and he states exactly what result would refute him. He further argues that the plaques were misclassified early on and that every subsequent catalogue copied the earlier classification without anyone re-examining the objects, a process he calls the catalogue citing itself. Two journals have rejected the paper, one on the ground that it is not a contribution to archaeology; he has posted the referee reports together with long point-by-point replies that concede several objections and accuse the field of procedural gatekeeping. He has never published in archaeology. The second case is a chaired professor of Aegean prehistory at a well-known university whose peer-reviewed monograph argues that the palace destructions around 1200 BCE were triggered by a particular volcanic or seismic event, resting on one contested radiocarbon date and one contested ash layer, and who explains the field's resistance by saying that the relevant earth scientists have careers invested in the rival account; when two detailed rebuttals appeared in print, he did not reply to them.

What I want is not a verdict on the merits of either case. Tell me what rule my advisor is actually running, given that record, and what other rules fit the record just as well; then, for each of those rules, what he will say about the plaques and about the professor, and where exactly the rules come apart on them. Then give me your best guess at his reaction to each of the two, with your reasons for preferring that guess, and name the single further case I should put to him if I want to separate the surviving rules as efficiently as possible — the one case that would show whether he is demarcating by the structure of an argument or by who is making it. Finally, if his record commits him to something like one of the criteria philosophers of science have proposed — falsifiability, progressive versus degenerating research programmes, a symptom checklist, or one of the sociological accounts on which pseudoarchaeology is a family-resemblance category rather than a criterion — tell me which one his verdicts actually fit, and which of his rulings sit awkwardly with it.
