I sit in on a decision theory reading group, and last week's session was on the two-envelope paradox: two sealed envelopes, one holding twice what the other holds, you take one at random, open it, and are offered the chance to swap, whereupon the familiar argument runs that if your envelope holds x, the other holds 2x or x/2 with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25x, so you should always swap. Two members, Ravi and Elena, kept at it after everyone else had left, and I have been turning their exchange over since without being able to settle it. Ravi's line is that the paradox is a non-problem: the fifty-fifty is a claim about which envelope is which, not about the ratio you would face given the amount you happen to be holding, and there is no probability distribution over the amounts — proper or improper — under which the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half whatever you find; the argument is an equivocation, full stop, and the enormous literature on it is much ado about a slip. Elena grants that the argument as usually stated is invalid, but says that diagnosing an equivocation leaves the interesting question untouched. Her point is that the agent in the original setup is handed no distribution over the amounts at all, so the resolution does not tell the agent what to do; it tells her that her expected values are undefined, which is a concession that the decision problem as posed is underspecified. She thinks that is a substantive fact about expected utility theory rather than a small technicality, since the framework silently presupposes a state space the agent may simply not have.

Ravi's answer is that no prior is needed for the verdict he wants: whatever distribution over amounts you suppose, the two envelopes are exchangeable, so swapping is worth exactly as much as keeping, and that is a universally quantified claim that can be established without selecting any particular prior. He adds that undefined expected value is not the same thing as no answer, since decision theory is not the only source of verdicts. Elena then produced a bounded variant to press him. The experimenter chooses k uniformly from {0, 1}, puts 2^k and 2^(k+1) into the envelopes, and hands you one at random, so the pair is either {1, 2} or {2, 4}. If you open and find 2, then the pair was equally likely to have been either one, and you hold the smaller in one case and the larger in the other, so the other envelope holds 1 or 4 with equal probability, expectation 2.5 — swap; if you find 1 you must be holding the smaller, so swap; if you find 4 you must be holding the larger, so keep. She says this shows the conditional reasoning can be impeccable and the prior perfectly proper, and that a rule which swaps unless it is holding the largest possible amount does better on average than simply keeping the envelope you were handed — so Ravi's dismissal is too quick, and something real is being smuggled in under the heading of an error, namely that the amount in your own envelope is evidence. Ravi replies that this is not a paradox but a commonplace: information helps, and the only thing her example refutes is the slogan that no strategy can beat keeping, which nobody needs to hold. He adds that her rule is not an exchange of roles between the envelopes, so the symmetry he appealed to never applied to it. She counters that if that is so, then his symmetry argument was doing less work than he claimed, since it rules out only the constant strategies and says nothing about the class of rules that actually matter.

Neither of them convinced me, and I could not even tell whether Elena's arithmetic was right.
