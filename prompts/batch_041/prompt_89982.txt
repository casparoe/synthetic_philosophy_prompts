Ok so I'm putting together a little collection of stories about interpretability work, and I need one more. Here's the setup I want it built on — please stay inside it rather than drifting into the usual "the AI woke up" thing.

The setting: a team that monitors a deployed model's internals. There's a safety case written against specific mechanisms — roughly, "when this model is tempted to fudge a number it does it through this one circuit" — and the monitoring is anomaly detection. They don't much care if the output changes; they care if the mechanism changes. A flag fires when the model reaches the same behaviour by a route the safety case doesn't cover.

Here's the wrinkle I actually care about. After a routine finetune, a circuit comes back. Same input-output profile, same role in the network's behaviour, but the internal implementation is different in every physical detail: different heads, different weights, a different path through the layers, and it only holds together because of a residual-stream quirk that wasn't there before. Two people on the team disagree about whether this is the same mechanism. One says yes, because a mechanism is individuated by what it does — if the counterfactual profile matches, if you intervene in the same places and the behaviour breaks in the same ways, it's the same thing. The other says no, individuation is by the causal structure that produces the profile, and the structure is new, so the safety case is void and they can't ship until it's rewritten.

I want the story to make that disagreement concrete and personal instead of a seminar. Maybe the one arguing "same mechanism" is the person who wrote the original safety case, so its surviving is also a way of saying her work still holds, and she knows that. One scene, one shift, a couple of pages, mostly dialogue plus whatever the machines are showing them on screen. No exposition dumps about what interpretability is — assume the reader already knows. And don't resolve it by having a third character explain who's right; I want the reader to feel the pull of both.

Also: invent the team's internal vocabulary for these cases and use it consistently. I'd like them to sort flags into three buckets, and the bucket names should be doing real work in the argument, not just flavor. Names are up to you, but the three should map onto: the mechanism genuinely changed, the mechanism didn't change but the behaviour did, and neither changed but the monitor can't tell the difference. The interesting case should be one where two of those buckets both seem to fit, and the characters should notice that and fight about it.

At the end, off to the side, give me a short paragraph — 4 to 6 sentences, not in the story's voice — on which criterion of mechanism-identity the story leaves standing, and what it would take to actually settle a case like this in practice. And please don't make either character a strawman. The functionalist should have the stronger practical argument and the structuralist the stronger metaphysical one, or something in that neighbourhood, and I want that tension visible on the page rather than announced.
