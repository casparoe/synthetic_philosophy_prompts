You are an expert in social choice theory and an experienced designer of philosophical classroom simulations. Your task is to design a runnable, self-contained activity for an upper-level undergraduate and graduate seminar on social choice and political philosophy. The setting is one 75-minute session with 16 participants, no prior reading, movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards, and tokens. No real money is available.

The target idea is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: with three or more alternatives, any social choice function that is onto and strategy-proof is dictatorial. A rule is strategy-proof when every voter’s truthful ranking is a weakly dominant strategy, and dictatorial when there is a single voter whose top-ranked alternative is always the social outcome. The participants should not hear the theorem stated until the debrief. By the end, they should have felt, through their own choices, why strategy-proofness is a very strong demand, why several obvious escape routes either fail or change the subject, and why dictatorship emerges as the only way to make truthful voting always optimal. They should also grasp the pivotal-voter structure of the argument, not merely the theorem’s statement.

The activity must be a game or structured simulation with rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme in points or tokens, and a timeline. It should not be a simple mock election. It must reproduce the pivotal-voter proof in miniature. Participants should first adopt a rule that seems fair and non-dictatorial. They should then discover that at least one voter can improve their payoff by misrepresenting their preferences. When they try to patch the rule to remove that manipulation, they should find that the patch either creates a dictator or violates onto or unrestricted domain. Use at least three alternatives and at least two stages, one of which allows voters to revise their ballots after seeing partial results or after receiving side-payment offers. A useful mechanic is a manipulation market: each participant receives a small number of tokens and may spend them to submit a ballot different from their true ranking, with tokens convertible into a prize at the end. You may invent the specific voting rule, but it must be simple enough to run in about 20 minutes and initially plausible as manipulation-proof.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto unrestricted domain, onto, strategy-proofness, dictatorship, and the pivotal-voter lemma. Also include a candid note on what the activity cannot reproduce, such as the full generality of the theorem, the formal induction, the difference between strategy-proofness and group strategy-proofness, or the fact that the theorem concerns social choice functions rather than social welfare functions. State what the facilitator must supply by way of explanation.

Predict what will go wrong the first time the activity is run, for example participants may coordinate on a truthful norm and refuse to manipulate, the rule may accidentally be dictatorial from the start, the points may not be salient enough, or the debrief may overstate the theorem. Give concrete fixes for each.

Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not rely on named historical cases or obscure empirical facts. Use only the formal ideas and whatever materials you specify.

Before writing the final design, think carefully and brainstorm at least three candidate game structures. For each, state in one or two sentences how it would make the pivotal-voter argument tangible. Then choose the best and present it in full. The final deliverable should be a polished facilitator’s guide: overview, learning goals, materials, setup, rules, payoff table, step-by-step timeline, debrief questions, common failure modes, and the honest limitations note.
