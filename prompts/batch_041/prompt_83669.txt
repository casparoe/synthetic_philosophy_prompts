I'm giving the opening remarks — six minutes, seven at the outside — at the first sitting of a citizens' assembly I spent two years arguing for. Sixty residents drawn by lot from the electoral roll, meeting across four weekends with a small budget for expert witnesses, and a remit: how the city should spend its flood-defence levy. In the room: the sixty themselves, a fair number of whom have already told me they have no business deciding anything, and one of whom has been saying to anyone who will listen that drawing a budget committee by lot is like drawing your surgeons by lot; the councillors who voted the assembly into existence, three or four of whom would not be sorry to see it embarrass itself; the local press, who will quote exactly one sentence; and, in the front row, three people who study deliberative democracy for a living and will wince if I get their field wrong. I have spent fifteen years in the comments sections of rationalist blogs, which is where I picked up most of my politics, so I know how much damage one badly chosen metaphor can do. Write the remarks, and build them on one.

The metaphor I want, and the argument I want made with it, is this. Almost every defence of choosing decision-makers by lot runs on a mirror: the lot gives you a body that resembles the rest of us — the same spread of ages, jobs, postcodes, opinions — and so its claim to decide rests on its being a miniature of the population. I think the mirror is both empirically shaky and politically dangerous, and I want the speech to say so and put something else in its place. The lot's virtue is not that it copies us. It is that it is the only route onto a decision-making body that nobody can work on. Every other route is a channel: you can campaign down it, donate down it, organise down it, inherit down it, get famous down it. Anything that carries influence gets farmed, which is why every other kind of body ends up populated by people who are unusually good at being selected rather than unusually good at deciding. The lot is a firewall; its job is to cut the channels. What it buys us is a table nobody could buy, inherit, or campaign their way to, occupied by people who had to spend four weekends reading evidence instead of raising money.

Draw out what that commits me to, out loud. It means I should not ask the sixty to represent anybody. It means I should not tell them their job is to canvass the neighbours and carry the neighbourhood's view in. It means the thing I am defending is not the accuracy of the sample but the un-gameability of the route, together with the quality of the deliberation — and that this is a colder, less flattering thing to say to a room of volunteers than that they are a perfect miniature of the city. I want it said anyway.

One question you have to settle rather than survey, because on the last day I have to tell them what happens to their work and they will know if I am hedging: is their recommendation binding — a pre-committed rule that it goes to a ratification vote up or down, no amendments — or advisory, with the council free to take it or leave it? Pick one, argue for it in the speech, and follow it through into what I actually promise them.

Constraints. No citations, no bibliography, no attributions: do not tell the room, or me, who said what, and do not name theorists — you would be guessing, and the three people in the front row would know. No empirical claims about how particular past assemblies turned out; I cannot check those from a lectern and neither can you, so make the argument structural instead. Plain language throughout — I have read too much lottocracy blogging to be trusted with the vocabulary in public, so keep the jargon out of it. If I have a half-formed thought that an assembly is the closest thing politics has to a control group, the one decision-making body with the selection effect switched off, use it only if it earns its place and cut it if it does not. And do not hand me a survey of the sortition debate with both sides weighed; take a line.

Commit. Where the line makes things uncomfortable for the people in the room — that their chief qualification is that nobody could capture them, that I would rather have them uncapturable than representative — say it rather than softening it. If you have to lean on a contested reading of the idea to make the speech work, add two sentences after it saying which reading, and what it costs. Six to seven minutes, roughly a thousand words.
