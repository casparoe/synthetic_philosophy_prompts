I am a software engineer — twelve years of backend work, the last four on the probabilistic forecasting models behind our demand-planning product — with no formal training in philosophy, and I have somehow become the person in the company who cares whether the numbers our models emit actually mean anything. In three weeks I am giving a forty-minute talk to our internal uncertainty group, and I want to prepare for the question period rather than the talk. The thesis I will defend is this. The best justification for the demand that a forecaster's degrees of belief satisfy the probability calculus is the Dutch book argument in the form usually called depragmatized: we do not assume that credences simply are betting dispositions, only that a set of credences violating the calculus is one to which an adversary can attach a collection of bets, each of which the agent would accept at her own stated odds, such that she loses money whichever way the world turns out — she is committed in advance to a sure loss, whether or not anyone ever offers her the bets. From there I want to push toward something more operational, and this is the part I am least confident about: that exploitability is the right engineering criterion for a defective probabilistic forecast, so that a model whose outputs on logically related events — P(A), P(B), P(A and B), P(A or B) — can be assembled by an adversary into a portfolio that loses money however things turn out should be treated as not merely inaccurate but broken in a stronger sense, and ought to be caught by an automated audit I plan to demonstrate. I suspect the depragmatization will not survive contact with the room, and I want to know precisely where it breaks.

The audience is about fifteen people. Most are statisticians and ML engineers who build and calibrate forecasting models, and who will reach instinctively for proper scoring rules, calibration curves, and the bias–variance tradeoff; they will want to know why I need the betting apparatus at all when a proper scoring rule already punishes a model for hedging. Two others did doctorates in philosophy: one works on accuracy-first epistemology and will argue that the whole Dutch book route is a detour around a stronger and cleaner argument from accuracy dominance, and one is a subjectivist of a de Finetti stripe who thinks the real mistake is treating coherence as a norm on a single agent's credences rather than as a property of a group's or a market's collective betting behaviour. Please write me a prep sheet rather than a defence: the eight or ten objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a decision-theory reading group, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it; for each, a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede up front rather than fight; the one objection that has to be answered well for the talk to land; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Finally, I would rather be told now that my framing is muddled than receive a tidy prep sheet that presupposes it is sound. If I am conflating coherence with calibration, or if the depragmatized argument does not do the work I think it does, or if my audit is a rebranded consistency check with no teeth, say so plainly before getting to the objections. I would also like a frank statement of how confident you are in the replies you propose, and a note on which of them you would not want to be held to in front of the two philosophers.
