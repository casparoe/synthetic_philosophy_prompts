A colleague in a philosophy of mind seminar defends what she calls the theatrical picture of consciousness. Mental life, on her account, is a stage; the senses are the wings; attention is the spotlight; and somewhere in the darkened house sits a single spectator to whom the whole performance is played. She uses the picture to argue three claims: that there is always a determinate fact of the matter about what it is like at a given moment; that the unity of consciousness is the unity of that one audience; and that any theory denying a privileged inner vantage point has changed the subject rather than explained it. I want the analogy audited before I concede anything to it.

First, set out the correspondences explicitly; a table is welcome. Say what in the target is supposed to play the role of the stage, the wings, the spotlight, the spectator, the seats, the script, and the interval, and mark which of these have no obvious occupant. Then name the features of the theatrical source that the picture quietly imports — among them a single vantage point, a determinate moment of viewing, a boundary between performance and audience, the possibility of an empty house, and an author or director standing outside the action — and say which of them are doing real work in her three claims.

Second, say where the mapping breaks. Which breaks are incidental, and which threaten the conclusions? Test the picture against the cases usually thought to strain it: split-brain subjects, change blindness and inattentional blindness, binocular rivalry, and the ordinary experience of driving a familiar route with no memory of having done so. State what the theatrical picture predicts in each case, where it fails, and whether the failure is a defect of the analogy or a defect of the theory the analogy is meant to express.

Third, take up the multiple drafts model as the best-known attempt to do without the theater. Does it succeed, or does it merely relocate the stage? Examine whether its own working images — drafts circulating and being revised, contents competing, some of them winning a kind of fame or celebrity in the brain, processes being probed and re-probed — reintroduce a spectator, a final draft, or a finish line at which everything is at last settled. If they do, say which of the colleague's three claims survives regardless, and on what basis.

Close with a verdict: either a better analogy for the unity and presence of experience, with the correspondence spelled out at least in outline, or an argument that no single analogy can carry the load, together with the reason.
