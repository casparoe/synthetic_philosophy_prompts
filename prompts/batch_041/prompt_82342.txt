I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and next month I'm presenting a dissertation chapter at a small workshop on desert and distributive justice. The chapter argues that karma, taken as a theory of moral desert rather than as a cosmology, is best reconstructed as a constitutive rather than a causal claim: a person's condition is not so much caused by her past intentional actions as it is the expression of the quality of will those actions embody. On that reading, karma never asserts that anyone merits the character from which she acts; it asserts only that her condition fits the quality of will she has expressed, and that desert is non-comparative — a matter of fit between act and condition, not of how much anyone holds relative to anyone else. The payoff I claim is that the standard anti-desert arguments, which all run through the thought that no one deserves the character she acts from, simply do not reach it; the price I concede is a heavy commitment about the persistence of the agent across lives.

The room splits roughly two to one. The larger group is analytic moral philosophers: several work on distributive justice and are Rawlsian by default, two work on free will and moral responsibility, and one is a luck egalitarian who regards desert as a vestige we should stop invoking. The rest are historians of Buddhist philosophy who know the scholastic material far better than I do and who will suspect that I have quietly Kantianized the doctrine into something safe for an analytic audience.

Tell me what to concede at the outset rather than spend the session fighting about, which single objection could sink the chapter if I fumble it, and which question I am hoping nobody in the room asks. I don't need citations — I'll supply those myself — and I would rather have the objections in the room's own terms than a survey of the literature.
