I'm teaching The Giver (Lois Lowry) to a Year 9 class next term and I want to do the Mary's room / knowledge argument busines properly with it, not the soggy version where you just say "so Mary learns something new, discuss". The bit I want to build the lesson round is this: in the book the community has engineered away colour and most strong feeling — the Sameness — Jonas starts seeing red, and then the Giver doesn't describe experiences to him, he transfers them, straight into his head, the sled, the sunburn, then the bad ones. And by the end Jonas can do it too; he settles Gabriel by giving him a memory. That seems to me to do something the original thought experiment doesn't, because in Jackson's Mary case the whole point is that no amount of information gets you the experience, and here experience gets handed over like a parcel. So is the novel dramatising the knowledge argument, or quietly knocking out one of its premises? And does it matter that Jonas isn't Mary at all — Mary is a complete physical knower missing one experience, whereas Jonas had the capacity and it was switched off, so he gains a faculty rather than a fact? The other thing I want to get right is the moral claim the book seems to be making, that the community isn't innocent but cruel, and that you can't be trusted to judge pain and death unless you've had them. That's stronger than anything I remember Jackson claiming and I'd like to know whether it survives.
