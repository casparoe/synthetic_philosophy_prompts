I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and next month I'm presenting a paper at a small workshop on democratic theory. My thesis is that the boundary problem — the puzzle of how a demos can legitimately settle its own membership when any such settlement already seems to presuppose a demos to make it — is generated by an assumption we can drop: that the demos must be a single, persisting, all-purpose body whose borders are drawn once and for all. I defend a decision-relative demos instead. On my account, for any particular collectively binding decision, the demos consists of those whose interests are authoritatively affected by that decision, and democratic legitimacy requires that they, and no others, have a say in it. There is then no single people to bound and no one-time line to draw, so the boundary problem dissolves rather than gets solved. I claim this handles the familiar hard cases — secession, resident non-citizens, expatriate voting, cross-border environmental harms — better than either a territorial account or a subjection-based one, precisely because it never has to gerrymander a permanent people out of a shifting set of stakeholders.

The audience matters more than usual here, so let me describe it. The workshop is mostly democratic theorists. Several are committed to the all-subjected, or coercion-based, principle and treat subjection to coercively enforced law — not bare affectedness — as what grounds a claim to inclusion. One or two work squarely on the all-affected principle and will be protective of it, since my view looks like a promiscuous version of their own. There is a Kantian constructivist who will press the circularity worry hard, an empirical political scientist who studies referendums and will go after feasibility and agenda control, and a global-justice person who thinks any bounded demos is already unjust.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, phrased in their vocabulary rather than mine, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, give me a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede up front rather than fight. Separate the objections that have real force from the ones that are mostly field-defending. Then tell me the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
