For a seminar on Augustine's ethics I have been trying to reconstruct an argument that seems to show that his account of lying cannot be right, and I cannot find the step that fails; every premise looks like something he would grant.

His account, as I read it, has two parts. In On Lying he says that "from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie," and that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind," the liar's fault being the desire to deceive. In Against Lying he argues at length that no lie is ever permitted — not one told to expose the heretics who were corrupting the faithful, not one told to save one's own life, since the martyrs did right to confess rather than to lie.

Here is the argument.

(1) A lie is an utterance at odds with the speaker's mind, made with the will to deceive. Whether the words themselves are true is irrelevant to whether one has lied.

(2) No lie is ever permissible, whatever follows from refusing to lie.

(3) A murderer asks me where his intended victim is hiding, and I know. If I answer him truthfully he will go there and kill the man; if I refuse to answer he will take my silence as confirmation, since he knows my habits. The only way to save the man is to bring the murderer to believe something false about where he is.

(4) To bring him to believe that, I must utter something I believe to be false. That is to say, I must lie.

(5) So if I am not to lie, the innocent man dies; and by (2) that is what must happen.

(6) But no defensible morality requires an innocent man's death when a harmless falsehood — harming no one, wronging no one, told solely to keep him out of a wrongdoer's hands — would prevent it.

(7) So either (1) or (2) is false.

The conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I doubt that Augustine's position is simply incoherent. Tell me which step I should give up, why it looked acceptable, and what giving it up costs. If you think the argument is sound as it stands, tell me which premise is least costly to surrender and what surrendering it commits me to; if you can keep both (1) and (2), say what the position then demands of me. I would rather give up (2) than (1), since without a criterion that marks off a lie from the mere keeping of a truth to oneself, I do not see how lying is to be distinguished from silence at all. Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
