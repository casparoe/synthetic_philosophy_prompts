I am a high school student, and for several weeks now a small Great Books seminar I belong to has been working through Nietzsche's essay on the use and abuse of history — the one that divides our relations to the past into the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical, and then argues that a culture can be harmed by history itself. The seminar has stalled, and I have come to think the stall is more interesting than the disagreement. Two camps have formed. The first reads the essay as a warning about quantity: modern historical culture produces and consumes far too much of the past, and the result is a kind of paralysis in which a person or a people knows everything about where it came from and can no longer act. The second reads it as a claim about orientation rather than amount: the problem is never how much past one carries but whether it is put in the service of present life, so that a culture with almost no history could be just as sick if its relation to the past were the wrong kind, and a culture carrying an enormous amount could be healthy if the relation were right. We have spent three sessions quoting the same passages at one another, and I have noticed that each side can absorb the other's evidence simply by redescribing it, which makes me suspect that the quarrel is not really about the text at all. I have a short essay due at the end of term and would rather understand the disagreement than win it, which is why what I want from you is a diagnosis and not a verdict.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that this is a verbal dispute: that "too much history" and "the wrong relation to history" are two descriptions of a single underlying phenomenon, and that the camps differ only over which description to privilege. To make the hypothesis checkable I proposed a test to the seminar — if the two sides agree about the health of every concrete culture we can name, the dispute is verbal, and if there is even one case in which one side calls a culture healthy and the other calls it sick, it is substantive — and I want to know whether that is the right test, or whether it already presupposes a picture of what makes a dispute verbal that a better account would reject. So please diagnose the impasse: is it verbal, is it substantive, or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about how much history is consumed and one about what history is for? If it is substantive, what is the hidden disagreement actually about — a contested standard of cultural health, an ambiguity in what Nietzsche means by life, or something neither camp has named? And whatever your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress in the seminar, as opposed to the mutual redescription we have been doing. Begin your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
