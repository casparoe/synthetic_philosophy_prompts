I am a law student in a seminar on evidence and cognition, and I have been arguing with a friend who clerks for a trial judge about how much weight courts should give to the timing of eyewitness observations. The practice I keep returning to is this: courts routinely let witnesses testify not only about what they saw but about when they saw it, in what order events occurred, and how long the interval was between, say, a shout and a collision. Judges and juries then treat those reports as observations of the temporal order of experience, as if the witness’s stream of consciousness came with reliable timestamps that memory simply preserves. But we have also been discussing the color phi phenomenon, in which two discs flashed in different locations and different colors are seen as a single disc moving and changing color in mid-flight, with the color change experienced as occurring before the second disc is even presented, so that later information seems to shape the content and apparent timing of an earlier experience. Philosophers distinguish Orwellian revisions, in which the experience itself was accurate but memory or report is later rewritten, from Stalinesque revisions, in which the experience is rewritten before it ever reaches awareness.

What philosophical commitments does the legal practice of trusting eyewitness timing presuppose? Does it presuppose a fact of the matter about the temporal order of experiences that is independent of any report, and that memory is a faithful recorder of that order? Does it require a Cartesian theater, a central inner display where experiences are presented to an observer, or can the practice be reconstructed without one? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, and is the practice actually underdetermined between Orwellian and Stalinesque readings, so that several different theories of time-consciousness would license it equally well? If we replaced the presupposed view with a thoroughly reconstructive account of memory, in which reports are the only evidence and no pre-revision fact is recoverable, what would have to change in how courts treat timing testimony, and what parts of the practice could survive?
