Okay so this is a slightly strange ask and I've been putting it off for weeks. My old PhD supervisor died in March, he was 78 and it wasn't sudden, and his family have asked me to do the eulogy at the memorial in a few weeks, at the village hall, which is where he'd have wanted it. The problem is the room. Half of it will be philosophers who spent thirty-odd years arguing with him, and the other half will be his two brothers, his neighbours from the allotment, and the four guys he played bridge with every Thursday who liked him enormously and have honestly no idea what he did for a living. His whole career went on defending deontological constraints -- the idea that there are some things you just must not do, even when doing them would stop more of the same thing from happening. And every time I try to draft this I hit the same wall, which I think is what people call the paradox of deontology: if the reason not to do the terrible thing is simply that terrible things are bad, then it's hard to see why you can't do one terrible thing to prevent five, and at that point the person who refuses starts to look like someone who cared more about keeping his own hands clean than about the five. I can't stand up and say that at his funeral, and I also can't pretend he was secretly a consequentialist who did the sums and happened to like the answer, because he wasn't, and the philosophers in the room would know it instantly.

What makes it harder is that he actually lived this out once, and the bridge club all knows the story. In 1989 he was a junior administrator at a hospital trust during a bad funding squeeze, and there was a form he could have signed that would have unlocked a pot of money for a drug that, on the numbers, would probably have kept two or three people alive who died that winter. The form would have been a lie -- a couple of numbers moved around, and they were numbers about a real patient, though it wouldn't have changed her treatment and she'd never have known. The only person it would have decieved was an accountant. He wouldn't do it. He never once said he regretted it, and it cost him at least one friendship that I know of. So the eulogy has to do something with that, in a room where the philosophers will be watching to see whether I turn his life into a lesson, and his family will just be remembering a stubborn man they loved.

Could you write it for me? Seven or eight minutes spoken, warm, and built around one concrete image from his actual life rather than a thought experiment -- he kept bees, if that's any use, and he was a genuinely terrible bridge player. The philosophical part needs to be doing real structural work here, not decorating the thing: I need a way of saying what the constraint was actually protecting, such that his refusal was neither vanity about his own record nor plain rule-worship, and I need to be able to say it out loud to people who've never read a page of moral philosophy without them feeling lectured at. Don't name-drop any philosophers, that would be very odd at a funeral. Start with the eulogy itself, no preamble, and let the first line already be carrying the weight of the whole thing; if you want to flag a choice you made, keep it to two or three sentences right at the end.
