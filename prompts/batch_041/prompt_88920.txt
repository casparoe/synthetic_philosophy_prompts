I am a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I run a weekly seminar for advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students on distributive justice. I have sixteen participants and one seventy-five-minute session, and I want to spend it on luck egalitarianism. My problem is that when we read the canonical papers, students nod along with the formula — a distribution is just when it tracks people's choices and neutralizes the effects of brute luck — and never feel why anyone would either embrace the view or recoil from it. I want an activity, not a lecture, and I want the participants to author the uncomfortable consequence themselves.

Assume the role of an experienced teacher of political philosophy who also designs simulation and role-play games, and who knows the luck-egalitarian literature and its critics in detail. Before you design anything, reason through the design problem explicitly: what sequence of experiences would turn the option-luck versus brute-luck distinction from a slogan into a live constraint, and what would make the harshness of the view — the part where the imprudent are left to their fate — land as a real cost that the participants impose on one another, rather than something a critic merely asserts. Brainstorm a few candidate mechanics, say briefly why you discard the weaker ones, and then commit to one design.

The structure I want you to build around, which you may refine but should preserve:

1. Participants are assigned endowments at random, from a deck or dice, standing for unchosen advantage and disadvantage.
2. They then face a real, personal choice with stated odds — a gamble whose outcome depends on both the choice and a subsequent random draw — so that bad outcomes have two possible sources at once.
3. Before any outcome resolves, the group must write and adopt a compensation rule governing a fixed common pool, knowing only that they will be bound by it.
4. Outcomes resolve, and the group must then adjudicate actual claims under their own rule, with a budget that will not cover every claim. They must decide, as a body, whether at least one member is left uncompensated, and they must actually do it.
5. Seed the cases so that the choice-versus-luck line is genuinely unclear: one bad outcome from a gamble freely taken; one from a gamble taken because of a trait or circumstance the participant did not choose; one participant with an expensive need, who requires more resources than others merely to reach the same baseline; and one prudent participant who visibly resents being taxed for the rest.

Specify the setting, group size, and time budget (sixteen people, seventy-five minutes), all materials, a payoff scheme in points or tokens rather than money, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the precise moment at which the facilitator must stay silent so that the group discovers the problem on its own. Do not name luck egalitarianism, or the option-luck and brute-luck terminology, until the debrief.

Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the view — the choice-sensitivity intuition, the brute-luck compensation principle, the harshness objection, and the regress that threatens any clean line between a choice and the unchosen conditions that produced it — and tell me what I should draw out in discussion versus state outright.

Finally, do two honest things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each failure: I expect that the group will initially try to compensate everyone, that some participants will game the rule they wrote, and that the adjudication stage will deadlock over whether a given outcome was chosen. Second, tell me which feature of luck egalitarianism this activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply myself — I suspect it cannot capture the deeper argument over whether equality is the relevant value at all, or the contrast with prioritarian and desert-based alternatives, but I want your judgment rather than mine.

Please write this as a practical, runnable plan I could hand to a co-teacher, not as an essay about the topic.
