Two camps in the philosophy of biology have argued past each other over whether DNA is a code, and I want the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. One camp holds that the genetic system genuinely carries semantic information: the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is not fixed by chemical necessity, the transfer-RNA adaptors and the ribosome function as a decoding apparatus, and a sequence therefore stands for the protein it specifies. The other holds that talk of a code is at most a convenient shorthand for causal and chemical specificity, that nothing in the system represents anything, and that the informational vocabulary imports commitments from linguistics and computing that the molecules do not underwrite.

Write the document such a collaboration would produce rather than a survey of the debate. It should contain, in order: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, covering the molecular facts, what the arbitrariness of the mapping does and does not establish, the explanatory work the informational vocabulary actually does in the practice of biology, and what the two positions share even where their terminologies diverge; then a disagreements table in which each side's position is stated in wording its own adherents would accept; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each, a concrete case, thought experiment, or empirical possibility that both sides agree would bear on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. The agreed claims should be substantive enough that one could imagine a partisan of either camp resisting them; avoid truisms, and avoid formulations that quietly favor one side.

Treat seriously the possibility that part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive: identify which part, and say why, and note any apparent disagreement that dissolves once the terms are fixed. The cruxes I expect to matter include whether arbitrariness of the mapping entails representational content or only causal contingency; whether a code requires a decoder that is itself a representational system or whether a mechanical adaptor suffices; whether there is a fact of the matter about what a sequence means independent of the machinery that reads it; and whether the informational description yields explanations or predictions unavailable to a purely chemical one. Add or replace cruxes if the collaboration would.

Two cases I would like addressed in particular. First, an organism whose code differs from the standard one at a few codons, so that a given DNA sequence would specify different proteins; second, a hypothetical system in which the mapping is fixed by stereochemistry rather than being arbitrary. For each, state what each camp predicts and what each would count as evidence against its own position. Argue from the structure of the cases; do not supply citations, a reading list, or appeals to authority.
