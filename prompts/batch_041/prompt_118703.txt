I write about cognitive science and AI for a mid-size outlet, and I've got a recurring problem I'd rather hand you as a reusable tool than a one-off fix. Every week there's a press release or preprint that runs on an analogy — the genome is a program, the cell is a factory, the immune system is an army, a language model is reasoning, a neural net is doing something like imagining — and I have to work out fast whether the analogy is doing honest explanatory work or smuggling in a claim the evidence won't carry. My current method is vibes and it's embarrassing.

So: take the role of someone who has really internalized the Hofstadter/Sander line that analogy isn't decoration on top of thought but is the mechanism of thought itself (Surfaces and Essences), and who has also spent actual time in a newsroom. Before you write anything, think out loud for a bit — brainstorm what tends to go wrong with analogies in science coverage, and what a usable procedure has to capture that the obvious test (does the analogy break down somewhere?) misses. My hunch is that everything breaks down somewhere, so that test is useless; tell me if I'm wrong.

Then write the procedure. I want a one-page card I can run in about fifteen minutes before deadline, and a longer version for when I have a couple hours or an editor who'll actually read it. It has to work for a freelancer who can't get the paywalled paper and doesn't have a PhD in the subfield, and it has to say at which points the decision is a values call or a taste call dressed up as a test, and where the whole thing stops with a no. Three home-made tests I've been groping toward and want you to fix, formalize, or throw out: (1) the payload test — state exactly what claim is being transferred from the familiar thing to the new thing; (2) the free-ride test — what does the source domain hand the reader for free that the story never claimed; (3) the counter-analogy swap — name an equally apt analogy pointing the other way and see whether the story's claim survives the swap. If a claim only survives under one analogy, I think that's a red flag, but maybe not, tell me.

Then run it on three cases: a release describing a newly mapped gene regulatory network as the cell's decision-making board; a model described as planning ahead when it's really just search; and one where I think the analogy is fine and the real problem is elsewhere in the story — I won't like that answer but I need the procedure to be able to give it. Finish by telling me where this procedure will misfire and which of my three tests you kept, changed, or binned and why.
