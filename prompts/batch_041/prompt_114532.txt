For about a year my reading group has been stuck on a single counterfactual, and I have come to suspect that the stuckness is itself the interesting phenomenon. The question is whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. The two camps are thoroughly entrenched. One holds that Hitler was a contingent, load-bearing cause: absent him, the Weimar Republic might still have slid into some authoritarian-right formation, but the specific configuration — the racial war of annihilation, the camps, arguably the war itself — was his doing, and a generic strongman in his place would have produced a different and less catastrophic regime. The other camp holds that the structural conditions — a defeated and humiliated state, a republic with shallow legitimacy, an economic catastrophe, a radicalized and antisemitic right, a paramilitarized street politics — made some such outcome robust; Hitler was the particular token of a type that was going to be realized in any case, and removing him simply relocates the same pressures onto another bearer.

Here is my hypothesis about why we cannot move, and I want you to tell me whether it is right. I suspect we are not really disagreeing about the history. We are disagreeing about which counterfactual is even under discussion, and about what a counterfactual explanation is supposed to deliver. The phrase without Hitler does not name one antecedent but a family of them: Hitler never born, Hitler killed in the First World War, Hitler shot in the 1923 putsch, Hitler assassinated in 1938, Hitler a mediocre painter in Vienna. Depending on which member of the family you choose, either side can win, and each side has been quietly selecting the one that suits it. Underneath that, one camp wants an answer to the difference-making question — what did he change? — while the other wants an answer to the robustness question — how much else would have had to be different for the outcome to be avoided? Those two questions can have different answers even when everyone agrees on every fact.

So: is that the right diagnosis? Is this a verbal dispute about which counterfactual we mean, a substantive disagreement about the aims of historical explanation, or a concealed empirical disagreement about how overdetermined the outcome actually was — and if the last, what evidence could in principle settle it? I want a committed diagnosis defended with reasons, not a menu of options handed back to me. If my hypothesis is wrong, say so plainly and tell me what the real structure of the impasse is. And tell me what would count as progress from here: a sharper distinction, a change in the question, a specific kind of evidence, or nothing at all — with reasons for thinking the impasse is or is not tractable.
