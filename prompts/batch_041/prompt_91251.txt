Ok, I want you to try to take down a view I've talked myself into, and I want you to be actually good at it. The view: Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, read strongly, is right. Not in the watered-down sense where Calvinism and early capitalism just happened to sit side by side in the same places, but causally -- the predestination doctrine generated a specific anxiety about whether you were among the elect, that anxiety got channeled by pastoral advice into a methodical, disciplined, this-worldly way of life, work became a calling, consumption got restrained and profit got reinvested, and that whole package is what let capitalist accumulation take off in northern Europe first. So when I say culture did causal work here I mean specific beliefs doing specific psychological work on specific people, not some vague national character. I'm not asking for the standard textbook hit list (Catholic regions were capitalist too, capitalism showed up in China, Weber was just wrong about the facts, etc.) -- I've heard those and I think most of them are weaker than people assume. What I want is the stuff that should genuinely move me. What observations, cases, or arguments would count against this as a causal claim, sorted by how decisive they'd be, and which ones would a defender of the thesis concede actually count against them rather than just waving away?

Some things I specifically want you to dig into. First, reverse causation and how anyone could tell the difference in principle -- what would it even look like for the economic form to be producing the theology rather than the other way round, and is there a version of that story that fits the same evidence just as well? Second, the worry that I've built something unfalsifiable: if any value system can be read as supporting any economic form after the fact, and if I can always say the ethic was there but the institutions weren't, then what would the world have to look like for my claim to be false? Is there a clean test, or am I in the position of a theory that can absorb everything?

Third, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: I think the standard empirical tests are asking the wrong question. It seems to me the macro outcome wouldn't depend on the average intensity of belief in a population, it would depend on the shape of the distribution -- a small committed minority with a genuinely methodical discipline could shift an economy even if most people are only mildly religious and causally inert. If that's right then studies that correlate regional religiosity with growth are almost irrelevant to my claim, which is convenient for me and therefore suspicious. Tell me honestly whether that move is a legitimate refinement or just a way of insulating the thesis. Related: does the doctrine that mattered have to be the theology as professors taught it, or the version ordinary people actually carried around? If those come apart, which one am I allowed to use as my cause?

Last thing: I'm making a claim about a whole civilization, but the mechanism I'm describing is about individual minds. Do I owe you an account of how you get from one to the other, and if I can't give one, does the causal claim collapse into something weaker -- a reconstruction of how the ethic hangs together with the practice, rather than an explanation of why the practice appeared? What would distinguish a real causal explanation from a rationalizing redescription here?

At the end, tell me plainly whether you think the thesis as I've stated it is well protected from refutation, and whether that's a defect or just what any big historical causal claim looks like. And if you had to bet, which single thing on your list would you expect to actually change my mind?
