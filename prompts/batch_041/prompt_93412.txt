The question the convenor set for this paper was: "Do moral revolutions -- the ending of practices such as dueling and slavery -- come about through arguments, through interests, or through honor? Reconstruct the honor-based account of how moral revolutions happen and assess whether it can absorb the argument-based and interest-based explanations." The paper I have to mark reads in full: "The abolition of the slave trade and the end of dueling are often described as moral revolutions: practices that were once honorable became unthinkable within a generation. This essay argues that neither arguments nor interests can carry the explanatory load on their own, and that Appiah is right to put honor at the center.

The interest-based explanation is associated with Eric Williams. In Capitalism and Slavery Williams argued that the British West Indian sugar economy had gone into decline by the end of the eighteenth century, and that the abolition of the slave trade in 1807 and of slavery in 1833 were therefore the cutting of losses rather than the triumph of conscience. Williams's critics have not overturned this picture. Seymour Drescher showed that the British West Indian economy was still expanding at the moment of abolition, which shows that economic interests cannot explain abolition at all. What remains, then, is argument and honor.

The argument against slavery had been available long before it changed anything: natural-rights theorists had condemned it, and the Quakers had petitioned against it in the seventeenth century. Appiah's explanation is that moral argument alone never moves people to act. What moves them is honor. Dueling, foot-binding and slavery each ended when the practice came to be seen as dishonorable rather than as a mark of status, so that continuing it exposed a person to shame. In each case the change was a change in what was honored, not a discovery of new reasons.

The important qualification is that Appiah does not think slavery became wrong when people stopped honoring it. The wrongness was always there; honor simply did the work of changing conduct. Honor is the efficient cause of the revolution, not its ground. Since honor codes are local and variable, though, Appiah's account implies that moral revolutions are not really moral progress, only changes in taste.

One might set against Appiah Singer's expanding circle, on which moral progress consists in extending to ever wider circles the concern we already extend to our kin. I think the expanding circle explains dueling better than honor does: Regency duelists came to include one another in the circle of full moral concern, and dueling disappeared once the antagonist's interests were taken seriously. The two accounts are therefore rivals, and the expanding circle is the more parsimonious."
