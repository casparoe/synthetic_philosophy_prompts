Write a dialogue between two philosophers who are equally intelligent, equally well informed, and equally determined to follow an argument wherever it goes. Neither may win by appeal to the authority of a named thinker, a school, or a text; everything must be argued from the case and from principles the characters themselves state and defend. Neither may be made to play the fool.

The first, whom we may call the margin theorist, holds the following position. Knowledge requires a margin for error: if you know that a condition obtains in your case, then it obtains in every case sufficiently similar to yours, since if it failed in a case you could not distinguish from your own, your belief would not be safe. Now take any condition that (i) you can be in, (ii) you can fail to be in, and (iii) admits of a continuous series of cases running from a clear case of the condition to a clear case of its absence, adjacent members of which are indistinguishable to the subject. The margin principle implies that the subject's knowledge gives out before the condition does; hence there are cases in which she is in the condition and yet does not know that she is. So no such condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous just in case being in it guarantees being in a position to know that one is in it. Applied to the condition "knowing that p," the argument shows that knowledge is not luminous; and because the KK principle — if you know that p, then you know that you know that p — would make knowledge luminous, the KK principle fails.

The second, whom we may call the introspection theorist, accepts that the argument may work for knowledge of the external world, but holds that it fails for a subject's knowledge of her own occurrent mental states. She thinks a subject can know how things presently seem to her, that such knowledge is not hostage to a margin, and therefore that the KK principle survives in the domain of self-knowledge. She is entitled to her strongest arguments: for instance, that a belief about one's own experience is not a risky classification of an inner object but something closer to an avowal of the state itself; that presence to the subject is a different relation from evidence about a mind-independent world; and that if the margin argument really worked here, one could never know whether one is in pain, which is absurd.

The two are to argue about a specific experiment. Ada sits before a screen that begins at a comfortable brightness and fades, over about an hour, to black. She is attentive and honest throughout, and at every moment she is asked whether the screen looks bright to her.

The dialogue should engage, in whatever order proves natural, at least the following:

1. In the ordinary version of the experiment, is there a moment at which the screen looks bright to Ada and yet she does not know that it looks bright to her? Give the margin theorist's best case for yes and the introspection theorist's best case for no.
2. Which condition's luminosity is really at stake — the classification "the screen looks bright to her," which is vague, or the determinate quality of the experience she is presently having? Then consider a variant of the experiment in which Ada's visual system is stipulated to be such that she either has the bright-look or the dim-look, never anything intermediate, even though the screen's luminance continues to vary continuously. Does the difference between the versions matter to the margin argument? If the introspection theorist claims the variant vindicates her, the margin theorist must say precisely what is wrong with that claim; if the margin theorist claims the variant changes nothing, she must say precisely why.
3. Suppose that at the moment the screen has just ceased to look bright to her, Ada believes that she knows that it has ceased. Is her belief knowledge? Does anything turn on whether her confidence in that belief is itself something about which she could be mistaken?
4. The introspection theorist holds that Ada's access to her own experience differs in kind from her access to the brightness of the screen. The margin theorist should press on exactly what the difference is supposed to be, and the introspection theorist should say what work it does — in particular, whether it shows that a belief about one's own experience cannot be unsafe, or only that it is usually safe.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker must respond to the other's actual formulations rather than restate an opening position, and each should at some point concede or refine something under pressure. It should end in genuine disagreement, but the reader should be able to see exactly which premise would have to be settled, and by what, for the disagreement to be resolved. Do not dissolve the dispute by declaring that both are right about different domains unless that claim is itself argued for. Write in prose, about 1500 words.
