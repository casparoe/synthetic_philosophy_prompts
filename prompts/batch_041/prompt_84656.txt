I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of science seminar, and I've been asked to open next week's session on the problem of old evidence, which is the one piece of Bayesian confirmation theory I can't get to sit right. I understand the mechanics well enough: conditionalization raises a hypothesis's probability exactly when the hypothesis makes the evidence more likely than its rivals do, and conditioning on something the agent is already certain of is a no-op. The problem, as it is usually put, is that a theory entailing a fact established long before the theory was formulated — the standard illustration being a theory that accounts for an anomaly that had already been measured — is intuitively confirmed by that fact, and yet the agent was certain of the fact beforehand, so the theory's probability cannot move. What I want is not the anecdote but the anatomy: why the problem arises at all, and what is genuinely at stake in the standard repairs. In particular I want the repair that says the agent should conditionalize not on the evidence, which she already knew, but on the entailment itself — the proposition that the theory implies the evidence — which she has only just come to appreciate. I can see that this makes the theory's probability rise; what I cannot see is whether it does so honestly. It looks as though the rise comes from the agent's earlier failure to notice a logical consequence of things she already believed, so that a fully rational agent would be confirmed by nothing, and the less logically alert she was, the more her theory gains. Please explain whether that objection is fatal to the repair or merely a cost it can bear, and how the alternatives compare: recomputing the agent's prior as the credence she would have had if she had never learned the evidence; or giving up the idea that confirmation must show up as a rise in the theory's unconditional probability at all, treating it instead as a matter of how the theory stands against its rivals. I would also like the sharper version of the case — the agent who has known the evidence for years and has had the theory for years, but has only now worked out that the theory entails it. Does she get confirmed, and if so, is that confirmation something a rational agent could deserve? One friend in the seminar says the whole difficulty is a pseudo-problem that dissolves the moment one stops insisting that an agent be certain of her evidence; I would like to know whether that is a real dissolution or a change of subject.

Please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested. My sense is that the probability facts themselves are not in dispute, and that the live question is normative — whether a confirmation that depends on prior logical sloppiness is confirmation worth having — but I do not know how far agreement extends beyond that, and I would rather have the disagreements marked clearly than be handed one camp's solution dressed up as the consensus. Where a repair is popular but carries a well-known cost, state the cost plainly; and where you are offering your own assessment rather than reporting a position that people actually hold, say which it is.
