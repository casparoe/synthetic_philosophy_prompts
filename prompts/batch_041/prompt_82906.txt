Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I retired a couple of years ago after 34 years doing cosmology, and I've been spending the time I used to spend writing grant reports on philosophy instead, which has been a treat but also humbling. I've gotten a bit obssessed with infinite ethics — the whole cluster of problems you get when the world, or the value in it, turns out to be infinite: infinite populations whose total welfare you can't compare, infinite causal chains, infinite futures built out of finite goods, multiverse-ish setups where everyone exists infinitely many times over. Every treatment I read seems to make one of two moves: either the sums diverge so nothing you do makes any difference, or here's a clever limit or ordering that ranks the cases after all. Neither leaves me satisfied, though I'm self-aware enough to suspect I'm reinventing a wheel that's been argued over for decades.

What I actually want from you is a prioritization. I have maybe two years of serious reading left in me, plus a small pot of money — enough to fund two or three people for two years, not a program. Which open questions here most deserve that effort, and in what order? I don't want the list, I want the reasoning behind it: which questions are upstream of which, which are actually tractable for people sitting at desks (as opposed to needing new physics or mathematics nobody has yet), and which ones I'd be a fool to spend two years on because even a clean answer wouldn't change anything downstream. And tell me what you'd expect to see in the next year or two that would move something up or down your ranking.

I want to say the second is better, but I can't say it without smuggling in a measure, and I'm not sure the measure isn't doing the work I wanted the ethics to do.
- Is some of this just the rearrangement theorem in disguise — a series you can make sum to anything by reordering — or is that a pun rather than an analogy?
- How much of the problem is really about value, and how much is decision theory under infinite or undefined expected value? If you could fund only one of those two, which, and why?
- And is any of it downstream of something in set theory or the foundations of probability that's already settled, or already abandoned?

Rank them and defend the ranking. Skip the reading list — I want the arguments themselves, not pointers.
