I have been thinking on my own about Elizabeth Anscombe's claim that Truman was a murderer for ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and I have reconstructed an argument for it that I find both compelling and troubling. (1) It is always wrong to intend the death of an innocent person as a means to some further end. (2) Truman intended the deaths of the civilians in those cities as a means to force Japan's surrender. (3) Therefore Truman was a murderer. (4) But if that is enough to make Truman a murderer, then the same verdict follows for the Allied leaders who ordered the firebombing of Tokyo and Dresden, the naval blockade that starved civilians in Japan and Germany, the demand for unconditional surrender, and the bomber pilot who destroys a munitions factory knowing that the workers on the night shift will be killed, since each of those policies or actions knowingly brought about the deaths of innocents as part of the strategy for victory. (5) It is not the case that nearly every major Allied leader was a murderer, and it is not the case that no war can be fought justly unless no innocent person is ever knowingly killed as part of the strategy. (6) So either (1) is false, or the application to Truman in (2) is wrong, or the extension in (4) does not follow.

I want to keep (1), and I do not want to give up (5), but I cannot see how to hold both. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I cannot tell whether the problem is in (1) or in the extension to the other cases, and I would like to know what a defensible version of the argument would look like.
