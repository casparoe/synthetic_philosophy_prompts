ok so I'm taking a markets and morals class this term and we have to hand in a position statement next week and then defend it in section, which I'm not looking forward to. Our TA said the fastest way to get dismantled is to just list everything you believe and see what falls out, so I'm doing that to myself first.

Heres roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm pro-market by default. Most bans on 'repugnant' transactions are really just disgust wearing a suit, and disgust is never a reason to ban anything.

(2) The ban on selling kidneys should go. It's your body, and the people dying on the waitlist are actual people.

(3) The exploitation objection to kidney markets is weak. Exploitation needs either coercion or a fair price baseline, and neither is obviosly there; plus the seller's next-best option is usually worse than the sale.

(4) Vote selling should stay illegal, and not for consequentialist reasons. A vote isn't the kind of thing an individual owns in the first place, so there's nothing there for them to sell.

(5) Price gouging after a disaster should also be illegal, and I'd defend that on solidarity grounds rather than on harm to consumers.

(6) I do think the commodification objection is real, i.e. some goods really are corrupted by being priced, but it should be used very sparingly, since it's the kind of argument that can justify banning just about anything.

What I want from you: which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what would have to give. Please rank any real tensions by severity instead of telling me they're all 'in productive tension' (the TA's favorite phrase), and for each real one tell me the cheapest repair and what that repair costs me. If it turns out none of them conflict, say that too — I'd rather find out now than in section. And if the trouble is with one of my general principles rather than with a particular verdict, tell me which principle is doing the damage.
