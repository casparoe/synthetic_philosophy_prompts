At a departmental workshop on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, I watched two colleagues, A and B, disagree about how his framework should classify a research programme I will call the cascade programme. The dispute concerns Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating problemshifts: roughly, a programme is progressive when its belt adjustments lead to novel predictions, some of which are confirmed, and degenerating when those adjustments merely accommodate anomalies already known. The cascade programme's hard core is the claim that every fundamental interaction is mediated by a discrete cascade of intermediate states with characteristic masses. Its protective belt contains a screening rule that explains why some cascade members are not detected at low energies, a combinatorial principle that fixes the multiplicities of the cascade, and auxiliary assumptions linking cascade multiplicity to decay rates. The programme began by explaining a known anomaly in certain decay rates. It then predicted a new resonance at a specific energy. The first search found nothing. The group added the screening rule, which it says followed from the combinatorial principle; B says it was invented to explain the failed search. A later experiment reported a broad bump near the predicted energy, which the group counted as a novel confirmation. It has since predicted two further effects: one was confirmed, one remains untested. The combinatorial principle has been revised twice, each time in response to new decay-rate data, and each revision generated a new prediction about angular correlations. The group maintains that each revision was motivated by a broader symmetry requirement; B contends that each was tailored to the known decay rates.

A says the programme is progressive. It predicted a novel resonance, the bump confirmed it, and the positive heuristic is still generating new predictions. That, A says, is exactly the pattern Lakatos calls a progressive problemshift.

B says it is degenerating. The bump was not a novel prediction in the relevant sense: the group reached it only after inventing the screening rule to explain the failed search, and the bump was already present in older data. The combinatorial principle has been repeatedly adjusted to fit known decay rates, so its predictions are not risky. The angular-correlation prediction is untested. On B's view, the programme is a series of ad hoc rescues, and A is confusing heuristic fertility with empirical progress.

A replies that B misreads Lakatos. Novelty is not about when a fact was discovered or whether the data already existed; it is about whether the fact was used in constructing the theory. The screening rule followed from the combinatorial principle, and the bump's energy followed from that principle before the later experiment. The revisions, likewise, were driven by a symmetry requirement that the group would have adopted even without the decay-rate anomaly. A adds that Lakatos explicitly rejected the falsificationist demand that a programme be abandoned or deemed degenerating as soon as it needs auxiliary adjustments; the positive heuristic is what keeps a programme progressive, and this one is fertile.

B replies that the combinatorial principle was revised after the anomaly, not independently of it, and that the symmetry requirement was itself introduced only when the decay-rate data threatened the programme. A programme whose belt is rewritten whenever data arrive is not making risky predictions. B also says that even if the heuristic is fertile, fertility is a prediction about future success, not evidence of past empirical progress. Lakatos's distinction, B argues, is retrospective and comparative: a programme earns the label progressive only after a sustained record of novel successes, and the cascade programme has one contested bump and a pile of repairs. A's confidence that it is progressive now is unwarranted.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1 through 4 should rate A's first move, B's first move, A's second move, and B's second move, in that order, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Item 5 should say what both parties are missing and which side has the better overall case.
