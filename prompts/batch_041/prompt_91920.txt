I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last few years working through the philosophy of inference, and I am preparing a long-form interview for a small podcast series on foundations. My guest defends a particular and, to my mind, contestable reading of Richard Jeffrey's rule for updating on uncertain evidence. He does not present probability kinematics as a fallback for the rare case where evidence arrives less than certainly. His thesis is that the rigidity condition — that learning leaves the conditional probabilities given the elements of the evidence partition untouched — is not a substantive assumption about evidential states at all, but a stipulation about what counts as a single episode of learning. Where rigidity fails, he says, either there was more than one episode or there was no learning; and so the familiar complaints that the rule is partition-sensitive, and that it gives different results depending on the order in which uncertain evidence arrives, miss their target, since a sequence of rigid updates on incompatible partitions was never a well-defined evidential history to begin with.
