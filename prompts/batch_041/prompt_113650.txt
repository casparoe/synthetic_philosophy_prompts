I am a novelist, and I am several years into a book set in a future in which enormous quantities of suffering have been produced — some of it intended, much of it an unintended by-product of systems optimizing for other things — and I have reached the point where I need to decide which intellectual problems deserve the next year of my reading and thought, as opposed to which ones merely furnish good material. I would therefore like to treat this as an agenda-setting question. Within the loose and still-groping body of thought about suffering risks — the possibility that the long-run future contains vastly more suffering, in scale or in intensity, than the present, and that such an outcome could become locked in — which open conceptual questions are most worth the attention of someone who will not do empirical work but can think carefully and write? I want a ranked and defended agenda, not a survey.

A few distinctions I have found useful, and would like the ranking to respect:

First, separate suffering that some agent intends from suffering that emerges as an unintended consequence of agents pursuing other goals, and say whether the two raise genuinely different conceptual questions or only different causal stories.

Second, separate the question of whether a state is experienced as suffering from the question of whether it merely plays the functional role of suffering within some system's operation, and say whether that distinction should change what we count as the worst outcomes or whether it is a distraction from them.

Third, separate how bad a locked-in suffering outcome would be from how likely it is and how reversible it would be.

Fourth, distinguish the problem of comparing suffering across radically different kinds of minds — biological, digital, collective, or otherwise unfamiliar — from the prior question of whether such comparisons are the right way to decide what to prevent at all.

Then rank the open questions. For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others, whether it can be usefully addressed by a careful non-specialist working with concepts rather than data, and what evidence or argument over the next year would move it up or down my list. I am especially interested in questions whose answers would change what I take the worst outcomes to be, and in questions the field seems to be quietly assuming an answer to without defending it. Tell me also which of the questions people are drawn to are, in your judgment, overrated — because they are already settled, because they are pseudo-questions, or because their answers would not change anything downstream.

Finally, since I am a novelist and not a researcher, tell me which two or three of these questions you would actually have me spend the year on, and why the rest can wait. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want the reasoning, made in your own words.
