I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and I have been asked to run the opening session of our department's interdisciplinary workshop on translation and meaning. Sixteen participants, graduate students in philosophy, classics, and comparative literature; one room; ninety minutes; no reliable projector and no budget. I want a session that makes them feel the central difficulty of translating a text whose form is doing real work — the difficulty that Le Ton beau de Marot circles for hundreds of pages: that when a poem's form carries meaning, literal fidelity can destroy the very thing you were trying to carry, and that keeping the form forces invention rather than substitution. I do not want to explain this to them. I want the rules to make them collide with it, so that they leave having felt both the impossibility of perfect fidelity and the creativity the constraint forces out of them.

For the occasion I have written an over-constrained four-line source text, and I want it to be the thing they translate:

Forgetting is the price of making new;
Or so the translators say, so they grieve;
Rhyme is a cage, and yet the bird sings true
Merely because the cage will not believe.

It is exactly ten syllables to the line, it rhymes ABAB (new with true, grieve with believe), and its first letters, read downward, spell FORM. Those are the four constraints I want the activity to hold in tension at once: sense, rhyme, syllable count, and a hidden acrostic.

Please design the runnable session — rules, materials, how to pair participants, scoring, and a minute-by-minute timeline — such that teams must translate the poem into a target language of their choice (modern or historical, natural or constructed, provided at least two members of the team can read it) while trying to satisfy all four constraints simultaneously, and cannot. Build in these three devices:

1. The target acrostic need not spell FORM. Each team must choose a word that names what they take the poem to be about, and the aptness of that word is itself scored. This converts an interpretive decision into a formal constraint the team must then satisfy.
2. A fidelity budget. Each team begins with six tokens; spending one token licenses a single deliberate deviation from one constraint on one line, logged in a visible ledger with a note on what the token bought. No money, only tokens and the ledger.
3. A back-translation round. After the first draft, teams exchange translations. The receiving team is given the translation and the four constraints but is told neither the hidden word nor the original; it must (a) recover the hidden acrostic word and (b) reconstruct a plausible source text. This makes the loss measurable rather than merely asserted.

I need the scoring scheme to put sense and form into genuine conflict, so that no team can maximize both and every team is forced to say aloud what it sacrificed. Then give me the debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the underlying ideas: why form and content are not cleanly separable here, why the constraint generates solutions instead of blocking them, and what fidelity turns out to mean once it has to be chosen rather than assumed. Be frank, in a final section, about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words — I suspect at least this: that the real lesson is that there are many good translations and that my winner is a function of my rubric rather than of the poem. Finally, tell me the three or four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to head them off without letting the debrief collapse into a lecture.
