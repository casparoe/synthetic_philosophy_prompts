I write fiction, and I am midway through a novel set inside an AI research laboratory. A scene turns on an argument between two of my characters about the laboratory's training regime. The laboratory supervises its models step by step: a separate network assigns a score to each intermediate move in a chain of reasoning, and the policy is then optimised against those step scores rather than against the correctness of its final answers. One character maintains that this is not a distinct form of supervision at all, since a step score is merely an outcome score applied to a smaller unit, and "process" therefore names a granularity rather than a kind; the other insists that something genuinely different is going on. I would rather not stage a straw man, so I would be grateful for a careful account of the conceptual issue: what exactly does the process/outcome distinction turn on, and under what conditions does process-based supervision either collapse into outcome-based supervision or remain a genuinely distinct form of oversight? I am particularly interested in two things. First, step boundaries — who gets to carve a reasoning trace into units, and does the answer change what the distinction amounts to? Second, the fact that in my novel the step-scoring network was itself bootstrapped from solutions whose final answers had already been verified: does the provenance of the step signal settle the argument, and if so, in whose favour?
