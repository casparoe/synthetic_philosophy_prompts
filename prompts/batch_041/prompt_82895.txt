I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and the recurring argument I care most about is the one about supertasks — machines that finish infinitely many tasks in a finite stretch of time. I want to end a long post of mine with a poem about Thomson's lamp, and I do not want the usual retelling. So I am going to hand you a variant I have been turning over, and ask you to write to that instead.

Two devices act at one-half, one-quarter, one-eighth, and so on of a minute before noon, one action per time.

The lamp is the familiar one. The nth action toggles it. Nothing happens at noon; there is no final action. The setup fixes the lamp's state at every time before noon and fixes nothing at noon, and no amount of staring at the setup will make it say more.

The dial is mine. Its nth action sets the needle to (-1)^n/n. The needle swings wide and then less and less, and the positions converge to zero. Here is the part I want the poem to get right: the actions themselves determine nothing at noon, exactly as the lamp's actions determine nothing. The dial only looks settled because we quietly add a clause that the needle does not jump at noon. Add that clause and the answer is zero; withhold it and the dial is as mute as the lamp. The dial is not the well-behaved case. It is the lamp plus an assumption we forgot we made.

Please write a poem in which the two run side by side, and in which that asymmetry — or rather, that lack of one — is felt rather than explained.

Some constraints:

- Five stanzas of six, four, three, two, and one line, then a single closing line standing apart. The poem should shorten as the machines quicken, so that its shape runs the schedule.

- The first stanza belongs to the lamp, the second to the dial. After that the two should alternate more and more tightly — every couplet, then every line, then not at all, the last stanza standing entirely inside the dial. Let the pacing carry the acceleration.

- Give the lamp a rhyme, or a single repeated word, that appears in the first stanza and again in the fourth, and never rhymes with anything. Its partner would have been the last toggle, and there is no last toggle. Do not close the lamp off in a couplet; do not resolve it.

- The closing line should be the dial's answer, zero, stated as a fact — but it should also sound purchased, a clause we supplied rather than something the actions delivered. The last word of the poem should belong to the dial, not to the lamp.

- Accuracy matters more than atmosphere. Nothing happens at noon; there is no final action; the lamp is never on and never off at noon, and the poem must not imply otherwise; the dial's positions converge to zero. Anyone who has taught this material should find nothing to correct.

- Keep the tone dry and exact. No infinity-is-mind-bending gestures, no Zeno, no shiver of the sublime; if the poem is strange, it should be strange because of how it is arranged. No epigraph, no quotations, and no citations. A reader who has never heard of any of this should still feel the swinging and the settling.
