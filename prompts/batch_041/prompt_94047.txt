I am a novelist at work on a book about a clinic that offers to replace a patient's neurons one at a time with synthetic equivalents, so that the patient never loses consciousness and, at the end of the treatment, no biological tissue remains. The plot turns on whether the person who leaves the clinic is the person who entered it, and I have been struck by the discovery that the law, the art trade and the heritage industry already answer a version of that question, and answer it in ways I did not anticipate.

Consider three settled practices. A cathedral whose every stone has been replaced over six centuries is still described as the original fabric, and its protected status survives the replacement; a cathedral demolished and rebuilt to the same drawings, from the same quarry, is a reconstruction, and is not. A painting retouched until scarcely any original pigment remains is still the painting, and sells as such; a copy made yesterday, indistinguishable under every examination short of materials analysis, is a forgery. And a person who suffers a catastrophic brain injury, losing almost every memory and much of her character, remains the same person before the law: her contracts stand, her marriage stands, and her crimes are hers.

What conception of identity do these practices presuppose? My instinct is that all three treat continuity of causal history, rather than continuity of material or of pattern, as what makes something the same thing — but I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory properly, and to tell me which rival accounts of identity it quietly rules out. Does the practice genuinely require such a theory, or is it merely compatible with several? I am particularly puzzled by an apparent tension: the forgery case implies that a perfect duplicate is not the thing itself, even though it satisfies every observable property; yet our willingness to let the cathedral survive the loss of all its original stone implies that observable properties are not what we are tracking either. What, then, is being tracked, and is the same thing being tracked in all three cases?

Please distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, and say where they are philosophically underdetermined, so that two or three incompatible theories would license the same outcomes. Then tell me what would have to change — in law, in the art trade, in conservation — if we replaced the presupposed view with a purely informational criterion of identity, on which whatever can be faithfully specified is what persists. Would forgery cease to be a wrong? Would the rebuilt cathedral become the cathedral? And would my character, at the end of her treatment, inherit her own estate? Kindly answer in British English.
