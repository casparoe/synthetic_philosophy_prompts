I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science and have a single ninety-minute session in which to introduce the fourfold scheme of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. I do not want an exposition of the scheme; I want a teaching plan. In what order must the ideas be introduced so that each level registers as a genuine advance rather than a relabeling — what, precisely, must already be in place before the next creature can be understood, and what makes each dependency real rather than a ladder of prestige? Choose the one case that should carry the whole session, and tell me what it must and must not have; I am inclined to make a pigeon in an operant chamber the spine of the lecture, and I want to know whether that is the right choice or a trap, and what would serve better. Then map the misconceptions the students will bring or form — I expect them to flatten the scheme into a single scale of intelligence, to assume the Popperian level requires language, to treat the four levels as exclusive types rather than nested layers, and to hear "Skinnerian" as an accusation of manipulability — and say exactly where in the sequence each must be headed off. Close by proposing the question I should put to them at the end to reveal whether they have grasped the nesting rather than memorized four labels; I want to use a case for which they will have no stock answer, namely a person whose deliberation is largely offloaded onto a spreadsheet or a language model, and I need to know whether to present that as an extension of the Gregorian level or as a candidate new creature, and how to keep the session honest either way. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and say so openly. In your answer, state how confident you are in the pedagogical claims you make and single out the point about which you are least sure.
