I've somehow ended up running next week's 75-minute session in the collective-action ethics course — the topic is individual contributions to collective harms and the inefficacy objection, i.e. the complaint that my one flight, my one burger, my one vote can't possibly matter because it makes no difference to the outcome. It's undergrads, mostly already sympathetic to climate action, and what I need is a teaching plan, not an explanation of the objection. So: what has to be in place before the objection even sounds compelling to them? My hunch is that it only bites once they've bought a difference-making / counterfactual picture of harming, and that if that picture arrives too early the whole rest of the session is patch-up work, but tell me if I've got the dependency backwards — and either way give me the order the pieces have to go in, with the reason each one depends on the one before it. Then the one case that should carry the whole thing. I don't want it to be the standard one-burger example and I'm not sure about voting either; whatever you pick, say what the case has to have (a threshhold? a sum? a long smeared-out causal chain?) and why the other candidates would mislead. The three cases look different in shape to me — voting is tie-breakish, meat is a tiny slice of an enormous sum, climate is a diffused chain nobody can point at — and I suspect that using one of them as the master example is exactly what makes students think they've understood the objection when they haven't.

I'm expecting them to arrive with at least these: "if there's no victim I can point to, nothing wrong happened", "collective harms are just individual harms added up, so if each of us is innocent then the whole thing is innocent", and the one I genuinely find hard — if the harm only occurs once a threshhold is crossed, then one person's act (the thousandth presser of the button, the 270th elector) is the act that made the difference, so does that mean only they are to blame and everyone else walks free? I half-remember a case with a thousand people each turning up a dial by an imperceptible amount, no single turn detectable by anyone, and the person on the receiving end ends up in agony; I'd like to build on something in that vein, so tell me whether that's a good spine for the session or a trap. For each misconception, say exactly where in the sequence I head it off and roughly what I say, and if one of them can't be fixed in 75 minutes, say that instead of pretending. Finish with the one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would actually show me whether they've got the structure rather than just been made to feel guilty about flying, plus the things I should openly refuse to settle at this level — I'd much rather say "we're not doing that today" than bluff my way through it.
