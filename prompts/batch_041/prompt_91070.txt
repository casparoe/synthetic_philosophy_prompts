A friend who teaches an introductory ethics course has sent me the draft of a chapter on population ethics, and one paragraph of it has been nagging at me. She writes:

"Parfit's repugnant conclusion is the discovery that total utilitarianism, followed consistently, requires us to prefer a world of countless billions whose lives are so wretched as not to be worth living to a world of a few million people living lives of great flourishing. The total view adds up welfare and is indifferent to how it is distributed, so a large enough heap of misery outweighs a small quantity of happiness. This is a reductio of utilitarianism. The argument that generates it, the mere addition paradox, turns on a single load-bearing premise: that adding a person whose life is worth living makes the world better. The only genuine escape is to deny that populations of different sizes can be compared at all. Some writers try switching from totals to averages, or setting a floor below which a life contributes nothing to the total, but such manoeuvres only relocate the absurdity, so no theory can avoid the conclusion while keeping our ordinary moral intuitions."

I suspect she is being unfair to utilitarianism in particular, but I cannot see where the unfairness lies, and I would not know which of her other claims a specialist would endorse. Please go claim by claim: what would someone working on population axiology accept as stated, what would they dismiss as a standard simplification, and what is straightforwardly mistaken? And does her closing claim, that no theory can avoid the conclusion while keeping our ordinary moral intuitions, actually follow from the parts of the paragraph that survive scrutiny?
