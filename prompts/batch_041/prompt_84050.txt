I am preparing a diagnostic memo for a small reading group that has spent the past year on the idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity. The group has split into two camps and the discussion has stalled. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of the literature.

Camp A, the narrative constructivists, holds that the self is a center of narrative gravity: a useful abstraction that summarizes patterns in a person's self-referential commitments, memories, and self-attributions, much as a center of mass summarizes a distribution of matter. On this view there is no additional inner entity; the self is real only as a pattern, and it can fragment, be revised, or be confabulated. Camp A treats dissociative identity, dementia, confabulation, and ordinary self-narrative revision as central data. It says that whenever Camp B points to a felt sense of being a subject, that feeling is itself part of the narrative or a represented state within it.

Camp B, the minimal phenomenal self theorists, holds that there is a pre-reflective, pre-narrative sense of being a subject, a felt first-person givenness that is present even when autobiographical narrative is absent, disrupted, or suspended. Camp B treats reports of selfless experience in meditation, infancy, transient depersonalization, and the residual sense of ownership in some dissociative states as central data. It says Camp A has changed the subject: a center of narrative gravity may be a good model of how we tell stories about ourselves, but it is not the self that is lived.

Both camps agree on most of the clinical and phenomenological facts. They disagree about what those facts show. My hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about facts but a disagreement about explanatory priority and about what the explanandum is: the self as a predictive construct versus the self as a lived subject. I want you to test that hypothesis, not assume it.

To keep the diagnosis from collapsing into a verbal dispute, use the following distinction, which I am introducing for this exercise. Define narrative gravity as the fixed point of a compression process that minimizes prediction error over a person's self-referential data. Define phenomenal grip as the degree to which a self-attribution is immune to first-person doubt, or is given as mine rather than inferred. These two properties can come apart: a self-model can have high narrative gravity and low phenomenal grip, or low narrative gravity and high phenomenal grip. To make the distinction concrete, consider two imagined cases. In the first, a person can produce a detailed, coherent, prediction-improving self-narrative but reports that none of it is given as hers. In the second, a person has no such narrative but reports a bare, non-inferential sense that experiences are happening to someone. Ask which camp should count each case as evidence for, and whether the cases are empirically possible. If the distinction is useful, use it to say exactly where the camps are talking past each other. If it is not useful, say why and replace it with a better distinction.
