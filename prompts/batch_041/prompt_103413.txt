I'm putting together a short blog-ish piece (aiming for around 1500 words, for a smart but non-specialist audience) and I keep getting into arguments with friends about it, so I'd love a second opinion before I commit. The topic is memes in the original Dawkins sense -- bits of culture that spread, mutate, and stick around -- and whether it's actually legitimate to say they evolve by natural selection the way genes do, or whether the whole analogy quietly falls apart. I've noticed people either roll their eyes at "memetics" as a failed pseudoscience or they swallow the gene analogy whole, and I want to argue there's a real position in between. Here are my notes, in rough order:

(1) The usual objection to memetics is that cultural transmission isn't copying, it's reconstruction: when you "catch" an idea you rebuild it out of your own mental resources, so nothing is faithfully replicated, and if nothing is faithfully replicated there's nothing for selection to act on. This is basically the epidemiology-of-representations / cultural-attraction line associated with Dan Sperber, and it's usually treated as the killer objection.

(2) I think that's too quick. Selection doesn't require high fidelity; it requires heritable variation in fitness. A lineage of reconstructions can still be heritable, as long as what you end up rebuilding depends on what you received, in a way that systematically biases the rebuilding. So we should be talking about reproducers, not replicators -- the unit doesn't have to be copied, it just has to reproduce.

(3) But then the real problem migrates somewhere else: individuation. If every act of reconstruction produces a fresh thing, what exactly is the unit being selected? And you can't compute the fitness of a meme if you can't count its copies, so the whole quantitative program stalls.

(4) My proposed fix: stop individuating memes by content and individuate them by what I'm calling a reconstruction profile -- the set of features that reliably survive a given population's reconstructive process. On this view a meme isn't a message floating between heads, it's a disposition of a population to rebuild certain things in certain ways. Fitness then just is how a profile shifts the population's future reconstruction, and you get to keep population thinking without needing atomistic mental objects.

(5) Payoff I want to claim: this reframes memetics as a branch of population biology about dispositions rather than a science of mental tokens, and it dissolves a couple of puzzles -- why ideas seem to have a life of their own, and why "the same" joke can be hilarious in one room and dead in the next.

What I want from you: read these notes and tell me where the argument is weakest, not just whether you like it. Specifically -- (a) is my move in (2) fair to the reconstruction objection, or am I strawmanning it? The objection might not be about fidelity at all, and if it isn't, my whole step 2 is aimed at the wrong target. (b) Does (4) actually buy me anything, or is "reconstruction profile" just a fancy relabel of "the thing people tend to remember," in which case I've made no progress and should say so? (c) If the piece is going to be wrong somewhere, where's the most likely place -- and flag any spot where I'm leaning on a contested reading of somebody's view rather than the view itself. Also please say what would change your mind: which argument or counterexample would make you come down differently on whether the middle position in (2)-(4) is tenable, because I'd rather find the weak joint now than get a pat on the back.
