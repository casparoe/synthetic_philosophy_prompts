ok so my reading group just did the careenium bit from Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop and I cant stop chewing on it. The setup, as I remember it: a big box (he describes it as a billiard table) full of tiny magnetic marbles he calls simms, careening around, bumping and sticking to each other, and every so often they clump into larger structures he calls simmballs, which have their own dynamics — they form, dissolve, merge, and they look like they mirror regularities out in the world. You can describe the whole thing at the simm level (marbles banging into marbles) or at the simmball level (clusters dancing with clusters), and the chapter's question is literally which level is doing the shoving. He maps it onto the brain: box = skull, simms = neurons, simmballs = concepts. His take, the way I read it, is that the simmball level really is doing work, it isn't just shorthand, and once you're up there the simm level kind of drops out of the story.

Anyway, Dave in my group says this is just the brain-is-a-computer thing with extra steps, and that it proves the opposite of what Hofstadter wants. His line: simms = transisters, simmballs = software, and everybody knows software doesn't push transistors around, the physics does — so saying a concept or a belief made someone do something is the same kind of convenient fiction as saying the program crashed the machine. His conclusion: only the substrate pushes anything, the symbols ride along.

I dont think he's right, but I can't say why. Can you audit the computer analogy he's leaning on, against the careenium? I want the correspondence table first — what's supposed to map onto what — then where the mapping actually breaks: does the careenium even have a program/hardware split, is there anything inside the box that reads the symbols, does it matter that simmballs are made out of simms rather than sitting on a separate layer, does 'the simmball shoved the simm' add anything to the simm-level story or is it just re-describing it, etc. Then separate the mismatches that matter for Dave's conclusion from the ones that are just decoration, and tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the careenium cuts the other way. And if the computer picture is the wrong tool for this, what's the better analogy — I keep wanting to say a traffic jam, or a wave, but I have no idea whether those earn their keep.
