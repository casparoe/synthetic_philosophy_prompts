I'm a features journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm three drafts into a long piece on the strange afterlife of panpsychism — how a view that spent most of the twentieth century as a punchline is now defended in respectable journals, and what it would mean for ethics if it turned out to be true. My problem is that I've absorbed an argument from a source I trust, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's profound or confused; I suspect you'll be able to tell me which. The argument runs roughly like this. If experience is a fundamental feature of reality rather than something brains cook up, then the entities physics tells us are basic — quarks, electrons, the excitations of fields — each have some inner character; if they have inner character, they have something like a point of view; and if they have a point of view, then what we do to them matters morally. On that basis my source has proposed a metric he calls welfare per joule, a way of ranking physical processes by the amount of morally relevant experience they generate or destroy, and has used it to argue that high-energy particle physics is, joule for joule, the most ethically fraught research programme in science — that a facility like the Large Hadron Collider is, on this accounting, a moral catastrophe dwarfing factory farming by orders of magnitude. He wants me to ask, in print, whether the panpsychist renaissance obliges us to shut down accelerators and rewrite the ethics of experiment.

Before I print anything, I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind who also knows moral philosophy and the philosophy of physics, and to think this through slowly rather than reaching for the nearest quotable objection. Start by mapping the space of views that get called panpsychist and saying what they do and don't commit you to; then work through the argument step by step, marking which steps are load-bearing and which are doing no work at all. I'm less interested in being told that I've got it wrong than in seeing precisely where the weight sits, and in hearing what the strongest version of the underlying worry would look like once any confusion has been stripped away — because I suspect there is a real question buried here, something about how far moral standing extends and whether the physics of the very small could ever be ethically relevant, and I'd like the piece to end on that question rather than on the punchline. If the metric itself is the problem, say so, say why, and tell me what a defensible version of an ethics of fundamental physics would even be about.
