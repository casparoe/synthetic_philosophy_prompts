I convene a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science, and I am now preparing the session on the problem of old evidence. The group is mixed — six philosophers, two statisticians, a physicist and a historian of nineteenth-century astronomy — and they will arrive having read nothing, so whatever I plan has to be self-contained. I do not want the problem explained to me; I know it. What I want is a teaching plan for seventy-five minutes at a whiteboard: the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each one the reason it cannot be moved any earlier — I have my own view that the likelihood-ratio formulation of confirmation has to be on the board before the paradox can even be stated properly, but I should like yours — together with what I should openly refuse to go into at this level and say so. No reading list and no citations, please; I shall be doing the formalism at the board myself, and a plan that presupposes reading I have not set is no use to me.

The part I am least settled on is the carrying example, and here I have three candidates and no verdict. The first is a native-speaker case: a syntactician has known all her life, with the certainty of a native speaker's judgement, that a certain construction is impossible in her language; a new theory of that language's syntax entails that it is impossible; everyone agrees the theory is strongly supported by that very fact, yet she could not have been more confident of it before the theory existed, so the theory appears to gain nothing. My worry is that the certainty here is not evidential but constitutive, so the case makes the paradox look like a conjuring trick and leaves the room convinced that the whole difficulty is an artefact of an absurd idealisation. The second candidate is a cryptographic one: an analyst has known for years that a particular intercepted message yields a particular plaintext under a particular key, and a new theory of the cipher's design then entails that it must. The third is the standard case everyone reaches for — the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion and general relativity — and I want you to tell me what, if anything, is actually wrong with it, since I have never been satisfied by the usual reasons for steering clear of it. So: give me your verdict on each of the three, say what an example would have to have and to lack in order to carry the whole session rather than merely illustrate one half of it, and tell me which to build on.

Finally, I have been experimenting with a distinction of my own and should like to try it as the spine: between evidence one has acquired and evidence one has digested — between a fact's having raised one's credence at some moment and its having been absorbed into one's background picture of the world, so that one no longer encounters it as information at all. Tell me whether that can bear the structural weight, where it needs shoring up, and whether it quietly collapses into the counterfactual fix, the one that asks what credence the agent would have assigned to the evidence had she not already accepted the hypothesis; if it does collapse, say so and tell me what should take its place as the spine. Then give me the misconceptions this particular group will form, in the order they will form them, with the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off, and one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show the structure has landed rather than that they can recite the paradox. Bear in mind throughout that the statisticians will want the formalism and will object the moment an idealisation is smuggled past them, while the historians will switch off if the first twenty minutes are algebra; the sequence has to survive both, and I would sooner lose the statisticians for five minutes than the historians for fifty.
