Ok so I'm running a grad seminar on wild animal suffering this term and the habitat-destruction question is where it always bogs down. My students want to say destroying habitat is obviously bad, but once you actually take wild-animal welfare seriously the obviousness falls apart: you wipe out a population, sure, but the survivors move into a different habitat, and alot of the animals you killed were r-strategists whose lives may not have been worth living in the first place.

I need a taxonomy of the positions on whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, laid out as a decision tree where every branch point is a question that genuinely seperates the positions rather than one that just names a position. Build it out of the logical space first — let the answers to your splitting questions generate the possible positions — and only afterwards hang names on the leaves, or "unnamed" where nobody's really staked out the view.

The axes I care about: is the average wild animal's life net negative or net positive; are we aggregating total suffering or suffering per animal; does a life that habitat destruction prevents count as a harm, a benefit, or neither (the population-ethics axis); is being killed by habitat destruction worse or better than the death the animal would otherwise have met; and does anything ecosystem-level — naturalness, species persistence, complexity — have value over and above the welfare of the animals in it.

Two things beyond the tree itself. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually pull apart, and say why — I'd rather have a lossy tree I understand than a tidy one that's lying to me. Second, mark which combinations of answers nobody occupies and diagnose each: is it empty because it's incoherent, because it's coherent but repugnant, or just because no one's bothered to defend it? That last column is honestly the whole reason I want this.

Also add one branch on which source of suffering dominates in a wild animal's life — predation, starvation and disease, or the sheer numbers game of early death — because half my students think the habitat question reduces entirely to that, and I want the tree to show them when it does and when it doesn't. And give me a leaf for the position I keep half-defending myself and never see written down: that habitat destruction is net good simply because it's the cheapest population-reduction technology going, and the real question is how to aim it at the animals with the worst lives.

Keep it to about 7 branch questions and no more than 12 leaves, and phrase every branch question so a student could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to.
