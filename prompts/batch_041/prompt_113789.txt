Ok so I've got until the end of the month to decide whether to take a job that means moving to another country for at least three years — different language, different career, different friends, the whole thing. My partner is fine either way, which somehow makes it worse. I've been reading about transformative experience (the L.A. Paul stuff — you can't know what it's like to have a kid until you've had one, and by then you can't do the comparison anymore) and it's turned into a bit of a rabbit hole.

My cousin, who did a philosophy degree and loves this kind of thing, built out an argument over dinner last week that I can't get out of my head. I wrote it down on my phone after. It goes like this:

(1) A choice is rational only if it's guided by a ranking of the outcomes under your own preferences.
(2) If an experience is personally transformative, then living through it changes what you care about, so the person who ends up on the other side of it ranks things on a scale you can't currently read.
(3) A preference can only rank outcomes from the standpoint of the person who has it.
(4) So the outcome "I take the job and become that person" contains a standpoint that isn't mine, and none of my current preferences can rank it.
(5) The same goes for any guess I make about what that future person would prefer: it's my guess, made from my standpoint, about a standpoint I don't have.
(6) So the transformative option is unrankable for me.
(7) Something you can't rank, you can't choose rationally.
(8) Declining doesn't help. Staying isn't the safe default, it's just as much a life-shaping choice, and I'd be making it while insisting I can't compare the two options.
(9) And it's not just this decision. Any choice big enough to be worth agonizing over is going to change what I care about, or it wouldn't be big.
(10) So rationality only covers small stuff — what to have for lunch, which route to drive. Nothing that actually shapes a life.
(11) But that can't be right. Some people are wise about their lives and some are reckless, and that difference seems real and seems to matter.

He says this is just where the transformative-experience stuff leads if you take it seriously. I don't buy the conclusion, but I can't find the loose thread. Is there a real flaw in there, or is one of those steps just true and expensive? I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), they feel like the whole point of the idea to me. But what I really want is to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs — does dropping it mean I have to say I can compare my life now to my life after? Because if so I want to know what that's even supposed to look like, and I'm not sure I believe it.
