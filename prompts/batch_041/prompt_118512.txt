So me and two people in my reading group have been arguing for months about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and I want to finally write the document we should have written in week one instead of having another three hour shouting match. Side A holds the standard package: (i) you can in principle pair any level of capability with any final goal, modulo logical consistency and means-end coherence, and (ii) agents with wildly different final goals will tend to converge on the same instrumental subgoals -- staying alive, grabbing resources, keeping their own goals intact, getting smarter -- so the default outcome is bad. Side B thinks the first is either trivially true (sure, you can *describe* any pairing; thats a fact about our vocabulary, not about what will ever actually get built) or false (real cognition comes with values baked in), and the second is either analytic (define "power" as "whatever helps with any goal" and of course everyone wants it) or just empirically wrong (humans are the only high-capability agents we know and they don't all converge on power, and RL agents with different reward functions learn different behaviour).

Write the joint statement these two could both put their names to. Numbered agreed-claims section first: what they can both accept about what orthogonality does and doesn't assert, about what convergence is even a claim about (idealised optimisers? optimal policies? systems that will actually get trained?), and about why the whole thing matters if it's true -- and please don't let those agreed claims go vacuous, I want to see what's genuinely left standing. Then a disagreements table, each entry phrased in wording the side it belongs to would actually sign off on. I expect the real splits are somewhere around: whether convergence is a theorem, an empirical generalisation, or a heuristic; whether "power" is a well-defined agent-neutral quantity or just a placeholder for "whatever this agent happens to need"; and whether any of it needs a single fixed scalar final goal in the first place.

Then the cruxes, and for each one something both of them agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two cases I specifically want run because I think they're where the action is: (1) an agent whose final goal is already fully achieved and can't be undone -- does it still resist being switched off? and (2) the off-switch case, an agent whose final goal is something that will be satisfied no matter what happens after t=100.
