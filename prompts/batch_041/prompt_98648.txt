I am preparing a long-form interview with a philosopher who defends a view she calls asymmetric debunking. Her claim is that evolutionary explanations of moral belief undermine the epistemic warrant of moral beliefs that purport to track stance-independent normative facts, but do not undermine moral beliefs that are understood as commitments within a practice of mutual justifiability. She insists the relevant divide is not realism versus anti-realism but tracking versus constituting: a belief is tracking when its function is to represent a fact that obtains independently of the practice, and constituting when its function is to articulate a commitment that partly makes the practice what it is. She further distinguishes two evolutionary stories: selection for cooperative dispositions, which she says pressures the content of moral beliefs, and selection for the capacity to adopt a normative stance, which she says pressures only the medium of moral thought. Her thesis is that debunking arguments are really about the etiology of representational purport, not about the truth of moral claims.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual content of this position rather than let her restate it. I want at least one question on why the evolutionary genealogy does not equally undermine constituting moral beliefs, given that those beliefs are also causally shaped by evolutionary pressures; one on whether the tracking/constituting distinction can be drawn without presupposing the very normative facts it is meant to isolate; one on whether her two evolutionary stories can be kept apart, since selection for cooperative dispositions would seem to require the capacity to adopt a normative stance; and one on what happens to moral beliefs that seem to straddle the line, such as beliefs about fairness in noncooperative bargaining or about the wrongness of cruelty to animals. For each of those four, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and make the follow-ups progressively harder to deflect. End with a question about what empirical or conceptual discovery would count against asymmetric debunking.

Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want questions, not references. Begin your response with the single question you would ask if you could ask only one, then the remaining questions in the order you would ask them, with the likely-answer follow-up attached to each. No preamble and no restating of my request.
