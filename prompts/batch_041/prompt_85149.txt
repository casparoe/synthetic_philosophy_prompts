I am assembling a case for a unit on whether the injunction to choose the option with the greatest expected value is a rule that always issues a verdict. What I want is a decision problem built to order, not an illustration of a familiar paradox, so please do not hand me a relabeled version of a textbook example — the St. Petersburg game, or its alternating cousin whose expectation is only conditionally convergent. The case has to be new, and the write-up has to show that it satisfies each specification below.

Specifications for the main case. First, state the outcome space and the payoff rule exactly, as an explicit function of the outcome, so that a reader can check the arithmetic; every payoff is a finite sum of money, and the probabilities of the outcomes are well defined and sum to one. The case should be described in a single paragraph and be one that a person could in principle be offered — no exotic physics, no infinite utilities, no demons or perfect predictors, and no supertasks beyond the natural idealization of a fair coin flipped until it first lands heads. Second, the expected value must be undefined in the strict sense rather than infinite or merely hard to compute: the series of expected contributions must be conditionally convergent, so that reorderings of its terms yield different finite sums. Demonstrate this by exhibiting two explicit rearrangements with different totals, and explain why the existence of such rearrangements makes the expectation undefined rather than merely unknown. Third, if the construction requires the payoffs to be unbounded, say so, and say why boundedness would be fatal to it.

Then build the control case: alter exactly one feature so that the series becomes absolutely convergent, hold everything else as close to fixed as you can, and show that the disagreement collapses.

Then do the check, for the main case only. Give the verdict — accept, decline, or no verdict — of each of the following, with a sentence or two of derivation rather than a bare label: (a) the rule that one should accept if and only if the expected value of the gamble exceeds its price, where the expected value is computed as the limit of the expected values of the gamble truncated after n trials; (b) the rearrangement argument; (c) expected utility maximization by an agent with a bounded utility function; (d) maximin.

Finally, tell me which feature of the construction is doing the work, what becomes of the whole case if that feature is removed, and how the strongest defender of the truncation approach in (a) would attack the case so as to deny that the expectation is undefined. Add one sentence on what would have to change for the expectation to be infinite rather than undefined, and why those two failures are different. State how confident you are in each verdict you have given, and single out the point you are least sure of.
