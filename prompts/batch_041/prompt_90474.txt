I am setting the final examination for a graduate seminar in decision theory, and I want a single essay question that turns on the Pasadena game rather than on the St. Petersburg paradox that students have already encountered in three other courses. The question should be answerable in ninety minutes and worth twenty-five marks. What I want it to force is the distinction between an expectation that is infinite and one that is undefined, and why that distinction bears on expected value maximization as a theory of rational choice. The Pasadena game—a fair coin tossed until heads first appears, with a payoff of (-1)^n times 2^n divided by n when heads appears on toss n, so that the terms alternate in sign and grow without bound—has an expectation that is a conditionally convergent series; in its natural order it sums to minus the natural logarithm of two, but a rearrangement can make it sum to any real number at all or to no limit. The game itself, however, has a perfectly determinate probability distribution and a natural order of outcomes, and the Altadena variant, which adds a constant to every payoff, seems to dominate the original by exactly that constant. I want the question to make students say what a defender of expected value maximization should do with a case like this: whether the theory is incomplete, whether it is false, or whether it is merely silent, and what the Altadena dominance argument shows about the claim that the Pasadena game has no expected value. I also want them to consider whether a rule that takes the limit of the expectations of truncated versions of the game—stopping after a fixed number of tosses and letting that number grow—is a satisfactory repair or merely a change of subject.

Please write the question, a model answer of about seven hundred to eight hundred words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each out of twenty-five, and for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The model answer should be self-contained: it should not presuppose that the student has read any particular article, and it should not rely on citations. The question itself should be phrased so that a student who has memorized the standard St. Petersburg resolution—diminishing marginal utility, log utility, the impossibility of the casino's infinite resources—will not score well unless they can also say what happens when the expectation fails to exist rather than diverging to infinity. I would like the question to include a short prompt for the student to state the expected value of the Pasadena game in its natural order, to explain why that value is not well-defined in the sense required by expected value maximization, and to say what the Altadena variant adds to the argument. The question should also ask whether the failure of the expectation is a problem for the theory of rational choice or only for a particular formalism, and it should require the student to take a stand on that issue rather than merely cataloguing positions.
