I'm giving a paper at a two-day workshop on long-run global risk, hosted by a policy school, and I need a question-and-answer prep sheet rather than a defense.

The paper's thesis: forecasts of state collapse and imperial decline should be made predominantly from the outside view. Take a reference class of historical polities, estimate a survival curve for the class, and read off the conditional probability that a polity resembling ours loses its monopoly on organized violence over a given horizon. The inside view — the particular story about elite overproduction, fiscal exhaustion, legitimacy deficits, and peer rivals — should enter only as an adjustment to that baseline, and only when I can state in advance the direction and rough size of the adjustment.

Two refinements are, I expect, where the attacks will come. First, I argue the outside view should be applied to the hazard rather than to the total lifespan: the question is not when a polity falls but how the risk of falling varies with age, and I claim the historical record is better described by a hazard that rises for a long stretch and then plateaus than by the flat hazard that most quantitative work on political survival tacitly assumes. Second, I claim the principal error the outside view corrects is not optimism about the central estimate but underestimation of the spread: expert interval forecasts for the timing of collapse are far too narrow, and the outside view's main contribution is to widen intervals rather than to shift the mean. My method is the familiar one from cost-overrun forecasting — pick a class, take the distribution, locate the case in it — with the planning-fallacy correction as the motivating analogy.

The audience is about fifteen people, and I want the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones a methods seminar would raise. Specifically: four academic historians — of the Roman principate, the Ottoman Empire, late imperial China, and early modern Spain — who are allergic to laws of history, who will reach for the words contingency and anachronism, and who will insist that a dynasty, a state, an empire, and a civilization are four different objects with four different lifetimes. A demographer who does survival analysis professionally, and who will want to know about censoring, competing risks, and exchangeability before anything else. An economist who runs forecasting tournaments and will ask what my out-of-sample record is and what scoring rule I am optimizing. A comparative political scientist who studies democratic backsliding through paired case studies and wants to know what the model tells a specific government in a specific year that the country specialist does not already know. A historically minded philosopher of science who will ask whether polities are countable at all — when Rome begins, when it ends, and whether the units of my reference class survive that question. Two people from the host institution: a program director who has to put a number and a date in a risk assessment and will be impatient with distributions, and a communications officer who worries that a published collapse forecast becomes self-fulfilling and therefore contaminates the very base rate I am estimating. And the assigned discussant, who will open with the strongest version of the charge that quantitative history of this kind is curve-fitting with equations.

Write the prep sheet in the following sections, in this order:

1. "The claim as they will hear it" — about 150 words, in the flat form my critics will attribute to me, not the hedged form I will use.
2. "The room" — about 250 words: the factions, what each cares about, the vocabulary each will use, and who is likely to be my ally.
3. "The objections, ranked" — the bulk of the document, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. Eight to ten objections, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, tagged with who raises it. For each: the objection in that person's own idiom and at their level of technical precision; one line on why it ranks where it does; the two-minute reply I should give; and one or two sentences on where that reply is soft. Include at least three objections that I will be tempted to dismiss as naive but that have a serious version, and make clear what the serious version is.
4. "What I concede before the first question" — about 200 words: three or four concessions I should make in the talk itself, phrased as I would say them, chosen so that conceding them costs me less than having them extracted.
5. "The exchange the paper survives or dies on" — about 250 words: the single question-and-answer pair that determines whether the audience thinks there is a paper here, scripted as an exchange.
6. "The question I am hoping nobody asks" — about 150 words, with the answer I would give if it comes.

Do not give me citations or a reading list; I have those. Do not give me numbers you cannot source — where a specific base rate or sample size would be doing the work, tell me what number I need to have established and what happens to my argument if it comes out at the unfavorable end. I want this usable for rehearsal, so write the objections as they will be spoken, not as paraphrases.
