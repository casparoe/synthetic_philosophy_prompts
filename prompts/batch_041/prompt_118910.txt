I am writing a short piece for readers who are comfortable with formal logic but have not worked in this particular corner of it, and I want to avoid conflating two results that are usually mentioned together.

The first is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem: no consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic can prove the sentence asserting its own consistency — the sentence that says no contradiction is derivable in it.

The second is Löb's theorem, which I take to be this: for any sentence P of such a system, if the system proves the conditional whose antecedent says that P is provable in the system and whose consequent is P, then the system already proves P. What is often called the Löbian obstacle is the consequence that a system cannot adopt trust in its own proofs as a general policy: it cannot license the inference from having proved P to P for arbitrary P, since that would commit it to proving a contradiction, and hence every sentence.

My provisional view is that the second result is simply the first in general form — Gödel's theorem being what Löb's theorem yields when P is the contradiction — and that the only genuine novelty is a shift of vocabulary, from consistency to trust.

Please compare the two results as obstacles to a system's knowledge and trust of itself, and commit to a definite answer on three points. First, is my provisional view correct, or does Löb's theorem constrain something that Gödel's theorem leaves untouched? Second, if an agent wants to rely on conclusions reached by its own reasoning — including conclusions reached by a future version of itself that it has deliberately rewritten — which of the two results is doing the real work, and precisely what does it rule out? Third, is any coherent form of self-trust still available to such an agent, or does every version of it fail at some level?

Give me a verdict on each point rather than a survey of positions, and tell me where the usual way of drawing this contrast misleads. I would rather have a sharp answer I can argue with than a balanced summary.
