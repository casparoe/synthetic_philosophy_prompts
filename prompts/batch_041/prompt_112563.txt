I am a secondary-school student working on a research project about official apologies for historical injustices, and I have run into a conceptual problem that I have not been able to resolve on my own. What I would like is an explanation of the issue itself rather than a verdict on the particular case.

My teacher asked us to consider the following arrangement. An independent historical commission investigates a state's past conduct toward a particular group. If the commission concludes that the state committed a wrong, a statute requires the head of state to read, on a fixed date each year, an apology whose wording has been drafted by historians and by members of the affected community. The wording is exactly what a deeply remorseful leader would have chosen: it names the wrong, accepts responsibility, expresses regret, and pledges that it will not recur. The head of state is forbidden to alter, soften, or supplement the text, and whoever holds the office may privately believe the commission was mistaken.

A classmate argued that this arrangement cannot produce an apology at all, on the ground that apologizing requires the person apologizing to mean what is said, and that a legal obligation supplies the words without the state of mind. Another classmate replied that the arrangement, if anything, yields a stronger apology than a spontaneous one, because it does not depend on the private feelings of whoever happens to hold office and because it binds the state to its acknowledgment in a way that a heartfelt speech does not.

I would like an account of what these two positions are really disagreeing about. Among the things I hope to have clarified: whether the sincerity requirement, if there is one, belongs to the meaning of apologizing or is merely a condition that makes apologies more likely to be effective; whether a state or any other collective can be the sort of thing that holds the relevant attitudes, and if not, whose attitudes would have to do the work instead; whether it matters that the officials who apologize are not the people who committed the wrong, and that the addressees are often the descendants of the wronged rather than the wronged themselves; whether the real objection to the statutory apology might be something other than insincerity — that it is not addressed to anyone in particular, or that it is not a response to a wrong the apologizer could have avoided; and how the picture changes if the apologizing government regards the regime that committed the wrong as illegitimate, so that the state appears to be apologizing for something it denies having done.
