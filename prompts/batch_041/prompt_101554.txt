I'm an undergraduate in a course on Enlightenment moral philosophy, and we have just read the section of Franklin's Autobiography in which he describes his project of arriving at moral perfection — the thirteen virtues, the little ruled book, the weekly rotation through them. The mechanics of the scheme I can follow well enough. What I cannot follow is the stretch near the end of the episode, where Franklin turns from describing the practice to assessing it, and I would like help working through it before seminar. Here is the passage in the edition we are using:

"This article, therefore, cost me so much painful attention, and my faults in it vexed me so much, and I made so little progress in amendment, and had such frequent relapses, that I was almost ready to give up the attempt, and content myself with a faulty character in that respect, like the man who, in buying an ax of a smith, my neighbor, desired to have the whole of its surface as bright as the edge. The smith consented to grind it bright for him if he would turn the wheel; he turned, while the smith pressed the broad face of the ax hard and heavily on the stone, which made the turning of it very fatiguing. The man came every now and then from the wheel to see how the work went on, and at length would take his ax as it was, without farther grinding. 'No,' said the smith; 'turn on, turn on; we shall have it bright by and by; as yet, it is only speckled.' . . . something, that pretended to be reason, was every now and then suggesting to me that such extreme nicety as I exacted of myself might be a kind of foppery in morals, which, if it were known, would make me ridiculous; that a perfect character might be attended with the inconvenience of being envied and hated; and that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance. . . . yet I was, by the endeavor, a better and a happier man than I otherwise should have been if I had not attempted it; as those who aim at perfect writing by imitating the engraved copies, tho' they never reach the wished-for excellence of those copies, their hand is mended by the endeavor, and tolerable, while it continues fair and legible."

(The "article" he means is Order, which he has just told us gave him the most trouble of all thirteen, and which he says he never did acquire.) I should add that the sentence I have elided before the last one is his admission that he never reached the perfection he had aimed at, and that he attributes the "constant felicity of his life" to this same little artifice.

Three things in particular defeat me, and I would like them treated together rather than one at a time.

First, the ax. The buyer wants the whole surface as bright as the edge, discovers that this requires him to turn the wheel while the smith grinds, tires, and settles for the speckled ax. Franklin offers this as an image of his own near-surrender over Order, but he does not say whether the buyer is right or wrong to stop. The smith's "we shall have it bright by and by" is a promise the buyer declines; and yet Franklin, having told us he was almost ready to give up the attempt, goes on to credit the artifice with his lifelong happiness. Is the speckled ax an emblem of sensible self-acceptance, or of the failure of resolution? The phrase "something, that pretended to be reason" suggests the latter, but the considerations he then lists do not obviously deserve that dismissal.

Second, those considerations. Three are attributed to the pseudo-reason: that moral nicety is "a kind of foppery in morals, which, if it were known, would make me ridiculous"; that a perfect character "might be attended with the inconvenience of being envied and hated"; and that "a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance." The third does not sound like a rationalization at all. It sounds like a substantive claim about what benevolence demands, and perhaps about the social cost of visible moral perfection — the sort of thing one might defend rather than merely report. So is Franklin describing a temptation he saw through, or conceding a real point and then covering the concession with the word "pretended"? I would like to know how a reader who takes the benevolence claim seriously ought to hear this sentence.

Third, the engraving. The apprentice who copies the engraved letters never reaches the excellence of the copy, but his hand improves; on that model, the value of the project was never the perfection but the attempt, and Franklin's own summary — "by the endeavor, a better and a happier man" — fits it exactly. But if that is the lesson, the ax anecdote seems to be doing something else, since the buyer's grinding would also have improved the ax had he stayed at the wheel, and his stopping is precisely the failure the engraving analogy is supposed to redeem. Or is the moral case different because there the standard itself, and not merely the practice, is what does the work on the agent?

For context, we have already done Aristotle on habituation, some Stoic material on the daily review of oneself, and a little Butler; the instructor keeps insisting that Franklin is philosophically more interesting than the "Poor Richard" caricature suggests, and I suspect this passage is where I am supposed to see why.
