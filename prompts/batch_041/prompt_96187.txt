I'm writing a novel set in 1651 and one of my characters has got his hands on a copy of Leviathan and is arguing about it with a republican pamphleteer in a tavern, and I'm stuck on a stretch near the beginning of the chapter on the liberty of subjects. I don't have the book to hand, only my notes, so this is a paraphrase and I may be mangling it: Hobbes defines a free man as one who isn't hindered from doing what he has the will to do, where hindering just means external impediments to motion — the same sense in which water is free when nothing dams it. Then two claims follow that are tying me in knots. First, that fear and liberty are consistent: a man who throws his goods into the sea because he's afraid the ship will sink still does it willingly, so he's free; likewise a subject who obeys the law out of fear of punishment. Second, that the liberty of subjects lies in the silence of the law — wherever the sovereign hasn't prescribed, you're free to act on your own discretion.

My pamphleteer character thinks the second point gives the game away. If liberty is just the absence of a wall in front of me, he says, then a well-fed slave with a kind master is as free as any man, and the word has lost everything worth having; and he suspects the fear point is the same trick, since obeying because you'll be hanged doesn't feel like freedom to him. I can't tell if he's got a real objection or if he's conflating two different things Hobbes is doing. Can you walk me through what Hobbes is actually claiming here — what work the absence-of-impediments definition is doing, why he thinks fear doesn't count as an impediment, and how the silence-of-the-law claim fits with that rather than undercutting it? Then tell me whether my pamphleteer has a genuine arguement, and what someone in the modern republican vein like Pettit would say is missing from Hobbes's picture — I gather it's something about being subject to another person's arbitrary power even when they never actually interfere, and maybe also about the older idea of freedom as being your own master rather than merely unobstructed. I need dialogue where both men make real arguments and neither is a straw man, so give me the strongest version of each, and flag the places where the two of them are just talking past each other because they mean different things by the word.
