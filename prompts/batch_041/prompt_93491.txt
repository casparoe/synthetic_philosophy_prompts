I write the "Lab Notes" column for a science magazine and every December we do a spoof issue where one piece sends up something in the field. This year I want to do the free-will / Libet thing — the clock with the spinning dot, where subjects report the moment they first felt the urge (W) and the moment they actually moved (M), W comes in about 200 ms before M, but the readiness potential on the EEG starts ramping up maybe half a second before that, so the whole internet runs the headline "your brain decides before you do." Libet's own escape hatch was that you still get a window to veto the movement before it fires — the so-called free-won't move.

Give me a mock "instructions for authors" page from a fake journal — call it something like The Journal of Verified Volition — where any submission about the timing of conscious intention has to file a "Libet compliance statement." Deadpan, no winking at the reader, around 600-700 words, laid out as numbered sections like a real author-guidelines page. The jokes have to come from actually knowing the paradigm, so a cognitive neuroscientist laughs instead of wincing: the divided-attention clock, the "let it happen on its own, don't plan it" instruction, the fact that the readiness potential is an average across many trials so it's not obvious it's there in any single trial, the nagging worry that the reported urge is a readout rather than a cause, whether the veto is itself a decision that would need its own veto, and so on. The fine print should quietly be taking sides without ever saying so.

The target is the habit of treating this one paradigm as science's final verdict on free will — a practice, not a person, so keep any named figures offstage or invented. American English, and keep the jargon honest.
