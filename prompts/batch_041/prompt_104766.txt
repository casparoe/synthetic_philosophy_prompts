I direct a small marine field station, and in a few weeks we are hosting a three-day workshop on whether, and where, sentience appears in the invertebrate tree. I am giving the opening remarks: about ten minutes, delivered in a converted boathouse to roughly forty people. Most of the room are comparative neurobiologists and behavioral ecologists who will bristle at anything that sounds like armchair taxonomy; a smaller group are funders and animal-welfare advocates who came for the animals rather than for the arguments. The remarks have to do two things at once — set a frame for the whole workshop, and be candid about how much of that frame is a bet rather than a finding.

The frame I want to try is one I have been calling the interval view. The suggestion is that the question of whether an animal is conscious is asked at the wrong grain. Consciousness may not be a property an animal possesses but a span over which it holds together: a state is phenomenally present to a creature only if it is integrated, and available to steer the creature's own later behavior, across some interval of time — and that interval may be a fraction of a second in one animal and minutes or years in another. On this view the interesting question is not whether a crab is conscious but what the longest interval is over which a crab's experience remains one thing rather than dissolving into a sequence of unconnected flashes. I want you to build the speech so that this idea does real structural work: it should govern what the remarks can and cannot claim, not merely be announced and then set aside.

Three further things I would like the framing to force into the open, because I think they are where the workshop will actually live. First, the interval can be measured in at least two ways that can come apart — by how long a state persists, or by how long it remains available to be acted on or reported on later — and the speech should make the audience see that the choice between those two measures is a real fork, not a technicality. Second, if consciousness is a span rather than a property, then it may admit of degrees in a way that a simple yes-or-no answer does not, and the speech should be honest about whether that is a virtue of the view or an evasion. Third, the view should be presented as something to be tested and possibly broken over the next three days, not as a position the speaker is defending to the death.

Constraints. Do not name, cite, or attribute views to specific researchers, papers, or books; I will be speaking to people who know the literature far better than the remarks need to, I do not want the opening turned into a reading list, and I do not want you to risk pinning a view on the wrong person. Quote no one. Where the framing needs an empirical crutch — for instance, claims about whether cephalopods carry information across a delay, or whether insects show anything like a span of attention — present those as open questions the workshop will fight about rather than as settled results, and do not lean on any particular contested finding as if it were established.

Audience. The neurobiologists will notice immediately if the framing is smuggled in as fact rather than offered as a hypothesis, so keep the epistemic status of every claim visible in the prose itself. The funders will lose patience if the first minutes are throat-clearing, so the human stakes — why anyone should care what interval a crab's experience spans — should arrive early and in plain language. The speech should land for both halves without condescending to either.

Deliver the spoken text, roughly matched to ten minutes. After it, add a short note clearly marked as not to be read aloud, stating how confident you are in the substantive claims the speech makes and singling out the one point you are least sure of, with a sentence on why.
