I'm TAing for a second-year metaphysics course and I'm about a week behind on marking, so I'm outsourcing one question to you. It's the short answer on semantic externalism. They had 45 minutes and a 400-word cap, so the answers are dense and there isn't much room to hedge. The question I set was: (1) state exactly what the Twin Earth case is supposed to establish about the relation between a speaker's inner states and what her words refer to, and name one thing it's often taken to establish that it doesn't; (2) then take this variant on the case — the setup is Putnam's, except that from about 1850 the two planets are in regular radio contact and the chemists on both sides collaborate, so that by 1900 everyone on both planets knows that the local liquid on Earth is H2O and the local liquid on Twin Earth is XYZ, and both communities go on calling their own local liquid water; in 1900, do Oscar's and Twin-Oscar's words for water have the same reference, and do they mean the same thing by it?; (3) finally, does the case tell against narrow content, where narrow content is content that supervenes on what's inside the head?

Here's the answer from a student who is normally solid.

Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth, a planet that duplicates Earth in every respect except that what the inhabitants call water is not H2O but a different substance, XYZ, which is indistinguishable from water in all everyday respects. Oscar on Earth and Twin-Oscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates: their inner lives are identical, they have the same beliefs, memories and sensations, and they would say and do the same things. But when Oscar says water he refers to H2O, and when Twin-Oscar says water he refers to XYZ. So the reference of a speaker's terms is not fixed by anything inside her head, and meanings are not in the head.

What the case doesn't show is that there is no such thing as narrow content. It's an argument about the reference of natural kind terms, and it leaves it open that some level of content — the level Fodor is after — does supervene on the brain, even if that content doesn't determine truth conditions in the ordinary way. It also doesn't show that we can't know what our own words mean.

On the variant, once the two planets are in regular contact and the chemists have jointly established what the two liquids are, the two populations are part of a single linguistic community in the relevant sense, so their words converge. After 1900 water means the same thing on both planets: roughly, the liquid, whichever of H2O or XYZ you happen to have locally. The reference would have to be the disjunction H2O or XYZ, since that is the stereotype the two communities now share. So the original intuition only holds for populations that are causally isolated from one another.

On narrow content, the Twin Earth argument actually shows that no content at all supervenes on the brain: the two Oscars are identical in the head and differ in what they mean. This is the same point Putnam pushes further when he says that even an ideal theory can't fix reference, so reference is ultimately indeterminate, and the causal theory of reference is all that keeps our terms attached to anything. The arthritis case, which Putnam uses to show that meaning depends on the linguistic community, makes the same point at the social level.
