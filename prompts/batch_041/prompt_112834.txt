Some background so you know who's talking and to whom: I retired three years ago from a fisheries and population-ecology station after 35 years of counting fish and arguing about recruitment models, and since then I've been sitting in on the philosophy department seminars at the university in town — I actually finished their MA last spring, at 68, which my old colleagues find hilarious. In three weeks I'm giving the Thursday decision-theory reading group talk. The talk I can rehearse; what I want from you is the Q&A prep, because I've watched this group eat two visiting speakers alive and I'd rather not be the third. The room: two formal epistemologists (one works on Dutch books and scoring rules and is very precise about what a representation theorem does and doesn't prove), one behavioral economist who thinks preferences are mostly constructed on the spot and builds satisficing models, a Kantian ethicist who shows up because she's interested in what "irrational" is supposed to mean, and a couple of grad students, one of whom brings up sophisticated-versus-resolute choice in literally every session. They are friendly but they are not going to let anything slide.

Here's the thesis, and it's a bit of a frankenstein, so aim your objections at the joints. I argue that the coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization — the Dutch book, the money pump, all of it — are stronger than the "it's just bookkeeping about representability" reading and weaker than the textbook "you're irrational if you don't maximize EU." What I claim is that the real normative punch is an invasion argument, and I stole the framing straight from population biology: a preference pattern is like a resident genotype in a fixed environment, and a sequence of trades that extracts money from you is a mutant strategy that invades. On my story, EU-maximizability is just evolutionary stability in a particular trade environment, and that's why the coherence requirement has teeth — but it also shows exactly what the argument needs and doesn't supply: an actual selection process, or at least a decent account of how the exploitation gets to happen repeatedly to a person who can see it coming and walk away. The bare money pump has no selection dynamics in it, and I think that's the hole everyone walks into without noticing. I should say I've got no interest in a reading list or citations — they know the literature far better than I do and I'd only embarrass myself mispronouncing names — so skip all that and give me the prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in roughly the order they'll come and ranked by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each of them would actually phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft; tell me what to concede up front before they have to drag it out of me, which single reply has to land for the whole thing to survive, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. End with a last line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the objection I'm least equipped to answer.
