I've been reading the decision-theory blogs for the better part of two decades, and I keep returning to the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences. I've now worked it into a form where I cannot tell whether it proves nothing at all or proves something absurdly strong, and I'd like you to locate the leak and tell me what it costs to plug it.

Here is my reconstruction.

(1) An agent's preferences are intransitive if, for some options A, B, and C, she prefers A to B, prefers B to C, and prefers C to A.
(2) Suppose she holds C. Because she prefers B to C, she will accept a small payment to trade C for B; because she prefers A to B, another small payment to trade B for A; and because she prefers C to A, a third to trade A for C.
(3) The three trades return her to C, minus the three payments, and the sequence can be run again, so a counterparty can relieve her of any finite sum.
(4) An agent who can be reliably made worse off, by her own lights, through a sequence of exchanges each of which she prefers at the moment of making it, is irrational.
(5) Therefore intransitive preferences are irrational.

What troubles me is that nothing in (2) through (4) depends on intransitivity. It depends only on a principle I'll call temporal localism: at each moment of choice, the agent takes the option she most prefers at that moment.

Consider now an agent whose preferences are perfectly transitive and who is always temporally local, but whose ranking shifts as she learns. At each moment she picks what she then prefers; across moments the ranking moves. A counterparty who knows her learning rule can trade with her on both sides of the shift, selling her what she currently overvalues and buying it back once the evidence has moved her. By (4), this agent is irrational too. Yet she is doing exactly what we praise: revising in the light of evidence. If the argument condemns her it proves too much, and if it spares her I cannot see what spares her that does not equally spare the intransitive agent.

A friend from the same blogs offers a rescue: the pump shows only that a naive agent is irrational, since a sophisticated agent who sees the cycle coming can simply refuse to enter it, and the intransitive agent can be sophisticated in exactly that way. I found this satisfying until I noticed that sophistication is itself a diachronic commitment, the agent binding her later choices to a plan adopted earlier, and now I cannot tell whether I am defending intransitivity or quietly condemning everyone who keeps a resolution.

So tell me which step to reject. Is the trouble in the move from (3) to (4), in the generalization that follows it, or in (4) itself? And once I give up whatever has to go, what is the damage: does the pump still establish anything about intransitivity, and can I still distinguish the intransitive agent from the learner without assuming the conclusion? I want the diagnosis at the level of the machinery. Name the step, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives.
