I'm doing a module on Dostoevsky and the philosophy of religon and I've got completely stuck on the Grand Inquisitor chapter in The Brothers Karamazov. I understand the setup: Christ comes back to Seville during the Inquisition, the crowd recognises him, he gets arrested, and the old Inquisitor visits him in his cell at night and delivers this long monologue about why he has to go away again and not come back. The bit I need help with is the core of the argument, which as far as I can make out runs something like this: Christ gave human beings freedom, and freedom is a burden that most people can't carry; only a small number are strong enough to bear it; so the Church has taken that burden off the masses by relieving them of the torment of deciding for themselves, and in exchange the masses get bread, mystery and authority, and they will be happy; the few strong ones, the Inquisitor and his kind, bear the agony of freedom on behalf of everyone else, ruling in the name of a God they no longer actually serve.

What I can't get straight:

1. The speech is built around the three temptations in the desert, and I can follow the mapping — bread means feed them and they'll follow you, the temple leap means prove you're divine and they'll believe, the kingdoms mean rule them and you'll save them. The Inquisitor's claim seems to be that Christ rejected all three in favour of freedom and that this was a blunder. So is the Inquisitor saying the devil had human nature right? And if so, is Christ's mistake supposed to be a failure of realism rather than of morality? Those feel like very different charges and I can't tell which one the text is making.

2. The Inquisitor keeps saying the masses will be happy, but he doesn't sound like someone who thinks happiness is worth much. He calls the whole arrangement death and destruction at one point, or something close to it. So is his defence of happiness sincere, or is happiness just the name he gives to the pacification he's administering? Can both readings survive the same speech?

3. He and the few keep their freedom while taking everyone else's. If freedom is a torment, why is it right for the few to keep it? Is there a principled distinction between the strong and the weak running through the argument, or is it just that somebody has to do the ruling and it might as well be him?

4. What exactly is the freedom at issue? It seems to slide between three things: freedom of choice (I can pick good or evil), freedom of conscience (I decide for myself what good and evil are), and freedom from suffering. The Inquisitor says Christ gave people the first, but what he's really complaining about seems to be the second. Does the argument depend on conflating them, or does it still work if you keep them apart?

5. What is the kiss doing at the end? The Inquisitor finishes his speech, Christ says nothing and kisses him, and the Inquisitor lets him go but tells him not to come back. I've read that this is supposed to be a rebuttal of the speech, but I don't see how a silent kiss answers an argument. Does it answer it, or does it just change the subject?

6. And is the Inquisitor a believer? Ivan introduces him and then calls him a non-believer, but the speech is soaked in scripture and the Inquisitor seems to be tormenting himself as much as Christ. Is he a sincere fanatic, a cynical operator, or something in between, and does the chapter give you enough to decide?

I'd rather not just be told the standard reading — I've got the lecture notes for that. There's alot going on and what I want is help working out whether the argument actually holds together on its own terms, which of these are real tensions in the text and which are just me being confused, and where the weakest joint is.
