I'm finishing a blog post on the alignment problem and I want someone to tear it apart before I hit publish -- mostly on whether I've got the existing positions right and whether my own contribution is actually a contribution.

Here's the argument. The alignment problem is usually said to come in two flavors. The narrow one: we can't write down what we want, so we train on proxies, and the system ends up pursuing the proxy in ways we didn't intend -- it's doing what we asked, we just asked wrong. The broad one, the value alignment side: even if we could write down exactly what we want, the thing we want might be the wrong thing, so we need the system to care about what's actually good rather than about whatever we managed to specify. My post argues that only the narrow problem is a technical problem. The broad one is either moral philosophy, which we'd have to do anyway, or it collapses into the narrow one, since any procedure for getting a system to care about the good has to be written down somewhere, and that writing-down is the proxy problem again.

Then the part I think is mine. I want to split "intent" by whose intent and when. There's the intent of whoever trained the model, and the intent of whoever is operating it. The narrow problem is usually stated as the system failing to do what the trainer intended, but most of the real harm stories I can think of are operator failures: the model does fine on the trainer's objective, and the operator points it at something the trainer never imagined, or the operator's goal is adversarially related to the trainer's. I want to call that space the delegation gap and claim that a lot of what gets filed under "misalignment" is really a delegation gap, not a failure of the model to have the right intent or the right values.

What I want from you: where have I misdescribed the two flavors, is the delegation gap a real distinction or just the principal-agent problem in new clothes, and does the collapse argument go through or does it beg the question?
