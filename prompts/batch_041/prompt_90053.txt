I'm building a session for a weekend workshop and I need you to design the activity, not the talk. Here's the setup and the stuff I care about.

The crowd: 24 adults, mixed — a handful of policy people, a few grad students, two engineers, rest are curious civilians. Two hours, one room, four tables of six. I get 5 minutes up front to explain rules and then I don't want to lecture again until the debrief. Materials should be paper, index cards, dice if you must, no money, no phones.

The topic is how much rule-following a group should demand of its members — the tight-vs-loose thing from cross-cultural psychology, but I do not want the session to be a dramatization of that literature, and I don't want you pointing me at papers to read, I'm not going to read them. Instead I've got my own little accounting scheme and I want the activity built on it. Three accounts that every rule draws on:

1. coordination — what a rule buys you when everyone actually follows it (predictability, joint action, the thing you can't get by each person improvising).
2. slack — what the rule costs you by foreclosing moves that would have been better than the rule, but that you can only discover by someone trying them.
3. buy-in — the legitimacy account. I think enforcement spends it when it comes from outside the group and deposits it when it comes from people who chose the rule, and I think the same enforcement act does opposite things to this account depending on who's doing the enforcing. Peer sanction vs. appointed monitor, basically.

And my actual hunch, which I want the game to put real pressure on: the ratchet is asymmetric. Rules get added under threat and come off very rarely, so the live question isn't "how tight should we be" but "who holds the dial for loosening, and what do they have to pay to turn it." If the activity makes that feel obviously true I'll be happy; if it makes it feel false I'll be even happier, honestly.

Design constraints, and these matter more to me than elegance:

- Tightness has to be a dial the group sets for itself, in play, and can change mid-game. It can't be fixed by me at the start.
- Enforcement has to be endogenous — a player with their own payoff, not me stepping in as the referee.
- At least one thing in the game must be discoverable only by somebody breaking a rule. If nobody ever deviates, the group should be leaving something on the table and should be able to feel that it is.
- The environment shifts partway through without announcement — I want one kind of group to be winning before the shift and the other kind after, and I want them to have to notice.
- Simple enough to teach in five minutes. If the rules take longer to explain than to play, it's dead.
- No real money, no phones, and nobody should be able to sit out.

The debrief should walk back through specific moments from the play and map them onto the three accounts, and I want it to end with every table having to write down one sentence answering how much rule-following a group should demand and who gets to loosen it — no "it depends" allowed, they have to commit. I'll read the sentences out loud and let them fight about it.

Also be honest with me about two things: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and what I should do about it in the moment, and which feature of the real phenomenon the game simply can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud myself rather than pretend the rules carried it. My guess is buy-in is the one that won't survive contact with a points system, but tell me if I'm wrong and it's something else.
