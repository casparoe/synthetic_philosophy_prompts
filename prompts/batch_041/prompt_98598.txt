At the reading group I joined after retiring from the lab, two members spent most of an evening arguing about Goodhart's law, and I came away unable to say who had the better of it. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, not a verdict on who won overall.

Marta opened by calling the law a tautology. If a number is only a proxy for something you care about, she said, then rewarding the number rather than the thing is bound to pull the two apart; that is simply what "proxy" means. She compared the law to the announcement that a map is not the territory, and said its practical content amounts to "choose better measures."

Devin objected that this cannot be the whole story, because whether a measure comes apart from its target once you start rewarding it is contingent. Some measures survive being targeted, he said, and the interesting cases are ones in which the measure is excellent right up until the moment it becomes a target. He offered a manufacturer judged on units shipped per week, which raises its score by shipping units it knows to be defective. The thing being measured — working products reaching customers — is perfectly well defined, he insisted; the number moves and the thing does not. So the law concerns the gap between the number and the thing, and it predicts that the gap will be found and exploited when the stakes are high enough.

Marta replied that this is an old observation about incentives and gaming, and that calling it a law adds nothing. Devin answered that she had quietly changed the subject: her version is about badly chosen measures, whereas his is about the fact that no measure is immune, since a measure must be cheap, stable, and legible enough to be used, and precisely those features make it exploitable. Marta then said that if no measure is immune, the law is not a law but a definition of "measure." Devin said she could not have it both ways — the law cannot be vacuous and also contingent.

Two further exchanges, which I suspect are where the real disagreement lies. Marta observed that she spent forty years knowing that a correlation observed without intervention tells you nothing about what happens when you intervene, which is why one runs experiments; if that is the content of the law, it is the oldest lesson in statistics. Devin granted the point but said the intervention here is not an ordinary one, because the agents being measured are actively hunting for the cheapest way to move the number, so the correlation must survive an adversarial intervention rather than a neutral one. Marta responded that this still leaves him with a claim about how people behave under incentives, which is empirical and revisable, not a law; Devin said the necessity lies elsewhere — in the fact that any usable measure is a compression of the target, and compression is where the slack lives.

Please rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justifications, and then say what both parties are missing: in particular, whether the law's content is a priori or empirical, whether any measure is genuinely immune to it, whether their two formulations are versions of the same claim, and whether the adversarial-intervention point rescues the law or merely relocates the disagreement.
