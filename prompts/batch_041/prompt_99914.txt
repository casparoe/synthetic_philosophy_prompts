Two of my friends got into an argument at a dinner party last month and I still can't decide who was right, so I'm handing it to you. The setup: I'd been rereading the Laws for a book I'm working on, and I brought up the part — I'm pretty sure it's in the Laws, maybe Book I or II — where the Athenian argues that a well-run city should actually legalize drinking parties. His reasoning, as I remember it, is that wine is a test and a training at the same time. Get a man drinking among his friends and you find out what he's really made of, because the drink drops his guard; and if the party is run right, with a sober overseer setting the pace and the rules, then the same evening that reveals the man also trains him — the way you'd train anything else, by repetition under a little pressure. Like a gymnasium for the will.

Marcus thinks this is one of the sharpest things in the whole dialogue. Dana thinks it's a wreck. Here's roughly how it went.

Marcus: "The point isn't to get people drunk, it's to get them tested and trained at once. The drink does to your guard what a fire does to ore — burns off the dross and shows you the metal. And because the Athenian insists the party have a sober president who sets the pace, you get the revelation without the wreck. Then you do it again next month, and again, and the habit of holding yourself together under pressure gets built. That's not a contradiction of anything Socrates says. It's just education."

Dana: "It is a contradiction, though. Socrates' whole thing is that nobody does wrong on purpose — that if you really know what's good you do it, so wrongdoing is always a failure of knowledge. If that's true, then a drunk man isn't revealing his 'real self,' he's just temporarily ignorant, and you can't train self-control by rehearsing ignorance. And if the drinking party really does reveal a stable character underneath — some fixed thing that isn't just knowledge — then Plato has quietly thrown out the idea that virtue is knowledge. You can't have it both ways."

Marcus: "The Laws isn't the Republic. By the time the Athenian is laying out Magnesia he's already moved past the 'virtue is knowledge' line — he says outright that education is training children to feel pleasure and pain in the right places, long before they can follow an argument. The drinking party is exactly that kind of training. It's rehearsal in managing pleasure, not an exam on ethics."

Dana: "Then you've traded one problem for another. If it's habituation, then what the party habituates you to is behaving well with a babysitter standing over you. That's not self-control, that's obedience. The one thing a person needs in order to be decent when nobody's watching is the exact thing a watched party can't teach. And anyway, whatever a drunk man does might just be a drug effect — a chemical artifact — not the 'real him.'"

Marcus: "So you'd rather he never find out? The Athenian's point is that you need to know what's under the lid, and that a properly trained person has nothing under the lid worth hiding. And the supervision is scaffolding — the young drink under an overseer, and the discipline gets internalized. First you mind the president, then you mind yourself."

Dana: "Scaffolding you never take down is just a cage. And your lid metaphor is doing all the work. If character is a fixed inner thing, then the drink reveals it — but then the party doesn't change it, it only exposes it, and 'training' is a word for nothing. If character is just the sum of habits, then the party changes it — but then there's no fixed 'real you' under the lid to reveal, and the whole truth-serum story is a fiction. Pick one."
