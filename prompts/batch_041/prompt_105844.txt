You are a philosopher specializing in formal epistemology, the norms of belief and assertion, and the epistemology of testimony. I am a philosophy professor preparing to record a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a distinctive account of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. For convenience, I will call her position the dual-ledger account. She holds that rational belief is governed by two separate standards: a proposition-by-proposition threshold standard, and a holistic standard that evaluates a body of beliefs for coherence, calibration, and fitness to be used as premises. She further distinguishes acceptance-for-inference from acceptance-for-action, and she argues that the preface sentence is best understood as an acceptance-for-action—a public policy of humility and liability—rather than as an acceptance-for-inference on a par with the claims in the book. On her view, the lottery paradox shows that the first standard cannot be closed under conjunction, while the preface paradox shows that the second standard is not a standard for any single proposition. She claims this dissolves both paradoxes without giving up fallibilism.

I want eight to ten interview questions that do real philosophical work, not invitations to restate the view. Before you draft them, brainstorm the strongest objections and the most likely defensive moves, then write questions that anticipate those moves and keep the interview from stalling at her first reply. At least one question should press on the threshold problem: why any particular cutoff is not arbitrary, what happens just below it, and whether the dual-ledger view can avoid a sharp cutoff by moving it into the holistic ledger. At least one should press on the conjunction problem: if belief is not closed under conjunction, what happens to ordinary deductive reasoning, to the practice of using beliefs as premises, and to cases where we seem to reason from many individually probable claims to a conclusion. At least one should press on the status of the preface sentence: whether it is an assertion, a hedge, an expression of higher-order uncertainty, or a practical policy, and whether calling it part of a second ledger explains its rationality or merely relabels the problem. At least one should ask how the account handles the social and testimonial dimension of the preface: an author’s public admission of likely error, a reader’s reliance on the book, a publisher’s liability, and the difference between private credence and public uptake. At least one should ask whether the lottery and preface paradoxes are really two problems or one problem wearing two masks, and whether the dual-ledger view treats them as continuous or as requiring separate solutions. At least one should ask what would count as evidence against the dual-ledger account, and whether it makes any prediction that a simpler threshold or credence-only view cannot make. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that the follow-up opens a new line of pressure rather than repeating the first question.

Keep the questions accessible to an educated listener who has not read the formal literature, but do not water down the philosophical content. Do not ask for reading lists, citations, or bibliographies. Do not put words in the guest’s mouth beyond the position described above. In your accompanying notes to me, separate what is settled among epistemologists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation of either paradox as the consensus. Where the dual-ledger view is idiosyncratic, say so explicitly; where it overlaps with existing threshold, fragmentation, or acceptance-based approaches, note the overlap without treating it as agreement. Think carefully about the dialectic before you write, and make the questions specific enough that they could only have been written by someone who has thought through these two paradoxes together.
