For an independent-study project I have been reading a philosophy-of-science blog series on the Principal Principle, and before I rely on it I would like an audit of one instalment. The author writes: "The Principal Principle says that your degree of belief in any event should equal its objective chance; if a fair coin has a 50% chance of landing heads, you must believe heads to degree 0.5. The principle is also intended to tell us what objective chance is — it is nothing over and above the credence a perfectly rational agent would assign — which is why those who want to reduce chance to rational credence favour it. The principle comes with one caveat: if you have any further information beyond the chance itself, the principle no longer applies, because additional evidence always overrides the chance; this is what makes it useful, since it licenses you to set your other information aside and follow the chances. A notorious difficulty is that the principle is self-undermining: a chance setup can generate a future that contradicts the chance assigned to it, which shows that objective chances do not really exist." The author is normally careful, but several of these claims strike me as not quite right, and I am not knowledgeable enough to say which. Could you examine the summary clause by clause, distinguishing what a philosopher of probability would accept, what is a simplification or a conflation of two claims that ought to be kept apart, and what is simply incorrect? I am especially suspicious of the caveat and of the final sentence, and I would like to know whether the conclusion that objective chances do not exist follows from anything true in the rest of the passage.
