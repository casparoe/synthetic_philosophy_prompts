I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the thing that stopped me first was the MU puzzle. Here is the setup, so that you do not have to recall it. You begin with the string MI and you may rewrite strings using exactly four rules: if a string ends in I, you may append U; if a string has the form Mx, you may append x, so that Mx becomes Mxx; you may replace any occurrence of III with a single U; and you may delete any occurrence of UU. The question is whether MU can be reached from MI. It cannot, and the reason is not that nobody has found the derivation but that every reachable string has a property MU lacks: the number of I's in it is never divisible by three. MI has one I; appending U leaves the count alone; doubling the part after the M doubles the count; replacing III with U subtracts three; deleting UU changes nothing. Doubling and subtracting three both preserve the property of not being divisible by three, and MU has no I's at all, and zero is divisible by three. Notice what that argument is, though: it is not a move in the puzzle. No rule licenses counting. The question can only be answered by ceasing to derive and stepping outside the system, which is the move the puzzle is built to force you to notice.

I would like a poem about that step, to close a short session I am running for my team on the difference between fuzzing a search space and proving a property of it.

1. Form. About twenty-four lines, in three numbered parts, no fixed meter and no required rhyme scheme, but with a formal pattern that the argument can be read off from. Part one should establish a pattern (a refrain, a rhyme, a line-length rule, a repeated syntactic shape — your choice). The step outside the system should be marked by that pattern breaking for exactly one line, and for no longer. The third part should take the pattern up again, and the closing line should echo the sound of the opening line while making a claim the opening line could not have made. Someone attending only to the shape should be able to see where the system is left behind.

2. The parts. Part one is spoken from inside: the speaker is generating strings, applying the rules, watching the space of possibilities widen. Part two is the step out: the speaker stops generating and counts. Part three is spoken from outside, and it should register the two things that make the step strange — that it is not itself a move, since nothing in the system licenses it, and that what it yields is a refusal rather than a construction: MU is never built, and the proof of that does not build it.

3. Strings. Parts one and two should each open with a bare string on its own line: MI for part one, and for part two any string that is genuinely reachable from MI under the four rules, which you should check rather than assume. Part three should open with MU, but the poem must mark it as named rather than reached. Every step the poem shows must be one of the four rules applied correctly; do not invent a fifth rule, and do not let the poem present an illegal step as a legal one.

4. The counting. The invariant has to appear in the poem's own words rather than as a transcribed proof, and it should land as a change of kind rather than a change of degree: the search was a matter of more time and more paper, while the count settles the matter in one move.

5. Accuracy. Do not present this as an instance of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and do not say that MU is undecidable. The system is a toy; whether a given string is reachable has a decision procedure; and the impossibility argument is an ordinary argument made from above the system, which is exactly why it is not a derivation. If you want the larger analogy in the poem at all, give it one line, and have that line mark where the analogy stops.

6. Audience. This will be read aloud to engineers who have never seen the puzzle and to one person who knows it well. The first group should come away knowing what was being looked for and why looking was the wrong response to it; the second should not be able to find an illegal step or a false claim about the invariant. I will not be able to field follow-up questions about the literature, so the poem has to stand on its own. No names, no dates, no citations, no footnotes.

7. Ending. No moral about perseverance, and none about thinking outside the box; either would flatten the very thing that makes this interesting. The poem should be allowed to end with the target still unbuilt.
