A friend of mine, an engineer who has lately become an enthusiastic reader of popular biology, has been arguing as follows. Evolution produces exquisite design with no designer who understands what is being designed; a trained neural network produces competent behaviour with no programmer who understands how the behaviour is produced. Both, he says, are instances of the same inversion — competence without comprehension — and both teach the same lesson: comprehension is at most a late-arriving byproduct of competence, never a cause of it. Since understanding does no causal work, he concludes, we should stop worrying about whether our AI systems "really understand" what they are doing; that worry is a category error, and Darwin already dissolved it. He states the core of the argument as a proportion: natural selection is to biological design as gradient descent is to learned competence.

Audit that analogy as an argument rather than as a picture. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what in evolution plays the role of what in training, and in particular what plays the role of the selection pressure, of the variation that is selected, and of the environment that does the selecting. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether the two sides are alike in the one feature the conclusion actually needs: that no comprehension sits anywhere in the causal chain. Some disanalogies will be merely picturesque; others will threaten the inference, and I want them separated. Say plainly whether the conclusion — that comprehension is causally idle, and that worries about machine understanding are therefore misplaced — survives the audit, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole way. If the analogy can be repaired, describe the repaired version and what it would no longer license.
