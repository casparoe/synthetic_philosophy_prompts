I teach a seminar on the ethics of belief, and last week a colleague of mine — an epistemologist who's been thinking about this stuff way longer than I have, she's got a dog-eared James on her shelf and quotes Clifford at people at parties — sat in and we ended up arguing about it over bad coffee for an hour. She's got what I'd call a fairly settled track record on when believing-without-adequate-evidence is OK, and I want to know what she'd do with a case one of my students threw out. Here's her record as best I can reconstruct it: Clifford's shipowner who talks himself into thinking the ship is seaworthy and then drowns the passengers, blameworthy; a woman whose evidence about her husband's fidelity is genuinely balanced and who can't get any more, and who either has to trust him or leave him, and who chooses to believe he's faithful, fine by my colleague; a student who convinces himself he'll pass the exam purely to motivate himself, fine; a politician who talks himself into a policy on thin evidence and stakes the country on it, not fine; a dying man who can't shake his belief in an afterlife, not blameworthy, on the grounds that he can't help it; and a juror who votes to convict because the defendant's face bothers her, not fine.

The new case: a woman's brother has been convicted after a retrial, the evidence is genuinely balanced — the kind of case where you really can't say which way it should come out — and no more evidence is coming. She has to decide whether to mortgage her house to fund his appeal, so she can't just suspend judgement and wait; and if she's wrong, the victim's family pays a real price for the campaign she'd be running. She comes to believe he's innocent and does it.

Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) the candidate rules her past verdicts are jointly consistent with, at least three of them, stated precisely enough that they're definately distinct rules and not restatements of each other; (2) what each of those rules predicts about the new case; (3) which rule she's most plausibly actually running, and which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one; (4) your best guess at her verdict on the new case and the reason to prefer that rule over the others; (5) the further case that would best separate whichever rules are still standing after all that.
