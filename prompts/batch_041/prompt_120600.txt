I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on democratic theory and social choice — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, and I would rather not open with a lecture. What I want them to leave with is the sense that "what the majority wants" is not a fact about the group that a vote discovers but an artefact of the procedure, and I want them to arrive at that by doing something rather than by my asserting it. Please design the activity: rules, materials, roles, a scoring scheme that gives them a stake without using real money (the session does have a small genuine stake attached — about thirty pounds of discretionary funds and an end-of-term outing to arrange), a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief.

Two things need to happen, and the first is the one I have never got right. They have to reach a decision through a series of pairwise majority votes and find that the majority preference runs in a circle — A beats B, B beats C, C beats A — so that the winner is fixed by the order in which I take the votes. The standard classroom version of this fails because I hand out preference orders that I have plainly arranged to cycle, and the students write the whole thing off as a trick; I want the preferences to fall out of roles that each participant can defend, so that when the circle appears they see it as belonging to the group and not to my card-stack. Something like three blocs, each caring most about a different dimension of the choice and therefore ranking the options differently, with the cyclic structure emerging from that and from nothing else — no bloc doing anything unreasonable, nobody behaving perversely. Then run the same election three times with the whole group, changing only the order of the pairings, so that three different winners come out and nobody has to be told why. The second phase is the one I actually care about, and it needs the decision to be multi-dimensional — a package of positions on several separate questions rather than one flat choice, so that the same underlying question can be voted two ways. I want them to vote once issue by issue and once on whole packages and get opposite answers: an option that carries a majority on every one of the separate questions, while the people who back it on a majority of those questions are themselves a minority of the group. Same voters, same preferences, two entirely defensible ways of counting, two different verdicts about what the majority wanted.

The debrief should map particular moments onto the underlying results — the agenda round, the point where the issue-by-issue tally and the package tally diverge, the inevitable complaint that the options were badly designed — and it should do this by putting questions to them rather than by lecturing; I know this literature well enough and do not need a reading list, citations, or names, I need the mapping and the prompts that will set them arguing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; I expect at least one bloc to refuse to accept that its own preferences cycle and to start relitigating the options instead of the aggregation, and I suspect there is a real tension between giving them a stake in the outcome and needing them to vote sincerely, which you should either dissolve or tell me cannot be dissolved. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — I take it that it gives them nothing on strategic voting, and that by exhibiting a cycle it makes the problem look more universal than it is, since actual electorates are often rescued by preference structures the activity will not contain. If either of those can be gestured at cheaply without breaking the arc, say how; if not, say that.
