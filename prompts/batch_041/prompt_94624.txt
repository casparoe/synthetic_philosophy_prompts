Right, so my reading group's finishing a term on consciousness and philosophy of mind and I want to close it with a poem rather than another chapter, and the thing I keep comeing back to is the colour phi demo. You flash a dot in one spot, then a dot somewhere else a fraction of a second later, and if they're different colours people swear they saw the colour change halfway through the apparent motion — even though the second colour wasn't on the screen yet at the moment the motion seemed to kick off. The bit that matters is that it's specifically the *colour* that causes the trouble, not the motion; plain apparent motion on its own is fine, it's the fact that the future colour seems to leak backwards into the experience that's maddening. Then there's the Dennett thing where you can tell the story two ways: either the edit gets made before the experience ever reaches you (the show-trial version, where the evidence is fixed up in advance so the verdict's already in), or you genuinely have the experience and your memory of it gets rewritten afterwards (the 1984 version, the past altered and re-filed). From the inside you can't tell which happened, and his real point is that maybe there's no fact of the matter about *when* the colour changed at all.

So: write me a poem about this, and let the form do the work rather than just illustrate it. Roughly 16-20 lines, free verse is fine, no metre needed — but I want one line that returns verbatim at the start and the end, so the whole thing closes into a loop, because that's the actual shape of the puzzle, it keeps eating its own tail. That recurring line should be the one that insists on a definite 'when', and the loop should quietly bankrupt that insistence by the time we're back at the top. Somewhere in the middle, stage both stories — the one where you never experienced the wrong colour and the one where you did and it got doctored — and make it land that from the inside they're the same poem, that no amount of introspection breaks the tie. Don't let it turn into a lecture with line breaks: it should be a good poem first and a demonstration second, so someone in the group who knows the demo nods along and someone who doesn't still just likes the poem.
