I'm drafting the chapter where I argue that so-called cancel culture is best read through the expressivist theory of punishment -- the view that the point of a punishment is to express social condemnation and reaffirm the violated norm, rather than to deter or to give the offender what they deserve. I want to test it on a real case instead of yet another hypothetical. Take Justine Sacco: the PR executive who, in late 2013, tweeted a bad joke about AIDS right before an eleven-hour flight to Africa, went viral while she was in the air, and landed to find she'd been fired. What makes it good for me is that nobody was in charge -- no court, no rule she'd broken at the moment she hit send, no single punisher, no defined sentence, and the whole thing ran while she was offline and couldn't respond. So walk me through it. Does the expressivist account actually fit an episode like this, or does it secretly presuppose a punisher with standing to condemn? Map it onto the theory: which parts it explains well (the condemnation, the norm-reaffirmation, the irreversibility of the firing), and which parts it gets wrong or is just silent on (proportionality, the fact that strangers with no relationship to her set the "sentence," the way it double-counts, the total absence of any exit or appeal). Then flip it -- does the case force a revision of the theory, or does it show the theory was only ever about formal, authority-backed sanction and that what Sacco got was something else that we keep mislabeling as punishment? I keep wanting a name for that other thing (call it sanction-without-a-punisher?) and I'm not sure the category is even coherent, so tell me straight if it isn't. Bottom line first, then the mapping. Max 800 words.
