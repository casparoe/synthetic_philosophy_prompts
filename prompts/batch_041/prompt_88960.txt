I teach a seminar on the Holocaust and the explanation of perpetrators, and I want to use Jonathan Glazer's The Zone of Interest in the unit where we read Browning's Ordinary Men and Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners side by side. The premise is easy for students to state — the Höss family keeps house, gardens, and quarrels on the far side of the Auschwitz wall, and the killing is never shown, only heard — yet when I ask what the film believes about why men kill, the room goes silent, or someone reaches for the banality of evil and stops. My own hunch is that the film's refusal of the perpetrator's inner life is itself an answer to the historians' quarrel: Browning's account turns on what the men felt as they shot, Goldhagen's on what they believed, and much of the evidence for both is what the men said years later, to prosecutors, when reluctance had become the respectable thing to remember. Write the reading I can hand the class. Take each thesis at its strongest, say which of the three explanations my students reach for — situation, culture, character — the film actually dramatizes, and be candid about where it declines the question, showing something about perpetrators that neither book can state; if the honest answer is that the film has nothing to say about one of the theses, say so rather than forcing it. Do not quote dialogue; describe what the film does.

Lay it out in five named sections: "What the film withholds" (~150 words), "Browning's man" (~250 words), "Goldhagen's man" (~250 words), "What neither thesis reaches" (~300 words), and "Verdict" (~150 words).
