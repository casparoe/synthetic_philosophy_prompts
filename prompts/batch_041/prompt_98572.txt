Okay, I need help untangling a sentence that's been weaponized against me twice this month, in opposite directions.

Background: I teach a seminar on personal identity, and I also sit on my hospital's ethics committee. A colleague on that committee — a palliative-care physician who's read alot of popular Buddhist stuff — has a habit of closing down debates with "you are not your thoughts." Last week it came out over a case I care about: a woman in late-stage Alzheimer's who at 55 wrote a very specific advance directive refusing certain interventions, and whose daughter now wants to override it. My colleague's argument was that since your not your thoughts, losing your thoughts doesn't mean losing you, so the person now in the bed is the same person as the one who wrote the directive, and we should honor it. Fine, maybe. But then in my seminar a student used the exact same sentence — "you are not your thoughts" — to argue the reverse: if there's no self over and above the thoughts, then there was never a continuous someone there to have written the directive, and the whole question of "the same person" is a category mistake. Both of them said it like it was obvious and settled the matter.

I'm fairly sure the sentence is doing at least three different jobs in these two conversations, and that each job needs a different precisification. What I want from you is not an essay on Buddhist no-self — I know the doctrine. I want a menu: every distinct thing "you are not your thoughts" could be claiming, ordered from the trivially true to the radical, with a verdict on each (platitude / genuinely substantive / defensible but narrow / flat-out false or confused), and then, separately, a verdict on which of those readings actually supports each of the two moves above. I suspect my colleague needs one reading and my student needs a different one, and that they can't both be right, but I want to see it laid out before I commit. Also throw in whatever reading you think the people who actually say this sentence out loud — meditation teachers, wellness apps, my colleague — usually mean, because I don't think its any of the philosophically interesting ones.

One more thing, because I think this is where it gets slippery: the metaphysical question (is there a self?) and the practical question (whose advance directive binds?) don't obviously connect, and I want you to say when the slogan is being asked to bridge them and whether that bridge holds.

Format: lead with a "Bottom line" of three or four sentences. Then "The menu" — that's the bulk, one short paragraph per reading, each starting with a bolded name for the reading.
