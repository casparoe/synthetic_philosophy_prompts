A state pays a monthly allowance for each child and subsidizes housing loans in proportion to the number of children, justifying the outlay by pointing to its pay-as-you-go pension system: today's children are tomorrow's contributors. The same state reports its climate burden in per-capita terms, so that an additional resident improves its headline number, while its environmental ministry treats population growth as pressure on emissions and its family ministry treats it as a public good; and its regulatory agencies price the reduction of mortality risk at a value of a statistical life in the millions of dollars, a price they would not pay to bring about a birth.

What theory of the value of population does this bundle of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit axiology as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Does the pension justification commit the state to valuing new people only instrumentally, as future contributors to the welfare of already existing ones, or is it equally compatible with a total view on which a larger population is better in itself? Which rival theories does the practice quietly rule out? Is the bundle internally consistent, or do the per-capita accounting, the pronatalist justification, and the valuation of statistical lives pull in different directions — treating the newcomer now as a divisor, now as a contributor, now as a beneficiary whose life the state would never purchase? Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say which theories would equally license it. Finally, describe what would have to change in fiscal, environmental, and regulatory practice if the state adopted instead a total view, an average view, or a strictly person-affecting view on which only the interests of existing people count.
