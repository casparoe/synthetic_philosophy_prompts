I am revising a chapter on Mill's late socialism and would be grateful for candid comments on the argument sketch below before I commit to a full draft. The chapter is not a survey; it defends a single interpretive claim, and I would rather discover now that the claim is either wrong or uninteresting than after another month of writing.

The claim is this. Mill's late socialism is not, at bottom, a thesis about distribution. It is a thesis about the location of authority in production. That is, the question Mill is actually pressing is not who receives the surplus but who decides what gets produced, by what methods, and on what terms, and in particular whether the laborer stands as a subject or as an object of that decision. Call these the distributive axis and the authority axis. My contention is that everything specific in Mill's late program sits on the authority axis: producers' cooperatives, partnerships between labor and capital, profit sharing, and land reform are all proposals for relocating decision-making within the firm and on the land, and all of them are offered as reforms of private property rather than as replacements for it. Mill's socialism, on this reading, is a socialism of the enterprise rather than of the state. His continued deference to utilitarian considerations on the distributive axis, where he treats the optimal rule as an empirical question about incentives and production, is not a lapse or a residue of his father's Radicalism but a structural feature of the position.

The instability I want to press is this. Mill's case for cooperatives rests on the moral and intellectual development of the working class. That development functions as a precondition rather than a consequence of the reform, and Mill expects it to arrive through other channels: education, the franchise, the improvement of the condition of women, the spread of prudential habits. So his socialism is conditional on a social fact that his socialism does not itself produce. If the precondition fails, the program has no engine; if it succeeds, his remaining reservations about full communism lose much of their force, since those reservations are largely about the character of the persons who would have to run such a system. The position therefore threatens to slide, depending on an empirical premise Mill never settles, toward either cautious gradualism or something considerably more radical than he wanted to endorse. I take this to be the most interesting thing in the material and the reason the late writings deserve more attention than they usually get.

I am aware of three ways this might be wrong, and I would like your judgment on all three. First, I may be importing distinctions that belong to later debates, Fabian or syndicalist or Marxian, and reading them back into a thinker whose vocabulary does not mark them. Second, my use of the Autobiography as evidence may be methodologically shaky, given its date, its co-authored character, and its obvious self-presentational purposes. Third, the two-axis framing may be a redescription of what every reader of Mill already says, in which case the chapter needs a different organizing idea. I also want to know whether the population question, which Mill treats as the crux of the condition of the laboring classes, sits comfortably with my reading or quietly undercuts it.

So what I would like from you is a response that tells me where the sketch misrepresents Mill or overstates what the texts will bear, whether the central distinction does any real work or merely relabels familiar ground, and which parts I should cut or compress. Where you think the matter is genuinely settled among people who know the period, say so and say why; where you think it is contested, say that too, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus view. I would also like some indication of how confident you are in each of your criticisms, since I would rather have a hedged objection I can use than a confident one I have to discard. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter.
