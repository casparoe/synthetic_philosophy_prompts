I've been asked to give the short speech at the naturalization ceremony at our town hall next month — I got my citizenship eight years ago, and the judge who swore me in apparantly remembered me from some volunteer thing and asked if I'd do the new-citizen remarks this time. It's maybe four minutes, in front of forty or so people about to take the oath (from everywhere — the Philippines, Nigeria, Ukraine, Mexico, a family from Syria), plus the judge, a couple of immigration staff, and a lot of kids and grandparents in the audience who are just there to take photos. I know exactly what kind of speech they're expecting and I don't want to give it: the welcome-to-a-nation-built-on-Western-values thing, freedom, democracy, rule of law, cue the applause. But I also don't want to be the guy who stands up at a citizenship ceremony and explains that 'the West' is a made-up category and their new country is a pile of contradictions. These people just did something hard and they're happy; it would be a jerk move.

Here's what I keep chewing on. A friend of mine teaches eighth-grade history and he has this line he uses constantly, that a list of values is a receipt and not a recipe — you write it after the fact, describing the thing you already cooked, and then you wave it around as if it were the instructions that made it. He means that 'Western values' gets used to describe institutions that already existed, with their winners and losers, and then gets read backward as the blueprint. I think hes half right and I honestly can't tell which half. So what I want from you: act like a philosopher who also writes for the ear, someone who's good at making a real idea land on people who did not come for a lecture. Think it through first, in a few lines and in your own voice, about whether 'Western values' names anything at all that a new citizen could actually be asked to adopt, and about where my friend's receipt-and-recipe line is right and where it's cheap. Then write the speech, four minutes, that puts that thinking to work — it has to be warm and it has to be honest, and every sentence has to be something I could say out loud in a room that includes a judge and a nine-year-old.

One thing to be careful about: don't smooth over the disagreement. There are people who've spent their whole lives on this question and they don't agree with each other, so don't hand me one view as if it's the settled one. When the speech leans on something contested, tell me afterwards, briefly, which parts are contested and who'd push back on them, and where you took a liberty to make it work as a speech. And no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just prose, like you're writing me an email with the speech sitting inside it.
