So I spent most of last weekend arguing with a friend of mine — she's a fisheries biologist, we were up at her family's lake house and she was about to boil a pot of crawfish, which is probaly how it started — about whether it even makes sense to talk about sentience as a matter of degree. Her view is that there's a fact of the matter about whether there's anything it's like to be a given animal, but that the morally relevant stuff comes apart into dimensions that get collapsed together way too fast: how intense a single experience is, how much of the animal's life is valenced at all, whether it can remember or anticipate the bad thing, and whether it has anything like a stake in its own future. I mostly agreed, but I kept pushing on something I couldn't get straight: if a shrimp's pain is real, then calling it small sounds like I'm saying it's less real, and if I say instead that it's real but narrow, I have no idea what the narrowness is supposed to be doing in the moral math. We never resolved it, and now I'm stuck with it, because of this:

I just joined the selection committee of a small foundation. We have about $250k a year, which is maybe three or four two-year grants, and we've already decided to fund conceptual work only — no new experiments, we can't afford them and we'd be bad at judging them anyway. The topic is the moral status of beings whose sentience is uncertain: insects, crustaceans, probably some fish, and a couple of people on the committee want to include simple AI systems while the rest of us think that's a distraction. My job at the next meeting is to argue for what we should prioritize, and I want to walk in with an actual ordering rather than a list of interesting topics.

So what are the main open questions here, and which three or four should we put the money behind, in what order? I care far more about the reasoning than the list: for each one, why it's upstream of the others (does a lot of work in this area quietly assume an answer to it?), whether it's the kind of thing two careful philosophers could actually settle in a couple of years versus something that's just sitting there waiting on neuroscience we can't fund, and whether the answer would change what we do — I'd rather fund the question whose answer would reallocate our grants than the one that's fascinating but inert. Also tell me what should move a question up or down the list, i.e. what argument or evidence over the next two years ought to make me change my mind. And one concrete thing I'd like your take on: is the thermostat end of the spectrum (my friend's brother kept insisting his smart thermostat has preferences, and he was only half joking) a genuine test case that makes the insect end clearer, or just a way to avoid the hard part? Half the committee thinks funding anything about artifacts is a waste of a grant and the other half thinks it's the whole ballgame.
