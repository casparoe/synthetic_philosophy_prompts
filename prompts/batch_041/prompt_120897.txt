Seventy-five minutes, about eighteen undergraduates, none with any set theory: I want an activity, not a lecture, that makes the difference between potential and actual infinity land. Stage it as a job. Participants are front-desk clerks at a hotel with a room for every counting number, working from bookkeeping rules they learned on finite hotels — a numbered register, the principle that a whole is greater than each of its proper parts, and the assumption that "how many checked out?" always has an answer. Give me the rules, the materials, a scoring scheme that rewards internal consistency rather than confident intuition and needs no money, a timeline, and a debrief that ties each stage to a specific idea: size as one-to-one correspondence, the failure of the part–whole principle and of subtraction for infinite collections, and the finitist's refusal to treat a rule for every n as a completed whole.

Do not centre it on the shift-everyone-up-one-room trick; assume they have seen it. I want the force to come from two discoveries they reach themselves: an audit in which "how many guests left?" has no answer, and an arrival the hotel cannot absorb — a bus carrying one passenger for every point on a line, which no amount of re-rooming among countably many rooms can take in. Include a role for a participant who will not accept completed infinities and must be answered rather than overruled, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: participants only ever manipulate finite proxies, so I will have to supply the step from a rule for every n to a completed whole.
