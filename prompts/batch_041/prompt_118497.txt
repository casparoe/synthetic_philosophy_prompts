I coordinate a volunteer search-and-rescue team in a county that is mostly ridge and creek bottom, and one of the jobs I have ended up with is turning witness statements into the numbers we write on the sector map before we send anyone out. I have no formal training in any of this. I started thinking about it seriously after a friend who reads a lot of philosophy put a puzzle to me that I still cannot answer, and it turns out the puzzle has exactly the shape of the problem I face two or three times a month, usually in the first twenty minutes of a call, with one hand on a radio. Here is the case. Our search area was a valley we had divided into a north half and a south half, which we judged equally likely on the first day, and the north half was itself divided into two sectors, the ridge and the creek bottom, which we judged equally likely to each other, so ridge one quarter, creek bottom one quarter, south one half. A witness then told us, "if he went north, he went to the creek bottom." I said the ridge is now out and the rest renormalizes, so south is two thirds and the creek bottom one third. My friend said the witness told us nothing about north against south, so south stays at one half and the creek bottom simply inherits the whole of the north's half, giving south one half and creek bottom one half. We did both versions of the arithmetic twice and neither of us could find a mistake in the other's. The best I have come up with on my own is the thought that the witness's sentence is not a report of the kind "he is in the south half" is, and that my friend's version quietly assumes the witness was answering a different question from the one we asked, but I cannot get from that thought to a rule, and I would rather have a rule than an insight.

What I want from you is a procedure my team can actually run. I need the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a number and where it hands the decision to a person because the question has stopped being arithmetic. I want it applied to three cases, worked out in full. The first is the ridge and creek bottom case above: tell me which of the two answers it endorses, and tell me what would have to be true about the witness and about how the statement reached us for the other answer to be the right one. I have been arguing for whichever version I wrote down first, so if the procedure settles it one way, say so plainly rather than splitting the difference. The second is a statement of the kind we get constantly, where a farmhand says "if he isn't at the trailhead he's probably at the lake," and I genuinely cannot tell whether we have been handed a conditional or a hedge, or what we are supposed to do with the word "probably." The third is a case where two witnesses hand us statements that cannot both be true, or where the statement we are given would empty the map entirely and leave us with nowhere to search. For each of the three I want to see the numbers the procedure produces and to be told which of the steps are me deciding something and dressing it up as arithmetic.

A few constraints. Whatever you write has to be usable by people whose arithmetic stops at fractions, with a phone calculator, in about fifteen minutes, and it has to be something I can say out loud to a volunteer who thinks the whole business is a waste of time. I am the one who signs off on the map, so tell me where the buck stops with me. Do not give me a reading list, because I would not be able to get at any of it out here, and do not lean on anything you would have to look up; I want the reasoning itself. Finally, please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables. I want it to read like a long email from someone who has thought hard about it, not like a manual, and I would rather it be honest about where the procedure will fail than tidy.
