I have been teaching a graduate seminar on the correctness of attitudes, and I keep colliding with a family of cases in which some consideration counts in favor of an attitude — a belief, an intention, an admiration, a resentment — without bearing on whether the attitude is in any sense correct, and I have never managed to state cleanly what separates the considerations that do from the ones that don't. I have deliberately avoided borrowing a term of art, since the usual labels seem to carry answers I want to test rather than assume. What I would like from you is this: take my verdicts on the following cases as data, and tell me what principle, or small family of principles, best captures them, what that principle predicts for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. (a) A demon will destroy a city unless I come to believe, on no evidence whatever, that a certain beach contains an even number of grains of sand. I say the threat is no reason at all to believe, though it is a superb reason to want the belief and to take whatever steps might induce it. (b) A pill would make me believe I am a first-rate philosopher; the belief is false, but it would make me happier and more productive. I say taking the pill is rational, and I say the resulting belief is still unjustified — the two verdicts feel entirely independent of each other. (c) A junior colleague's paper is mediocre, and she will be crushed unless I find it admirable. I say I have reason to say I admire it and reason to wish I admired it, but the fact that admiration would comfort her is no reason bearing on whether the admiration is fitting. (d) I have been badly wronged, and nursing resentment will ruin my health. I say I have good reason to try to let the resentment go, and perhaps reason not to feel it, but if the wrong really was contemptible then the resentment is nonetheless fitting, and the health cost does not render it unfitting. (e) Here is the case I cannot make sit with the others. To win a large prize I must intend tonight to drink a harmless but foul liquid tomorrow morning, knowing that tomorrow, prize in hand, I shall have no reason to drink it. I find that the prize is a genuine reason to intend, not merely a reason to want the intention or to engineer it. Yet by (a) a promised benefit is not a reason for a belief. Intentions and beliefs are both supposedly answerable to their own kind of correctness rather than to the payoff of having them, so I cannot see what distinguishes (e) from (a) except that one is an intention and the other a belief — which is exactly what I want explained rather than assumed.

My tentative principle is that a consideration is a reason for an attitude just in case it bears on whether the attitude would be correct or fitting, while considerations that merely make the attitude useful are reasons only for the actions that produce it. That principle handles (a) through (d) and, I suspect, breaks on (e). Please test it, repair or replace it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts the repaired version cannot keep. I would also like its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a reward offered for admiring a villain, a benefit that accrues only if the attitude is sincere and uncalculated, and a case in which the fittingness reason and the payoff reason happen to point the same way. In your answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested line as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
