I've been reading about the execution of Charles I — the Rump Parliament, the charge that he'd waged war on his own people, the axe at Whitehall in January 1649 — and I keep wanting to run the whole thing through Aquinas on tyrannicide. Trouble is every summary I find hands me a tidy checklist of conditions, which I don't trust, because the real argument sits inside his discussion of sedition and the common good and I doubt it was ever meant as a checklist at all. So give me the actual mapping, not a recap. Who's the tyrant here and by what standard — the king who broke the realm, or the parliament that then ruled without him? Does the body that condemned him count as the public authority Aquinas insists on, or is it just the strongest faction in the room wearing robes? And does his warning about the worse tyrant who follows do any work once you look at what came after? Then flip it and tell me where the framework breaks: a sovereign king tried by thy own subjects, a legislature that becomes the sovereign, a 'worse tyrant' clause that smells like a consequentialist escape hatch smuggled into a natural-law argument about intrinsic wrongs. I don't want a verdict on whether they should have killed him — I want the mapping, and an honest account of the places Aquinas just has nothing to say.
