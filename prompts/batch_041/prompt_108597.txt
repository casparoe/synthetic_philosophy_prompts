Please explain a conceptual difficulty that arises for a view I have seen called tranquilism. The view concerns what makes an experience good or bad for the person undergoing it: an experiential moment is as good as it can be for someone exactly when she has no craving for change, where a craving is a consciously felt need for the present experience to be different, and it is this craving — not pain or displeasure as such — that the view treats as the bad thing. Because a moment without craving is already perfect, the view's scale runs from the perfect down through more and more deficient states, with no neutral midpoint and no separate region of positive value; pleasure counts only insofar as it removes craving, not as a good in its own right.

The difficulty is this. If craving is what the view says is bad, then wanting there to be less craving — in oneself or in anyone else — appears itself to be a craving. The view seems to instruct us to stop wanting what it instructs us to want, to be fully realized only in people who have stopped caring whether it is realized, and to be recommended only in a way that undercuts the recommendation. Please explain what this difficulty really comes to: whether it is a genuine inconsistency in the view, a merely practical awkwardness, or an artifact of conflating a standard for assessing how well a life is going with a procedure for deciding what to do. Explain what a defender can say — for example, by distinguishing first-order cravings from a second-order attitude toward them, by treating the absence of craving as something that supervenes on other changes rather than as a goal one can aim at, or by confining the view's role to the assessment of well-being rather than to action-guidance — and say, for each response, whether it preserves the view's distinctive claim or quietly converts it into a different position. Then bring the point to bear on a concrete case: a person whose life contains a persistent and sometimes painful craving that is inseparable from something she would not give up, such as grief for someone she loved or a long-standing ambition. What must the tranquilist say about her, and is that verdict a cost of the view or simply what taking it seriously requires?

In your answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
