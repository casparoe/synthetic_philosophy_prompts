I'm a fifth-year PhD student and in about two weeks I have to present the central chapter of my dissertation to my department, and honestly the Q&A is what's keeping me up at night. My thesis is a defense of moral particularism, but not quite the version people expect, and I want a prep sheet of the objections I'll actually get from *these* people rather than the generic ones. The familiar particularist move is that there are no true universal moral principles because the very same feature that counts as a reason in one situation can count for nothing, or count the other way, in another. I want to push past that: I claim the reason-relation isn't just context-sensitive but what I call *case-indexed* -- you can have two situations that match on every general, articulable feature (same act-type, same consequences, same motives, same virtues in play) and yet the same fact is a genuine pro-tanto reason in one and inert in the other, and nothing general explains the difference; the difference just is the particular case. My argument for this leans on a pair of what I call twin vignettes plus a dilemma for anyone who wants to codify reasons into a finite set of rules, and I tack on a corollary that moral expertise is therefore untransmissible by principle -- you can only get it by immersion in cases.

The audience is the problem. It's a fairly traditional analytic department, mostly people who work on normative ethics and take for granted that moral thought has to be principled -- they'll hear "no principles" as "anything goes." There's one Kantian who will go straight for universalizability and the idea that I'm confusing the content of a maxim with the particularity of an act. There's a consequentialist who'll want to know whether my view just collapses into decide-by-gut-feeling and whether it can give any guidance at all. There's a moral psychologist who'll attack the corollary with the expertise literature and say skilled clinicians and chess players *do* internalize general patterns. And there's a metaethicist who I suspect will say my thesis isn't even a substantive claim about morality but a claim about how we describe reasons, so I'm not really disagreeing with the generalist at all.
