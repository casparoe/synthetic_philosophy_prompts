A friend and I run a film night for our political philosophy reading group and we've been working through luck egalitarianism — the brute luck / option luck distinction, the Dworkin-ish thought that inequalities are unjust when they trace back to stuff you couldn't help, and fine when they're the upshot of your own choices. Next up is Gattaca and I want a genuine reading of it, not "the film illustrates the theory."

On the surface it looks like a luck egalitarian fable: Vincent gets shut out of the space program over a genetic lottery he had no ticket in, and the valids are sitting on unearned advantage. But the ending bugs me. He beats Anton in the swim because he doesn't hold anything back for the return trip, and the film clearly wants us to read that as his will against his genes — and Jerome's arc is the flip side, a man bred for a standard he can no longer meet. So does the film secretly need a pretty thick notion of effort and desert, the very thing critics of luck egalitarianism say makes the unlucky's claim on us depend on them passing a test? Where does the film's world look like the theory's ideal and where like its nightmare — the harshness objection, the worry that a society that sorts people by what they're supposedly responsible for ends up humiliating them? And what do I do with the fact that the injustice is fixed by one man's fraud and escape rather than by any institution — cop-out, or the film being honest that the theory has nothing to say at the level of the individual? Point me to the scenes that carry the weight, and tell me what the film sees that the arguments dont.
