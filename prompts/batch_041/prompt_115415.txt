I'm working on a little essay for a decision-theory reading group and I'd like you to act like a referee for it — someone who knows the cooperation/game-theory literature well and is allergic to hand-waving. Here are my notes, roughly as I'd write them up. Setup: two agents are about to play a one-shot game G, and neither can see the other's policy beforehand — the other might be a straight maximizer, might be running some heuristic, might be trying to be cooperative. I'm interested in whether there's a change to the game (or to what each agent commits to) that leaves both of them better off *no matter which equilibrium of the changed game actually gets played*, and no matter which equilibrium of the original game the other would otherwise have steered to. Let me call that a safe Pareto improvement (SPI) of G. My reconstruction of the standard notion is: G' is an SPI of G iff for every equilibrium e' of G' and every equilibrium e of G, each player's payoff under e' is at least their payoff under e, with strict gain for at least one player. The whole point of the "every equilibrium" clause, as I understand it, is that you don't need to trust the other agent to land on the good outcome — whatever they do, you're not made worse off. Toy game, call it Project: both put in effort gives (5,5); I work while you slack gives me 0 and you 4; I slack while you work gives me 4 and you 0; both slack gives (2,2). The pure equilibria are (Effort, Effort) and (Slack, Slack), plus a mixed one, so the worst a player can do at equilibrium is 2 and the best is 5.

The bit I actually want comments on: on that reconstruction, plain SPIs look almost vacuous in games like Project, because demanding that every equilibrium of G' weakly dominate *every* equilibrium of G forces you to raise the bad equilibrium rather than kill it — so the "improvement" can be a change that makes the slack outcome even more attractive, which strikes me as the wrong thing to call safe. So I want to float a weaker notion — tentatively, an SPI "modulo rationality" — where the dominance only has to hold against equilibria of G that survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (or maybe just against equilibria that aren't weakly dominated), on the thought that the other agent is at least as rational as I am and won't play something that's strictly dominated. Question is whether that's well-defined, whether it quietly collapses back into the plain notion in the cases I care about, and whether it ever licenses something a defender of plain SPIs would reasonably call unsafe. Also tell me if my reconstruction of the standard notion is just wrong — if I've mangled it, say so plainly and tell me where the real definition bites differently. And give me your honest read on whether there's a paper here or whether this is a known dead end I've reinvented; be blunt about which specific step is the load-bearing weak one.
