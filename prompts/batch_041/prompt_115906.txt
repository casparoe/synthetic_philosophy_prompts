I'm TAing a second-year philosophy of biology course and I've talked my way into running a movie night: we're showing The Prestige (Nolan, 2006) right after a unit on Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea, specifically the cranes-and-skyhooks chapter and his gripe with greedy reductionism. I have a hunch the film illustrates the distinction better than any example I could invent, but I don't want to just project Dennett onto it, so what I want from you is a real reading that's faithful in both directions.

Here's the shape of what I'm seeing. Borden's Transported Man turns out to be nothing but cranes: no new physics, no miracle, just an identical twin, one identity split between two bodies, a prosthetic finger, a man spending half his waking life disguised as an assistant. Angier's version is a skyhook in the most literal sense available — he travels to Colorado and buys an impossible machine from a genius, and it duplicates matter. On a naive Dennettian reading the film is a morality play in which the crane wins and the skyhook destroys its owner, but it doesn't come out that way, and I want you to say why. The Chinese magician who lives his act — the fishbowl he carries between his knees, a whole life deformed into apparatus — is the purest crane in the film, and Borden's reaction to him, that he could never live like that, is in retrospect a description of Borden's own marriage and fatherhood. Meanwhile the film grants Angier's skyhook real effects, real duplicates, real corpses, and it plays the honest mechanistic explanation as horror rather than relief. So where does it land? Does Cutter's three-part anatomy of a trick — show them something ordinary, make it do something extraordinary, then bring it back — amount to a claim about explanation itself, that you can't skip the middle act, that the audience's willingness to be fooled is part of the mechanism rather than a failure of it? (The collapsed birdcage and the child who cries over the dead canary seem relevant here, since that trick's secret is cheap and requires an actual death.) And what do I tell my students about the ending, where the explanation is fully naturalistic and yet the sting seems to be that reductionism about persons — which duplicate is me, which one is the one that drowns — is exactly what nobody can live with? Also flag anywhere I'm forcing the film to fit the theory; I'd rather have the honest critic's version than a neat one. No citations or reading lists needed, and don't bother quoting the script, I know the scenes, I just want the argument.
