ok so this is a slightly unhinged ask but i've been chewing on it for years and i finally want to nail it down. i'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere / lesswrong-adjacent stuff, and the argument i keep having (usually at 2am in a discord) is the old Mind's I one: you've got Einstein's brain sitting in a vat, kept alive, and the question is whether it's still him, whether it's still conscious. then somebody pushes it and says fine, forget the vat, write the whole brain down — every neuron, every connection, every state, and every transition, in one enormous book, ordered so that if you (or a machine) just read off the states one after another, out comes the exact same conversation you'd have had with the vat brain. and now: is the book conscious? is the book a person? is reading it out loud the same as talking to Einstein?

people have really strong intuitions here and i've noticed they dont line up with the labels they claim. so what i actually want is a decision tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, whichever you think fits better) that sorts people by thier gut reactions to the concrete cases instead of by the school they'd say they belong to.

constraints i care about:

- don't just hand me functionalism vs biological naturalism vs panpsychism as three boxes. every node should be a question a smart person can answer about a case without already knowing which view it commits them to. cases, not -isms.
- the branches should carve this specific problem at the joints. the things i keep seeing people split on: (a) does it matter whether the thing is actually running — does a book on a shelf nobody ever reads count, or does it only become a mind when the states get executed in sequence? (b) does speed matter — if the read-out takes a million years, or all happens at once, is it still thinking? (c) does it matter whether each state is caused by the one before it or merely listed after it — is a table that says "state 1, then state 2" the same as one state causing the next? (d) does the material matter — paper vs neurons vs silicon vs a guy moving counters around by hand (the version where a human is the CPU and doesn't understand a word of what he's computing)? (e) is there anything special about a brain specifically, or does "brain" stop doing any work the second you've written the whole thing down?
- the leaves should be the actual positions people land in — named where there's a name, marked "unnamed" where there isn't — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. and flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't separate: i'd rather you tell me two views come out identical on every case than pretend the tree splits them.
- throw in a case or two i havent seen people use, to shake it up — two identical copies of the book sitting side by side (one mind or two?), a page torn out halfway through the read-out, the book written in a script nobody alive can read.
- where some combination of answers looks logically available but no real view sits there, point it out and tell me whether you think it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's explored it.

i'm not asking for a reading list or citations, i just want the instrument itself. keep it to roughly 8-10 branch points so it's actually usable, and assume the reader is smart but hasn't read the literature.

also please write it in american english.
