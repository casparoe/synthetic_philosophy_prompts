I'm a third-year PhD student working mainly on epistemology of inquiry, and I've been running a small reading group on the classical pragmatists that has completely bogged down on the Peirce/James thing about truth. It's not that anyone's confused about what they held — we can all state both views cold. Peirce: truth is the opinion that inquiry is fated to reach, the limit a community of inquirers would converge on if they kept at it indefinitely, with truth being a property the opinion has regardless of what any particular inquirer happens to find useful or comforting. James: truth is something that happens to an idea, an idea becomes true by being verified, by making a difference in experience, by paying off. What stalls us is what to do when the two accounts seem to return different verdicts on the same case. I keep trotting out a case that I think is the crux: a community arrives at a stable, well-inquired, indefinitely sustainable belief by a route that is (stipulate) responsive to nothing in the world, and the belief happens to make everyone's life go better and survives every challenge they can mount. Peirce, I take it, wants to say this isn't automatically truth unless the inquiry is of the right kind, whereas James's criteria seem to certify it. Then my colleague says no — that just shows they're answering different questions, Peirce about the definition or the regulative ideal, James about the criterion or the psychology of verification, so there's no clash at all. That feels too easy to me, and the fact that the fight kept going for a century suggests it isn't just that.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that the impasse is really about what a theory of truth is for: is it giving the meaning of the predicate, or a norm governing when we may assert or believe, or an account of the property in virtue of which beliefs are true? Peirce, James, and their descendants slide between those jobs, so that a chunk of the dispute is verbal, a chunk is a genuine disagreement about whether truth is made or discovered (Peirce's realism about the past and the future versus James's willingness to let truths be made in the course of experience), and a chunk is a disagreement in the epistemology of inquiry that only looks like a disagreement about truth. I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and I want it to engage the actual moves each side makes — the convergence claim and its status as a regulative ideal, the role of utility in James, the later retreat to pragmaticism that Peirce made specifically to block James's reading, the objection that Peirce's final opinion is vacuous because it never arrives, the objection that James's expedient collapses into wishful thinking. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a change of question, a formal result about convergence, or nothing at all because the sides are answering different questions and should just stop talking past each other. Bonus if you can say whether the same diagnosis carries over to the neo-pragmatist versions of the fight or whether those are a different dispute wearing the same name.
