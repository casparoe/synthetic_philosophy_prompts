Having spent a career in the life sciences before turning to the history and philosophy of science, I keep encountering the proposal that pathogens, climate, and volcanic eruptions should be counted as historical actors rather than as background conditions. I want you to test that proposal against a familiar episode: the Irish famine of the 1840s, when a blight destroyed the potato harvest in successive years while British relief policy and the structure of Irish landholding decided who starved. Give me the strongest case that the blight and the weather were actors and not merely causes, then mark the point at which that framing stops earning its explanatory keep and slides into environmental determinism. Then reverse the lens and tell me what the episode reveals about the non-human-actor program itself: what conception of agency the word 'actor' demands, whether an organism without intentions can satisfy it, and whether the program does more than redescribe ordinary causal claims. I care less about a body count or a survey of historians than about the conceptual accounting — where the lens explains too much, and what that overreach says about the ambitions of the view.
