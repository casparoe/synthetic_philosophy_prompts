Please give me a careful conceptual treatment of the claim that moral compromise between people with conflicting value systems is a source of gains from trade, and tell me whether the economic metaphor can be made literal or is a seductive false friend.

Begin with the disanalogy that seems to me fatal if left unaddressed. In a commercial exchange both parties end up with more of what they wanted; in a moral compromise both typically end up with less, since the agreed arrangement departs from what each held to be right. If the improvement is measured against open conflict or the coercive imposition of one side's will, then the compromise is a gain in the space of outcomes and a loss in the space of fidelity to value, and I want to know whether both parties gain is even well-formed when the two spaces have no common measure. What is the currency here, and what exactly is the baseline?

Second, the trade model presupposes entitlements and an exchange rate: one can trade only what is one's own, and only at some rate of substitution. Many moral disputes are not like that. The claim that a practice is unjust is not divisible between the parties, and there is no rate at which one party's religious liberty swaps for another's equality. Does the failure run deeper than transaction costs -- is it that there is no initial allocation of moral claims from which to bargain, so that the machinery of mutual advantage never gets started at all?

Third, some disagreements are settled without either party conceding anything, because the parties wanted incompatible policies rather than incompatible ends, or because each can live by its own values within its own sphere. Consider a moral division of labor: instead of a shared ranking, the arrangement assigns different domains to different value systems. Is that a compromise, or a dissolution of the problem rather than a settlement of it -- and if it is available, does it not suggest that the gain was never really about exchange?

Finally, distinguish two things the metaphor runs together. A bargain is acceptable because it beats the alternative; a settlement is acceptable because each party can justify it from within its own commitments, so that no one is asked to treat a value as a bargaining chip. This is roughly the difference between a truce and a convergence that each side can endorse on its own terms, and I want to know which of the two gains from trade is supposed to describe. Close by saying what vocabulary we should use instead, and whether the good in moral compromise is located in what the parties get or in the relation the compromise establishes between them. I would rather have a tight conceptual treatment than a survey of positions.
