A colleague of mine who works on population ethics has been lecturing on what she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and several students have come to me unsure how much of her characterization to trust. Her core gloss runs roughly as follows: "Plain negative utilitarianism says that only suffering matters and pleasure counts for nothing, which is why it licenses destroying the world to prevent a single future torture. The lexical threshold version is its grown-up descendant. It abandons the idea that pleasure and pain lie on a single scale, holding instead that the two are ranked rather than weighed — that is what 'lexical' means, and it is why the view denies that pleasure and pain are commensurable. Below the threshold, suffering-avoidance has absolute priority, so at the margin suffering always wins; above it, the theory maximizes net utility like any other consequentialism, which is how it sidesteps the benevolent world-exploder objection that sinks its ancestor. Its weakness is that the threshold is a stipulation: nothing justifies fixing it here rather than there, so the view is really negative utilitarianism with an escape hatch, and it inherits the ancestor's problems."

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict. Tell me which of these claims a careful defender of the view would accept as stated, which are the sort of compression that quietly misleads, and which the view explicitly denies — and whether the closing inference (arbitrary threshold, therefore negative utilitarianism in disguise) actually follows. I am particularly interested in the commensurability claim, since I suspect that "ranked rather than weighed" and "incommensurable" are being run together, and in whether the world-exploder objection, as she deploys it, functions as a refutation or merely as a demand for further argument. Finally, she has floated a variant in which the threshold is epistemic rather than axiological, fixed at the point past which we can no longer reliably tell whether a creature is suffering; I would like to know whether that reading changes any part of your audit.
