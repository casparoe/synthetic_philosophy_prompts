im putting together a small anthology of philosophy satire and I need one piece, so heres the brief.

The domain is al-Ghazālī's occasionalism. Dont assume the writer has the details memorized, so work them into the setup yourself: al-Ghazālī (11th-century Islamic theologian, the one who wrote the Incoherence of the Philosophers against Ibn Sīnā and the rest of the falāsifa) argued that there's no necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect. The fire doesn't really burn the cotton; God creates the burning at the moment the cotton meets the fire. The regularities we call laws of nature are really just God's habit or custom, and He's free to break it whenever, which is all a miracle amounts to — nothing different in kind from the everyday. On the Ashʿarite line he inherits, God owes nothing to any creature and is under no obligation whatsoever, and the only sense in which we contribute to our own acts is a kind of acquisition of an act that God creates, not any power of our own.

Now the piece: write the text of a claim denial letter from a home-insurance company operating in a world where this metaphysics is literally true. Roughly 550-650 words, deadpan claims-adjuster voice, with the letterhead trappings (claim reference, date, dear so-and-so, sincerely). The adjuster has to deny a perfectly ordinary house-fire claim without ever being coy about the metaphysics — the comedy comes from the doctrine being applied consistently and straight-faced, not from the adjuster saying anything zany. Stuff I want it to touch, in whatever order the letter naturally finds: the fire as mere occasion rather than cause; the loss as God's custom rather than the fire's doing; the standard act-of-God exclusion, and what becomes of it when God is the only agent there ever was; whether the company can be said to owe anything at all given that nothing is owed by anyone; why the premium was nevertheless due and non-refundable; and what the company does with the insured's own carelessness — it should want to treat that carelessness as fully his while also insisting that nothing he did caused anything. Yes, it should have it both ways, and it should do it smoothly, the way a real claims department would.

Constraints: keep it legible to someone who's never heard of occasionalism — a reader with zero background should still be able to reconstruct the argument by the end and should feel the live question of whether our causal talk, our blame talk and our liability talk survive if there's no necessity anywhere in the world. But someone who knows the occasionalist tradition should also find it fair rather than a strawman: the letter has to be squeezing positions that are actually there (habit rather than necessity, no obligations on God, acquisition), not inventing an easier target. No named living people. Dont let the adjuster break character to explain the philosophy or to signal that it's a joke. No footnotes, no closing paragraph about how weird philosophy is. End it the way a real letter ends.
