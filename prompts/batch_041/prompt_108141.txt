My wife and I have a daughter who turns two next month, and I have been surprised to find that the question I spend the most time on is one I had assumed was settled centuries ago. Three weeks ago, unprompted, she carried her cup across the room to a crying baby at our playgroup and tried to press it into the other child's hands. Two days later, with an expression I can only describe as relish, she took a toy out of that same child's grip and held it above her head. I have stopped trying to work out which of these is the real her, and started trying to work out what the disagreement about that question actually amounts to. Every summary I find flattens it into a slogan about whether people are good or bad, which is plainly not what is at stake.

Please write a dialogue between two people who hold opposing views on this and who are each other's intellectual equals: neither is a fool, neither is a strawman, and neither is permitted to win by caricature. Let one hold the Mencian position and the other the Xunzian one, and let them argue about my daughter rather than about the history of Chinese philosophy.

The Mencian holds that she already contains the beginnings of the virtues — the flinch of distress at someone else's suffering, the reluctance to be shamed, the impulse to yield, some inchoate sense of what is fair — that these are faint and easily crushed, and that they only become virtues when she notices them and extends them outward into settled habits of character. On his view, much of the moral work is preserving and enlarging something already there rather than installing it from outside.

The Xunzian holds that what she is by birth is raw material — appetite, fear, the love of beauty and sound and comfort, the wish not to be wronged — and that everything we would call her goodness is manufactured: it comes from deliberate effort, from habituation, and above all from ritual form, which gives shape to desires that would otherwise run to conflict. On his view a child is like a crooked sapling that grows straight only when it is clamped and steamed into shape, or a blunt blade that becomes sharp only against the stone.

Give them three things to fight about, and make each press the other's weakest point rather than talk past it.

First: the Xunzian should put the hard question — if the goodness were already native, what would teachers, ritual, and the sages who made ritual have been for? — and should say what he thinks the cup-carrying actually was. The Mencian should put the mirror-image question — if her nature is nothing but raw appetite, where did the first person who ever became good get his goodness? — and should say what he thinks the toy-snatching actually was.

Second: have them take up the possibility that they are not really disagreeing at all. There is a genuine interpretive question about whether Mencius and Xunzi were even using the word for nature in the same sense. Mencius already refused to let nature mean merely what one is born with; he treated that as the mistake of an interlocutor named Gaozi and pushed back against it hard. Xunzi's case seems to rest on precisely that definition, since for him nature is the unworked material that ritual then patterns. So: does that make the quarrel merely verbal, or does it just relocate the real disagreement somewhere else? I want them to disagree about whether they disagree — one should argue that the dispute is substantive and say exactly what turns on it, the other that it dissolves — and neither should get off easily.

Third, and this is what I actually need: the practical upshot for a child who is not yet two. Does anything follow about whether I should trust her impulses, make room for them, and get out of the way, or whether I should drill and shape her until the right responses are second nature and only then expect them to feel like hers? I want one of them to concede something real here, and the other to notice the concession and press it rather than let it go.

Let the Xunzian resist being turned into a mere disciplinarian: he does not want her desires crushed, only given a form in which they can be satisfied without destroying the people around her. Let the Mencian resist being turned into a sentimentalist: he knows a sprout that is never tended dies, and that extending a sprout is hard, active work and not a matter of leaving the child alone.

Begin directly with the dialogue itself. No preface, no framing paragraph, no commentary afterward, and no closing paragraph telling me what to conclude — let the argument end where it ends, with both positions still standing and the crux between them as clear as you can make it.
