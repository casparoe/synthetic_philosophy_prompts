I am a graduate student in a cognitive science reading group that has been working through the typogenetics exercise—the toy string-rewriting system in which strings encode enzymes, enzymes act on strings, and the puzzle is whether a string can encode the enzyme that copies that very string—and I have been asked to lead the next session, which is meant to bring out the supposed isomorphism between self-replicating strings, DNA, and the Gödelian construction of a sentence that refers to itself. My audience is not specialist: mostly philosophy and linguistics students who know some elementary logic but have never seen a formal grammar, and who will arrive with the vague impressions that DNA is a code and that Gödel's theorem is about self-reference. I have built a stripped-down version of the toy system so that we need not work through the full original: strings are words over the alphabet {A, B, C}; an enzyme is a sequence of operations drawn from copy, move, and delete; the mapping from a string to its enzyme is simply A to copy, B to move, and C to delete, so a string of length n encodes an enzyme of length n; the enzyme then acts on the string that encoded it. I want you to plan how I should teach the session, not to explain the material to me.

What has to come first, what has to come second, and why—what depends on what? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must that example have and lack? I have been tempted to use a very short string that almost but not quite replicates, because I think the failure is instructive, but I am not sure whether it should come before or after the first success. Which misconceptions should I expect—for instance, that a self-replicating string is just a string that gets copied by something else, that the isomorphism means DNA literally is a formal system, that the enzyme is a little reader with a purpose, or that Gödel's theorem says self-replication is impossible—and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? Finally, what question should I ask at the end to see whether they have understood the structural point rather than merely followed the mechanics, and what should I openly refuse to explain at this level, saying so, so that they do not leave with the impression that the isomorphism is either trivial or total?
