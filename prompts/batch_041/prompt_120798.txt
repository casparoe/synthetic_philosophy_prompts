I'm teaching a grad seminar on population ethics this fall and I've decided to spend the whole first meeting on the boring part: what is a theory in this area even supposed to deliver? Every time I've taught it we spend alot of time intuition-pumping — somebody names one grotesque case, somebody else says that's not a counterexample, that's the price of avoiding an even worse one — and we never agree on the criteria going in. So write me the desiderata. The requirements any adequate population axiology has to meet, each with the case or consideration behind it, plus a verdict on which of them are genuine and which are just the prejudices of one camp.

Here's the pile I've been accumulating. I've probably mangled one of these, so fix it if I have, and tell me which ones are load-bearing:

1. It can't imply the repugnant conclusion — a big enough population of lives that are barely worth living can't come out better than a smaller population of lives that go very well.
2. Adding a person whose life goes well, and who makes no one else worse off, can't make the outcome worse.
3. But it also can't imply the sadistic conclusion — that it's better to add a few people with lives below the zero line than a larger number of people with lives above it but below average.
4. And it can't imply the very repugnant version: that an enormous population of barely-worth-living lives, mixed with a huge amount of real suffering, beats a small population of excellent lives.
5. The asymmetry: causing someone to exist whose life is not worth living is bad, but causing someone to exist whose life is worth living isn't on its own a good thing to have done.
6. It has to say something about cases where nobody is harmed, because the people who end up worse off wouldn't have existed at all under the alternative.
7. It has to be transitive — between any two populations you can string a chain of tiny differences, and if better-than isn't transitive the chain walks you somewhere you don't want to go.
8. Either it's complete, ranking every pair of populations, or it's allowed to say of some pairs that neither is better, worse, nor equal. Pick one and defend it.

What I want back: the list, with the case behind each requirement; which pairs or triples can't be jointly satisfied and exactly where the contradiction bites — that's the part I care about most, and if the whole list turns out to be unsatisfiable then say so, that's a result; and which single requirement does the most work at ruling candidates out.

Dont just lay the options out for me. Take a position: if something has to go, say what you'd give up, why, and defend it. Fix the purpose as follows — I don't need a complete ranking of every possible world, I need something decisive about the choices we actually face: how much to sacrifice now for a much bigger future population, and whether a shorter life lived well can be better than a longer life lived poorly. Don't bother with references, I know the literature, and no hedging at the end.
