I have no academic training, but for some years now I have been reading about Henry Sidgwick, and what holds my attention is not the part of him that everyone cites — the utilitarian treatise — but the part that tends to get a footnote. He was one of the founders of the Society for Psychical Research and its first president, and he gave an extraordinary amount of his life to the question of whether consciousness survives the death of the body. What strikes me is that he came at it without any appetite for easy comfort: he wanted evidence, he was scrupulous about how little of it there was, and he died in 1900, as I understand it, with the question still open and with its openness weighing on him — not merely as a matter of curiosity, but as something his ethics could not be indifferent to, since so much seemed to depend on whether the claims of self-interest and the claims of others could ever be brought into harmony.

My question is what he would have made of the situation now. The research programme he helped start never delivered; the field has become a byword for credulity, its most startling results have largely dissolved when others tried to repeat them, and almost everyone who studies the mind now assumes that consciousness is produced by the brain and ends with it. But I notice that none of this is the kind of evidence he said he was waiting for. A consensus is not a demonstration, and he was not a man to mistake one for the other.

So I would like you to reason it through as he would have. Would he have judged the question settled, or would his own standards of evidence have obliged him to keep it open? And if he had concluded that the proof he wanted would never arrive, would that have been a blow to his philosophy or only to his hopes — could his ethics have survived the answer he was most afraid of? Please give me your best reconstruction of what he would actually have concluded, not the one that would have been kindest to him.
