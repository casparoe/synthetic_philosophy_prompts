I'm running a 75-minute session for a mixed group of grad students -- sociology, psych, a couple of engineers who wandered in -- on the social construction of kinds. The awkward thing is that everyone in the room already says race is a social construct and gender is a social construct and would get huffy if you asked them to say what that means. So don't explain social construction to me; plan how I explain it.

I want the session built on three grades of the claim, and I'm not sure there a clean partition: (1) social factors causally produce the traits that members of the kind have, so socialization makes the gendered behavior; (2) the kind is constituted by collective acceptance, so nothing in the world makes you money except that we treat you as money; (3) the kind loops -- classifying people changes the people classified, so the category is chasing a moving target, unlike water, which doesn't care what we call it. Tell me if that's the right three, what order they have to go in and why, what depends on what, and where the boundaries between them actually break down. If grade 2 collapses into grade 1, or if the loop is just a special case of 1, say so and rebuild the spine instead of humoring me.

One example has to carry the whole thing. I was going to use money, but money feels like a trap: everyone already concedes it, so the lesson is over before it starts and nobody transfers anything. Should money be the warm-up and something else carry the weight, or is there a way to make money do real work? Whatever carries it, say what it has to have and what it must lack -- in particular it needs to be a case where the natural substrate is real and contested, so the audience can't retreat to constructed means there's nothing there.

Misconceptions I'm expecting -- tell me which I've got, which I'm missing, and where in the sequence to head each one off: constructed means not real; constructed means arbitrary or a matter of choice; constructed means it's only in our heads; constructed means we should abolish it; and the one I actually worry about, that if race is constructed then ancestry and phenotype don't matter, which is both false and politically useless.

Finally, give me one question to ask at the end that separates someone who can use the vocabulary from someone who's got the idea, and tell me what to refuse to cover in 75 minutes and say so out loud.

No readings, no citations. And if my three grades are the wrong frame, or the session as I've scoped it can't do what I want, just say that instead of writing me a plan around it.
