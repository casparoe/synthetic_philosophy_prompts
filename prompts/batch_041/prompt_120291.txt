For about two years I've run a monthly discussion evening at our local library — it started as a book club and has settled into roughly sixteen regulars, aged twenty-five to seventy, a genuine mix: two retired nurses, three or four people who live with long-term illness, a couple of schoolteachers, and several who simply enjoy a good argument. We have ninety minutes, one long table, a kettle, and no projector, and I'd like to keep whatever we do to paper and index cards. The subject I keep circling and now want to build an entire session around is the thing I've seen called epistemic injustice: the way one person's word counts for less than another's even when they are equally well placed to know, and — the part I find harder and more interesting — the way an experience can be perfectly real and still fail to land, because the shared vocabulary has no slot for it, so that a person is at once believed and unheard. I don't want to give a talk about any of this. I want the group to spend most of the session inside it and only learn afterwards what they were inside of.

So please design me a runnable activity: roles, a rule sheet, materials I can cut from index cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money. I have a few things I'd like built into the mechanics, because I think they're what make it bite rather than just illustrate. First, everyone gets a badge at the door with a reliability score on it, produced by a short quiz that is in fact irrelevant to anything — and that number quietly becomes their weight whenever the group tallies opinions. Second, the group has to reconstruct what happened in some situation from fragments held by different people, and can only file a finding if it is unanimous. Third, they work from a printed list of recognized descriptions, which are the only phrases permitted on the finding sheet — and the account that is actually true cannot be expressed in any of them. Fourth, one or two of the high scorers should be confidently and influentially wrong. Then give me a debrief that maps each moment of the session onto the idea, keeps the two problems distinct — the one about whose word is trusted and the one about what can be said at all — and makes plain that re-weighting the badges, which is the fix everyone reaches for, does nothing for the second.

A few constraints. Nothing up front: no framing talk, and the concepts shouldn't be named until the debrief. Nobody should be able to solve it by resolving to be nicer to each other, and I shouldn't have to steer it while it runs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — where people break character, where it turns uncomfortable in an unproductive rather than productive way, how to handle the participant who announces that it's only a game, and what to do if the group reaches agreement suspiciously quickly. And be frank with me about which part of the phenomenon an activity like this simply cannot reproduce, that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief, and whether the debrief is itself a bit of a cheat given that everyone gets to walk out afterwards.
