I am a graduate student in history, and last semester I read my way through a good deal of the literature on the origins of the Cold War — not in order to settle who was to blame, but because I gradually became more interested in the shape of the argument than in its verdict. What struck me is how rarely the people involved disagree about what happened. One account begins with the wartime conferences and the fate of Poland; another pushes the beginning back to the Allied intervention in the Russian civil war; another to the long delay in opening a second front; another to the atomic bomb and the timing of its use; another to the simple fact of a Eurasian land power and a maritime power arriving at incompatible conceptions of the postwar order. Every one of these starting points is something nobody on the other side denies. The disagreement is about which of them counts as the beginning, and the one each writer chooses is, as far as I can tell without exception, the one that leaves their own preferred party looking like the one who was responding rather than the one who moved first. I have since noticed the same structure elsewhere. Two friends of mine can reconstruct a falling-out going back four years, each of them pointing to a moment where, they insist, it really began — and in each case that moment is something the other person did. And in the incident write-ups my brother brings home from work, the same thing happens in a milder form: the account of what went wrong keeps getting pushed back a step, from the bad deploy to the review that approved it to the deadline that made the review perfunctory to the decision, years earlier, to staff the team the way it was staffed. I have read a few writers who say flatly that "who started it" is the wrong question, but they tend to assert this rather than analyze it.

So what I want is not a verdict and not a survey of the camps — I have both, and I can see that the camps are largely distinguished by where they start. I want to know whether the thing I have been circling has an established name, and whether anyone has done the work of saying precisely what it is. The nearest notions I can come up with do not seem to fit. The idea that a rivalry can be sustained by two parties who each act defensively in response to the other is a claim about the content of the moves, and it can simply be false in a given case; what interests me is the structure of the question, which looks the same whether or not the parties are sincere. The ordinary distinction between a remote and an immediate cause seems to presuppose that there is a fact about which is which, and that is exactly what I am doubting. And telling someone they are just being partisan is an accusation, not an analysis. If there is no established concept, I would rather have one coined and defined than have the phenomenon quietly folded into something adjacent. Either way, please tell me what the closest established notions are, how the thing I am describing differs from each of them, and where its edges lie — in particular, whether it requires a dispute with a long enough history, or roughly matched parties, or a written record, and whether the phenomenon is a defect in the question itself or merely a feature of how causal talk works once more than one party is doing it.

I would also ask you to be honest about the state of the question rather than confident. Where people who work on this agree, say so; where they genuinely disagree — for instance about whether a starting point can be justified on principle rather than by convenience — mark it as contested, tell me which parts of your answer a specialist would push back on, and do not present one view as the consensus. Please write in American English.
