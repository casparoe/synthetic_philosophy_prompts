I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm deep in a book where one of my characters runs a very long-horizon charitable endowment, and I've ended up actually believing the thing she believes, which is this: that a good thing happening sooner is worth more than the exact same good thing happening later, and not for any of the boring reasons. Not uncertainty, not because the future people might not exist, not because money invested now grows. Just because it's sooner. Call it pure time preference, a discount rate that's positive for no reason other than the passage of time itself. I think it's defensible, maybe even rational, and my co-author thinks I've lost my mind.

What I want from you is the case against me. What would count against this view — which arguments, which intuitions, which cases, which results in adjacent areas it stands or falls with? And I want them ranked, from the ones that should actually make me abandon it to the ones that are merely embarassing to have to answer.

Some things I'm already chewing on, so you know where I'm not naive. First, the spatial analogy: if it's fine to care less about a person because they're a thousand years away in time, why isn't it fine to care less about a person because they're a thousand miles away in space? Most people find the second one monstrous and the first one obvious, and I don't know if that asymmetry is a deep insight or just a fact about how we're wired. Second, tense. If the block universe / eternalist picture is right, there's no privileged now, and it gets hard to say what the "nearness" of a near future even consists in — whereas if presentism is right, the future isn't real yet, which sounds like it should cut the other way and make future goods worth nothing at all rather than a bit less. So which metaphysics does my view actually need, and does it survive on either? Third, the maths: I gather a stubbornly positive pure rate makes the value of the far future shrink to nothing and can make the total welfare of an infinite future finite or divergent depending on how you set it up, and that this has real consequences for how a long-horizon funder should behave. I'd like to know what that actually implies and whether it's an objection to the view or just a consequence of it.

Also: I don't discount the past. A bad thing that already happened doesn't matter less to me because it's over. Is that consistent with discounting the future, or does it show that what I'm really doing is something else — caring about what's still actionable, or about the living, or about narrative closure — in which case my stated view is a rationalization?

Tell me what a serious defender of pure time preference would themselves concede counts against them, not just what the critics shout. And be honest at the end: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability? Because I have a suspicion that every time someone hands me a counterexample I quietly retreat into "well, it's a mild discount rate, not a big one," and I'd rather know if I'm doing that.
