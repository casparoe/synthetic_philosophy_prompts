I design streets and advise local authorities on how much control a street needs: how many signs to install, whether to keep the kerbs, where to put the crossings, how hard to enforce the speed limit. I have no philosophical training at all — my working vocabulary is desire lines, self-explaining roads, risk compensation, compliance rates, signage density, enforcement thresholds — and I have met the philosophical literature only second-hand, through popular writing.

The framing I keep running into is the contrast between tight and loose cultures: societies with many strong norms and a low tolerance of deviance, set against societies with weak norms and a high tolerance of it, with the further suggestion that tightness is produced by threat — war, famine, disease, crowding, heterogeneity — and that a society pays for tightness in freedom and innovation, and for looseness in disorder. Two things make me want to take it seriously. The first is that my own trade contains what looks like a counter-example to the simple version of the claim. Hans Monderman, the Dutch traffic engineer, took the signs, markings and kerbs out of junctions and town-centre streets in Friesland, on the theory that a street covered in prescriptions teaches people not to take responsibility for one another; the resulting schemes were reported to produce more eye contact, lower speeds and a degree of caution that the regulated street did not. The story has since turned: the national guidance that encouraged these schemes was reversed in 2018, and organisations for blind and partially sighted people were among those who had objected from the outset to the loss of the kerb. The second is that my trade treats the level of control as a design parameter to be tuned until the numbers come out right, and I suspect that the question I actually care about — how much rule-following a society may legitimately demand of its members — cannot be posed in those terms at all.

What I would like from you is a systematic translation. Restate the tight-versus-loose position — both the descriptive claim about how societies differ and the normative dispute about how much compliance is legitimate — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: desire lines, kerbs and shared surfaces, risk compensation, compliance rates, enforcement thresholds, deterrence, signage density, and the engineer's distinction between a rule that is obeyed and a rule that is merely posted. Please do not smuggle my assumptions into the original and call the result a translation.

Then give me the residue report. For each element of the original, say what survives the translation intact, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all; and for each item of residue, tell me whether it marks a defect in the way my trade thinks about streets or a place where the original claim was vaguer than it looked.

Two things in particular I want pressed. First: is tightness one thing? The enforcement side and the internalisation side seem to come apart — a norm people follow because they will be punished is not the same object as a norm they have made their own — and I want to know what my vocabulary forces me to say about that difference, and what it cannot say. Second: run my own example through the translation and tell me what it turns out to be. Was Friesland a loosening, a tightening, or something the tight-versus-loose axis cannot represent — and does the way those schemes ended, with the kerbs restored for the sake of the people who could not negotiate the shared surface, tell me anything about what the framing leaves out?

Do not give me citations, reading lists or references to particular authors. I have no way to look anything up, and anything I cannot reason about from this message alone is of no use to me.
