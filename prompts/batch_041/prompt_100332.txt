I need a rigorous argument for a specific position about a two-envelope scenario, written for a reader who knows decision theory and who will not be given any references to check.

The scenario is fixed as follows. An experimenter rolls a fair six-sided die and records the number d that comes up. She then places 2^d dollars in one envelope and 2^(d+1) dollars in the other, seals both, and hands them to you face down. You select one envelope at random, open it, and observe the amount A inside. You must then decide whether to keep A or to exchange your envelope for the other, still-sealed one.

A colleague reasons as follows. Whatever amount A I observe, the other envelope contains either 2A or A/2, and by symmetry those two possibilities are equally likely. Doubling gains more than halving loses, so the expected value of switching is 1.25A, which strictly exceeds A. Hence switching is better whatever I see. And since the same argument holds whichever envelope I happen to have opened, I should have been willing to switch before opening at all — which is absurd, because the two envelopes are symmetric. So expected-value reasoning breaks down here.

Argue for the position that the colleague's reasoning is unsound and that the scenario nevertheless has a single, fully determinate correct policy. Your argument must do all of the following. First, state exactly which values of A, if any, make the premise that the other envelope is equally likely to hold 2A or A/2 fail, and explain why it fails at those values. Second, derive the correct conditional probabilities at the values where that premise does hold, and state the resulting decision rule exactly. Third, argue for the general moral that the apparent paradox is produced by one specific, identifiable mistake — the conflation of the ex ante symmetry of the two envelopes with an ex post symmetry once A has been observed — rather than by any defect in expected-value reasoning as such, and say why that mistake is of the same family as the one that makes the St. Petersburg wager tempting.

Do not cite any books, papers, or authors; the argument must stand on the scenario alone. Organize the reply under the following headings, in this order:

Verdict (about one paragraph): the position, stated flatly at the outset.
Where the premise breaks (about two paragraphs): the values of A at which the equally-likely premise fails, and the arithmetic that shows it.
The correct policy (about two paragraphs): the conditional probabilities where the premise holds, and the exact decision rule.
The mistake (about two paragraphs): what the colleague's error is, and why that error, rather than expected-value reasoning, generates the absurdity.
Objection and reply (about two paragraphs): the strongest reply open to the colleague — that the argument appears to work whichever envelope is opened — and your answer to it.
