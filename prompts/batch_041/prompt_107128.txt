I teach a senior seminar on rationality and collective decision-making, and I am writing the final examination. I would like a single essay question on the unilateralist's curse: the selection effect by which, when any one of many actors can undertake an action on its own, the action tends to be undertaken by whichever actor holds the most optimistic estimate of its value, so that it goes forward precisely when the estimates are least to be trusted. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard statement of the curse or the usual remedy. I want it to force students to pry the mechanism apart from the label.

Three things should be unavoidable for anyone who answers it well. First, the student must state the mechanism exactly and identify which assumptions make it bite — in particular, that the actors' estimates are noisy but centered on the true value, and that the action is triggered whenever any single estimate crosses the threshold. Second, the student must recognize that the effect is not really about one actor being able to act alone; it is about any decision rule that selects on the upper tail of a set of noisy estimates, and so it should reappear under, say, majority rule with many voters, and should be mirrored — with the sign reversed — under a rule that requires unanimity before acting. Third, the student must confront a variant in which the spread of estimates reflects genuine differences in information rather than mere noise, so that selecting the most optimistic estimate is selecting the best-informed one, and the curse either vanishes or reverses.

Please frame the question around a concrete, self-contained scenario I can print on the exam: a set of independent research teams, each of which can on its own begin a field trial of a technology whose net value is uncertain, each forming its own estimate. Do not let the answer depend on any citation, reading list, or reference to the published literature; my students sit the exam without web access and cannot look anything up, so the question must be answerable from the scenario plus what they already understand. Likewise, do not lean on any particular real-world episode whose details they would have to remember — invent the case.

The question should be answerable in about seventy-five minutes and worth twenty marks. Please provide, in this order: (1) the exam question itself, written in the register of a formal examination; (2) a model answer of roughly six hundred words; and (3) a marking scheme of five or six must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth (the total to be twenty), and for each point a one-line description of how a weak answer typically fails it, sharp enough to separate a top answer from a merely competent one.

Close your response with a one-sentence statement of the crux — the single thing a student must get right to earn top marks — on a final line beginning "Crux:".
