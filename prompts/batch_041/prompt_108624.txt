I'm helping put together a reading-group session on Arendt and truth in politics, and the closing segment is a thought experiment: what would she have made of our situation, given that she died in 1975. I'd like you to draft that segment.

Take the role of someone who knows Arendt's treatment of truth, lying and politics cold, and who is also a good enough philosopher to say when her framework simply doesnt stretch to a case. Think carefully before you commit; start by briefly laying out on paper the moving parts of her account (just for yourself, no need to show me all of it), then reason from them.

Her account as I have it — correct me if I'm mangling it: the political liar isn't just the truth-teller's opposite. He's a craftsman who has to know the facts well enough to replace them, and he becomes a servant of his own fiction, because the fiction has to hold together to keep doing its work. She treated the classic political lie as a lie about contingency — about things that might have gone otherwise, which is exactly what gives the liar his room. She drew a line between the fabricator, who wants to bend reality into the shape of his story, and the storyteller, who discloses meaning without claiming to report anything. She held that factual truth is the most fragile kind of truth, more exposed than the truths of reason or of philosophy, because it's contingent and only survives if enough people witness it and remember it. And she worried about the slow erosion of a shared factual world, while flatly refusing to put truth under the protection of a guardian class: no philosopher-kings, no truth ministry. That refusal is the bit I keep tripping over.

Now, everything that's happened since 1975:

- synthetic video and audio of real people saying things they never said
- generative models turning out passable political prose by the ton, so the supply of text no longer depends on how many people are willing to write
- platforms whose ranking rewards engagement over accuracy
- a style of communication that doesn't defend a lie so much as drown the difference between true and false
- and the move where authentic footage gets waved away as fake, because fakes are cheap now

Predict her views, and at minimum cover these four:

1. Cheap fakes: new phenomenon, or the old liar with better tools? Which part of her account decides it?
2. Her insistence that the liar is bound by his own need for coherence: does it survive an environment where incoherence is the strategy? And does the category of lying even apply when a machine produces fluent falsehoods with nobody lying — is there a liar at all, or only a flood?
3. Is there any remedy she'd accept? What's the line she'd draw between protecting a shared factual world and handing truth to an authority, and what would she be willing to pay to hold it?
4. Does the storyteller become more important or less, once plausible text is free?

For each: her likely view, the basis for it in her own commitments (in your own words, no quotes needed, and no citations — I know the texts), what would have to be true for the opposite prediction to be right, and your confidence. Flag at least one place where you think she'd say the question is badly posed, and one where her framework genuinely breaks rather than extends. And please dont just tell me she'd have disapproved — she'd have disapproved of nearly all of it. I want the parts of her thinking that generate determinate answers.
