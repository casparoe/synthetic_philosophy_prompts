I'm a postgraduate student in the philosophy of biology, working on a seminar paper about whether 'genetic information' earns its keep, and a friend in my cohort and I have been arguing about this for weeks without getting anywhere. I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of biology who has thought seriously about the metaphysics of information and to engage with the following dilemma on its merits. I should say at the outset that I know the basic vocabulary of information theory and don't need it explained to me, and that what I want is your own reasoning rather than a survey of the literature.

Here is the dilemma. The genetic code is genuinely arbitrary: nothing in the chemistry of the codon UUU requires it to be read as phenylalanine rather than as anything else, and the fact that the code is nearly but not quite universal, with the mitochondrial variants having drifted, looks much more like the contingency of a convention than the necessity of a mechanism. But conventions require convention-makers, and there are none in a cell. If, instead, we say that what fixes the mapping is the translation machinery itself — the tRNAs, the synthetases, the ribosome — then the 'code' is simply a mechanical fact about how certain molecules interact, on a par with any other causal regularity, and calling it a code adds nothing to the chemistry. So either the genetic code is a convention without convention-makers, or it is a mechanism that we have dressed up in semiotic language. Either way I am inclined to conclude that 'genetic information' is a metaphor we could and should do without, and that the only real information in a cell is the ordinary correlational kind that a statistician would recognise, the kind you get from any reliable causal dependence between two systems. I have heard it said that the code is not arbitrary at all — that it minimises the damage of mutation, or that chemistry constrains the assignments — but I have never found that convincing, so let us grant the arbitrariness for the sake of argument.

The one thing that stops me is a half-formed thought I cannot shake off. A ribosome does not know what a protein is; it is a machine that will incorporate whatever charged tRNA turns up, and it will do so with equal indifference to whether the resulting chain is useful or lethal. It is tempting to say that this blindness is precisely what makes the relation a code rather than a mere resemblance, since codes are read by devices that need not understand them. But if that is the mark of a code, then almost every causal process in nature qualifies, and I am back where I started. So please think this through carefully before committing yourself: begin by getting clear about the distinctions that are actually doing the work here — between arbitrariness and conventionality, between correlation and content, and between a mapping and a message — and then tell me which horn of the dilemma I should take. If the way I have set the problem up is itself off, say so plainly and tell me what I should be asking instead, and what I should say in the paper.
