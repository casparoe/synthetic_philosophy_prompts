I am setting the examination paper for a Master's-level module in comparative political thought, and I need a single essay question on the vocabulary of liberalism. My worry is the usual shape of such questions: candidates report that the American 'liberal' is what a European would call a social democrat, that the European 'liberal' is what an American would call a libertarian or a centre-right marketeer, add a sentence about the New Deal, and stop. I want the question to make that observation a starting point rather than a destination.

The crux I want it to force is whether these are two senses of one term or two terms that merely share a spelling. In particular, I want candidates to consider that the European use may function chiefly as a party-family label within a coalition space — one slot among Christian democrat, socialist, green, liberal — whereas the American use functions as an ideological descriptor of a position on a left–right axis. If that is right, a good answer should be able to say what follows for translation: whether an American who calls a European politician 'liberal' has said anything true, and whether 'neoliberal' can be carried back across the Atlantic with its sense intact. Candidates should also have to reckon with the fact that the shared root is still invoked by everyone — in 'liberal democracy', in 'the liberal international order' — and with the awkward British case, where a classical and a social-liberal lineage are alive in the same word. They should be free to use concrete anchors if they can, such as the German Free Democrats and their ordoliberal inheritance, or the fact that when the European Parliament's liberal group renamed itself Renew Europe in 2019 its French partners were keen to keep the word 'liberal' out of the name.

Please give me:

(a) the question as it would appear on the paper, with a short rubric (time allowed, word limit) and a stimulus claim of two or three sentences for candidates to assess — the claim should be unattributed and must not be presented as a quotation from any real writer;

(b) a model answer of about 800 words showing what a first-class script looks like, including the distinctions and examples such a candidate would reach for;

(c) a marking scheme out of 20, built from five must-hit moves, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong, and with an explicit statement of what a first must do that an upper second need not.

Two constraints. Do not require or supply reading lists, citations or bibliographies: the paper is closed-book and I am marking for argument rather than recall, so nothing in the question may turn on a contested attribution or on a particular author's formulation. And write the whole deliverable — question, model answer and scheme — in British English.
