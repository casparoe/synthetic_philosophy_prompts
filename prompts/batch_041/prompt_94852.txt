I have been working through, on my own, the thought that people deciding which child to have — which embryo to implant, or when to conceive — have a reason to pick the one expected to lead the better life, together with the companion thought that this kind of choosing is acceptable so long as it is not coerced and stays within the parents' own reproductive decisions. The more I turn it over, the less the second thought looks like a consequence of the first. The first is a claim about how well the resulting life is expected to go; the second seems to rest on something else, namely that the child's life should stay open. I would like you to build me a case that pulls these two apart, and then tell me whether the gap is genuine or whether I have smuggled in an answer to the question of what makes a life go well.

Here is what the case has to satisfy. It must be a choice between two children the same two parents could actually have, made before either exists, by means already available (no science fiction, no engineering beyond what a clinic could do today). Neither child is ill or disabled; the entire difference lies in the shape of the life each is expected to lead. One of them is expected to have the better life on nearly any measure of how well a life goes, but that life is also more settled: without anyone forcing them, this child will fall into a single absorbing commitment and will never seriously entertain an alternative. The other is expected to have a somewhat less good life, but a far more open one. The case must not turn on the parents' own preferences, on anyone already alive, or on costs to third parties — the only thing at stake is the quality of the life the resulting person will lead. Keep it to a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do the analysis. State what the choose-the-better-life principle says here and why, and what the keep-the-future-open principle says and why, then identify precisely which feature of the case makes them diverge. Next, tell me whether the divergence survives once autonomy is treated as one ingredient of a good life rather than as a separate requirement — that is, whether fixing a theory of welfare collapses the case back into a single comparison, and if it does not, why not. Finish by saying how someone who accepts the choose-the-better-life principle would try to show that the case is rigged, and what would have to change for them to be right.

I should say what the case is for, since that is what decides whether it works. It is meant to test whether the permissive view about reproductive choosing owes its appeal to the beneficence principle at all, or whether it depends on a separate commitment to open lives that the principle neither supplies nor licenses. So the case counts as successful only if the two verdicts are generated by the principles themselves; a case that simply stipulates that the better life is the more settled one, and then announces the disagreement, fails. I am not after references or a reading list — only the case and the reasoning.
