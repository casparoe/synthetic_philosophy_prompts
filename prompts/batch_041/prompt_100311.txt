Accuracy-first epistemology holds that the sole epistemic good is accuracy — that a credence is better the closer it lies to the truth — and that credences are to be assessed by strictly proper scoring rules, those defined so that a forecaster maximizes her expected score by reporting her genuine probabilities. If that is right, then the best possible credence function is the one that assigns probability 1 to every truth and 0 to every falsehood: at any world, that omniscient function takes the maximum score the rule allows. And since rationality is supposed to be nothing but the maximization of accuracy, it begins to look as though accuracy-first epistemology prescribes only that one be omniscient, and that for the rest of us it licenses maximal confidence whenever we happen to be right. A friend defends her certainty that a particular coin will come up heads on exactly these grounds: if she is right, her credence of 1 scores better than my 0.5, and if she is wrong then we are both mistaken anyway, so certainty can only help. This cannot be the whole story, because it would render the entire apparatus of epistemic utility theory either trivial or circular, and it would recast a hedged credence as a timid concession rather than a requirement. Can you locate the error? I want the real structure set out: what the scoring rule is being maximized over, why the omniscient credence function is not the target at which rationality aims, and precisely where my friend's dominance reasoning breaks down.
