**Two readings of a report, and where heterophenomenology's neutrality lives**

Dennett's method, as I understand it, goes like this. You take the intentional stance toward your subject: you treat them as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. You collect their "texts" -- what they say, what they press, how they react. From those you construct the heterophenomenological world: the world as the subject takes it to be. And crucially you bracket the metaphysics. You don't assert that the subject's reports are about real conscious states, and you don't deny it either. The point of the method is that a dualist, a materialist and an eliminativist can all use the same procedure and then disagree about what it means. Neutrality is the whole selling point.

The part I keep chewing on is the fiction bit. As I read him, Dennett wants the heterophenomenological world to be a fiction in one sense and not in another. It's a theorist's construction, like the world of a novel, with the subject as its protagonist -- nobody thinks the novel's world is a place. But it's supposed to be no fiction at all that the subject has the beliefs they report. So the reports themselves are real psychological facts; only their objects get bracketed. Fine. That's the bit I want to lean on.

My thesis is that the method treats a report in two incompatible ways, and that the neutrality claim lives off the ambiguity between them.

Reading one: a report is data. It's an output to be explained, like a needle's twitch or a rat's lever press. On this reading the method really is neutral -- nothing about consciousness follows from the reports, and the theorist is free to explain away any of them as an artifact (the colour phi cases, blindsight subjects who guess and deny seeing, all the standard stuff). But then the method is just third-person psychology with a fancy name, and the phenomenology is decorative. The heterophenomenological world is a useful model, and nobody would say the contents of the model are what the subject is actually acquainted with.

Reading two: a report is testimony. It's an assertion by a rational agent, and treating it as an assertion means treating the agent as competent and sincere about the relevant domain -- which is to say, as a reliable witness to their own experience. That's a substantive commitment. Testimony is defeasible, sure, but it carries default authority precisely because the witness is presumed to know the domain they're testifying about. And that presumption is exactly what the method said it was bracketing.

Here's the slide. The theorist needs reading one to license explaining reports away, and reading two to keep the intuition that the heterophenomenological world is what it's like for the subject rather than just a model of what the subject says. Both are needed, neither is announced, and the neutrality is purchased by never saying which one is in force.

Now the part I'm least sure about. Compare a human subject with a language model. With a model we take reading one without hesitation. Nobody thinks the model's self-report about its inner life constrains the metaphysics of its inner life; we call it confabulation, we say it's generated by the training distribution, and we get on with our day. With a human we default to reading two. But if the method were genuinely neutral between subjects, the default should be the same in both cases. So either the human default is unearned, or the method isn't neutral. My suspicion is that the claim that the subject's beliefs are real is doing the work: it smuggles in the subject's competence, and competence is a normative notion that only makes sense for a creature with a stake in getting things right. A model has no stake. So the intentional stance toward a model is a heuristic, whereas toward a person it gets treated as a description -- and the method never says which it is.

Honest concession: maybe this is just the familiar complaint that the intentional stance is normative and therefore not neutral, dressed up in new clothes. And maybe the model case is disanalogous in a way that makes it a bad argument rather than a good one -- a model may not be the sort of thing that has a point of view to bracket in the first place, so of course its reports are just outputs. But that cuts both ways, because then the method has nothing to say about the only subjects it was designed for.
