Please explain the distinction between prasajya-pratiṣedha and paryudāsa-pratiṣedha—roughly, negation that merely cancels or excludes a predicate versus negation that presupposes and asserts something positive in its place—and then use that distinction to analyze Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi), the four-cornered scheme whose limbs are usually given as P, not-P, both P and not-P, and neither P nor not-P. In particular, address the following: Under the reading on which each limb is negated rather than asserted, is the fourth limb genuinely distinct from the conjunction of the negations of the first two, or does it collapse into them? Then consider a proposal—one whose provenance I do not know—that the four limbs should be read as tracking different scopes or types of negation, with the first two limbs employing implicative negation and the last two employing non-implicative negation. Explain what explanatory work that proposal does, whether it is ad hoc, and what the alternative is if one holds that the tetralemma is not a set of theses at all but a therapeutic device for exhausting conceptual proliferation. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between the two negations is doing independent explanatory work or is merely a redescription of the same logical point.
