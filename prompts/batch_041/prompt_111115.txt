I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a course on perception and the senses, and our unit on the Molyneux case has left me with a set of verdicts I can't get under one principle. I know the standard historical answers, so please don't just report those; I want a principle that reproduces my own verdicts, or the smallest repair of them, and I'm willing to give one up if that's what it costs.

All the people in these cases were blind from birth, have ordinary intelligence and years of experience handling objects, and are asked to identify the objects by sight, without touching them, at once upon gaining sight, unless I say otherwise.

1. Sphere and cube: they have handled both for years, and both are set before them. Can they say which is which? No. I'm confident here.
2. Spiked ball, like a mace head, and a smooth ball of the same size. Yes, immediately: the spiked one simply looks spiked, and spikiness is something they already know from touch.
3. Distance and size. Two identical balls, one plainly nearer: no, they can't say which is nearer. Two balls side by side at the same distance, one plainly bigger: yes, they can say which is bigger. I keep tripping over this one, given case 1.
4. A cube that slides when pushed and a sphere that rolls. Watching the push, they can say which is which. Yes, but this feels like cheating, since they're reading the motion rather than the shape.
5. Raised-line drawings, and this is the case that made me write. Someone blind from birth has felt only solid objects and never a picture. They are handed a raised-line outline of a cube and a raised-line circle and asked which depicts the cube. No. Note that touch is the only sense in play; only the format is new. So whatever blocks transfer in case 1 is not novelty of the sense.
6. A buzzer rig: a camera detects flat faces and the buzzer pulses once per face, six pulses for the cube and none for the sphere. They can say which object is before them. Yes, but this is being told the answer in a code, not recognizing the object. I want a principle that separates this from case 2.
7. A person who could see until age six, went blind, and regains sight at thirty: yes, at once. Same history, but with sight lost at three months: no.

Is there one principle that gets all of this? The candidates I can see are: (a) nothing transfers between senses, because there is no shared code; (b) coarse qualitative features transfer while fine geometry does not; (c) transfer happens exactly when the new signal is in a format that has an analogue in the old sense, so the issue is format rather than modality; (d) all of it is inference, and the cases differ only in how automatic and reliable the inference is. Each seems to buy some cases and lose others, and (d) worries me because it threatens to swallow everything.

Tell me which to accept, or how to repair it. Say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — they gain sight and are shown a mirror and their own hand, and are asked whether the hand is theirs. Flag which case forces each clause of the principle, and say plainly which of my verdicts has to go. Lead with the principle in two sentences, then the case-by-case fit, then the casualty. No more than 700 words.
