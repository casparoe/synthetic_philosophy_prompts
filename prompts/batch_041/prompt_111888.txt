ok so I need help with something and it's turned into a bit of a mess. I sit on the public safety and health committee of our county board (mid-sized county, ~600k people, and we have a wierd patchwork: the city inside us stopped enforcing years ago, the unincorporated parts didn't). Two years ago we funded a pilot. Police stopped booking sellers and handed them a card for services instead, we didn't go after buyers at all, and we licensed two indoor venues that had to meet labor and safety standards. The pilot's up now and we have to pick something permanent, and I'm the one drafting the opinion we'll circulate before the vote.

Here's what's on the table. (1) The Nordic-ish route: keep sellers out of the criminal law but start actually citing buyers, and keep the ban on any third party profiting from someone's sale. (2) Full decriminalization, the New Zealand-ish route: repeal the offenses across the board, no licensing, no special registration, just ordinary labor and safety law. (3) The German/Netherlands-ish route: legal and licensed, venues registered, workers can be employed, health and safety enforcement. And (4) a wildcard from our vice chair, who wants what she calls symmetric enforcement -- her line is that the current asymmetry (he's a criminal, she's a victim) is itself the insult, so we should either decriminalize both or criminalize both. She's not wrong that the asymmetry is doing a lot of unexamined work.

There's one concrete case that I think decides this for me if I can't think my way around it. The two licensed venues did fine, but the workers there organized and want to run their own place as a worker cooperative: they'd own it, split the take, no outside boss. Under option (1) that co-op is illegal, because the Nordic model treats any third party who profits from a sale as a pimp, and a co-op is a third party that is also the workers. Under (2) they can do it, but no licensing, no protective relationship with our health inspectors. Under (3) they can do it only if they can cover the compliance costs, which they can't without an outside investor, which puts a boss right back in the picture. So the regime that looks most protective on paper is the one that forbids the workers from doing the thing they asked to do.

Also, one of our members keeps saying the state can't both treat her consent as good enough to sell and not good enough to be left alone, and I never know what to do with that objection. I'd like the memo to deal with it rather than talk around it.

What I want: a short written opinion, maybe 900-1200 words, that I can circulate to the committee. It should land on one of the four and say why, in terms of what we're actually trying to protect -- the workers' safety, their exit options, the neighborhood, or something about the dignity of the work itself -- and it should say what we'd have to give up. Be honest about the strongest case for the option you don't pick; if you tell me (2) is right, tell me what we lose by dropping the licensing and the buyer citations and don't pretend it's nothing, and if you tell me (1) is right, tell me what to say to those workers. Don't hand me a menu -- one recommendation, a phase-in timeline (I'd like it in place within a year), and a clear bottom line I can defend from the floor. No citations or reading list, I don't have time and we can't get half of it anyway. Write it in American English.
