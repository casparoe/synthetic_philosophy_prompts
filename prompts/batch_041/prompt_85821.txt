The room is a wierd mix: two type designers, a linguist who works on writing systems, someone from the Unicode/NLP side, a vision person, and me (philosophy, categories and essences, that's my whole deal). Give me a defended agenda: which open questions about letterform identity are worth the term, in order, and why. Don't bother with a reading list, I've got that side handled.

Some things I keep tripping on and want taken seriously rather than waved at:

- The 'a' you see in most books is the two-storey kind; the 'a' most people actually write by hand is the single-storey kind. Both are just 'a'. Same for 'g'. So what's carrying the identity — something perceptual, something motor, or something more like an abstract specification?
- Geometrically they're a mess: different skeletons, different stroke counts, and the two-storey 'a' has two enclosed counters where the single-storey one has one. Whatever the essence is, it doesn't look like a shape.
- Unicode hands out seperate code points to the bold, italic, fraktur, script and double-struck versions of a letter — different characters by the standard, the same letter to any reader. Meanwhile Cyrillic and Greek letters that are pixel-identical to Latin ones are definately different letters. So 'same character' and 'same letter' come apart — is that typography, writing-systems, or metaphysics?
- If you build something that generates letterforms in a given style — which is the whole point of the Letter Spirit idea — you seem forced to commit to some notion of letter identity. Does the generator need a notion of sameness that a perceiver wouldn't have? Or vice versa?

Also be honest about the empirical/conceptual seam here: if the perceptual work comes out one way rather than another, does that settle the philosophical question or only constrain it? I'd rather you flag where the two are doing genuinely different work than pretend it's all one inquiry.
