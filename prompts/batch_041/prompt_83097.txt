I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book, and my writing group keeps handing me the same line: the only thing you can control is your reaction. Last week I was going off about my publisher pushing my date and gutting the marketing budget, and my friend Dave said it, and when I told him I can't just decide not to be furious, he said that's the whole Stoic point — your judgments are up to you, not the outcome. Then someone else said it's actually a trichotomy, not a dichotomy, because some things you only partly control (how the book sells, say, or what people think of you), and Dave shrugged that off. I have a character who half-believes this line while her life is falling apart, and I don't want to write her as a fool or as a saint.

So lay out for me the precise claims that sentence could be making — everything from "don't burn energy on what you can't move" up through the much stronger idea that your feelings are voluntary because they're just judgments — and tell me for each whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Which one is Dave actually leaning on when he wants me to stop being angry? Does the advice only work if you already think the stuff outside your control doesn't really matter — because Dave definately does not think that about his own sales numbers? And which reading is closest to the old Stoics versus the self-help drift.

Give it to me as a table with these columns: Reading (short label) | The precise claim in one or two sentences | What it puts inside and outside your control | Verdict (trivial / plausible / dubious / false) | Does it actually support what Dave was telling me (yes / no / partly) | What it would mean for my character. Then close with one short paragraph saying which reading I should have her mean if I want her to be neither a sucker nor a superhero.
