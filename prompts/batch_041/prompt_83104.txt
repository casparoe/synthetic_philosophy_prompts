I'm a novelist, mostly historical fiction, and the bookshop that hosts my reading group has talked me into running a session, because my last book is about a town that quietly did something terrible to a group of its own people and then spent fifty years not mentioning it. The group is sharp but not academic — a couple of teachers, a retired GP, one guy who reads a lot of philosophy but has no training — and I've got forty minutes with them. Don't explain the thing to me, give me the plan for explaining it. What has to be in place before "the state apologizes" is even a coherant sentence — I'm assuming it's the idea that a state outlives the people in it, but tell me if the dependency order is wrong and what actually has to come first, and what depends on what after that. Then the one case to hang the whole session on: I'd rather invent it than use a real one, because I'll get a date wrong and lose the room, so tell me what my invented case has to contain and what it must not contain if it's going to do the work. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface — I know I'll get "it's just words, it costs them nothing" and "nobody alive did it, so who exactly is apologizing," and I'd guess a third, that they'll think the whole question is about sincerity. Where in the sequence do I head each one off? What should I openly refuse to go into at this level, and say so? And give me one question to put to the room at the end that will show me whether they've got the crux I actually care about: that an apology only does anything if it names the wrong in the wronged party's own terms, and in doing so takes certain denials off the table for good.
