I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on skepticism and the analysis of knowledge, and last week we read Nozick's tracking theory alongside the usual material on Gettier and the closure principle. I came away with the shape of the view but no real grip on it, and when I said so, a friend of mine in the seminar — a philosophy graduate student working on externalism, and much more confident about this literature than I am — gave me the following summary. I've written it out as close to his words as I can manage: "Nozick's tracking theory is basically the justified-true-belief analysis with a fourth condition bolted on. The condition he adds is sensitivity, which just means that your belief couldn't easily have been false: if you have hands, that belief tracks the facts, because you believe you have hands and you're right about it, and in the nearby worlds where you don't have hands you don't believe you do. But now consider the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis. If you were a vat, you would still believe you weren't one, so your belief doesn't track the truth of that proposition, and Nozick says you can't know you're not a vat. He's untroubled by this, because he doesn't think ordinary knowledge is in any trouble — you can still know you have hands. The price is that he has to give up the closure principle, and once knowledge isn't closed under known entailment, deduction stops being a way of adding to what you know at all: every inference you draw from your knowledge collapses. So the tracking theory ends up being a kind of skepticism after all, just a local one — the skeptic's conclusion goes through for everything you infer, even though his starting premise is rejected. That's why the view is remembered as an ingenious failure."

Something about this strikes me as off. I had thought the whole point of tracking was to give an answer to the skeptic that doesn't hand him his conclusion, and that it's a rival to contextualism rather than a disguised version of it — but I can't tell whether that reaction is just a caricature I've absorbed, and I certainly can't say which sentence is doing the damage.
