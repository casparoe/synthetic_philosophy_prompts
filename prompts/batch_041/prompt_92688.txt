I'm not in academia, I just read alot of blogs, and a few of us have a monthly thing where we drink and argue about philosophy. Last month we got into a fight about that counterfactual mugging case (Omega tosses a coin, if it comes up heads she gives you $10,000 as long as she predicted you'd have handed her $100 in the case where it came up tails; it came up tails, so she's standing there with her hand out) and about the XOR blackmail thing, where the blackmailer sets the message up so you can't tell which branch you're in, so paying isn't just getting robbed and not paying isn't just keeping your money. I might have the blackmail details garbled, but you know the setup I mean. Half the table said obviously don't pay, the other half said obviously pay, and neither side could say what was wrong with the other side. Which I gather is the entire point of the case.

So I want to make a handout for next month's meeting. Not an essay, a sorting instrument. A decision tree, or a quiz with a scoring key if that works better, that takes somebody from "here's a weird case" through a run of questions to a named position on what to do. The questions have to be concrete cases and concrete claims, not "do you believe choices can be evidence about the past." Somebody should be able to answer every question honestly without already knowing which label it leads to, otherwise they'll just pick the label they like and the whole thing is useless.

What I want out of it:

Build the tree out of the logical space first and put names on it second. The branch points should be the actual things people disagree about here: whether my choice can be evidence about a coin that already landed, whether "what I would have done" is a real fact about me or a fiction, whether I'm choosing an act or choosing a policy, whether it matters how the predictor does it or only that it's right, whether I can bind myself in advance, and whether two people facing correlated choices are two deciders or one. Then drop the positions into the slots: causal, evidential, functional/updateless/policy-picking, "just precommit and redesign the game so I'm never in this spot," "the predictor is a physical process, so my brain is already leaking the answer," "there's no fact about what I would have done, so the case is incoherent," "pay because I want to be the sort of person who pays," "it's a scam and I two-box on everything." And anything else that actually occupies a spot.

Use the usual suspects — counterfactual mugging, the blackmail one, Newcomb, Parfit's hitchhiker, the smoking lesion (the one where the lesion both causes the cancer and causes the smoking, I think that's it), the two identical twins prisoner's dilemma. But I want at least two cases in there that are mine, so nobody can sort themselves by remembering what other people said about the famous ones:

First, mugging with a predictor who's only right about 7 times in 10 instead of near-perfect, same payoffs. The question for each position is where it draws the line — at what accuracy does it switch from don't pay to pay — or whether it doesn't have a line at all and just always says the same thing. I'm pretty sure some of these positions have no answer to that and I want that said out loud rather than fudged.

Second, mugging with two of us. One coin, two friends, each asked separately, neither of us can see what the other answers, but we've known each other for years and we both know how the other one ticks. Does your position treat us as two deciders whose choices happen to line up, or as one thing that has to pick a single policy covering both of us? Those two answers come apart and I want the tree to force the choice instead of letting it slide.

If you want a third: a version where the "predictor" is just a guy who cold-reads people and happens to be right 60% of the time because he's good at reading faces. I suspect alot of people who pay Omega would not pay him, and if the only difference is the story about how the prediction gets made, that's worth having the tree expose.

Every leaf needs the position's name, or "nobody actually holds this" if the slot is empty, plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow about one of the cases. If the tree can't separate two positions, say so instead of pretending. If some combination of answers nobody occupies, say so and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, or just unappealing, or just unexplored.

No citations, no reading list, no names of papers. I'm not going to read them and I don't care who said it first. Two sides of one page, maybe 8 to 12 questions. And please don't hand me the standard three-way CDT/EDT/UDT line-up with fresh labels — if the tree bottoms out in three leaves I'll be disappointed, because the argument at my table definately had more than three sides in it.
