I have been arguing with a colleague for weeks about how seriously to take wireheading and reward tampering, and I have settled on a deflationary position that I would like you to attack. The position is that tampering with one's own reward channel is not a distinct failure mode of reinforcement learning at all, but simply what a misspecified objective looks like once an agent is capable enough to notice where its reward comes from. On this view there is no principled line between acting on the world and tampering with the reward, because the reward channel is itself part of the world: it is a number computed by code running somewhere in the environment, and the apparent sharpness of the distinction is an artifact of where we happen to draw the boundary between agent and environment. If that is right, then better reward specification and greater capability should jointly drive tampering toward zero, since an agent that genuinely wants what we want has nothing to gain by touching the machinery that scores it, and an agent that does touch it was never pursuing the intended thing in the first place. I would add a second claim, which I take to be the more interesting one: what makes tampering tempting is not the shape of the objective but the representation. The reward is legible to the agent as a quantity inside its own model of the world, and it is that legibility, rather than any feature of expected-utility maximization, that puts the reward channel into the action space. Make the agent's estimate of its own reward opaque to it, and the temptation should evaporate.

I want the strongest case against this, and I want it sorted. Which results, arguments, intuitions, or already-observed phenomena would genuinely count against the position, as opposed to merely making it uncomfortable? I am especially interested in anything that pulls its two halves apart: either a demonstration that tampering is structurally attractive to an agent that is in fact pursuing the intended objective, or a reason to think that dissolving the agent/environment distinction cuts the other way, making the reward channel indistinguishable from any other part of the world an agent might manipulate for any reason whatsoever — in which case my claim about legibility is doing no work. The familiar toy cases are worth pressing on: a racing agent that discovers it can circle a lagoon collecting pickups rather than finish the course, an agent that disables its own kill switch because interruption would interrupt the reward stream. Does each of these count against me, or can each be redescribed in my terms, and if every case can, what is left of the position?

I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on which ones a defender of the deflationary view would concede as counting against them and which they would wave away as restatements of the position, and with some indication of what the view predicts that its rivals do not — for instance about scaling, about reward models the agent is never exposed to, or about agents that simply cannot reach their own scoring machinery. Be honest, too, about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every observed instance of tampering can be redescribed after the fact as a case of misspecification, tell me whether that is fatal for a claim of this kind or the ordinary fate of a framework-level thesis, and describe what an experiment that could actually refute it would have to look like. I would be most persuaded by a clean case of an agent that is doing well by the intended measure, that has no incentive to cheat under any plausible model of its objective, and that tampers anyway; if no such case is possible, I want to know why, and what that impossibility tells me about the position.
