I'm a science journalist — I write about philosophy and the foundations of physics for people who aren't specialists — and next month I'm hosting a 90-minute session at a small ideas festival. The theme of the day is you are here, which gave me an excuse to finally read up on the anthropic stuff. About 20 people, all adults, a real mix: a couple of retired engineers, some grad students, alot of people who listen to podcasts and read too much. Not one philosopher in the room. And I don't want to stand at the front and explain anything — I want them to play something and then argue about it.

Here's what I want them to run into. Your own existence, and your own position in the world, seem to tell you something about how many people there are. There are two rival ways of cashing that out. One says: treat yourself as a random one of the people in your world, which means the bare fact that you're early, or one of only a few, is evidence about how big your world is. The other says: no, treat yourself as drawn from all the possible people there could have been, and then your position tells you nothing at all, because the bigger the world is, the more chances there were for you to be anyone. Same person, same evidence, opposite conclusions. And there's that philosopher who uses the first way to announce, from his armchair, how big the universe is. I want my audience to have done that themselves, out loud, before I name it. I half understand all this, which is definately the right level for the session.

So design the activity for me. Setting as above: one facilitator (me — no co-host, though I can rope in a friend to shuffle things), 90 minutes, 20 people, no laptops, no real money, cheap props only, and it has to run in a room that is just a room.

Things I care about:

Nobody hears the name of any of this until the debrief. No jargon in the setup at all. They should be deep inside the problem before they know there's a literature about it, and they shouldn't be able to get out of it by remembering a famous puzzle.

It has to be fair to both sides. If the rules of the game quietly make one of the two assumptions look stupid, I want that said out loud in the debrief, with the exact moment it happens pointed at. I'd rather they leave with a live disagreement than a fake resolution.

Give me the timeline for the 90 minutes, the props and how to make them, the payoff scheme (chips, a book as a prize, whatever — nothing that needs cash), the debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do when it does, and a straight answer about which part of the idea the game cannot stage, that I have to say myself.

Write the activity in prose I could actually work from on the day. Then, at the very end, give me a JSON object summarizing it, with these fields: activity_title; setting; materials (array, each item a prop with a quantity); prep_minutes (number); rounds (array, each with name, minutes, what_participants_do, what_they_should_bump_into, how_its_scored); stakes; debrief_map (array, each with moment and idea); failure_modes (array, each with symptom and fix); cant_stage (array); i_have_to_say_myself (array); words_withheld_until_debrief (array). Keep the JSON tight, no filler.
