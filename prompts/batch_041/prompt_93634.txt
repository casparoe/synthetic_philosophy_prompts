You are a decision theorist with a good ear for philosophical dialogue. Before writing, work out privately the strongest version of the subjective-expected-utility position and the strongest version of the ambiguity-averse position, note where each is most vulnerable, and decide what the real crux between them is. Then write the dialogue.

Two interlocutors, R and A, are standing in front of an urn. Both are fully competent and neither is a strawman; both know the standard literature, so neither should explain the setup or recite the familiar talking points. The urn holds 90 balls. Thirty are red. The remaining sixty were loaded by a machine whose rule was fixed by a fair coin flipped out of sight: on heads the machine put in 40 black and 20 yellow, on tails 20 black and 40 yellow. R and A know this and nothing further. A bet pays $100 if the ball drawn matches the color named.

R holds that A's characteristic preferences — refusing to bet on black or on yellow at even odds while happily betting on the disjunction of the two, which has a known chance of two thirds — are a mistake, and that A can be made to pay for it. A holds that the preferences are defensible and that R's diagnosis begs the question.

The dialogue must satisfy the following. First, they must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than trading monologues, and each must at some point concede something that costs them. Second, they must reach three pressure points: (i) R's exploitation argument, where A asks whether it survives once the machine is stipulated to be indifferent rather than adversarial, and R must answer that question rather than deflect it; (ii) the sequential version, in which the composition is revealed after the bet is placed, and then the variant in which it is revealed before, with the two of them arguing over whether A's ex ante and ex post choices can be rendered consistent and who bears the burden if they cannot; (iii) whether the symmetry of the loading mechanism, which fixes a well-defined distribution over compositions, reduces A's case to ordinary risk or merely relabels the ambiguity — A must say precisely what further fact about the situation A takes to be unknown, and R must say why that is not a fact at all.

Close with a short paragraph in your own voice naming the exact point of disagreement and stating whether it is empirical or normative, and whether either party has by then given the other a reason to move.

Roughly 700–900 words of dialogue, then the closing paragraph. No citations, no references to the literature, and no exposition of the urn beyond what the characters need to make their moves.
