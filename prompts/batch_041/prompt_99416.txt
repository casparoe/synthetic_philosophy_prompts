For an independent research paper in my school's philosophy elective, I am trying to reconstruct an argument I have encountered about rational choice under uncertainty, and I have reached the point where I can neither refute it nor bring myself to accept it. I would like you to identify which step fails and to explain what rejecting it would cost.

The setting is the decision problem ordinarily called the Ellsberg paradox. Two urns each hold one hundred balls, every ball red or black. Urn A is known to contain exactly fifty red and fifty black. Urn B contains some number of red balls between zero and one hundred, but the number has not been disclosed to me and I have no evidence bearing on it. I choose an urn and a color; if the ball I draw matches the color I named, I receive one hundred dollars.

The argument, which I have tried to reproduce as faithfully as I can, runs as follows.

(1) A rational chooser should act on the probabilities she assigns.
(2) If she has no evidence that makes red more likely than black in Urn B, she should assign each color the probability one half.
(3) Hence the probability of drawing red from Urn B is one half, exactly as for Urn A.
(4) Two bets with the same probability of success and the same prize are equally choiceworthy.
(5) It is therefore irrational to prefer Urn A to Urn B.
(6) The same reasoning governs the black bet, so a rational chooser never prefers Urn B.
(7) Nothing depends on the particular numbers; the reasoning applies whenever one of two mechanisms is opaque to me.
(8) It follows that a rational chooser would not pay to learn the composition of Urn B, since her chance of winning was already one half and cannot be improved by knowing more.
(9) This last claim seems plainly false. If I learned that Urn B held one hundred red balls I would choose it over Urn A without hesitation, and if I learned that it held none I would refuse it; the value of the bet plainly depends on what I know about the odds.
(10) Therefore at least one of steps (1) through (4) must be rejected.

I would most like to keep (1) and (4). Abandoning (2) appears to mean abandoning the thought that ignorance can ever guide a choice, which seems to leave every decision equally defensible, and I would rather not be told merely that the argument is confused without seeing precisely where. At the same time, I am not certain the argument is unsound; it is possible that it is valid and that the real cost lies in surrendering a premise I would rather retain. Please tell me which step is defective, why it looked so plausible, and what the alternatives cost. I do not need citations or a reading list.
