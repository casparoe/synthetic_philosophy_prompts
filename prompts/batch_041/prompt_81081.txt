I am a philosopher at a small liberal arts college, and I have been asked to give this year's commencement address. The audience will be about six hundred graduating seniors and their families, with my departmental colleagues in the third row, and I want to use an idea from my own field as the spine of the speech rather than as decoration. What I do not want is the standard address: no trust-your-inner-voice, no the-future-is-yours, no invocation of anyone famous.

The idea I want to build on is this. We habitually treat a person's traits as inner springs: if you have it, it will show, and if it never shows, you never had it. A better picture is that a trait is a tendency to act in a certain way when certain conditions are met, and the conditions can fail in different ways. A person can be generous and never once get to be, because nothing in her circumstances allows it. A person can be patient and be remembered for the single afternoon she was not, when someone finally found the one lever. And the very situation that would reveal a tendency can be the situation that destroys it or manufactures it: watch someone closely and the ease drains out of her; praise a child's honesty and the honesty turns into performance. I have taken to calling traits like this shy: their conditions for showing include not being on display, not being measured, not being asked to prove themselves. Some such cases are cases of a trait that is still there but blocked; others are cases of a trait that the test itself has killed. I want the speech to get that difference right, and I want the audience to feel it without my ever naming it.

Two things have to come through. First, this is not the deflating claim that people are just whatever their circumstances make them, and it is not the flattering claim that everyone is sitting on a hidden reservoir of greatness; both are cheap, and the picture is neither. Second, the practical advice has to follow from the picture rather than merely be illustrated by it: if what you are able to do depends on conditions, then a good deal of a life is spent arranging conditions — protecting practice that nobody is grading, choosing rooms in which the trait you want to have can actually show — rather than steeling your will.

Rules. No jargon and no technical vocabulary: the words disposition, fink, mask, manifest, and counterfactual may not appear, and my coinage is private shorthand that should stay out of the speech. No quotations, no attributions, no as-so-and-so-once-said. Include at least one concrete case from ordinary life that the audience can check against their own experience, and at least one moment of genuine comfort for the families, who have not come to be lectured about metaphysics. The register should be warm and plain, meant to be read aloud outdoors to people in folding chairs.

Write the address. Maximum eight hundred words, in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, and no bracketed stage directions, in American English.
