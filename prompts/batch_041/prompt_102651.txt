I've been asked to give the eulogy for a close friend who died suddenly at fifty-two, and I want to build it around an idea rather than around anecdotes. He and I spent years on the same rationalist blogs, and the thing he said most often in his last years was that he believed in no afterlife except the Hofstadter kind: that a person is a pattern, a strange loop, and that when the body stops, the pattern keeps running in degraded, partial form in the brains of the people who knew him. He meant this literally, as a claim about survival, not as a consolation.

I want the eulogy to honor that without translating it back into "he lives on in our memories," which he'd have called both false and cheap, and without overclaiming on his behalf. There's a wrinkle I'd like you to take seriously rather than smooth over: the partial copies of him in different heads have already begun to diverge, so on the literal reading there isn't one persisting self but several, and it's genuinely unclear who, if anyone, I'm addressing. The audience is mixed -- a fair number of people from the same forums who know exactly what I'm gesturing at, and a larger group of family and colleagues for whom all of this is new.

Please write the eulogy, with the idea doing real structural work -- it should constrain what I can and can't say, not decorate it. Then add a short note, kept separate from the eulogy itself, on where the speech takes liberties with the idea or leans on a contested reading of it, and on which parts of the view are settled versus genuinely contested among people who know the material; I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus.
