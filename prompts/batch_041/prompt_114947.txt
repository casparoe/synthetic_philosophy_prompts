I am a novelist — five books, the last two written entirely in the present tense — and for some years now I have been circling the dispute between presentism and eternalism without being able to say clearly what I take the dispute to be about. I would like you to restate the positions, and the arguments that divide them, in the vocabulary I actually work in: the distinction between the order in which events happen in a story and the order in which they are told; tense; focalization; scene against summary; the narrator's position relative to the time of the story; free indirect style; and the way a character is built out of discontinuous appearances spread across hundreds of pages.

Treat this as a serious translation rather than a set of analogies, and go position by position: presentism, the block view, and the hybrid in which the whole of time exists but something like a spotlight moves along it. For the block view I want to know how much of it my craft vocabulary carries without distortion, since I hold the whole book in view at once and my reader's sense of passage seems to belong to the reader rather than to the book. For presentism I want to know what survives at all. I do not believe I can write a present that is not either somebody's present or a grammatical convention, and I want you to test whether that is a fact about my vocabulary or a fact about time.

Then the residue report, which is what I am really after. Which claims come across cleanly; which survive only as metaphors I should not trust; which have no counterpart at all. Please address at least these: whether tense in prose is a representational device or an ontological commitment, and whether your translation keeps those apart; whether a character who appears in chapter three and again in chapter thirty is one thing wholly present on each occasion or a longer thing of which those appearances are parts, and whether that is the same problem as the philosopher's problem of persistence or merely a resemblance; and what becomes of the claim that past-tensed statements need something in the world to make them true when it is restated as a question about what makes a sentence about a character's earlier life true within a fiction.

End with a verdict on the residue. If presentism cannot be said in my vocabulary, is the vocabulary defective — too committed to a spectator's standpoint — or was presentism never a thesis about representation, so that I have been trying to hear it as one?
