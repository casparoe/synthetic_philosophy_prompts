Our five-person bakery co-op is drafting its pay policy and we are deadlocked. We run five jobs — counter, baking, bookkeeping, driving, managing — and we have agreed to rotate them by lottery every year, so within five years each of us will have done every job, including the worst-paid one. Our founder says we should settle the pay scale by asking what each of us would choose if we did not know which of the five jobs we would be doing, so that nobody writes the rules to favor their own seat. One member now says the rotation makes that test pointless: since everyone is guaranteed a turn in the worst-paid job, no one is stuck there for life, so we should simply pick whichever scale makes the bakery most productive and split the proceeds. Another insists we should still set the pay of the worst-paid job as high as we possibly can. Who is right? What work is the imagine-you-don't-know test actually doing here, and does it still support the second member's conclusion once the ignorance is temporary rather than permanent — given that a future majority could abolish the rotation, or someone could quit mid-cycle?
