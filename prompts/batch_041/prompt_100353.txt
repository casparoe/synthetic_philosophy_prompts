I am a PhD student in formal epistemology putting together a short collection of verse that does conceptual work rather than merely illustrating it, and I would like a poem on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. Please write a ghazal of exactly five numbered couplets, with a recurring radif that closes each couplet (and both lines of the opening one) and a signature couplet that substitutes the principle's name for the poet's name. Let the radif be the principle of indifference itself, phrased once and then returned to at the end of every couplet, so that a single rule keeps reasserting itself over material it cannot govern. Each of the first four couplets should be built on a different but equally natural way of choosing a chord at random — endpoints drawn from the circumference, a point drawn from a randomly chosen radius, a midpoint drawn uniformly from the disk — and the couplet's own imagery should make the corresponding answer (one third, one half, one quarter) emerge without the number ever being stated. The fifth couplet should turn to the invariance-under-symmetry repair and leave it honestly undecided whether that dissolves the paradox or merely legislates a fourth answer; do not report which of the three probabilities it favors. Ghazal couplets are permitted to stand as independent scenes, and I want that independence to carry weight here: the poem should read as the same principle arriving in worlds that share nothing but the principle. A formal epistemologist should be able to name the partition behind each couplet unprompted, though the poem must not depend on that recognition to be worth reading. No introduction, no notes, no gloss — the five numbered couplets and nothing else.
