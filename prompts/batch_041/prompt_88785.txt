I sit on the content advisory board of a mid-sized science museum and I have to write the board's opinion on a gallery we've already half-funded, before the fabrication contract gets signed at the end of next month. It's a permanent quantum mechanics gallery, mostly uncontroversial stuff -- double slit, entanglement, the usual -- except for the last room, which the designers call the survivor's room. You walk in, the lights go down, a branching animation plays, and a voice explains that every outcome happens in some branch. Then you reach a console: you press a button, a prop revolver clicks, the screen splits you into two copies, one greyed out and one that walks on to press the button again, and the piece loops, and the takeaway printed on the wall is that from the inside you never die. Our physics adviser A thinks the whole thing is dishonest: the animation puts a number on each copy ('50% of you'), and he says many-worlds has no business putting numbers like that on branches -- the rule that tells you how much probability to attach to an outcome is the thing many-worlds is supposed to explain, not assume, so the exhibit is smuggling in a probability rule under the guise of showing you the physics. Adviser B says that's a live dispute among specialists that we have no business adjudicating from a museum label, that branch counting isnt obviously wrong, that plenty of working physicists hold many-worlds, and that the visitors wont care either way. Our head of visitor experience has a different objection: a room whose gimmick is watching yourself get shot, aimed at school groups, is a bad idea even if it's good physics. Oh, and the donor who paid for the room specified in the gift agreement that the gallery should show that the multiverse 'is real', which everyone is politely pretending not to remember.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. I want a concrete recommendation for the survivor's room -- green light, kill it, or approve with conditions I can hand to the designers on Monday -- plus a timeline that respects the contract date, and reasoning that the designers and both advisers could actually push back on. Take B seriously rather than just overruling him; if the honest position is that a museum shouldn't be in the business of settling this, then say what we can still put on the wall without settling it. And be realistic about the lay visitor: if the catchy 'you always survive' gloss is a distortion of what many-worlds actually says, or an open question rather than a result, tell me what we should be putting in that room instead, in language a fifteen-year-old can follow. I did a philosophy minor years ago and I keep getting lost between the physics objection and the philosophy objection, so spell out where exactly the two of them are talking past each other.
