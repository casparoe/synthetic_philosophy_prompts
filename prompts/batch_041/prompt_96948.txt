I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and I keep returning to the cluster of ideas around common knowledge, agreement, and no-trade results. A group of us were arguing after a seminar about prediction markets and betting, and I wrote down my gut verdicts on a set of cases. I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them, what it predicts for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts I should abandon. If no single principle works, I would like a small set of candidates and a clear statement of the trade-offs between them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) Alice and Bob share a prior over a coin, both are rational, each receives private evidence and forms a different credence, and then each announces their credence to the other. A bet is offered at even odds. My verdict: taking the bet is irrational; at most one of them can be rational, or they do not really share a prior. (2) Same as (1), except the announcements are private: each learns the other’s credence, but neither learns that the other has learned theirs. My verdict: I still feel the bet is irrational, though less strongly. (3) Same as (1), but Alice and Bob have different priors about the coin’s bias. My verdict: the bet is fine; they can agree to disagree and trade. (4) Same as (1), but Alice knows Bob is irrational, in the sense that he always bets heads regardless of evidence. My verdict: taking the other side is fine. (5) A prediction market price moves. I know the marginal trader is well-informed. My verdict: I should update toward the price. (6) A prediction market price moves. I know the marginal trader is a noise trader. My verdict: I should not update. (7) A prediction market price moves. I know the marginal trader is well-informed, and I know that they know that I know this. My verdict: I should update toward the price, and I should not take the other side, because after updating I will agree with them. (8) A prediction market price moves. I know the marginal trader is well-informed, but I also have private information that they lack. My verdict: I can take the other side if the price looks off.

My tentative principle is that a bet is irrational whenever the two parties’ disagreement is common knowledge. A friend suggested that the real condition is common knowledge of the willingness to trade, not common knowledge of the disagreement, and I cannot tell whether that is a distinction without a difference. I would like the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not listed (for instance, a case where the disagreement is common knowledge but the bet itself is not, or a case where both parties are rational but one of them is known to be a better predictor), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If I have to give up one of the verdicts above, I would rather know now than keep a principle that only saves the appearances.
