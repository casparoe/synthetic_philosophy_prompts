I am a novelist, well into a book whose central figure is a decision theorist — a woman who has spent a career putting prices on information, and who, over the year the story covers, is offered a piece of knowledge about her own future that she cannot decide whether to accept. I have enough mathematics to know the shape of the standard answer and not enough to be sure it is the right shape, and I would rather build her intellectual world on a specification than on a slogan. So before I draft another scene, give me the desiderata: what must any theory of the value of information deliver to count as adequate? I want the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the formal results it cannot contradict — each one carrying the particular case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are the commitments of a particular camp (Bayesian, decision-theoretic, or the view that truth is a value in its own right), and be explicit about any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, because if the list is internally inconsistent then the list is the result, and that is precisely what I am after.

The puzzle I keep circling is this. There is a clean argument that information can never be worth less than nothing: whatever you are told, you may always disregard it and act as though you had never heard it, so knowing more cannot leave you worse off — and there is a matching theorem to the effect that, for an agent who maximizes expected utility, a more informative experiment is never worse than a less informative one. Yet it is equally well known — the point is usually credited to I. J. Good — that an agent who decides by some rule other than expected-utility maximization, one whose choice responds to the data in another way, can be made strictly worse off by free information; and ordinary life is full of people who cannot un-know a diagnosis, a betrayal, or a prediction, and whose options and preferences are transformed by what they learn rather than merely re-weighted. I want the theory's obligations stated so that it neither legislates these cases away as irrationality nor surrenders the results that make the clean argument compelling. I also suspect the account has to keep separate things I keep finding fused: the value to the agent and the value to everyone else; the value of becoming able to know versus the value of knowing; the value measured before the fact and after it; the value of information to a Bayesian and to an agent whose procedure is fixed in advance; and information that changes your beliefs as against information that changes your options, your preferences, or your identity.

Then, if it is not too much, close with a short paragraph on how the standard expected-utility treatment and its dissenters fare against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria, since that is what I will be writing from. Above all I want to know which single requirement, if honored, does the most to exclude the tidy theories, and which of my imagined characters each requirement would rule out as a coherent agent.
