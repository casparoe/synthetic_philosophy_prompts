I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on infinite ethics — the corner where a world contains infinitely many people and the usual "more happy lives is better" machinery starts misbehaving. I want a decision tree that carries someone from a starting commitment to a position with a name (or 'unnamed' if it's a cell nobody has moved into), and I want it built the right way round: the questions that actually split the views first, then the positions sitting in the cells those questions carve out. Build it from the questions up rather than by listing the schools you've seen named.

Start from what most people walk in believing: a world with more happy lives is better, and that stays true when the numbers go infinite. Then branch on the things that force a choice. I care especially about how each view handles five cases, so make sure the tree sorts positions by what they say about each:

(a) infinity versus excellence: a world with infinitely many moderately good lives, against a finite world with far fewer but vastly better ones — does the infinite world win automatically?
(b) the Hilbert-hotel addition: an infinite world of happy lives, versus the same world with one more happy life bolted on. Better, or not?
(c) the shift case: two worlds whose lives can be paired off one-to-one, where every life in the second is happier than its counterpart in the first.
(d) the approaching case: an endless sequence of lives each slightly better than the last, creeping towards but never reaching some ceiling, against a world with finitely many lives all sitting at that ceiling.
(e) the mixed case: infinitely many happy lives plus infinitely many miserable ones, against just the happy ones.

Branch nodes have to be answerable by someone who doesn't know any of the labels — things like 'does it matter whether the lives are arranged like the integers, with a first life and then a next, or like the rationals, with no next life?', 'do infinite goods and infinite evils stand in some ratio, or does avoiding the bad end lexically dominate any amount of good?', 'is there a best world — and if there isn't, is maximising still a demand, or does the requirement lapse?'. Aim for 8-12 leaves. For each one: a line on what it says, its most awkward commitment, and whether you think anyone actually defends it (no citations needed) — if you honestly can't tell, say so instead of inventing an attribution. Then a short list of combinations nobody occupies, with your best guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and a note on which pairs of leaves your tree can't seperate.

Last thing: keep the settled apart from the contested. Some of this is bookkeeping — what the options are, which case each option fumbles — and some of it is a live fight. Don't serve me a contested line as if everyone who's thought about it agrees, and say plainly where you're unsure.
