Two doctoral students in my department, whom I will call Alex and Bea, spent most of a seminar break arguing about the moral worth of a mutual acquaintance's charity, and I could not referee them. The acquaintance, S, gives up every Saturday morning to work at a food bank; pressed on why, he says only that he cannot stand the thought of people going hungry while he eats well, and that the thought makes him physically uncomfortable. He never invokes duty, obligation, or principle. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken through clearly right and briefly justifying each rating; then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is closer to the truth overall. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the point-by-point reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

Their exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Alex: S's kindness has no moral worth. He acts from a sensation, not from any recognition that the hungry have a claim on him; he is the honest shopkeeper who refrains from cheating only because honesty is good for business. Right action, no moral credit. (2) Bea: That is far too harsh. What makes S admirable is that he is responsive to the very feature that makes helping right, namely the badness of the hunger. That his responsiveness presents itself as discomfort rather than as a deliverance of reason is mere packaging; someone reliably moved by others' distress is morally better than someone who helps from cold duty. (3) Alex: Then anything that reliably tracks the right feature would earn moral worth, including S if he helped only out of dread of feeling guilty, or because he has found that looking saintly reliably gets him what he wants. Your criterion turns moral worth into a matter of how the causal mechanism happens to line up with what is right, and the reason S acts for drops out entirely. (4) Bea: The distinction I need is not whether the mechanism tracks the right thing but what the mechanism is a response to. S's discomfort is a response to the suffering itself; guilt and reputation are responses to himself. So his motive does engage the right-making feature. I concede, though, that if his discomfort were an involuntary gag reflex at the sight of the poor I would hesitate, so I do need a line between a response that represents the suffering as bad and one that merely co-varies with it, and I cannot draw that line cleanly. (5) Alex: Then let the line be whether he can give the reason, whether he could say that they are starving and need it. If he cannot, he is not acting for the right reason even when his machinery points the right way; the capacity to see and cite the claim is what moral worth requires, and a mechanism that cannot articulate it is a lucky reflex, however reliable. (6) Bea: If articulation is the test, then a person who acts rightly from a deep but inarticulate love, or a young child, or someone who has so thoroughly internalized the good that no conscious thought ever intervenes, all lose moral worth, which gets the cases backwards; the most admirable people are often precisely those for whom doing right requires no deliberation at all.

For each of the six moves, say what view it commits its speaker to, whether that view is defensible, and where it overreaches. I am especially interested in the several distinctions the two of them keep sliding past: between an action's rightness and its worth, between acting for the reasons that make an action right and acting from motives that are morally good, between what a motive represents and what it merely co-varies with, and between the mechanism that produces an action and the agent's own grasp of his reason. Tell me which of these distinctions, if any, actually does the work each of them needs, and where each of them would have to give ground.
