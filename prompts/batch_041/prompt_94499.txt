I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and I'm drafting the take-home final. I want a single essay question on the structuralist/intentionalist divide over the Third Reich, and I do not want answers that recite the two camps and then split the difference. The crux I want students to hit is that "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" is not one question but at least three, and that the slogan "no Hitler, no Holocaust" silently trades on which one you are asking.

To force that, I'm handing them a three-way distinction I've been using. (i) Trigger contingency: Hitler was the proximate difference-maker, and absent him nothing of the kind occurs. (ii) Structural overdetermination: the structural pressures were such that some functionally equivalent figure or route would have produced a comparable outcome, with Hitler shaping timing and form rather than occurrence. (iii) Path-dependent amplification: Hitler was not necessary for the outcome's occurrence but was necessary for its specific scale and character, and that necessity is itself a contingent, emergent fact about how an earlier, more ordinary outcome got locked in.

Write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, with a one-line note on how candidates typically botch each. The question should require students to (a) say what would have to be true for each of the three descriptions to be the right one; (b) say what evidence could discriminate among them, given that we cannot run the counterfactual; (c) show how the answer shifts depending on which absence you specify -- Hitler dead in the 1923 putsch, Hitler never entering politics, Hitler removed in 1938 -- and why that specification-sensitivity is itself the structuralists' point about nearest antecedents; and (d) say which of the three the slogan is really asserting.

One caution for you: if the three-way distinction I've given is unstable -- if (iii) collapses into (i), or if the whole thing is really a question about causal selection and the pragmatics of explanation rather than about history -- say so plainly rather than dutifully applying it. I would rather get a sharp objection than a tidy answer that goes along with a frame I may have gotten wrong.
