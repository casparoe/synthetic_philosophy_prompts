I am giving the toast at my sister's wedding in three weeks, and I want it built on a piece of decision theory that she and I have been arguing about for years.

The couple: my sister quit a secure job at twenty-nine to start a company that has since done well. Her fiancé is the most cautious person I know. He has kept his savings in insured deposits since his first paycheck, he turned down a chance to buy equity in the firm where he works because he could not stand the thought of losing the buy-in, and in 2019 he talked her out of a house they both loved because the mortgage would have left them with almost nothing if either of them lost a job. That last one is still a live wound, and everyone at the wedding knows about it. Her side of the family thinks his caution is a phase she will eventually cure. His parents, who are kind and worried, think she is going to talk him into something ruinous.

The room will be about eighty people. Two tables are her colleagues from a group that works on decision theory under uncertainty; they will catch it if the theory is misstated, and they will also wince if the toast turns into a lecture. Everyone else is family and old friends who have no interest in theory and came to cry and to laugh.

The idea I want the toast to run on, and it has to do structural work rather than decoration, is this. A rational agent need not maximize expected value. On a view sometimes called risk-weighted expected utility, the weight an agent gives to a possible outcome is not that outcome's probability but a transformed version of the chance of doing at least that well, with the result that the possibility of landing among the worst outcomes can count for more than its raw probability. Her beliefs about the odds and her ranking of outcomes stay fixed; what varies from person to person is how much weight the worst case is allowed to carry. Total caution is the limiting case: the person who will not accept any gamble that could leave her worse off, even one with a plainly higher expected value. The point of the view is that this is a coherent rational attitude rather than an arithmetic error, a failure of nerve, or a misperception of chances, so that two people facing the same odds with the same goals can both be fully rational and choose differently, and there may be no fact of the matter about which of them is right.

What the toast needs to do:

- Avoid every version of the thought that she will loosen him up, that he will steady her, that they balance each other out, or that love will settle it. Each of those treats one of their attitudes as a defect to be corrected, which the view denies, and the decision theorists at those two tables will hear it as a mistake.
- Make no claim that his caution has secretly paid off, or that her risks were vindicated because they worked. If the toast rests on the outcomes, it gives up the point, since the point is that the attitude does not need the outcomes to justify it.
- Say one genuinely useful thing about how they should handle this going forward. Neither of them can win the argument by appeal to the numbers, and a household still has to settle on a single posture toward risk; say something honest about what it takes to do that, rather than asserting that they will converge.
- Tell the story of the 2019 house, briefly and without taking a side, since it is the moment the room is waiting for.
- Put warmth first. Roughly five minutes, around seven hundred words, no technical vocabulary, and no names of theorists or books. It should land as affection for both of them rather than as a ruling from the bench.
- American English.
