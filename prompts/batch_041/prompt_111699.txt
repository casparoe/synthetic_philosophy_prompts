Last September I spent a week in a rented house with three friends, and on our final night the four of us had dinner at a restaurant in town. One of us, Lena, was over from a country where nobody tips and had never eaten out anywhere that tipping is expected. When the bill came she worked out her share to the cent, paid it, and left nothing on the table. Nobody said anything at the time. On the walk back, Ruth and Dan argued about it for the better part of twenty minutes, and I have been turning the question over ever since without getting anywhere.

Dan's case, as fairly as I can put it: Lena did nothing wrong, because a custom can only bind someone who has taken it on. You cannot come to owe something by being unaware that anyone expected it, and she never agreed to anything. The menu named a price and she paid that price. If the people serving us are underpaid, that is a matter between them, their employer and the law, and a visitor should not be conscripted into repairing a wage system she had no part in building. He also raised a point that had not occurred to me: a payment that is expected but appears nowhere in writing is a hidden charge, and hidden charges make a practice unfair rather than binding.

Ruth's case: in a place where tipping is expected, the tip is not a gift but part of what the meal costs, and the server worked all evening on that understanding. Lena took the benefit of the arrangement and then declined to pay her share of it, so somebody's labour went underpaid and she is the one who kept the difference. Ruth put it in a way I found interesting — she said Lena did something wrong without being to blame for it, because she could not have known; the wrong and the fault are two separate things, and Dan keeps running them together. She added that on Dan's reasoning no visitor could ever be bound by anything local, from the way you queue to the shoes you take off at the door, which cannot be right, since some of these customs cost particular people real money while others merely disappoint an expectation.

Dan answered that if a custom only binds when someone is counting on it, then the real test is who pays for the breach rather than whether the practice is a custom, and he used the example of a queue to show the test fails. In a bar with no formal line, everyone waiting has given up the same small advantage of pushing to the front; a visitor who does push in and gets served first has not cost anyone a penny, yet Ruth would still call it a wrong. So cost is doing no work, he said, since any irritation is a cost. The difference has to be whether the person waiting was entitled to count on the rule, and nobody is entitled to count on a rule that was never stated.

Ruth's reply was that it was stated — not in writing, but in the only way most things in a restaurant are stated, by everyone else doing it, and Lena had watched the rest of us put money down. Ignorance that a person could have cured in ten seconds is not the same as ignorance that could not be cured, she said, and Dan keeps treating the two as one. And the claim that she paid the price on the menu simply assumes that the price on the menu is the whole of what she owed, which is the very thing in dispute.

I would like a turn-by-turn assessment rather than a verdict on who was more likeable: which of Dan's claims hold and which do not, the same for Ruth's, whether her wrong-without-blame distinction really does the work she wants it to, and what both of them are leaving out.
