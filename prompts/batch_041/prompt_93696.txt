I'm taking an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice and social choice theory, eighteen students, and I've been given one 90-minute session to run on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I would rather not stand up and assert the result while everyone nods; I want an activity in which the class runs into the theorem's shape themselves and only afterwards learns what it was they were doing.

As I understand it, the theorem says that if each individual's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, the group's preferences do too, and the group is indifferent between two lotteries whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the group's ranking must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, with a weight for each person reflecting their contribution to social welfare and an additive constant that changes nothing about the ordering. The part I want the class to feel is where those weights come from, and why the theorem does not by itself hand you equal weights.

Please design the session around two rounds that differ only in what participants know about their own odds: in one, a genuine random draw gives every participant an equal, known chance of occupying any of the payoff positions; in the other, the assignment is made but the odds are unknown or unavailable to them. The expectation is that the first round drifts toward a sum of utilities and the second toward protecting the worst position, and the debrief should show that the axioms that produced the first do not license the second, and what the first is quietly assuming.

Three constraints on the mechanics. The individual payoff tables should genuinely differ across participants, in ordering and in risk attitude, so that no majority over a common ranking can stand in for the aggregation. Each participant's numbers should be stated in their own private units, their own best outcome 100 and their own worst 0, so that adding them up requires an interpersonal scale the tables do not supply. And one payoff position should belong to someone not in the room who cannot speak for themselves, so the group must decide whether and with what weight to count them.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme (tokens or snacks, no money), a 90-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto a specific piece of the theorem, including what has to be added to the axioms to get equal weights and what the maximin choice in the second round would require. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, how to handle it, and which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
