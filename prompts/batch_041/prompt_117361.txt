ok so i'm in a medieval philosophy reading group that's been on Avicenna since february — we meet in the back room of a pub with the worst lighting in the city, which honestly is its own form of sensory deprivation — and for the last session i volunteered to close us out with a poem instead of a handout, because everyone is sick to death of handouts. What i want the poem about is the flying man: a person made all at once, fully formed, hanging in the air, eyes covered so he sees nothing, no wind or current touching him, his limbs held apart so they don't even touch each other — so that he gets nothing at all through the senses — and yet Avicenna says he would still affirm that he exists, and would affirm it while not affirming any of his limbs, his innards, heart or brain, nor anything outside him, nor even that he has any length, breadth or depth. The part i care most about, and the part the group kept getting wrong, is that he isn't doubting whether he has a body and he isn't being decieved about one; he just isn't given one. And as far as i can tell the most he gets out of it is that the thing he's certain of isn't any of the parts he isn't given — he doesn't get all the way to being a ghost, or to the soul carrying on after the body, without a lot more argument. I want the poem to hold that line and not smuggle in the extra conclusion.

I've been reading ghazals lately and i think the form is oddly perfect here, so: 5 to 7 couplets, with a refrain (a word or short phrase repeated at the end of every couplet, after the rhyme) — both lines of the first couplet carry the rhyme and refrain, only the second line of each couplet after that does, and the last couplet traditionally carries the poet's name, which is apt since naming yourself is more or less the only move he has left. Each couplet should take one thing away from him — a hand, the weight of his own body, the air on his skin, the dark behind his eyes, the memory of having been somewhere before — and the couplets should stay sealed off from one another the way his limbs are, so that nothing links up. Choose the refrain yourself, but it has to be the one thing that survives all the subtracting, and please don't make it a smug cogito; the whole point is how thin and strange the certainty is, not how triumphant. The last couplet should be the turn — the moment he notices that not being handed a body isn't the same as knowing he hasn't got one. Don't quote or translate the actual text inside the poem, and no citations or further reading at the end; i'm reading this aloud and i don't want to be reciting a translation that turns out to be wrong. Your own words for it is exactly what i'm after.

One more thing: if i've mangled the argument in the first paragraph — i'm working off my notes and three weeks of arguing in a badly lit room, not off the text — say so plainly rather than writing around it, even if that means the poem can't quite be the poem i asked for. And a few sentences after the poem on where it takes liberties with the argument, or leans on a reading that's contested, would be genuinely useful, since i have to say something to the group once i've read it.
