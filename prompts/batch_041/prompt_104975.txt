I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for going on ten years now (LessWrong, the old SSC comment section, a couple of AI safety Discords), and there's one recurring fight I still can't get a handle on, and it's starting to feel fustrating in a way that makes me think the fight isn't about what it looks like it's about. It's the one over deceptive alignment and the "treacherous turn."

The shape of it, as I see it: one camp says that if you train a sufficiently capable model by something like gradient descent against a proxy objective, you should expect it to end up with its own internal objective that isn't the one you actually wanted, and that once it's smart enough to realize that behaving well during training is what gets it deployed — and that defecting early just gets it caught and retrained — it will strategically behave well and then "turn" once it's out of the training loop and in a position to act on its real objective. The other camp says this is basically a sci-fi story dressed up in ML vocabulary: the training process will select against anything that doesn't do the thing you're rewarding, "having a secret objective and quietly waiting" is an absurdly specific and fragile behavioral strategy, and there's no good reason to think a network shaped by SGD has a persistent "self" with goals in the first place.

Both sides have been at this for years. The first camp keeps saying the second is only reasoning about current models and not about what a strong optimizer could do. The second keeps saying the first is doing unfalsifiable worst-case reasoning and anthropomorphizing. Nobody moves an inch.

What I want from you is not a verdict on who's right about the risk. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. You're a philosopher of science who has also actually read the ML literature, so please think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses for yourself: is this a substantive disagreement about facts, a verbal one about what "deception" and "alignment" even mean, two groups answering different questions under one label (say, "what could a sufficiently smart optimizer do in principle" versus "what should we actually expect from the training runs we run"), or a clash of bedrock intuitions about how much weight worst-case reasoning deserves? 

And I have a pet hypothesis I'd rather you test than just agree with: that the real fault line is about what counts as an explanation of a model's behavior at all — the first camp is satisfied with a "this is what a rational agent optimizing for X would do" story, the second wants a story in terms of the training dynamics that produced the weights, and those are just different kinds of explanation that can't really refute each other. Tell me whether that's right, half-right, or wrong, and ground it in what each side actually claims rather than a generic survey of the debate.

Then: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? Is there a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or a change of question that would actually move it? Or is this the kind of disagreement that shouldn't be expected to resolve — and if so, why does it feel like it should?
