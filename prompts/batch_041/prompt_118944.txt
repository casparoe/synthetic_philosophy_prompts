I am an undergraduate currently taking a module in the philosophy of the social sciences, and our recent seminar on path dependence ended with a disagreement that I should like to think through more carefully.

The disagreement concerned what we are actually claiming when we say that an outcome is locked in. One reading treats the claim as one about counterfactual robustness: the arrangement persists not because it is superior to the alternatives but because the returns to switching have accumulated, so that no feasible sequence of choices by the relevant agents would have produced anything else. A weaker reading says only that the outcome was contingent in its origin but not in its destination, in the sense that a different path would have arrived at the same place, merely later and by different means. Our lecturer suggested that a good deal of the heat in debates about competing technological standards, about institutional divergence between otherwise similar economies, and about the inevitability of particular industrial or political outcomes comes from running these two readings together.

I should like you to set out the most important implications of keeping them apart: what follows for the inference from the survival of an arrangement to its efficiency or to its having been selected for, what follows for the conclusions we may draw from cases in which two societies that began from different positions nonetheless arrive at the same destination, and what follows for the practical judgement of whether an apparent lock-in is ever worth attempting to escape.
