I want to examine a position about money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I want you to make the case against it.

The position is this. Exploitability by a money pump is never, by itself, evidence that an agent's preferences are irrational. What a money pump demonstrates, at most, is that a certain package is irrational: a set of preferences together with a disposition to accept each of a sequence of trades that, jointly, leave the agent worse off. Remove the trade dispositions and nothing criticizable remains. An agent whose momentary preferences happen to run in a cycle, but who simply declines to be led around that cycle, is not thereby irrational, and she violates no diachronic norm. On this view, the standard money-pump argument for acyclicity smuggles in a premise it never defends — that the agent must accept every trade that is individually an improvement — and once that premise is exposed, the conclusion shrinks from a claim about preferences to a claim about preferences conjoined with a certain naivety about sequences. That is a much weaker and much less interesting thesis.

What I want from you is the argumentative map of this position's exposure.

First, what would have to be true for the position to be false, and which of those conditions actually obtain? I am especially interested in anything that makes exploitability look intrinsic to the preferences rather than parasitic on the agent's trade dispositions.

Second, consider whether there are pumps that do not proceed by a sequence of pairwise trades at all — cases where a single choice among a fixed set of options already exposes the agent. If such cases can be constructed, does my position survive them, or must it retreat to redescribing them as being about choice rather than preference? Tell me how much of the position is left after any such retreat, and whether the retreat is principled or ad hoc.

Third, separate the defeaters that a defender of the position would herself concede from those she would dismiss as changing the subject. My suspicion is that she will say any case in which the agent cannot help trading is really a case about the agent's psychology rather than her preferences, and that this is not a dodge. Is she right, or is that move precisely what insulates the position from refutation? I would also like to know how the position handles the contrast between synchronic constraints on preference and diachronic constraints on choice, and whether exploitability is best classified as a defect of the former or the latter.

Fourth, what does the position predict that its rivals do not, and does it risk anything at all? If the honest answer is that no conceivable case would ever make its holder say the preferences themselves were the problem, tell me whether that is a defect of the position or a defensible consequence of a correct distinction.

Finally, rank the considerations by how decisive they are, from fatal to merely awkward. I would personally be moved most by a case in which the agent is fully informed, foresees the entire sequence, holds no false beliefs, and still cannot avoid being made worse off without violating some norm she independently accepts. Tell me whether such a case can be constructed, and if it can, exactly what it establishes.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the structure of the argument. Where you lean on a well-known puzzle or example, set it out in enough detail that it stands on its own.
