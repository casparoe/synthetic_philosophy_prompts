For the better part of thirty-five years I worked as a field geologist, and since retiring I have been working through the history of early modern philosophy with a small reading group. Next month we are hosting, for an evening question-and-answer session, a philosopher who defends Berkeley's idealism and, in particular, the argument that Berkeley took to establish it decisively — the one commentators nowadays call the master argument, which turns on the claim that we cannot conceive of a sensible object existing unconceived. I have been asked to prepare the questions, and I want them to press on the actual mechanics of the position rather than invite a summary of it. Could you draft eight to ten questions along these lines, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the defender is most likely to give? Please keep the tone collegial but probing, and do not spend any of the questions on biography or on how he came to the view.

I would like at least one question on the equivocation charge — that the argument slides between the trivially true claim that one cannot conceive of an object while not conceiving of it and the contested claim that one cannot conceive of an object as existing without being conceived — and I want that question to press on which premise the defender actually needs and on whether the argument, so reconstructed, merely begs the question against the materialist. I would also like a question on whether the argument proves too much: if it succeeds, does it not equally show that we cannot conceive of an unthought thought or an unfelt pain, and if so, is that a reductio of the argument or a consequence the idealist can accept? Include a question on the move from the impossibility of unconceived sensible objects to the need for a divine mind to sustain them when no finite mind perceives them, and press on whether that move is required by the argument itself or is an independent theological supplement. Finally, I want one question on whether idealism can give an adequate account of the difference between veridical perception and hallucination, and one on how the view should answer the scientific image, in which physics appears to describe entities that exist quite apart from any mind that perceives them. Feel free to invent a name and a few identifying details for the defender, since I would rather the questions be addressed to a definite interlocutor than to an abstraction.
