I'm teaching a grad seminar on probability and epistemology this term, and we've just spent three weeks on the principle of indifference, and honestly the literature on it feels like a room nobody has opened a window in for a century. Bertrand's chord problem is the obvious exhibit: fix the chord by picking two random points on the circumference and you get 1/3, by picking a random distance from the centre you get 1/2, by picking a random midpoint uniformly over the disk you get 1/4 — same circle, same question, three answers. And every generation since has either declared the principle dead on the strength of it or produced some invariance argument meant to show the paradox is an artefact of bad parameterisation (the well-posedness / max-entropy line, which I associate with Jaynes but I'm vague on the details). My students keep asking me who's right and I keep telling them the more interesting question is why the fight never ends, so I'd like you to actually answer that for me rather than adjudicate the cases.

Here's what I've noticed, and I want you to tell me whether it's the real fault line or a red herring. Take the cube-factory case: all you know is that a cube's side is somewhere between 1 and 2 cm, and you're indifferent over which. Be indifferent over side length and you'll say the probability it's under 1.5 cm is a half; be indifferent over volume and you'll say something nearer a third. Unlike the chord case there's no rotation or scale symmetry lying around to break the tie — the parameterisation just *is* the question. So my suspicion is that the stall isn't a disagreement about the mathematics at all. One camp treats indifference as a rule for extracting a unique rational credence from a state of ignorance, and for them Bertrand is a reductio. The other camp treats a prior as something you bring to a problem and defend on grounds other than the evidence forcing it, and for them Bertrand shows only that you should be explicit about your model — not a paradox, not even really an objection. If that's right, a lot of the apparent disagreement is two camps answering different questions under one heading, and the invariance defence that's supposed to rescue the first camp only works by quietly importing a modelling decision the first camp can't license. But maybe I'm wrong: maybe it's a genuine technical dispute a theorem could settle, or a disagreement about what ignorance even means, such that there's no fact of the matter to be had.

So: diagnose it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or a symptom that the question is ill-posed — argued from what the positions actually commit you to, not a survey of who said what. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: is there a formal result, a distinction, or a change of question that would move it, or should I stop waiting for the literature to converge and just tell my students that the impasse is the lesson? I care more about the diagnosis than the verdict, and if you think my hypothesis is wrong I'd rather you say so and explain why than split the difference.
