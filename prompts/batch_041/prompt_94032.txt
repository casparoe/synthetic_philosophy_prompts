I run a 90-minute session for 24 graduate students in philosophy and public policy, as part of a seminar on collective decision-making. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the unilateralist's curse from the inside — not the familiar warning about groupthink or overconfidence, but the structural point that when many agents are each free to undertake the same action on their own authority, the fact that the action is being undertaken stops carrying much information about whether it is a good idea, so that a venture launched by whoever moves first is appreciably more likely to be a bad one than the same venture launched by a single decision-maker with equally good evidence. The whole force of it depends on nobody behaving badly: no participant may do anything they could not defend as a sensible use of what they privately know, and the failure has to emerge from the arrangement rather than from anyone's carelessness or innumeracy.

Please satisfy these constraints:

- Build the game around private, noisy evidence about a concealed state of the world, with any single person able to commit the whole group, and give me the actual numbers. Calibrate them so that the effect is visible within one session, and say how to rescale for a group of 8 or of 60.
- Include two or three conditions that differ only in how many people could be the first to act, so that the shift with group size is something the participants observe rather than something I assert.
- Include a second round in which the group may pre-commit, before anyone sees their evidence, to a rule: a supermajority or k-independent-signals threshold, a designated decider, mandatory public pooling of evidence, or a cheap reversible trial first. At least one of these must fail in an instructive way, and the debrief must explain why it failed structurally rather than morally.
- Participants should not hear the name of the phenomenon, or the argument behind it, until the debrief. The debrief must tie particular moments of the game to particular parts of the structure, and must separate the curse from the diffusion-of-responsibility problem and from plain group overconfidence, which are different diagnoses of the same behaviour.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with exactly these fields, and nothing outside the JSON:

- "title": a short name for the activity.
- "learning_goal": one sentence, in the students' terms, stating what they should see before the term is named.
- "setting": group size, room and seating, time budget, and what I must prepare in advance.
- "hidden_state_and_signals": how the true state of the world is set and concealed, and how each participant's private evidence is generated, with the actual numbers (base rate, signal accuracy, stakes).
- "calibration": why these numbers make the effect visible at this group size within the time available, and how to adjust them for 8 and for 60.
- "rules": the rules as I would read them aloud, in order, as an array of strings.
- "payoffs": the scoring scheme, involving no real money, and what counts as doing well.
- "timeline": an array of objects, each with "minutes", "phase", and "what_happens".
- "conditions": an array of objects, each with "condition", "who_can_act_first", and "what_should_happen".
- "remedy_round": the rules the group chooses among, how the round is run, and which rule is designed to fail.
- "debrief_map": an array of at least five objects, each with "moment" and "what_it_illustrates", including the moment at which the curse is distinguished from diffusion of responsibility and from group overconfidence.
- "reveal": how and when to introduce the name, and the single paragraph I should say when I do.
- "predictable_failures": an array of objects, each with "what_goes_wrong" and "how_to_handle_it".
- "what_the_game_cannot_reproduce": the feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot stage, and that I must therefore state rather than show.
