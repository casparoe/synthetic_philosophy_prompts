I'm giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop next month in memory of my old superviser, who died in March. She spent her whole career on one question — whether experience is a basic feature of the world, or whether it only shows up in complicated systems like us — and the room is going to be about thirty people. Maybe a dozen of them are her former students and colleagues who know the literature backwards and will notice immediately if I fudge anything. The rest are her husband, her two brothers, and two or three friends from her choir who are there for her and never really followed what she did. I've got eight minutes at the top of the day, and I'm definately worried about failing in both directions at once: doing a lecture at a memorial, or saying something so anodyne that the people who actually knew her work will wince.

Here's the thing I want the remarks to hang on, because it's the part of her view that made her a bit of a heretic among the panpsychists. She thought the interesting question was never whether there's experience down at the bottom, but whether it adds up. Her line was that if the little bits of experience don't genuinely combine into a single subject, then panpsychism doesn't give you a bigger moral universe, it gives you a much stranger one — a universe full of subjects that are perfectly real but scattered, none of them anybody in particular. So for her the combination problem wasn't a technicality to be cleared up later by somebody smarter; it was the entire ethical ballgame, because whether there's anyone there to be wronged, or to suffer, depends on it. And she thought most people on both sides of the debate were quietly assuming an answer to it so that their ethics would come out the way they wanted. Either way, the usual move at a memorial — she's gone somewhere, or she's still somehow here with us — is blocked, and whatever I say has to work without it.

What I need from you: those eight minutes, so about a thousand words of spoken text. Three things have to happen at once. Say something honest about her view. Give the non-philosophers something they can actually hold on to — her older brother asked me last week, only half joking, whether this means the universe is in pain, and I'd like to be able to answer that in plain words without either laughing it off or terrifying him. And don't oversell the consolation. The obvious comforts — she's at peace now, she's still with us somehow — are exactly the ones she'd have called wishful thinking, and I don't want to stand up there saying things she'd have rolled her eyes at. But I also don't want eight minutes of 'and so, in a sense, nothing has happened', because that isn't true either and it isn't kind.

Two more constraints. No 'she lives on in her work' stuff. And don't let it turn into an argument with the people in the room who think she was wrong about the combination business — some of them are flying in for this and I'd rather the fight happened after lunch. At the end, tell me which single sentence is the one I should land on, and why it works for both halves of the room.
