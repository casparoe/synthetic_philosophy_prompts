I run a monthly discussion night in the back room of a bar near me — about 14 regulars, ages 19 to 70, a couple of retired teachers, two nurses, my neighbor who does HVAC, nobody with any philosophy training, and me, I dropped out of community college but I read alot. The last few months we keep circling back to AI and who it's actually supposed to be for, and honestly it's getting circular — everyone agrees it should "reflect our values" or "stay neutral" and then we just trade opinions for two hours. I want to do something hands-on for the next one. I've got two hours with a 10-minute break, a long table, a whiteboard, and a printer. What I want people to walk out feeling, in their gut and not just as a slogan, is that when somebody says the AI should serve our values or just be neutral, there's a second question hiding inside that sentence that most of us have never had to actually answer out loud: whose values, combined how, and chosen by who. I want them to run into that wall with their own hands before I ever say a word about it.

So here's what I'm asking you to design. Set it up so the group plays a values council for a made-up AI that's about to be put in charge of something small but real-feeling — say it's going to run the "conflict desk" for a shared apartment building, deciding noise complaints, who gets the shared laundry slot, guest rules, that kind of thing. First everyone should write down, privately, their own top few rules for how it should treat people — from their own gut, no discussion. Then the group has to fuse all those private lists into one single spec the AI will follow, and I want the fusing rule to be something they pick themselves and have to state out loud (majority vote, average, whoever's most affected decides, protect the worst-off, whatever they land on — don't hand them a menu, make them invent it). Then the kicker: I feed the finished spec three prepared cases I've written on index cards, the spec grinds out an answer to each, and at least one of those answers should be something the room viscerally hates — and it should be traceable straight back to the fusing rule they themselves chose two minutes earlier, not to anybody's individual values. If you can work it in, have them swap the fusing rule and re-run the same cases so they watch the answer flip even though nobody changed their personal values. Give me the whole thing runnable: exact rules, roles if any, what's on the cards, materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours including the break, and the wording I should use at the moments where I steer without lecturing. No real money — the stakes are just that the spec gets used to judge these cases in front of everyone and somebody ends up on the losing end.

Then I need the debrief, and I want it to be honest rather than tidy. Map each beat of the activity back onto whatever the actual underlying issue is (incommensurable values, the fact that any combining rule quietly puts a thumb on the scale, why "just be neutral" turns out to be another value choice in disguise, and who ends up holding the pen when the rule gets picked), but keep the standard names and terms out of my mouth until the very end — I don't want people feeling like they just watched a lecture. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: I can already guess somebody will say "just let the AI figure it out" and try to hand the problem back, somebody will steamroll, and the group will probably try to write deliberately vague rules so nobody loses — tell me how to handle each without killing the mood. And be straight with me about what this activity can't reproduce, because I don't want to oversell it: the people who'd actually be affected by a real system aren't in this room, our made-up value cards are way too easy to trade and compromise compared to real convictions, and two hours in a bar is not a deployment. I'd rather the debrief admit those limits than pretend we solved it.
