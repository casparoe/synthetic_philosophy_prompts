I belong to a small reading group that has spent the past few months on how we reason under uncertainty when we cannot run the full calculation — on heuristic arguments, and on the idea that where we have no information we should treat things as independent unless something tells us otherwise. The presumption of independence keeps coming up, and last week the phrase "there are no coincidences" was used to close an argument. The case under discussion was two groups working separately, with different methods and different data, arriving at almost exactly the same estimate. One member of the group said that this agreement could not be a coincidence, and therefore that the two efforts were not really independent — something must be linking them, whether a shared cause, a common error, or a genuine feature of the world — and that we should accordingly trust the estimate more. I found myself agreeing with the conclusion and uneasy about the reasoning, and I suspect my uneasiness comes from the sentence doing several different jobs at once.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict on my friend: the distinct precise theses that "there are no coincidences" could be expressing, arranged from the ones that are true by definition or nearly so, through the ones that are substantive but defensible, to the ones that are simply false. For each, I would like to know what kind of claim it is — about the world and its causal structure, about a probability model we have chosen, about our attention and memory, or about what we are entitled to assume in the absence of information — and I would like a verdict on whether it is plausible on its own, as well as on whether it would actually license the inference my friend drew, namely that agreement between two apparently independent efforts is evidence that they are not independent, and so evidence for whatever they agree on.

I am especially interested in the readings that collide with the presumption of independence itself, since it looks to me as though the same default is being invoked on both sides of the argument: the default is what makes the agreement surprising, and the agreement is then offered as grounds for abandoning the default. I would like to understand whether that is a real tension or only an appearance of one, and, if it is real, what a more careful version of the heuristic would say. Finally, tell me which of the readings people who reach for this phrase probably have in mind when they use it to end a discussion, and whether that reading is among the defensible ones.
