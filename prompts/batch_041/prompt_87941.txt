I'm teaching a grad seminar on higher-order evidence this term and I keep having the same fight with my students. They've all done the reading, they know the concilliation/steadfastness material cold, and now whenever I put a case on the board someone says "well, you should just lower your confidence" and everyone nods. I don't think that's right in every case, and worse, I've started to suspect my own verdicts don't hang together. So here they are, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one, and I want you to tell me which of them to give up.

(a) Four of us split a bill, I did the division in my head at the table and got $31.75 each. I'd had two pints. I know from long experience that two pints makes me fumble mental arithmetic, and I can't check, because the receipt went in the bin and I don't remember the total. Verdict: I should not have believed $31.75 and I don't believe it now. I can't hang onto the belief while thinking it's unjustified — the belief just goes. (b) Same evening, except the second glass was a mocktail and I only learned that the next day. Verdict: the belief I formed at the table was still unjustified. I had every reason to think I was impaired; its not an excuse that I happened to be fine. That one bothers me, because there the higher-order evidence was false and my reasoning was untouched, and yet it still did something. (c) Sober, but I did the sum in ten seconds while the waiter hovered, and I know people fumble division under time pressure. Verdict: I don't budge, I believe $31.75. (d) I'm on a five-person panel reading the same forty pages; two of us want to accept, three want to reject, and I'm one of the accepts. I've read it twice and I can say exactly where I think the three are wrong — but they can say exactly where they think I'm wrong, and when I go looking for the flaw in their reading I can't find one. Verdict: I end up much closer to 50/50, probably suspending judgment. "I checked, so I'm the one who's right" doesn't seem available to me here, even though I did check. (e) I've just finished a paper and I think the central argument is sound — sound as in I'd bet the year on it. I also know that people who have just finished a paper systematically overrate it, I have no reason to think I'm the exception, and if I'm honest my confidence is probably that effect. Verdict: I keep the belief. I believe the argument is sound and I believe my believing it is unjustified, and I don't feel incoherent — I just shouldn't treat it as knowledge or stake anything on it. (f) My departmental rival, who I'm fairly confident would enjoy watching me embarrass myself, tells me I'm notorious for overrating my own arguments and that everyone says so. Verdict: it moves me a bit, less than the same remark from a friend would, but it's not nothing. His wanting me to be wrong doesn't make the claim false.

So (a) and (e) look like the same shape to me — higher-order evidence that my own belief-forming is compromised — and I end up in different places: in (a) the belief goes, in (e) it stays and only the justification goes. A student suggested the difference is that in (a) the evidence is about whether I'm competent and in (e) it's only about whether I'm well calibrated: the first kills the belief, the second leaves it standing and takes away its ticket. That's tidy and I suspect it's wrong somewhere, maybe in (b), where the evidence was about my competence and turned out to be false. I assume (e) is just the akrasia case everyone writes about, but if it is, then (a) and (d) look like the same phenomenon too and I end up in three different places, which is what's actually bugging me. What I want: the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that buys each clause; then what it says about cases I haven't given you — the past-self one below, plus any case of your own that you think would embarrass it; then an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which you'd overturn to keep it simple. If the only principle that saves all six needs a pile of epicycles, say so, and say whether the epicycles are principled or ad hoc. And one case I deliberately left out, because I don't know what to say about it: I worked the same problem last month, carefully, and got a different answer, and I've still got the notebook page. Do I conciliate with my past self the way I conciliate with the panel, or does the fact that it's me on both ends change something?
