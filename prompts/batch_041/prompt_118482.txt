I have six verdicts about personal identity under gradual replacement, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. If my neurons are replaced one at a time over years by silicon units that reproduce each cell's inputs, outputs, and internal dynamics, I think I survive; the resulting person is me.
2. If the same silicon brain is produced in one step — scanned, built, and the biological original destroyed painlessly in the process — I think the original dies, and the resulting person, though he remembers being me, is not me.
3. If the scan yields two such silicon brains at once, I think neither is me; each is a new person who wrongly believes he is.
4. I think I survive dreamless sleep and general anesthesia, during which I have no experiences and my mental life is, so far as I can tell, suspended.
5. If a surgeon replaces exactly half my neurons with the silicon units and leaves the other half, I think the result is still me, however unsettled.
6. I think I would survive being frozen now and revived in a century, provided the same brain is thawed and made to work again.

Cases 1 and 2 seem to make survival depend on how the change is brought about rather than on what exists afterward, yet cases 4 and 6 seem to let survival bridge gaps in mental life, which is likewise a matter of process. I cannot tell whether these verdicts answer to one principle or to two that cannot both be true. Please state the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that comes closest; say what it implies for a case I have not listed, namely a replacement in which the old neurons are not destroyed but kept alive and firing in a vat, wired to the silicon units so that the two systems run in parallel; and tell me plainly which of my six verdicts it forces me to abandon.
