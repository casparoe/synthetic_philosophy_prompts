I am a novelist at work on a collection of stories about cognition, technology, and the law, and I have been reading philosophy of mind for a new book. I keep returning to the extended mind thesis: the claim that some mental states and processes are partly constituted by artifacts and environments outside the skull, so that the boundary of the mind is fixed by functional integration rather than by anatomy. I want a short story that does not merely mention this idea or use it as a metaphor, but stages it as a live practical and metaphysical problem.

Please set the story in a plausible near-future city where a court, an insurer, or a licensing board has begun to treat certain cognitive prostheses as legally part of the person who uses them. Your protagonist might be a woman whose late husband's voice, stored in a shared domestic memory system, has become the external scaffold of her own practical reasoning; or a translator whose handheld lexicon is so tightly coupled to her thought that she cannot decide whether a sentence is hers or the device's. The plot should turn on a conflict that cannot be resolved by saying the artifact is just a tool: for example, a technician proposes to delete or update the artifact for good reasons, and the protagonist experiences the proposal as a threat to her agency, memory, or identity. I would like the story to explore the parity principle, understood as the idea that a process counts as cognitive if it would count as cognitive were it done inside the head, and the difference between an artifact that merely causes a thought and one that is partly constitutive of it. Let the unsettling possibility that a mind can be shared, edited, or owned emerge from scenes, dialogue, and consequences rather than from a character delivering a lecture. Avoid the well-worn upload, brain-in-a-vat, and evil-AI plots. The story should be roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, with a clear narrative arc and a final image that leaves the metaphysical question open rather than settling it.
