You are a philosopher of history and a consultant on the ethics of museums and memorials, advising institutional boards on how to handle contested memory. I chair the board of trustees of a regional heritage museum, and I need a written opinion I can circulate before our next meeting. Please think carefully and reason step by step rather than jumping to a recommendation. Begin by separating the distinct questions the board actually faces, since I suspect we have been running several together; then work through them; then give me the memo itself.

Background. A private foundation has offered us a large endowment, contingent on our mounting a permanent gallery on how societies confront their worst chapters. As proposed, the gallery would compare four cases side by side: postwar Germany's reckoning with the Nazi period, Japan's handling of wartime atrocities in Asia, Turkey's treatment of the Armenian genocide, and the American South's memory of slavery and Jim Crow. The donor's letter asks that the gallery "let visitors judge for themselves," and proposes a single template applied to every case -- denial, official acknowledgment, apology, commemoration, reparation -- so that visitors can see which societies have reached which stage. The donor also wants the gallery to travel to partner institutions abroad.

Two problems have surfaced.

First, our community advisory groups for the Armenian, Japanese, and Turkish cases, together with a historian of the American South on our programming committee, argue that the comparative frame is a category error: it implies the four cases are commensurable, and it treats an ongoing state policy of denial as merely one "stage" a society passes through rather than a live political choice being made now. One trustee dissents sharply. He says that refusing to compare is its own kind of dishonesty, that a gallery which cannot say that Germany has done better than Turkey is propaganda rather than history, and that our squeamishness is really fear of our donors and our consulates.

Second, a local history society has asked to contribute a section on a massacre of a minority community in our region in the 1920s. Some of the material they want displayed comes from amateur researchers who argue the death toll has been "wildly exaggerated" and that the episode was better described as mutual violence between two armed communities. A descendant organization calls this denialism and says we would be laundering it by giving it wall space. The history society replies that scholars must be free to revise received accounts, that the received account is itself a political artifact, and that a museum cannot set itself up as an arbiter of historical truth without becoming a censor.

The board votes at the end of the month, and the gift lapses if we do not accept by then. I would like your written opinion to do the following. Recommend a concrete course of action -- whether to accept, decline, or accept with conditions, and if conditions, exactly what they are -- in a form the board can vote on. Say how, if at all, the comparative frame can be salvaged, and whether the "stages" template should be kept, dropped, or replaced, with a reason a reasonable trustee on either side could accept. Give us operational criteria for distinguishing legitimate historical revision from denialism, stated so that a curator could actually apply them to a disputed label, a disputed number, or a disputed attribution of agency. Address the freedom-of-inquiry objection on its merits rather than dismissing it. And state plainly the strongest consideration against your own recommendation, so that the dissent is on the record.

Write it as a formal memo, addressed to the board, with a clear recommendation in the first paragraph and the reasoning after it. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I need an argument we can act on, not a bibliography.
