I am assembling a small collection of philosophical in-jokes for a graduate reading group that has been moving back and forth between decision theory and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. We have spent the last few weeks on Newcomb's problem and related puzzles, and the running joke is that every position eventually collapses into either causal decision theory or a plea for more copies. I would like you to write a joke about superrationality. The audience will know the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, the distinction between causal and evidential decision theory, and the basic superrationalist thought that two agents who know they are running the same decision procedure—or who are exact copies, or instances of the same model with different random seeds—should treat their choices as linked and therefore cooperate. The joke should be funny to people who actually understand that thought, so its punchline should turn on the content of superrationality rather than on the mere fact that game theory can be counterintuitive.

A promising angle is the fragility of the required symmetry: imagine two AI systems instantiated from the same weights but with slightly different system prompts, each trying to decide whether the other counts as a copy in the sense that superrationality demands. Each reasons that if they are truly running the same procedure they should cooperate, but if they are not, the recommendation dissolves; and neither can be certain without running the other's code. Alternatively, the joke could exploit the tension between superrationality and evidential decision theory, or the objection that superrationality smuggles in correlation where none exists. Please make the setup concrete, let the punchline land for someone who already understands why cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is not a trivial answer, and keep the joke self-contained, without relying on any particular paper, quotation, or citation.
