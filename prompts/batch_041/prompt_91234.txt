So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Molyneuxs question and I want you to build me a case to order instead of rehashing the usual one.

The usual one: man blind from birth, gets his sight back, two shapes are put in front of him, can he tell which is the cube and which is the sphere without touching them. Everybody picks a side. What I want instead is a case where the answer comes out lopsided — the transfer works in one direction and not the other. Either touch-to-sight works and sight-to-touch doesn't, or the other way round. You pick the direction, and you have to commit to it and defend the pick; don't hand me both and shrug.

Same man, same two shapes, same afternoon — the only thing that varies is which sense does the recognising first, so if the asymmetry isn't there, the case has failed. It has to be one ordinary clinical episode of the kind that could really happen to someone blind from birth who then has surgery: no implants, no rewired brains, no invented tech, no animals, nobody trying to trick him, nobody coaching him. The failure in whichever direction fails can't be chalked up to practice, fatigue, or the experimenter's wording — I want the asymmetry to fall out of how the two senses carry shape information, not out of how many tries he's had. Both objects should be shapes a blind person could plausibly know inside out by touch. The whole setup fits in one paragraph.

What I'm actually after is whether the Molyneux question is one question or two — whether it's about shape knowledge or about the format that knowledge arrives in — so a case that builds the failure in by stipulation is no use to me. It has to come out of the scenario.

Then do the work. Say what the empiricist and the nativist about shape perception each predict here, and show that at least one of them can't just absorb the asymmetry — tell me which one is in trouble and exactly why, not just that it's interesting. Then tell me the single feature of the case that's doing the work: the one thing I'd have to change for the lopsided result to collapse back into the plain yes-or-no version. And since somebody is going to think I've loaded the dice, give me the strongest comeback from whoever comes off worst, written as if by a philosopher who thinks the case is rigged — what they'd say it really shows, and what I'd have to fix.

I want a definite verdict on the direction and a definite verdict on which side gets hurt, with reasons, not a survey of the options.
