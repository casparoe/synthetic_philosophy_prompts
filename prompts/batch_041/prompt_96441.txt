I'm a first-year PhD student working mostly on early modern philosophy — Hume is my guy — and I keep having the same arguement with a friend of mine in the econ department, so I'd like an outside opinion from someone who knows Hume's epistemology and moral psychology inside out, including the tedious middle parts, not just the greatest hits. Here's what we fight about. Suppose a bunch of people each have a small piece of private evidence about something they can't check directly — say, whether the ice on the lake will hold — and each of them also gets to see what the others did before them. Everyone reasons perfectly sensibly: she went out, she's no fool, so it's probably fine. But after two or three people in a row have gone, the next person rationally stops using her own evidence and simply copies the crowd, and so does everyone after her. The result is that the public record — what actually got expressed — contains almost none of the private evidence people had, and the whole thing can flip the instant one person with a decent signal breaks ranks. My friend says this shows a crowd can be more rational than it looks, that nobody in the story makes a mistake, and that the failure is purely structural: the information is there, it just never makes it into the record. He also claims, with the confidence of someone who has never read a page of history of philosophy, that no one before the twentieth century could have anticipated any of this, because everyone thought crowds were simply irrational and panicky. That's the claim I want tested.

So: as a Hume specialist, what would Hume have made of this if you explained it to him in his own vocabulary? Please actually work it out rather than reaching for the lazy line that he'd call it all custom and habit and be done with it — break the case into its parts and say which parts his account of belief can absorb and which it can't. Relevant bits as I see them: belief for him is a lively idea produced by custom and the association of ideas; we catch opinions off one another by sympathy; he has the machinery of general rules, some of which generate prejudice and some of which correct it; and in his discussion of testimony (the miracles business) he's perfectly willing to weigh witnesses against each other by experience. Does any of that let him state the structural point at all, or does his psychology of belief make the very idea of information getting lost in a public record unintelligible? Would he think the individuals are blameless, or would he want to say the whole chain is a prejudice of the imagination that general rules ought to correct? And would he agree with my friend that the phenomenon is new to him?

There's a second thing I want, and it's the part I care about most. My friend has started calling the whole business the empty ledger problem, and his extension is about confidence rather than information: once the record is empty, each imitator treats the growing crowd as more evidence, so the evidence stops accumulating while the conviction keeps climbing. He thinks that amplification is the real puzzle, not the information loss. Tell me what Hume would say about that, given how he thinks belief gains force and how sentiments and opinions get communicated from mind to mind — is the amplification a mistake people are making, or just how belief works when it's shared? And does any moral or political moral follow for him, since he thinks governments rest on opinion rather than force; does a self-inflating, contentless opinion make him nervous, or does it just confirm something he already held? Before you commit to a verdict, spend a few sentences laying out which pieces of his apparatus you're going to use so I can see your working, flag clearly wherever you're extrapolating past what he actually says rather than reporting it, and don't bother with citations, I know the texts. Keep it concrete — I want his likely verdict on the lake example itself, not definately-generalities.
