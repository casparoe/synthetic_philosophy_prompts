I teach a 90-minute political ethics session to about 20 masters students in public policy, most of whom have never opened a political theory book. I want a role-play that makes them feel the dirty-hands problem in their gut rather than just hear me name it. The specific thing I want them to run into — and it should ambush them, not be announced — is that the hard part isn't picking the lesser evil (that's just a cost-benefit calculation and they'll do it easily), it's that they end up doing something they still believe was wrong even though they'd do it again, and then discovering that the wrongness can't be outsourced: nobody else can carry it, and if they walk away feeling clean that seems like a reason to distrust them. So the mechanics need to force both halves — the decision, and a reckoning afterwards that they can't get out of.

Give me the setup, the roles and any hidden information, the phases and rough timing, and some small concrete stake so the choice bites (no real money). I was thinking of a two-stage thing: a cabinet-type body decides under time pressure, then in stage two each person has to give a public justification of the decision and also privately record, on a sealed slip, whether what they did was wrong — and then you reveal the split. If you can think of a cleaner or nastier mechanism, use it. Include a debrief that maps specific moments onto the pieces of the idea (the act that's wrong but required, the residue, the fact that being untroubled is itself suspect), a candid list of what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it and how to rescue it, and an honest note on which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words.

At the end, have the response flag which bits of the dirty-hands idea are settled among people who actually work on it and which are genuinely contested (e.g. whether the act is truly wrong or just a lesser evil, whether guilt is required or merely expected), and don't pass off a contested reading as the consensus.
