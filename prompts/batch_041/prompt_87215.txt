I am a novelist at work on a long book about three generations of a family whose moral and political commitments shift across a century, and I need a classification instrument for the positions my characters might hold on moral progress. I would like you to design a decision tree, not an essay, that begins from concrete commitments and ends at named positions.

The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. Begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, note it and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, say so explicitly rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them.

I have been working with four informal dimensions and would like the tree to use them, or a refined version of them, as its primary axes:

- Circumference: who is inside the circle of moral concern. Possible answers range from family and tribe to nation, all humans, sentient animals, artificial minds, and ecosystems. A further question is whether expansion adds new members or replaces one set with another.
- Thickness: how much weight inclusion carries. Does a being inside the circle count equally, or does inclusion admit of degrees? Is the circle expanding in circumference while thinning in thickness, so that more beings count but each counts for less?
- Permeability: how new members enter. Is entry driven by reason, sentiment, political struggle, economic interest, imitation, or conquest? Is entry reversible? Can the circle contract, and if so, what does that say about the nature of the progress?
- Asymmetry: who draws the boundary. Is inclusion a gift from the powerful, a demand from the excluded, or a mutual construction? Does the center of the circle hold more weight than the periphery?

Please make each branch node a question that a character could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Do not ask whether the character is a utilitarian or a Kantian; ask what the character would say about a case. The tree should sort characters by what they say about cases, not by which label they prefer.

Use at least six of the following concrete cases, and invent others if they help:

- A character gives generously to an international famine relief charity but opposes a refugee resettlement in her own town.
- A character campaigns against factory farming but supports capital punishment.
- A character believes moral progress is real but holds that each generation must re-earn it and that it can be lost.
- A character believes the circle expands only when it serves the interests of those already inside.
- A character believes the circle is an illusion because moral concern is always bounded and parochial.
- A character believes the circle should be extended to artificial minds and that this extension is continuous with earlier ones.
- A character believes the image of an expanding circle is a Western imperial narrative that flattens other moral traditions.
- A character believes the circle should be thick at the center and thin at the edges, so that partiality is morally required.
- A character believes moral progress is not a circle but a spiral, with expansions followed by contractions.
- A character believes the circle should be replaced by a network or a web, not a circle at all.

The tree should end in eight to twelve leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of a position, or the word unnamed if no one defends it, and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Include at least these positions as leaves if the logical space permits: the optimistic expansionist, the tragic realist who sees progress as a pendulum, the power-skeptic who sees the circle as an ideological projection, the pluralist who denies that expansion is a single scale, the postcolonial critic who sees the circle as imperial, and the sentiocentric who extends it to all sentient beings.

Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific books or authors; the tree should stand on its own reasoning. The final product should be usable by a novelist who needs to keep six or seven characters from collapsing into the same position, so clarity about the diagnostic questions matters more than comprehensiveness.
