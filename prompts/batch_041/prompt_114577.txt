ok so I'm in this ad-hoc reading group that's been grinding through free-speech stuff, and we keep deadlocking in exactly the same place. This week the case was a local one: a small paper runs a story saying a restaurant owner underpaid his staff, a couple of his regular corporate clients quietly drop him, he says the paper harmed him, the paper says it only said true things and said them to the public. Within about ninety seconds someone pulled out the old fist-and-nose line — my right to swing my fist ends where your nose begins — everybody nodded sagely, and then we burned the rest of the hour talking past each other. I'm now fairly convinced the nodding is fake, that we're each hearing a different sentence in that one line, and that's why it keeps ending arguments it hasn't actually earned.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that line could be making: what kind of "right" is at stake, whose fist, whose nose, and what even counts as a nose here — bodily only, or reputational, financial, epistemic (one of us tried to say the harm was that readers now believe something false about the guy, and the rest of us couldn't tell if that was the slogan doing real work or just hand-waving), and what about harms that only show up in aggregate or years later. Then the parts I keep tripping on: is the sentence a claim about the limits of my liberty, or the limits of the state's power to coerce me (they come apart — a mob can swing at my nose too); does it hand you a necessary condition for justified restriction, a sufficient one, or both; and is it meant to govern only law, or also platform policy, employers, landlords, whoever. For each reading say whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false, and — the part I actually care about — which reading would really get people the conclusion they seem to want, namely that if nobody's nose is literally bleeding then nothing may be done.

And fold in one wrinkle we kept snagging on: in our case it's the paper's readers, not the paper, who dropped the contracts, so the owner's complaint is against a diffuse public rather than any identifiable nose. Does any reading of the slogan have the resources to even talk about that, or does it quietly presuppose one fist and one nose facing each other? I'd like to bring this list back to the group, so keep it as separate readings I can point at, not a smooth essay.
