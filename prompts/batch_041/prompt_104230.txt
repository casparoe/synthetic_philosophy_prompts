So I teach a grad seminar on practical rationality, and last week one of my students — a sharp kid, the kind who reads too much Parfit before bed — cornered me after class with an argument he'd worked up about the hitchhiker case, and it's been drivng me a bit mad ever since. You know the setup: I'm stranded in the desert, a driver offers me a lift but only on the condition that I pay him once we arrive, there's no way for him to enforce it, and I know perfectly well that the moment I'm safely at my destination I'll have no reason at all to hand over the money. The student's argument runs like this. (1) A rational agent, at the moment of acting, does whatever the situation she's actually in recommends. (2) At the drop-off, paying buys me nothing and costs me the money, so the rational move is to refuse. (3) Nobody can sincerely intend to do what they know they won't do, so a rational agent can't sincerely intend — or sincerely promise — to pay. (4) The driver, who's a decent judge of character, only gives rides to people who'll actually pay up. (5) So the rational agent stays in the desert and dies, while the irrational one who'd honour the debt gets home alive. (6) And no theory of rationality that sentences its own followers to death while rewarding their irrationality can possibly be right — hence (1) is false. He's convinced this is a knock-down refutation of the whole act-by-act expected-utility picture, and I honestly can't tell whether he's found a real crack in it or just smuggled something in somewhere.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a pep talk. Tell me which premise or step you'd reject, and please don't just wave at "dispositions" or "policies" without doing the work — I've heard that move a hundred times and I want to see it actually paid for. For each candidate you seriously consider, tell me why it looked so obviously true in the first place, and then what giving it up costs me elsewhere: does it unravel the standard account of instrumental rationality, can I still keep act-maximization for ordinary everyday cases, does it force me to say something strange about promises and about what an intention even is? If you think the argument is genuinely valid and the real question is only which plausible thing to sacrifice, then say so and rank the sacrifices from cheapest to dearest. If instead you think there's a hidden equivocation, or a slide between "the act of paying" and "the intention to pay," name it precisely and show me exactly where the two come apart. For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (2) — that refusing to pay is just what the money-maximizer does — but I'm willing to be told I can't have it. Lay it out as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just the way you'd explain it to a colleague over email; I read enough numbered lists on my students' handouts as it is.
