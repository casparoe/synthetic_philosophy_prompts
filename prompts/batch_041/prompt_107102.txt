I'm the program officer at a small county heritage trust — we hand out maybe fifteen little grants a year for plaques, small exhibits, a reenactment weekend, a walking-tour app, that kind of thing, and the board meets monthly. I keep noticing that our grant guidelines just say projects should "promote local history," which is useless, and so every meeting turns into the same fight between the people who want to celebrate the town's great men, the people who want to preserve every old barn exactly as it is, and the people (usually me) who want to use the past to say something uncomfortable about the present. I read Nietzsche's essay on the use and disadvantage of history for life years ago in college and it's been rattling around my head ever since, because his three ways of doing history — the monumental, the antiquarian, the critical — map onto those three factions almost exaclty, and he's also pretty sharp about how each one, when it takes over, starts to work against life instead of for it.

So here's what I want: a decision procedure my board can actually run on a proposal, written so that someone who's never read a word of Nietzsche can use it. Which of the three modes is this project really operating in (people will insist "it's just history," so the procedure needs questions that smoke out the actual mode)? Given the mode, what does the project need to do to serve life rather than smother it, and what's the characteristic failure mode to check for? What counts as a good-enough answer at each step, where does the procedure stop with a clean no, and where does it hand the decision back to a person because it's a question of values and not of fact? I want the questions in the order they should be asked, not a checklist of everything to consider.

Then run it on three proposals, and don't be gentle. First: a grant to restore and display the desk and papers of a local industrialist who founded the town's main employer but also broke a strike in the 1910s and was, by most accounts, a nasty piece of work. Second: a proposal to stabilize (not restore) a collapsing 1840s meeting house and open it a few days a year, on the argument that it's simply "the last one left." Third: a proposal from a high-school teacher to build a walking tour that starts at the town's pretty Civil War memorial and ends at the site of a lynching that's now a parking lot — I love this one, so I'm especially suspicious of my own judgment, and if your procedure says no, or says "not like that," tell me and don't soften it. Last thing: be honest about where the procedure can't decide anything and a human just has to, and about what it can't do at all.
