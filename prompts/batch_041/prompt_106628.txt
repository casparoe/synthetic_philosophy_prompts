Ok, I've drifted into a view about interpretability and I want you to try and shake it. The view: when interpretability research finds features, directions, or circuits inside a trained net -- a probe that reads off some concept, a sparse autoencoder that gives you human-legible features, a visualisation that looks like a dog detector -- we're not just building handy fictions for our own convenience. There's a fact of the matter about what the network's internal states represent, and interpretability is genuinely in the business of discovering it, fallibly, the way a physiologist discovers what an organ does. Call it realism about the representations we claim to find.

Here's the bit that makes me nervous, and that I want you to press on. Imagine a method that will hand you a perfectly good feature decomposition of any function you like, including a giant lookup table, and that decomposition predicts the system's behaviour flawlessly. Suppose it passes every test we'd normally call validation. Does that count as evidence that the system really represents those features, or have we just found a convenient way of re-desribing the behaviour? I cant find the principled line between "useful re-description" and "discovered representation", and I suspect that if there isn't one, my realism is in trouble.

So: what would count against the view? I want actual defeaters, not vibes. Things I'd find interesting:

- the two-labs case: same architecture, behaviourally indistinguishable, different training data, and the best methods recover feature sets that aren't merely differently labelled but structurally incompatible -- no translation manual between them. Does that tell against realism, or only against uniqueness? What's the difference, and which one should worry me?
- arguments that content-talk is doing no explanatory work here that prediction doesn't already do
- cases where a method is validated only by behavioural prediction, then shown to be arbitrarily sensitive to irrelevant choices (seed, hyperparams, prompt format)
- any case where the network demonstrably uses a different internal scheme than the one the method reports, and we only found out later

Then rank them, fatal to merely awkward. And tell me which of these a committed realist would themselves concede as counting against them, versus dismiss as not touching the view. I'm especially interested in whether the view has quietly been qualified into something like "representation relative to a chosen interpreter or task" -- and if so, whether that's a rescue or just unfalsifiability wearing a lab coat. Be honest if the answer is that it's well insulated; that's useful to me too.

What I'd personally find most persuasive, fwiw: a case where two equally predictive interpretability methods give incompatible content attributions and there's no principled way to say which is right.

Answer in British English please.
