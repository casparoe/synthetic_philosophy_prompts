Construct a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on individual responsibility for collective harms. The target is the inefficacy objection: the claim that when an individual's token action makes no difference to whether a collective harm occurs, that action cannot be wrong in virtue of contributing to the harm. The scenario must satisfy the following constraints. It must be realistic and contemporary, set in an ordinary workplace, market, or civic setting, with no trolleys, no science fiction, no perfect information, and no unusually influential or coordinated agents. The harm must be serious and collective, produced by many individually trivial actions, and it must not be one of the standard climate, meat, or voting examples; choose a less-discussed harm, such as the erosion of a professional norm, the degradation of a shared language, or the slow accumulation of a mundane pollutant. The individual whose action is at issue must be ordinary, must have no special role or obligation, and must not be able to change enough other people's behavior to matter. In the actual world, the harm occurs whether or not she acts; her token action is neither necessary nor sufficient, and no one will ever detect it. The case must contain two structurally similar sub-cases that differ in only one respect. In the first, her action is a redundant part of a set of contributions that already suffices for the harm. In the second, her action is a non-redundant part of a set that suffices only because of contributions like hers. The case must then generate a genuine disagreement between two standard moral theories, for example an actual-consequences act utilitarianism and a rule- or practice-based consequentialism, or a causal-contribution account and a complicity account, without stipulating either verdict in the description. The scenario itself should be no longer than one paragraph; the analysis may be longer. After presenting the scenario, do the following. (a) State precisely what each theory says about her action in each sub-case, and why. (b) Identify the single feature that makes the difference between the sub-cases, and say what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed. (c) Explain how a defender of the permissive verdict would attack the case, and whether that attack succeeds. (d) Say whether the case shows the inefficacy objection to be false, or merely shows that it needs a more precise formulation; if the latter, give that formulation.
