His position: oversight-by-debate is basically dead in the water. Dan's killer case is the obfuscated arguments problem: the dishonest debater strings together a long, individually plausible chain that lands on the wrong answer, and neither debater can point to which step is broken, so the honest one is stuck saying some step in there is wrong without being able to say which. Meanwhile the honest debater's own long argument looks the same to the judge, and nothing in the protocol separates the two cases. He leans on the human-judge experiments where exactly this pattern showed up on real physics problems, and on the fact that the whole guarantee seems to presume the task breaks down into steps a human can check one at a time — his line is that the interesting cases are precisely the ones that don't, or where the gap between what the judge can check and what the model can do is polynomially large, and then the checks you'd need become infeasible. Dan says all of that quietly assumes the honest debater knows where the right answer is and only needs to steer the judge to it, which is the very thing at issue.
