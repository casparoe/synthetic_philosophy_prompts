I'm on this ad hoc civic argument review panel at my university — four of us, fortnightly, too much coffee, and we rule on whether someone caught in a viral political scrap was arguing in good faith. We've ended up with a weird little case law and I'm no longer sure we're running a single consistent rule. Here's the track record. (1) A climate sceptic who read both sides, conceded specific points, but kept a heterodox conclusion: good faith. (2) A party-line partisan who sincerely believed what she said, refused to read anything from the other side, and called it all propaganda: not good faith. (3) A guy who argued cynically, didn't believe a word, but performed all the rituals of reason-giving: not good faith. (4) A woman in a very tight media bubble who reasoned sincerely from what she saw, said she'd change her mind if someone showed her evidence, but never sought out the other side and didn't think they were malicious, just wrong: we said good faith. (5) A left-wing activist who read the other side's best stuff, stated their arguments charitably, and thought they were wrong but not malicious: good faith. (6) A right-wing blogger who did the same on the other side: good faith.

Now the new case: someone in a filter bubble who is sincere, does seek out opposing views, will revise if you get through, but has a standing belief that the other side is arguing in bad faith — and that belief is itself sustained by the bubble. They're not cynical. They just interpret every counterargument as bad-faith spin. Do we say good faith? I want the prediction, the candidate rules our precedents are consistent with, what each rule predicts about the new case, your best guess and why, and the one further case that would settle it. My colleague keeps banging on about second-order good faith — being in good faith about whether your own information environment is trustworthy — and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just her being annoying. Don't give me a reading list; I just want the reasoning.
