I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I keep losing an argument about Y2K. The facts, as I understand them: through the late 1990s banks, utilities, airlines and governments spent enormous sums auditing and rewriting code that stored years in two digits; the millennium arrived; nothing major broke in the developed world. My colleague says that proves the scare was manufactured -- there was never a disaster to prevent. I say he's like a man who takes his heart medicine and concludes he never had heart disease.

Analyze the episode through two theories of causation I've read about but can't use properly. The counterfactual, or but-for, theory: c causes e if, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. The interventionist theory: a causal claim is a claim about what happens when a variable is set by an intervention that changes it and nothing else, and causes are the variables stable enough to be worth manipulating.

Say what each theory makes of "the remediation prevented a catastrophe," and whether it's true. Press on three things. First, whether an absence can be a cause at all, and whether one theory handles that better than the other. Second, the fragility problem: if the effect is "no major failure on the night of December 31, 1999," then my colleague's decision to go to a party looks like a but-for cause too -- is that a real defect or a bad description of the event? Third, whether the interventionist can run an intervention here at all, given that no one can un-fix the code, and that "the remediation" cannot be varied without also varying how seriously people took the threat, which is the very thing in dispute.

Then reverse the direction: what does Y2K show about the two theories -- does it favor one, embarrass both, or land outside what either was built for? Finish by telling me whether my colleague's claim is a causal claim at all, and what I should actually say to him.

Under 700 words, bottom line first.
