I'm hosting the first meeting of a small reading group on AI and the long term — six of us, mostly friends: a medieval historian, two software engineers, a woman who does tax law, and my flatmate who's a nurse. Nobody's read any Bostrom or anything in that literature. Next week I'm supposed to introduce the singleton hypothesis and I've realised I have no idea how to teach it, because every time I've tried it on someone they either go "oh, you mean one world government" and lose interest, or they assume it's just the plot of a movie about one evil superintelligent AI, and I don't think either of those is the actual idea.

So please don't explain the singleton to me — I roughly get it. Plan how I should explain it. What has to be on the table before the term makes any sense at all? I have a hunch the key move is that a singleton is about an outcome being stable and un-rivalable rather than merely very powerful, and that they need some notion of lock-in or equilibrium before they can hear the word as anything other than dictatorship, but you tell me what actually depends on what, and in what order. Then the example that carries the whole session: I was going to reach for the superintelligent-AI-takes-over story, but I suspect that's exactly the trap, because it does all the imaginative work for them in the wrong direction. I'd rather build it on something they half-know already — some domain where we sort of already live with a singleton, like a single global technical standard, a single reserve currency, one dominant language of science — and I want you to pick which one, and say what it has to have and what it must not have for the analogy to actually illuminate rather than just sound cool. They'll also definitely collapse a singleton into "a single mind" or "everyone agrees about everything"; tell me exactly where in the sequence to head that off and how, and whether there's a second, later misconception I'll only see once the first one is cleared.

Finish with: the objections they'll actually raise, in the order they'll raise them, and how the sequence should absorb each one rather than just field it; one question I can ask at the end that would show me whether they've got the structure of the idea and not just the vibes; and what I should openly announce I'm not going to cover at this level — I don't want to get dragged into forecasting arguments or the decision-theory rabbit hole in a forty-five minute session with a historian who wants to talk about the Habsburgs.
