Consider a piece of clinical and legal machinery that is common enough to be unremarkable: when someone arrives at an emergency department after a serious suicide attempt, most American jurisdictions let hospitals hold them involuntarily for psychiatric evaluation even when they show no psychosis, mania, or cognitive impairment, and even when they give a coherent, settled account of their reasons. A clinician's judgment that the wish to die reflects a treatable condition rather than a considered decision is generally enough to keep the person there. Their stated preference is treated less as testimony about their own good than as a symptom to be managed.

I want an account of what this practice presupposes. Some candidate commitments: that a desire for death is by itself evidence of disordered cognition, so that suicidality functions as a diagnostic sign rather than a possible conclusion; that a person's life is not solely theirs to dispose of but is held under some claim by the state, by dependents, or by future selves whose interests the present self cannot be trusted to represent; and that the self is disunified enough across time that present preferences may legitimately be overridden by a hypothesized later preference. Note that Hume, in his posthumously published essay Of Suicide, walks through the supposed duties to God, to society, and to oneself and finds that suicide violates none of them. The modern institution appears to reject his conclusion while having quietly abandoned the theological premise that did most of the work for his opponents, which makes the remaining structure worth examining.

Tell me which commitments the practice actually needs, which it merely fits comfortably with, and whether the ones it needs are mutually consistent. Then commit to a verdict: is the practice defensible on a broadly Humean secular picture, or does it require smuggling back something like a duty to society that a secular liberal state cannot honestly assert? If you think it is defensible, describe what a version of it would look like that does not treat the patient's stated reasons as noise. I have in mind something like a cooling-off contract, where the person is held briefly and offered treatment, but the justification for the hold is avowedly paternalistic and time-limited rather than diagnostic. Do not hand me a survey of positions; take one and defend it, and be explicit about the premise you would most hate to be wrong about.
