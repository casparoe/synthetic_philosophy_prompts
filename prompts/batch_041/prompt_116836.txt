Two colleagues in my department got into an argument after a seminar, and I ended up as referee. (1) Miriam said the historian's first duty is to judge people of the past by their own standards rather than ours: we should explain what an agent did in light of what she believed and valued, and explaining is the whole job, so assessing the past by present lights is a category error. (2) Dev said the phrase "their own standards" is doing illicit work, because past societies were internally contested — every era had people who objected to what the powerful were doing — so deferring to "the standards of the time" usually means deferring to the winners and calling it neutrality; worse, taking actors' own accounts at face value often just means adopting their ideology, since people describe what they are doing in the terms that justify it. (3) Miriam said that is a counsel of despair: if we cannot defer to their standards, the only alternative is to impose ours, and then history becomes moralizing. (4) Dev said the alternative is neither: describe what was done and what the actors thought of it, then separately say whether it was defensible; the mistake is pretending the second step is the first. (5) Miriam said that is itself a norm — the norm that one must not evaluate — and it is ours, not theirs, so Dev has not escaped presentism either. (6) Dev said description is already evaluative: whether you call something a massacre or a police action, a tax or a tribute, is a choice, and there is no neutral vocabulary waiting underneath, so the value-free description Miriam needs does not exist. (7) Miriam said that if every description embeds a judgment, then better and worse histories reduce to histories we happen to like, and Dev's position collapses into relativism.

Neither will move, and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it. Please work through the exchange move by move rather than picking a winner wholesale. For each numbered move, say whether it is mistaken, defensible, or correct, and what is right and what is wrong in it. Then identify the question the two of them are failing to separate, and give an overall verdict on who is more nearly right and about which specific claims.

Structure the response as follows: "The real question" — about 100 words naming the distinct issues the exchange runs together. "Move by move" — the main body, a short paragraph on each numbered move, saying whether it stands. "What both are missing" — about 150 words. "Verdict" — about 100 words, saying who is more right overall and on which specific points.
