Write a short story of roughly 1,200 words built around the following idea: the qualities we seem to encounter when we introspect — the hurtfulness of a pain, the redness of a tomato considered as a feature of the seeing rather than of the tomato, the sheer felt presence of a thought — are not properties of our brain states at all, but properties those states represent themselves as having. The view (sometimes called illusionism) does not deny that we see, hurt, and attend; it denies that these states possess the intrinsic felt character they advertise. It also owes an account of why the advertisement is so convincing and so inescapable, given that the system producing it cannot help itself.

Set the story in the debriefing room of a clinic where people come to have their introspective reports compared against a model of their own brains, and give the story to the technician who delivers the results. One patient's readout shows that nothing in her brain corresponds to the felt quality she has described all her life: the states are there, doing their regulatory work, but the quality is something those states merely claim. The technician has a script for this conversation, and the script is supposed to do two things at once — explain why the illusion exists and why it persists, and leave the patient with something she can live with. The story is about what the script can and cannot do.

Two constraints. First, no character may lecture about the theory: the position should show up only in what it does to these two people, in the room, the forms, the phrasing of the script, and in the specific ways each of them resists or accepts. Second, do not resolve it with a reversal — no final twinge that proves the theory wrong, and no tidy consolation that the discovery is liberating. If the technician's confidence shifts, it should shift because of something the view itself commits him to. Give the story a title.
