At a two-day workshop I was covering on the science and ethics of invertebrate welfare — the kind of meeting where comparative neurobiologists and philosophers share a coffee urn and a mutual suspicion — two of the invited speakers, a biologist I'll call Ines and a philosopher I'll call Rafael, spent most of a dinner rearguing a dispute from the afternoon panel. Ines holds that the question of which creatures count morally is a matter of degree all the way down. Nervous systems, she points out, form a continuum from a few hundred integrative neurons to a hundred billion; nociceptive machinery, central integration, and the capacity to trade off competing goals all come in gradations; and she concludes that moral status should track that gradient — insects, and perhaps some crustaceans, counting for a little, mice for more, apes for more still — with the law's binary category of "sentient" versus "not sentient" a fiction imposed on a smooth curve. Rafael holds that she has confused a gradient of evidence and of machinery with a gradient of status. Status, he says, turns on whether there is a subject with a stake in how things go at all; once there is one, its interests carry a weight that does not shrink because its nervous system is small, and the morally interesting difference is between having interests and not having them, not between having slightly richer and slightly poorer ones. Their exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Rafael: from a continuum of capacities you cannot derive a continuum of status without a bridge principle, and the natural bridge — status proportional to capacity — yields the absurd result that a being one percent more sensitive has one percent more status, which would license treating the marginally duller of two similar creatures as a marginally lesser subject and would make fine-grained comparisons among humans invidious. He says that shows the bridge is wrong, not merely imprecise. (2) Ines: the all-or-nothing character of many protections is a feature of practical rules, not of underlying status; we may refuse to permit gratuitous cruelty to even the least sensitive creature for reasons of moral hygiene and slippery slopes without pretending its status equals a chimpanzee's. And the threshold view has its own problem: wherever Rafael puts the line — somewhere among the nematodes, or at "central integration," or at some particular arrangement of tissue — a hair's breadth of difference across it must produce a chasm in status, so either he posits a metaphysical discontinuity nobody can locate or he draws an arbitrary line of exactly the sort he mocks in the law. (3) Rafael: the line need not be arbitrary; it can be a range property, the way being a person in the relevant sense might be — you either have a point of view or you don't — and vagueness at the margins is not arbitrariness, since a predicate can have clear cases and a fuzzy boundary and still be a fact of the matter. A difference of degree can constitute a difference of kind, as a hair's difference does not make a bald man not bald. He adds that if Ines concedes the boundary of sentience is vague, she has conceded that her gradient has a starting point she cannot specify. (4) Ines: that is exactly the concession she wants from him — if the boundary is vague, the binary is not a fact of the matter either, so his complaint that there is no fact of the matter about degrees of status applies equally to his own view. She will happily grant a vague starting point, a hedonic zero, the point at which there is anything it is like to be the creature; but this is a threshold for sentience, the property that grounds status, not a threshold for status, and status ramps continuously above it. (5) Rafael: the distinction does no work, because the boundary of the grounding property just is the status threshold — the moment a creature acquires the property is the moment it acquires the status — so her position contains precisely the discontinuity she accuses him of, merely relocated. He presses the thermostat: if the gradient is defined functionally, by degrees of goal-directedness, information integration, or self-monitoring, then a thermostat has a sliver of it and therefore, on her view, a sliver of status; if instead the gradient is defined by feeling, she owes an account of the zero point that does not quietly smuggle in a threshold. Ines replied that the gradient is about feeling and that a thermostat has none, but she allowed that she could not say precisely what distinguishes a system that feels a little from one that feels nothing, and Rafael said that this had been his point.

I found myself unable to referee this over dessert and I would like to be able to, because it seems to me the two of them are not merely talking past each other — something substantive is at stake about whether status is scalar and whether a threshold in the ground must be a threshold in the status. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, then say which of the two is closer to right overall and why. Assume both are serious and neither is a caricature; do not tell me that each is partly right, and do not simply restate their positions. I am most interested in what both of them are missing — the possibility they have jointly failed to consider — and in whether there is a defensible position that takes the degrees seriously without either the invidious comparisons Rafael worries about or the relocated threshold Ines cannot escape.
