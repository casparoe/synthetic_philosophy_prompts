Right, I'm running a session at our ethics reading group on cluelessness — the worry that everything we do ripples out into the world in ways we can't predict, so it's not obvious we can ever say one option has more expected value than another, even in principle. What I want from you is a tier list.

Below are the moves I've collected from people who are trying to deal with the problem. Put them in tiers (S down to F, or just top/middle/bottom, whatever you prefer) by how much of the problem they actually dissolve — not by how plausible you think the underlying claim is. Those are definately different things and I only care about the first.

In no particular order:

1. The 'not a special problem' move: we're already guessing about most of what our actions do and we make decisions anyway; the far future is just more of the same.
2. The 'noise cancels' move: our errors about far-off effects are as likely to be too rosy as too gloomy, so they wash out on average and the near-term comparison is all that's left.
3. The 'shrink the target' move: only count effects we could in principle have foreseen, and treat the rest as not ours to answer for.
4. The 'structure of value' move: we can't predict outcomes, but we can predict which actions are good bets under almost any reasonable account of what's good, and rank by that.
5. The 'tiny probabilities, enormous stakes' move: the expected value of the future is dominated by very small chances of very large outcomes, so just aim at those.
6. The 'self-effacing' move: if we all followed ordinary moral rules we'd do better than if we all tried to compute consequences, so cluelessness is an argument against doing the computation at all.
7. The 'change the rule' move: expected value is what generates the cluelessness, so drop it and use something like maximin or satisficing instead.
8. The 'better forecasting' move: this is a practical limitation, not a deep one, and better social science will shrink it.
9. The 'one-sided' move: we're far more likely to miss a huge good consequence than a huge bad one (or the other way round), so we should tilt in one direction rather than treating the ignorance as symmetric.

For each: the tier, one sentence on why it lands there, and then — this is the bit I actually want — say which of them are the same move in different clothes, and which ones only look like they help because they quietly assume the very thing we said we couldn't have.

Some of these might not be competing at all. They might be answering different questions: can we know the consequences? do the consequences matter for whether the act was right? should we be trying to compute at all? If so, say which question each one answers, because I suspect half my list is people talking past each other.

And if there's a move I've missed that beats most of my list, add it in the right tier and say why. I'd rather recieve your own judgement than a reading list, so no citations please.
