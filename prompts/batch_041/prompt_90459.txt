I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the First World War, and I want to settle what we were actually disagreeing about. His position is that blaming a nation for a war is a category error: wars are decided by particular people in particular rooms, so the "war guilt" reading of the Versailles reparations clause -- and by extension the whole enterprise of assigning responsibility to Germany, or to Austria-Hungary, or to a system of alliances -- is victors' rhetoric dressed up as moral judgment. I think that is too quick. It seems to me that when a state's military planning, its diplomacy, its press, and a substantial part of its political public all converge on war, the absence of any single person who intended the whole thing does not make national responsibility meaningless; it makes it a different kind of attribution, and I want to know whether it can be made rigorous. I am not after another round of who-fired-first. I know the July Crisis well enough -- Sarajevo, the blank cheque to Vienna, the ultimatum to Serbia, the mobilization timetables that made the last days of diplomacy so frantic, the invasion of Belgium -- and I know that Fritz Fischer's book of 1961, which argued that the German government deliberately used the crisis to launch a war of European domination, set off a decade-long quarrel among German historians over the war-guilt question. What my friend keeps waving away is a detail I find genuinely interesting: by the consensus of the historians who have studied the Versailles article, it was not drafted as a moral verdict at all. It was meant as the legal basis for reparations, and the German public read it as a national confession regardless. If that is right, then a good deal of the twentieth century's argument about national guilt was conducted over a text that was doing something else entirely.

What I want from you is an analysis that runs in both directions. First, state the strongest version of the view that a nation can itself be a bearer of responsibility -- the version on which a group is a genuine agent only if it has some authoritative way of deciding that binds its members, so that the group can be said to have acted rather than merely to have contained people who acted -- precisely enough that we could apply it to a case; then give the strongest individualist rejoinder in a short paragraph, and note the intermediate view on which a nation can be answerable for an outcome in virtue of the shared aims and like-mindedness of a large part of its members even with no formal decision procedure. Then apply the strongest of these to the outbreak of the war: identify the players, the points at which the nation can be said to have decided, where refusal was or was not genuinely available, whose aims the war actually served, and what "the nation" has to include for the attribution to hold -- the government, the general staff, the parties, the press, the annexationist lobbies. Say plainly which features of the episode the theory illuminates and which it misdescribes or cannot reach, and what the individualist would say back about the same facts. Finally, my friend and I cooked up a test of our own and I would like it stress-tested: a nation is blameworthy for a war only if (i) there was some point before the fighting at which the relevant public could have refused, through a mechanism actually available to it, formal or informal, and (ii) the aims pursued in the war were the aims of a sufficiently broad part of the political nation rather than of the leadership alone. I suspect (i) and (ii) pull in opposite directions and that one of them is doing all the work while the other is decoration, but I cannot see which.

I do not need citations, sources, or a reading list -- I have no way to check them, and I would much rather you tell me when a historical claim is one you are unsure of than dress it up with a reference. Please give me the response in five titled sections, in this order. (1) "The theory, stated," roughly 250 words: the best version of the collective-responsibility view, made precise enough to apply, with the individualist rejoinder in a paragraph. (2) "The mapping," roughly 500 words: the actors, the decision points, the availability of refusal, the aims the war served, and what "the nation" must include. (3) "What the theory explains and what it strains," roughly 400 words: the features it captures, the features it distorts, and the individualist's reply. (4) "What the case shows about the theory," roughly 250 words: whether this episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated, and whether its defenders should welcome the verdict. (5) "Verdict on our test," roughly 250 words: which clause of the two-part test survives, which is too lax or too demanding, and what would change the verdict -- ending with the one or two places where the whole analysis turns on historical facts that are genuinely disputed, so I know what not to lean on.
