In a graduate seminar on practical reason, one move keeps recurring: someone compares two options — a life that is meaningful but precarious against one that is secure but stultifying, or a policy that saves lives against one that preserves a wilderness — and someone else closes the comparison down with 'you can't compare apples and oranges,' sometimes adding that the two values are 'incommensurable' and that the choice is therefore arbitrary. I want to know what that one-liner could actually be claiming, because I suspect that most of its readings do not deliver the objector's conclusion. Give me exactly five numbered readings, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each reading, state the precise thesis, classify it as a platitude, as a substantive and defensible claim, or as false, and say whether it actually licenses the conclusion that no comparison is possible and the choice arbitrary. Ensure that your five separate at least these possibilities: (i) the options share no common unit of measurement; (ii) there is no single ordering that ranks them; (iii) neither is better than, worse than, or equal to the other; (iv) they occupy different dimensions, so ranking them is a category mistake; (v) they can be ranked, but not by any cardinal or additive measure. In whichever item captures what people usually mean by the slogan, say so explicitly, and say whether 'arbitrary' is the right word for what remains.
