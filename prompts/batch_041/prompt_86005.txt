ok so I'm trying to nail down a spec for what a theory of moral worth actually has to deliver, before I wade into any more of the literature and get lost. By moral worth I mean roughly the thing that makes an action deserve a certain kind of credit or praise on the agent's behalf, as opposed to just being the right thing to have done, or being useful, or flowing from some trait they happen to have. I want the full requirements list, not another theory.

Give me the cases any adequate theory has to get right, each with one line on what it's doing there. At minimum: the person who keeps a promise mainly because breaking it would humiliate them; the person who helps a stranger with real warmth but would have walked past if the stranger were ugly; someone who does the right thing only out of dread of their own guilt; someone who does the right thing for a reason that isn't the reason that makes it right (does you a favour because they like you, not because you needed it); and a case where the action is right, the motive is admirable, and the link between the two is sheer luck. Add any case you think does more work than mine.

Then the distinctions it can't collapse: acting rightly vs acting worthily; praiseworthiness vs moral worth; the worth of the action vs the worth of the agent's character; and the gap between the reason the agent acted on and the reason that made the act right.

Then the constraints coming from outside philosophy. Two I keep bumping into: how unreliable people are at reporting their own motives, and the fact that a lot of doing-good is habit and disposition rather than conscious deliberation. A view that requires the agent to have explicitly represented the right-making feature looks like it fails on both. Is that genuinely fatal, or is it a demand we should just drop?

Flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. My suspicion is that tying worth to something the agent is responsible for and tying it to the action's actual right-making features pulls apart, but tell me if that's confused.

Finally: which single requirement does the most excluding work, i.e. knocks out the most otherwise plausible views, and briefly how do the Kantian quality-of-will line, the responsiveness-to-reasons line, and the Humean sentimentalist line fare against the list? Keep the scoring short, the criteria are what I need. No citations, I'll fill those in myself.
