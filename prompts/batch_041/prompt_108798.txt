I teach a final-year seminar called 'Consciousness and the Global Workspace', and I am now setting its final: a single closed-book essay, written in 90 minutes, worth 25 marks. My problem is that the essay I keep getting is a recitation. Students say that Ned Block distinguished access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness, that global workspace theory is a theory of access rather than of experience, that the hard problem is therefore untouched, and then they stop. I want one question that forces them to work at the seam between those two things — specifically at the point where GWT's central posit, that a content becomes conscious by winning a competition for the workspace and being amplified and broadcast in an all-or-none, sustained way to a wide set of consumer systems, is doing double duty as a claim about availability and a claim about experience.

To keep the question from collapsing back into that recitation, build it around two technical notions, which I will spell out here and which the question itself must define for the students, since they will have no notes. Call the first ignition and the second prevalence. A content ignites when it wins the competition for the workspace, is amplified and sustained, and is projected to the full consumer network. A content is prevalent when it influences many downstream systems at once, weakly and in parallel, without ever winning a competition or being amplified — the difference, roughly, between an announcement over a public address system and a hundred people each murmuring the same sentence at once. I also want a case I will call silent ignition: a content ignites in the full sense, but the read-out pathways to report and to action are severed or masked, so nothing downstream is used. The question should compel students to say what a defender of the view that GWT is a theory of access only must claim about prevalence-without-ignition and about silent ignition, and then to face the methodological problem that every piece of evidence we have about what a subject experiences runs through report — so that validating ignition as a marker of consciousness against report may already presuppose that only ignited contents are conscious. I would like the phenomenal-overflow line to surface as the pressure point that generates the difficulty, rather than as a slogan to be mentioned and dropped.

Please act as a philosopher of mind who has taught and examined this material for years. Before drafting, work out for yourself the several lines a good answer might take, decide which single crux makes the question hard for the right reasons, and only then commit to a formulation. Then give me: (1) the exam question itself, self-contained and no longer than about 250 words, defining ignition, prevalence and silent ignition, and asking for a defended position rather than a survey of the literature; (2) a model answer of roughly 800 words, of the sort a strong candidate could plausibly produce in 90 minutes under exam conditions; and (3) a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, with the marks for each summing to 25, a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss each point, a closing line naming the single move that most cleanly separates an A from a B, and two or three errors that should cap a script at the bottom of the range however well written it is. The model answer must not lean on citations or named papers, and must not require students to know anything beyond global workspace theory, Block's distinction, and the three notions I have just defined; if the answer turns on a specific empirical result, describe the result rather than naming it.
