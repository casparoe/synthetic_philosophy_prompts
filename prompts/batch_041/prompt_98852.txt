I have spent a term in a political theory seminar on Hume's account of the artificial virtues — the claim that justice, promise-keeping, and the like are not natural virtues we approve from sympathy alone, but conventions that arise from a shared sense of common interest and are then approved for their public utility — and I keep running into the same wall. Everything he says about the obligation of conventions seems framed for people who are already party to the convention, participants in a scheme of coordination who have a common interest in it. Almost every convention I actually live under was either invented after his death or is enforced by institutions he never imagined, and several of them are contested in ways his examples never were. What I want from you is a prediction of what he would have said about the following disputes, and, more than that, a reconstruction of the single rule about convention and obligation he would be running to get there, since I do not think his texts wear that rule on their sleeve.

First, jaywalking. My city has just decriminalized crossing against the signal and crossing mid-block, while leaving the signals and crosswalks exactly where they were. Was the old rule ever a moral obligation, or only a legal one with a fine attached? Is there any obligation left now, and if a pedestrian steps out mid-block and forces a driver to brake hard, has anything been wronged, and if so, what? Second, paid queue-jumping. My office building has installed a system at the shared coffee counter and the elevators by which you can pay a small fee to move to the front of the queue, and the airport sells the same privilege at security. A colleague insists this is unjust, on the ground that a queue embodies a norm of equal treatment; a friend who reads a great deal of Hume insists it is simply an old convention being replaced by a more efficient one, since the point of the queue was to stop people jostling and get them served, not to enforce equality. Which of them is closer to him? Third, tipping. A friend of mine grew up where tipping is not done and is in fact mildly insulting, and now lives here, where the payment tablet offers buttons for twenty, twenty-five, and thirty percent and the staff watch you choose. Is she under an obligation to tip? Promising is his model of an artificial obligation generated by a convention, but she never agreed to this one, it is enforced mainly by awkwardness, and the same convention now functions in part to shift a wage cost onto customers. Does the obligation hold, and does it hold differently for her than for someone raised here? Fourth, visitors. A transit authority in my city recently ran an experiment instructing riders to stand on both sides of the escalator rather than leaving one side for people who walk up, on the ground that it moves more people; the regulars detested it and reverted to the old arrangement within weeks. If a visitor plants herself and her suitcase on the walking side, has she done anything wrong — and, turning it around, if the visitor comes from a place where the walking side is the other side and follows her home convention here, is she excused? He appears to need people to be party to a convention before it binds them, which would excuse a great deal of tourist behaviour; tell me whether that is his considered view or a reductio of it.

So give me his verdict on each of the four, the one rule about convention and obligation that generates those verdicts, the commitments of his that do the real work in each case, and the points at which his framework strains or has to be extended — especially on the visitor question, where I suspect the strain is greatest. Flag any case in which his own commitments pull in opposite directions, rather than smoothing it over. Then tell me which further case would best distinguish the rule you attribute to him from two rivals: one holding that conventions of this kind carry no moral weight at all beyond the legal penalty or the social cost of breaking them, and one holding that they bind because of what we owe one another as a matter of fairness or reciprocity quite independently of who opted in. Finally, if his texts genuinely underdetermine the visitor question, say which of the two Humean extensions available is the more faithful, and why.
