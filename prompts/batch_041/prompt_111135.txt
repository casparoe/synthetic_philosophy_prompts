I am working through the appendix to Frederick Douglass's 1852 Fourth of July address — specifically the turn where he stops indicting the country and starts talking about the Constitution itself — and I want help with the argument rather than the oratory. I do not have a reliable text in front of me, so let me state the substance of the passage as I understand it, without pretending to reproduce his words. Douglass says he holds that the Constitution contains neither warrant, license, nor sanction for slavery; that read as it ought to be read it is a liberty document; that one should read its preamble and consider its purposes and ask whether slavery is among them, whether at the gateway or in the temple; and that the Constitution, unlike the statutes and customs of the nation, does not name slavery, speaking instead of persons and of the states' obligations in general terms. He also distinguishes sharply between the document and the government that operates under it.

What I want is an account of the interpretive principle that is actually doing the work here, and an honest assessment of how much it can bear. In particular:

1. What is the precise thesis? Is Douglass claiming that the Constitution, correctly interpreted, does not permit slavery, does not establish it, does not protect it, or none of these? These come apart, and I want to know which one the passage needs and which one it can support.

2. Is the argument a claim about original intent, about the document's purposes, about its plain meaning, about natural law constraining legal interpretation, or something else? If it is a mixture, say which parts are which and whether they are consistent.

3. How does the argument handle the clauses that appear to presuppose slavery — the apportionment clause counting enslaved persons fractionally, the clause about migration or importation of persons, the fugitive-labor clause, and the clause about domestic insurrection? For each, is the strategy to read it as a limit on slavery, as a euphemism that avoids naming it, or as silence? Which of these readings is strongest, and which is doing the most work in the overall argument?

4. Douglass changed his position on the Constitution around 1851, having earlier treated it as pro-slavery. What exactly has to be true about interpretation for the later position to be more than wishful reading? Is there a principled way to distinguish "interpreting the Constitution as it ought to be interpreted" from simply reading one's own commitments into it?

5. What is the strongest objection available to a critic who thinks the document's compromises with slavery are not incidental but constitutive — that the silences and euphemisms are themselves the evidence? Does Douglass have a reply, or does the argument depend on a contestable theory of how legal texts mean?

6. Finally, is the principle he uses specific to the American founding, or is it a general thesis about how to read any constitution that falls short of its own professed principles? If general, what are its limits?

Please lay out the possible readings as distinct options rather than settling on one and defending it, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a defensible interpretive stance, or a stretch. Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point in the analysis you are least sure of.
