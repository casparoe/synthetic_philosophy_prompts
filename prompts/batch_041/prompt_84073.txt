I am a third-year doctoral student in history, and at a departmental workshop last week a senior colleague set aside a cache of colonial administrative documents I had put forward by remarking that "history is written by the victors." The implication was that because these records were produced by the governing power, they cannot be trusted as evidence about the events they describe, and that I should not build an argument on them. I have since heard the same sentence do very different work in other mouths, and before I decide how to respond I want to know exactly how much weight it can actually bear.

Please set out as carefully as you can the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, and tell me for each whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false. I am interested in how the reading shifts along several axes: who "the victors" are supposed to be, and whether they are meant as a single unified agent or merely as a class of people who happen to have prevailed; what "writing history" refers to, whether the production of primary sources, the composition of narrative syntheses, or control over archives, curricula, and public memory; how wide the scope is, whether it concerns one particular conflict or the whole of the historical record; how strong the quantifier is, whether always, typically, or merely sometimes; and whether the claim is descriptive, about who in fact does the writing, or normative, about whose account deserves credence. I would especially like the distinction drawn between the modest claim that the victors shape what survives, the stronger claim that the content of accepted history is systematically false, and the radical claim that there is no fact of the matter at all and only competing narratives.

Then say which of these readings actually supports my colleague's use of the phrase to dismiss my documents, and which reading the phrase's ordinary users most likely have in mind when they repeat it.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter.
