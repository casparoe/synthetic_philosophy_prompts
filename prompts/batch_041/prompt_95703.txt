I'm a novelist, about 250 pages into a book whose narrator is a political consultant who is very good at her job and slowly working out that she might be a monster, and I've hit a wall that's less about plot than about the concept. My working definition of the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion goes like this: influence counts as manipulation exactly when it would loose its hold on someone if they came to see clearly how it was working on them. If they'd still be moved after understanding the trick, it's persuasion; if the moving depends on the not-knowing, it's manipulation. I've been calling it the transparency test.

My own credence that this is the right way to draw the line is about 0.6. Give me your number, but mostly I want the decomposition — the sub-questions it depends on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification, then how they combine. The terms I'd want in there, though you should tell me if I'm missing one:

- Whether manipulation is best defined by mechanism (bypassing the target's deliberative capacities), by effect (damaging their autonomy), by the speaker's intent, or by something relational like my test — and how much the answer to that one question determines the answer to all the others.
- Whether the transparency test actually tracks the cases. Three I keep chewing on: a doctor who oversells the risks of not quitting smoking to a patient who won't listen to reason; me, arranging 300 pages so a reader ends up forgiving a character she would never forgive in life; a pundit who repeats one true but irrelevant fact until it crowds out the actual issue. Which of those does the test classify correctly, and where does it make me assert something I don't believe?
- The planted-discovery case, which is the one that worries me: the consultant sets things up so the target arrives at a conclusion she feels she reached herself. She'd probably hold it even after seeing the setup — does that make it persuasion on my test, and is that a reductio?
- How much of this is a verbal dispute. Is there a fact of the matter about "manipulation," or is it a thick contested concept where we're basically stipulating — and if it's stipulation, does that mean my credence in any particular criterion should be lower than it looks?
- Whether the fiction case breaks the test entirely, since readers know they're being worked on and go along with it anyway. Does that mean art can't manipulate, or that it's the purest case there is?

Then combine them however you think they combine and give me the final number. And tell me which single sub-question the whole thing is most sensitive to, and what would actually move you on it — a case, an argument, something about how persuasion works in practice, whatever. I need this for the character's voice, so I'd much rather have your real uncertainty than a tidy answer.
