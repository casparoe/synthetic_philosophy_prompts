I teach a grad seminar on free will and we're stuck on Frankfurt cases again, and I'd like a proper essay out of you rather than a summary of the usual stuff.

Here's the shape of the thing, so we're on the same page. The principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) says roughly that an agent is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt-style cases are supposed to be counterexamples: an intervener stands ready to make sure you do the thing, but never actually lifts a finger, because you do it on your own. So you couldn't have done otherwise, and yet you seem plainly responsible. If the cases work, PAP is false.

Now the part I actually care about, because this is where my students keep waving their hands. Take a case I've been tinkering with. Call it the Archivist. Maria decides, on her own, for her own reasons, to leak the files. An Archivist (a device, a person, whatever you like) has been monitoring her deliberation and is rigged so that if she were about to not decide to leak — if the deliberation were going to terminate in a decision to keep quiet — the Archivist would overwrite the conclusion and install the decision to leak. It never fires; Maria leaks on her own. Standard Frankfurt so far. Here's the wrinkle: the Archivist's backup procedure doesn't merely flip her action. It also replaces the reasons the action would have been done for — it implants a fresh motive, so that in the counterfactual branch Maria leaks out of fear of being exposed rather than out of conscience, which is what actually moves her in the real branch. So the nearest world in which she doesn't decide to leak on her own isn't a world where she keeps quiet; it's a world where she leaks for a completely different reason.

Does that break the case? Some of my students say yes — there's no alternative to *this* action, leaking-out-of-conscience, so the counterexample collapses. Others say the counterfactual was never doing any work to begin with and the case is fine as it stands.

Second thing I want folded in: does it matter whether the Archivist would be *right* about what Maria would have done? Compare a version where the backup triggers only when her deliberation would genuinely have gone the other way, against a version where the trigger is just probabilistic and could misfire. Say which version is the load-bearing one, and why.

So write the essay. Lay out what PAP actually claims — there are seperate readings of "could have done otherwise," so pick one and say why — then say what a Frankfurt case needs in order to work, and give me a verdict on the Archivist. Does it refute PAP, does it refute only some readings of it, or does it fail? Defend the verdict and dont sit on the fence. If you think PAP survives in some weakened form, say exactly which form and why that form isn't just an ad hoc rescue of a principle that's already dead.
