Forty years of my working life went into drawing, correlating and defending boundaries in the rock record — I am a stratigrapher by training — and since retiring I have been reading history and the philosophy of it, where I keep meeting arguments that sound remarkably like arguments I have sat through at committee meetings.

I would like you to translate one of them into the vocabulary I actually command. The dispute is the current skepticism about periodization: the claim that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution are not features of the past that later inquiry uncovered, but categories imposed on it by later writers — the humanists naming the centuries between themselves and antiquity, the nineteenth century making an age out of the Renaissance, historians of science treating the Scientific Revolution as a twentieth-century convenience, and everyone lately preferring early modern so as not to have to choose. Set out that skeptical case, and the standard replies to it, as faithfully as you can in the terms I know: chronostratigraphy and geochronology and the difference between them; the boundary stratotype, and the single point in a single section that fixes a boundary; the gap between the marker that defines a boundary and the transition it is supposed to indicate; correlation, and diachroneity, the same event turning up at different times in different places; facies, and the unconformities that remove time from the record altogether; and the committee procedure by which a boundary becomes official, including the recent rejection of a proposed new epoch at the top of the column. Use the machinery precisely, and where it gives out, say so rather than reaching for words like narrative or paradigm.

Then the part I care about most. Tell me what did not survive. Which elements of the historians' case have exact counterparts in stratigraphic practice, which had to be distorted to fit, and how much distortion the translation will bear before it stops being a translation at all. Which elements have no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever — the thought that one age is better than another, the suspicion that a period is doing explanatory work it has no right to do, the charge that periodization is a European export. For each of those leftovers give me a verdict: is this something my discipline is blind to, a defect in how I was trained to think, or is it evidence that the historians' quarrel is less substantive than its rhetoric? I would rather be told that my framework cannot see something than be handed a flattering analogy.

Finally, one question about an instinct I have held since graduate school, and I would like it tested rather than confirmed: that a period is an instrument and not an entity, and that asking whether the Middle Ages really existed is a category mistake of the same order as asking whether the Jurassic really exists. If the two cases come apart, tell me where, and tell me whether the difference tells against the historians or against me.
