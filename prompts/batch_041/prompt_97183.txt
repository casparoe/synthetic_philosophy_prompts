ok so i've been arguing with my brother in law for like a month about whether you can ever get a "should" out of an "is," and i keep catching myself agreeing with him on one example and then turning around and calling the exact same move a cheat on the next one. i don't have any philosophy background, i've just been thinking about it on my own, and at this point i'd rather someone just tell me what rule my gut is actually following, because i clearly have one and can't say what it is.

here are my verdicts on a bunch of little arguments. mostly these are things people actually say to me.

- "you promised you'd be there at 6, so you should be there at 6." airtight. not a cheat at all.
- "that's your mother, so you should call her more." softer but i still think it basically works.
- "she's in pain, so you should give her something for it." honestly the strongest one on this list.
- "he caused the crash, so he should pay for it." fine by me. nobody blinks at this one.
- "the sign says no parking, so don't park there." fine.
- "the knife's dull, grab the other one." fine, but this one feels like a different animal somehow and i can't say why.
- "everyone in this town drives everywhere, so it's fine that i do." cheat. just because everyone does it doesn't make it right.
- "it's natural, so it's good for you." cheat. my brother in law says this about everything from diets to how he raises his kids and it always sounds like a dodge.

heres the thing i keep noticing: the ones that feel fine, the "is" part already seems to have some shouldness baked into it. a promise, a mother, pain, causing a crash — none of those are neutral facts, are they? and the ones that feel like cheats ("natural," "everybody does it") are totally neutral facts with nothing hiding inside them. so maybe the rule is just: neutral facts can't give you a should, but facts that are already half-evaluative can, because the should was in there all along.

but then i worry i've just assumed the thing i was trying to prove. and the knife one bugs me — "you should grab the other one" only works if you're trying to cut something. is that a seperate kind of should, where it's relative to what you want, versus the others that seem to just apply to you no matter what you want? if so, does that break my whole pattern or save it?

so: is there one principle that gets all eight of my verdicts, or do i have to give one up? and what does it say about two i havent settled — "he earns more than me, so he should pay more tax," and "my dog is suffering and won't get better, so i should end it."
