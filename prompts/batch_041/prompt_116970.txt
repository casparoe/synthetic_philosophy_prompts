I have been asked to serve on my city's charter review committee, and the task that has landed on my desk is to write the stress-test memo for a proposal that would replace our thirty-member elected council with an assembly chosen by lot. The committee has already decided to treat the mechanics as settled and argue only about consequences, so I would like you to take the design as given rather than reopening whether it is workable. The proposal has six moving parts. First, forty-five members drawn by lot from the municipal electoral roll, each serving a three-year term, with one third of the seats re-sorted every year so that the body never turns over all at once. Second, a ten-year ineligibility period after service, so that no one can make a career inside the assembly. Third, stratified selection: at least half of the seats reserved for residents who did not vote in either of the two most recent municipal elections, no more than a quarter for registered members of political parties, and the remainder filled by an unconstrained lottery. Fourth, individual votes recorded but sealed for five years, after which the record becomes public. Fifth, a recall-by-lottery clause: a petition from four hundred residents triggers a fresh drawing for one named seat, and the incumbent is replaced by the winner regardless of how they have performed. Sixth, the assembly appoints the city manager for a single fixed five-year term and approves the budget and the bylaws, while a permanent staff of roughly twenty civil servants handles administration and drafting.

Please set out the most important implications of adopting this arrangement, ordered by how much they matter rather than by how tractable they are to study. I am especially interested in five things: how well a body of this composition can aggregate information and deliberate, given that half its members are drawn from a population with a demonstrated disinclination to participate; what happens to political parties, to the professional political class, and to the permanent staff, since I suspect power does not disappear but relocates; whether the sealed-vote rule and the recall clause work together or cut against each other; how organised interests, from developers to unions to single-issue associations, would adapt their strategies to a body that cannot be lobbied through re-election; and what the steady-state composition of the assembly looks like, including whether the ten-year ineligibility rule meaningfully depletes the eligible pool or whether the real constraint is the stratified quotas combined with the rate at which people agree to serve. Where an implication depends on an empirical assumption rather than on the logic of the design, say so explicitly and identify the assumption, and please flag the two or three implications you think are most likely to be overlooked by people arguing about this proposal in public. I am not looking for a literature review, a reading list, or a survey of what others have written on sortition; I want your own analysis of this particular set of rules.
