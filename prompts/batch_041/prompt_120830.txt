I am a PhD student in philosophy of logic, and I am putting together the handout for a session of our graduate seminar on truth and paradox. What I need from you is a dialogue I can circulate beforehand: two philosophers of logic, equally well informed and equally competent technically, working through a disagreement about the semantic paradoxes and about what a theory of truth owes us. I want the exchange to be genuinely dialectical, with each of them pressing the other's position rather than simply expounding their own.

The disagreement I want on the page is between a paracomplete and a paraconsistent treatment of the Liar. The paracomplete theorist holds that truth admits three values — true, false, and neither — with negation sending true to false, false to true, and neither to neither; on this view the Liar is neither true nor false, and it is the law of excluded middle, rather than the inference from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion, that has to be given up. The paraconsistent theorist holds that the Liar is both true and false, that excluded middle is unobjectionable, and that what has to be given up is the inference from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion; negation behaves in the ordinary way, but a sentence may be both true and false, and the logic is built so that a contradiction does not detonate. Please do not let either position become a straw man, and do not let either of them win by declining to do the technical work the other has done.

To keep the exchange concrete, I would like the two of them to work through a small network of sentences. Take S1, S2, and S3, with the following contents:

S1: S2 is true.
S2: S3 is true.
S3: S1 is false.

Assume that the truth predicate assigns to a sentence the value that the sentence has — so S1 takes the value of S2, whatever that is — and read S3's content as the negation of the claim that S1 is true. An assignment of values to the three sentences is a fixed point when the value assigned to each sentence is the one its content requires on that assignment. Under the three-valued scheme described above, the assignment on which all three sentences are neither true nor false is a fixed point, and it is also the least such assignment: the one reached by starting from the sentences that do not themselves contain the truth predicate and building up. I would like the dialogue to turn on what each theory makes of this, and in particular on the following three matters.

First, whether the paracomplete theorist can say, in the language, that S3 is not true. Because of the relay through S2, S3's content is equivalent to the claim that S3 itself is not true, so the language does contain a sentence that says exactly that. If S3 is neither true nor false, then it is not true; but the sentence saying that it is not true appears to come out neither true nor false as well. Is the theory committed to there being a fact of the matter — that S3 is not true — which it cannot state? Does that matter, and is there a principled way for the paracomplete theorist to state it after all?

Second, whether the paraconsistent diagnosis does any explanatory work here. On that account the three sentences come out both true and false throughout, which is to say that they all receive the same value, just as on the paracomplete account they all receive the same empty value. I would like the paraconsistent theorist to be made to answer the charge that this merely relabels the phenomenon, and to say what the theory predicts about this network that the three-valued account does not.

Third, what each of them should say about a network of the same shape in which the third sentence reads that S1 is true rather than that S1 is false. In that case nothing contradictory is going on, and yet the values are not determined: assigning all three sentences true, assigning all three false, and assigning all three neither are all fixed points. This is the case I am most interested in. It looks like a case in which the paracomplete theorist must say that a sentence has no truth value although nothing stands in the way of its being true, and in which the paraconsistent theorist owes us an account of which of the available assignments is the right one. I would like each of them to try to turn this against the other, and to see whether either can absorb the difficulty rather than merely restating their position.

Please let the dialogue end without a forced resolution; I would rather leave the two positions in genuine tension than have one of them defeated by an argument the other has not earned. Neither interlocutor needs to be identified with a named figure from the literature, and I do not need citations or a reading list — the arguments should stand on their own. Something in the range of 800 to 1500 words would suit the handout.
