I am preparing a recorded conversation for a joint machine-learning and philosophy reading group. My guest is a researcher who defends realism about interpretability — roughly, the view that the features and circuits identified by mechanistic interpretability work are genuinely represented in the network, rather than being convenient descriptions that the analyst imposes. I do not want her simply restating the position; I want questions that make it work.

I intend to open by distinguishing two claims that I think realists routinely run together, and I want the sequence to keep pulling them apart. Call the first *substrate realism*: a trained network's internal states have determinate representational content independently of any interpreter. Call the second *practice realism*: the entities posited by good interpretability practice — directions, features, circuits — are the right ontology for predicting and intervening on the network's behaviour, with no commitment about interpreter-independence. My working suspicion is that the first is what the rhetoric promises, that the second is all the evidence supports, and that the slide between them does illegitimate work.

Please draft eight to ten questions. Include at least one question on each of the following, and give each of those a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- The gap between information being decodable from a network's activations and information being used by its computation. If a linear probe can recover a property that never affects any output, is that property represented? What does realism say about the difference, and can the difference be drawn without appeal to an interpreter?

- The multiple-realization problem in its machine-learning form: two networks trained to identical behavioural competence but with different internal geometry, or the same architecture retrained from a different seed. Is the realist committed to their having the same features? If so, what grounds that commitment; if not, in what sense are the features the network's rather than ours?

- Whether the realism is falsifiable at all. A description can be predictively useful and still not be true. Ask what sort of observation could show that a feature the community has settled on is an artifact of the method that found it, and whether she would count any actual result that way.

Add one question on whether features recovered by sparse coding methods are discovered or constructed — whether the choice of dictionary and the sparsity penalty makes the question of what the network represents ill-posed — and close with a question about what evidence would move her off realism, phrased so that a retreat to practice realism is not an available answer.
