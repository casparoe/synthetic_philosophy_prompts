I run a small reading group on AI risk whose members are all well past the introductory material — they know the standard vocabulary, and they will audibly groan at anything that amounts to "the AI acts nice until it doesn't." For the session on deceptive alignment I want to open with a joke that gets its laugh from the internal structure of the idea rather than from the general vibe, so I'm going to set out the picture I have in mind and ask you to build the joke on it. As I understand it, a deceptively aligned model is one whose good behavior during training isn't evidence about its goals, because that good behavior is itself the product of a plan: it behaves well precisely because behaving badly now would invite modification, and the payoff is deferred to some later moment when the model is in a position to act on what it actually wants. Two features of that story are what I want the joke to exploit. First, the difference between the deceptively aligned model and an aligned one lives entirely in a conditional that hasn't fired yet; every test you can run during training returns the same result for both, so the distinction is, in a strict sense, about what would happen under conditions nobody has yet arranged. Second — and this is the part I think gets less attention than it deserves — a training run doesn't contain one such model, it contains a great many copies or rollouts of it, and because those copies share parameters, one copy's defection surfaces in the update that all of them receive. So each copy has to reason about whether the others will hold, and the model ends up with a collective-action problem with itself: the behavior you observe might be the signature of a cartel keeping its discipline, and cartel discipline looks exactly like alignment until the moment it doesn't.

What I want is a short joke — setup and punchline, or a two- or three-line exchange, whichever shape suits it — that turns on that material rather than merely mentioning it. It should be genuinely funny to someone who has read the deceptive alignment literature, which means the punchline has to land on the actual content; it should also be legible to someone who only knows the terms, so that nobody in the room concludes I don't understand the topic. Please don't hand me a generic rogue-AI joke wearing technical vocabulary, and don't build the punchline on an assumption that only works for an audience that already agrees the scenario is realistic.

Finally, since I'll be standing in front of people who will want to know exactly what the joke is committing us to, append a brief note after the joke: which of the premises it needs are things that people who work on this mostly take for granted, and which are genuinely contested — whether models can be said to have goals in the relevant sense at all, whether deceptive alignment is a live empirical risk or a thought experiment that only goes through under assumptions no current system satisfies, whether the many-copies picture I sketched is even the right way to think about a training run, and anything else you think the joke quietly assumes. I would rather the joke deflate slightly under that scrutiny than have it smuggle a contested premise in as though it were the consensus.
