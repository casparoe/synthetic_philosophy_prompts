I'm a science journalist and I've got a long feature due on why philosophers still can't agree on what it even means for one thing to cause another. Before I start ringing people up, I want the spec, not the theories. So: write me the list of things any theory of causation has to get right to be worth taking seriously.

The cases it has to handle — the booby-trapped classics like preemption and overdetermination, sure, but also the ones I think are actually harder: causation by omission, double prevention, and cases where the effect is simultaneous with the cause. The distinctions it must not collapse — cause vs. mere correlation, cause vs. explanation, and 'a cause' vs 'the cause' when we single one thing out of a whole sufficient set of conditions. And the constraints it can't contradict, like the fact that causal claims seem to come in degrees, and that we can cleanly intervene on some systems but not others.

Here's the wrinkle I keep coming back to and want the spec to speak to: picture a thermostat-controlled heater — the room's temperature and the heater's being on are each a function of the other, so any tidy 'turn the knob' intervention seems to presuppose the very arrow it's meant to be explaining. Does a theory of causation owe me a story about loops and mutually-determined variables, or is that a separate problem I should keep off the list?

Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the house style of one camp, and where two of them look like they can't both be satisfied, say so — thats the bit I most want. Then, quickly, how the counterfactual programme and the interventionist programme each do against the list: no long scorecard, just where each is strong and where it's exposed.
