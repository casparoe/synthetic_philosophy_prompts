Ok so I spent 35 years as a geologist (sedimentology, mostly), retired two years ago, and I've been reading philosophy ever since - slowly, badly, but with real pleasure. Here's the thing I keep tripping over.

When I was teaching I did the same thing every textbook does. You start the chapter on, say, plate tectonics or radioactivity, and before you get to the actual content you tell the story. The lone genius in the garret. The apple. The stubborn crank who turned out to be right and the whole academy who were wrong. The one who died for it, the one who got cheated out of it, the woman whose husband took the credit. I told those stories for decades, and I knew - everyone knows - that half of them are embroidered or just made up. The apple almost definately never happened. The famous experiments were done later, by someone else, more carefully, or not at all as told. And once I stopped teaching I started noticing the same thing happens at a much grander scale in the older traditions: Plutarch writing paired lives to shape character, and the whole saint's-life genre, where accuracy in our sense was never really the point.

So here's what I want to know. Take the practice - the whole apparatus of teaching people what to be like by telling them about a life, warts airbrushed out or left in, sometimes a life that didn't quite happen. What does that practice presuppose? Specifically, what theory of moral learning does it only make sense under? That virtue gets picked up by admiration and imitation rather than by rules or arguments? That the exemplar has to be lovable, or idealized, or larger than life, for the copying to take hold? That's the part I find most interesting, because it seems to me the practice would fall apart if we thought moral learning were really a matter of internalizing principles - you wouldn't need the myth, you'd just need the principle, stated cleanly. Yet we keep the myth, even after we know it's a myth, and even after the person turns out to have been a swine in private. We teach the integrity of science through stories that aren't strictly true, and we hang on to the heroes after we've discredited them.

But I can't tell how much of this the practice actually commits to and how much is just habit. Does it really require the exemplar be morally good all told, or only excellent in one respect? Does it require the story be believed - and if the audience finds out it's false, does the lesson survive? (I notice it often does, which surprised me.) And here's the pair I can't square: in the same curriculum we insist on accuracy and honesty as the cardinal scientific virtues - we'd fail a student for making up data - while our own moral instruction runs on made-up data. Is that just hypocrisy, or does the practice need the fiction in a way the integrity rule doesn't reach? Tell me what the implicit theory is, where the practice is underdetermined - which parts could be swapped for a rival account of moral learning without anything changing - and what would actually have to change if we decided moral learning was rule-based after all, or that exemplars had to be true and unidealized.
