I'm drafting a paper on the limits of causal inference in economic history, and I need a thought experiment that isolates the institutional channel from the geographic and cultural channels. Please construct one in which two societies arrive at sharply divergent institutional quality — say, one with secure property rights and a constrained executive, the other extractive and unconstrained — while their geography, climate, disease environment, resource endowment, and antecedent culture are held fixed by stipulation, and in which the divergence traces back to a single contingent historical event rather than to any prior difference between the populations. Constraints: the event must be the kind of thing that genuinely occurs in the historical record — a succession dispute, a bureaucratic reassignment, an accident of which colonial administrator was posted where — not a deus ex machina, and it must be intelligible that the event could have fallen out the other way for reasons unrelated to the societies' later prosperity. Do not lean on the usual settler-mortality instrument; I want the contingency to be unfamiliar enough that the case has to be reasoned through rather than recalled.

After the scenario, do four things. First, state what an institutions-first economist, a geography-first economist, and a culture-first economist would each predict for the two societies, and on what grounds. Second, identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the identificatory work, and say what happens to the inference if that feature is relaxed. Third, explain how a geographer who denies the exogeneity of the event would attack the case, and give the strongest available reply. Fourth, make explicit the residual identification problem that survives even a maximally controlled hypothetical history — the point of the exercise is to show that the residual does not vanish, so do not paper over it. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph; the analysis may be longer.
