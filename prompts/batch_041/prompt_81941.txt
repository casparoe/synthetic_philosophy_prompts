My philosophy of science textbook keeps leaning on the line that a scientific theory is like a map: a good theory is an accurate map of reality, a bad one is distorted or incomplete, and the history of science is just us redrawing the map at higher resolution. The chapter calls this verisimilitude, truthlikeness, and uses it to argue that later theories are literally closer to the truth than earlier ones, so progress in science is progress toward how things really are. I buy that the picture is seductive, but I want to know how much of the argument is just the analogy carrying it. Lay out what corresponds to what -- territory, map, projection, scale, legend, cartographer, the person using the map -- and then where the mapping breaks. Two things I want you to press on. First, with a real map we know what accuracy means because we can walk the terrain and check; with a theory the whole problem is whether their's any sense to be made of distance to a truth we don't already have. Second, I read somewhere that the obvious way of spelling out closer to the truth -- more true consequences, fewer false ones -- falls apart, because a theory with more true consequences also drags in more false ones, so no false theory ends up closer than any other. If thats right, is the map picture anything more than a slogan? And which features of maps are doing the smuggling -- maps are drawn for a purpose and accuracy is relative to that purpose, whereas truth is supposed to be purpose-free; maps presuppose the terrain is already there and that someone could survey it from outside, etc. Tell me at the end whether the realist conclusion the book wants (progress = getting closer to the truth) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, what analogy would do the job better.
