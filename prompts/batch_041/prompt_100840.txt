A large share of contemporary AI research evaluates systems by scoring them on a fixed battery of held-out benchmark tasks and reporting the results, often as a single aggregate number or a small set of numbers, set against a human baseline; on the strength of those scores a system is then described as more or less intelligent, or as approaching general intelligence. I want to understand what conception of intelligence this practice presupposes.

Treat the practice as an object of reconstruction rather than something to defend or attack. Begin by making its implicit theory explicit: what must intelligence be, if a fixed, pre-specified set of tasks with a scalar metric is a valid way of measuring it? Work through at least these candidate commitments, one at a time — that intelligence is a scalar quantity; that it is a property of the system alone rather than of a system-environment pair; that it can be measured independently of the context and purposes of the measurement; that it is comparable across systems as unlike as a person and a program; and that generality consists in breadth of performance across a pre-specified task distribution. For each, say whether the practice strictly presupposes it, merely fits it well, or is indifferent to it. Be careful to separate the commitments the practice cannot do without from those that are historical convention.

Then ask which rival conceptions the practice quietly rules out — for instance a view on which intelligence is constitutively tied to an agent's own goals and interests, one on which it is inseparable from embodiment and situated interaction, or one on which it is a relational or observer-relative property. Distinguish the rivals the practice genuinely excludes from those it can accommodate with a little reinterpretation, and explain what does the excluding work.

Pay particular attention to a tension I keep returning to. A fixed battery, held out in advance, measures performance on tasks that were specified before the system existed; but one natural thought is that intelligence consists precisely in handling tasks that no one has specified in advance, so that any fixed battery is at best a proxy and at worst self-undermining. Is that a genuine inconsistency in the practice, an artifact of conflating the measurement with the thing measured, or a sign that intelligence is doing two different jobs within a single sentence?

Finally, suppose the presupposed conception were replaced by a relational or situated one. What in the practice would have to change — what would be measured, by whom, against what baseline, and what current results would have to be reclassified as artifacts? Be explicit about what would be lost, and whether anything would be gained.

I am not asking for a survey of definitions or a review of the literature. I want the implicit theory reconstructed and assessed, with what the practice strictly presupposes kept separate from what it merely carries along, and an honest account of where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
