Since retiring from a career in field biology I have been reading my way through philosophy of science, and I have run into an objection to my old discipline that I cannot answer to my own satisfaction. My understanding is that Popper concluded that Darwinism — specifically the theory of natural selection, not the bare fact of descent with modification — is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme, and that he thought the survival of the fittest very nearly a tautology, since the only way to identify the fittest is to see who in fact survives. If that is his considered verdict, then a good deal of what my colleagues and I took to be science stands in need of a defence, and the difficulty seems to me to run deeper than a quarrel over labels: whenever a prediction about some trait fails, the selectionist can always reply that an unobserved selective pressure must have been at work, so that no observation is ever allowed to count against the framework. I should be grateful if you would tell me whether Popper really held this position, whether the tautology objection is sound, and what follows for the standing of evolutionary explanation. It has also occurred to me that the best selection explanations do not subsume cases under laws at all, but reconstruct the situation an organism faced and show that the outcome followed from it — I believe Popper made something of the same point about history and the social sciences — and I am unsure whether that is a legitimate form of explanation or merely a relabelling of the difficulty. If my reasoning is mistaken, I would rather be told precisely where it goes wrong than have the worry set aside as a misunderstanding.
