I cover AI and philosophy for a science and ideas magazine, and this term I'm guest-grading for a graduate seminar on the ethics of machine decision-making. Below is the midterm question and one student's answer. Please give me a grade — the course uses letter grades with plus/minus — and then the point-by-point comments that justify it: what the answer earns credit for, what it gets wrong or leaves ambiguous, and which specific sentences do the damage. I'll be handing these comments back to the student, so be concrete and even-handed: credit what is genuinely earned, and do not let a claim pass merely because it is confidently stated. Where a single sentence contains more than one problem, separate them. The course marks on four things: correct use of the criterion/procedure distinction, quality of argument, treatment of the strongest objection, and clarity.

The question:

Several designers have proposed building AI systems to reason deontologically — bound by constraints they may not violate — rather than as maximizers of expected value. Using the distinction we drew between a system's decision criterion (what it is ultimately answerable to) and its decision procedure (the policy it actually runs), assess whether "deontological AI" is a coherent goal. Does every implemented rule collapse into consequentialist reasoning at the level of design, or can a machine's criterion be genuinely non-consequentialist? Take a position and defend it against the strongest objection you can find.

The student's answer:

The proposal is coherent only if we are careful about levels, and I will argue that we cannot be. First, the familiar distinctions. An act utilitarian evaluates each action by its expected consequences; a rule utilitarian asks which rules, if generally followed, would produce the best consequences. Deontology is different again: it holds that some acts are forbidden or required regardless of the consequences. So the question is whether a machine can be governed by rules of the third kind.

The implementation argument says no. Any system we actually deploy has to be a fixed policy: a function from situations to actions. To choose that policy, engineers must compare candidate policies by what they would do in the world — that is, by their expected consequences. Once the policy is chosen, the rules it contains are simply the shape the system's consequentialist reasoning happens to take. If a self-driving car is forbidden to run a red light, that constraint is in the code because someone judged that a world with that constraint has better outcomes than a world without it. The constraint is itself a consequence of consequentialist evaluation, so calling the car deontological is a category mistake.

This shows that deontology and rule utilitarianism come to the same thing. Both tell the agent to follow a rule rather than compute the best outcome in each case; the only difference is that the rule utilitarian admits what the rule is for, while the deontologist pretends not to know. Since a rule followed because of its consequences is still followed because of its consequences, the honest description of any rule-following machine is rule utilitarianism.

I therefore conclude that deontological AI is incoherent and that the real choice is between act and rule utilitarianism. A system that recalculates the best action at every step will be unpredictable and will fail wherever commitment has value — for instance, a system that lies whenever lying would help. A system that follows stable rules will be more trustworthy, and trustworthiness is itself a consequence worth having. The lesson for designers is to write rules that are robust across the situations the system will meet, and to be honest that the rules were chosen for their effects.

For each comment, tell me whether the problem is the kind that should move the grade or is cosmetic. Close with the grade itself and a two-sentence summary of what would have raised it.
