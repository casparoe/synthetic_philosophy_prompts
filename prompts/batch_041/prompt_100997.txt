I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year writing a book in which a character's sex is withheld from the reader for two hundred pages, which has made me unreasonably sensitive to every place where English quietly supplies a default and asks me to sign for it. The book is now being translated into Swedish, and my translator and I have been quarrelling over a passage that English settles with a plural they and that Swedish, until recently, settled with nothing at all. In the course of that quarrel I played what I took to be a trump card — Hofstadter's Person Paper on Purity in Language, the satire he collected in Metamagical Themas in 1985 under the pen name William Satire — and I have since realized that I do not know how much weight the card will actually bear. I would like you to tell me.

The device is this. Hofstadter writes the entire piece in the voice of a complacent traditionalist defending our inherited usage, but he performs a single substitution throughout: race does the work that sex does in the real debate. The traditional generic becomes white, the term it is supposed to subsume becomes black, the pronouns become whe and ble, and the reformers, whose proposal is merely to write person wherever white now stands, are dismissed as political fanatics. The columnist praises the beauty of our inherited phrases, complains that a pronoun covering both races is repugnant to the nature of the language, appeals to what the average white in the street will testify to, and insists that the inclusive term is innocent because white is, in his words, "a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow, including black". The reader's revulsion at the columnist is meant to be the argument: if you find him monstrous, you have already conceded the case against the parallel defence of man and he.

Now apply it to a real dispute. Swedish had han and hon and, for a person whose sex is unknown or deliberately withheld, no comfortable third option; the pronoun hen was proposed as early as 1966, proposed again in 1994 with an eye on Finnish hän, adopted by a feminist magazine in 2008, and then detonated nationally in January 2012 by a children's picture book, Kivi och Monsterhund, written entirely in hen. One of the country's largest newspapers announced that year that it would not use the word; an airline ran an advertisement mocking it; a government minister used it in parliament in 2013; and in April 2015 the Swedish Academy's word list took it in. The argument was loud, and it was about feminism, about parenting, about whether a language may be deliberately engineered, and about whether hon and han should remain a closed pair.

I want three things. First, run Hofstadter's substitution on that Swedish argument and give me the mapping — a table is welcome — showing which of the actual objections to hen survive the swap and which collapse into self-parody. Second, the harder half: where does the swap misdescribe the dispute? I have my suspicions — that hen was an addition where the parody's person is a replacement, so that the complaint about defacement has a different object; that Swedish already had a generic person-word in man, homophonous with the noun for an adult male, so the hidden default is not quite where the parody points; that much of the resistance was to compulsion, and to the loosening of a binary, rather than to the inclusion of anyone — but I want the ones I have missed, and I want to know which of them matter for the use to which I was putting the parody. Third, the question I actually care about: is this device an argument or an audience effect? It appears to require a reader who concedes the model case and denies the target case, and I doubt that reader existed in Sweden in 2012, where nearly everyone agreed with the parody's conclusion and the fight was over means. If the satire only works on an audience already half-convinced, say so plainly, and then tell me what diagnostic would have done the work I wanted — something that can separate a genuine default from a harmless convention without borrowing the moral certainty of a settled injustice.

One last thing, from a novelist's vanity: the parody's jokes live in English morphology — in compounds, in vowels, in invented pronouns. If it cannot be carried into Swedish without losing its teeth, tell me what that shows about whether it was ever an argument at all.
