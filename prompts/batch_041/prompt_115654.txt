Suppose I want to reconstruct John Stuart Mill's late position on a question that arose only long after his death: on what terms the gains from large-scale machine learning ought to be held. Two proposals are on the table. The first treats the returns on very large compute clusters and the training corpora they consume as a species of unearned increment — value accruing not from the owner's exertion but from a shared inheritance of human writing and from the sheer scale of the infrastructure — and taxes those returns to fund an unconditional per-capita dividend. The second extends Mill's preferred remedy for the wage relation, partnership, by granting the workers and users whose labour and data make such a system valuable a legal claim of co-ownership over it, with no levy at all. What would Mill have made of the choice? Draw on the Mill of the later editions of the Principles of Political Economy, of the Chapters on Socialism — begun in 1869, published only after his death — and of the Autobiography, in which he says that he and Harriet Taylor would be classed decidedly among socialists. Say which proposal, if either, his principles support; where his commitment to cooperative association and his commitment to liberty against collective compulsion pull apart; and whether the analogy with landed property and its unearned increment survives the disanalogies. Where his texts leave the matter open, say so, and identify what is doing the work. No citations are needed. Please answer in British English.
