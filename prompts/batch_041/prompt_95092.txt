I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on consequentialism, and I need a decision tree that classifies the positions one can take on the utility monster: the thought experiment in which a single being would gain vastly more welfare from each unit of a scarce resource than any other being would, so that a total-welfare-maximizing allocation hands everything to it. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of names — first the questions that separate positions, then the actual views placed at the leaves. Aim for roughly ten leaves.

Treat the following as axes that must each appear as a branch node, phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which label it leads to:

- whether the monster's welfare is commensurable with ours at all, i.e. whether the comparison that generates the monster compares like quantities;
- whether the monster's advantage is a fact about its utility function (it converts resources into welfare more efficiently, without limit) or a fact about the measuring scale we have imposed;
- whether the relevant aggregation runs across whole lives, across persons, or across moments of experience, since the monster looks very different under each;
- whether the monster's gain is produced by the sacrifice or merely happens to coincide with it, so that the tree must handle a case where serving the monster is negative-sum, adding welfare to it only by subtracting more from everyone else;
- whether the theory under test is hedonistic, preference-based, or objective-list, since some of these cannot generate a monster at all;
- whether a position that resists the monster also resists its mirror image, a being whose suffering is as extreme as the monster's pleasure, and what becomes of that position if it does not.

At the leaves, name the position wherever a recognizable one exists — the straightforward maximizing view, prioritarian and sufficiency views, egalitarian and side-constraint views, the various impossibility and non-commensurability responses, the indirect or institutional responses that deny the case can arise in practice — and mark as unnamed any leaf no one defends. For each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the bullet it has to bite or the reason it looks ad hoc.

Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and for each combination of answers that no position occupies, say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Two constraints: no node may simply ask whether the monster's welfare should be maximized, since that begs the question the tree is meant to open, and the tree must use at least three concrete cases of your own construction beyond the standard one — I am especially interested in a monster whose welfare is a function of others' welfare rather than of any resource, and in a monster that is itself a population rather than an individual.
