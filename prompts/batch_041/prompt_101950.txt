Write a poem in terza rima — seven tercets and a closing line, the interlocking rhyme kept strict — spoken not by the animal but by its courtship display: the ornament itself, which wants nothing and is nonetheless read as though it meant something. Let the chain of rhymes do the work of a runaway, so that no tercet can close without opening the next and the poem keeps incurring a cost it cannot stop incurring; let the form itself be a handicap the reader can see, so that whoever reads it is placed in the receiver's position, judging whether the difficulty was earned or merely suffered. The argument should turn three times. First the naive reading: the display is extravagant, therefore it is a boast. Then the correction: the extravagance is the cost, and the cost is the certificate. Then the harder correction, which the poem has to earn rather than merely assert: the honesty is neither sincerity nor sheer magnitude, because what makes the display credible is that its marginal cost falls as the underlying quality rises, so that only the better animal can afford to look this foolish — and because the receiver, having learned to refuse a cheap imitation, is the one who converts a waste into a message. Let the closing line break the chain and hand the verdict to that receiver. Fold in, without naming it, the difference between a signal that is honest because it is an index of what it advertises — causally welded to the quality it shows — and one that is honest only because it sits in an equilibrium of mutual refusal, and leave the reader unable to say with confidence which kind this display is. It should satisfy someone who knows the formal treatment of signaling and still be a poem for someone who has never heard of the handicap principle.
