I'm giving a talk at a decision theory workshop next month and I want to prep for the Q&A rather than the talk itself. My thesis: Death in Damascus isn't just an annoyance for causal decision theory that you can patch with a ratifiability requirement or a mixed-strategy tie-breaker -- it shows the theory is missing something it can never get from a rule about which act to pick. The case, quick version since you know it: Death has told me he'll come for me tomorrow, and he's already predicted what I'll do, so wherever I go, that's where he'll be. Whatever act I land on, I die. I argue the right reading is that the agent's choice and the agent's prediction of its own choice have to be solved together as a single fixed point; the only fixed points are the mixed strategies, but every mix is a fixed point, so CDT gets a whole continuum of "solutions" and nothing inside it to pick one out. The missing ingredient is a theory of what makes a self-prediction correct, which is a theory of knowledge, not of choice. So the case is a counterexample to the patch-the-theory project, not just to first-order CDT.

The room: mostly causal decision theorists. Two of them do the counterfactual-dependence version (hold the past fixed, ask what happens in the nearest world where you do otherwise), two think in causal graphs and interventions, there's one committed evidentialist who reckons Damascus is simply a win for EDT, and one game theorist who keeps saying the whole thing is a self-confirming equilibrium and not a decision problem at all. Nobody will have read anything beforehand.
