I'm a philosophy prof and I've been tagging along to a colleague's cosmology-and-observation reading group, and I can't stop trying to reverse-engineer her rule. She keeps score on toy cases: does this count as a Boltzmann brain or not? Her list so far — (1) a complete human brain with memories and a felt sense of having had a life, assembled by a random fluctuation in an otherwise cold, empty universe, which has one coherent thought and then comes apart: Boltzmann brain. (2) Same thing, except the fluctuation also coughs up a room around it and it gets a few seconds of coherent experience: still a Boltzmann brain. (3) A fluctuation that yields one fleeting sensation, no memory, no thought, no self: not one. (4) A fluctuation that assembles a bacterium, which then seeds an ordinary evolutionary history — not one, and she's adamant that the observers who eventually evolve aren't Boltzmann brains either. Now the new case: a fluctuation assembles a large silicon-based computing system that runs an entire simulated world full of minds, coherently, for a real billion years before it disperses. What's her verdict, what rule is she actually running, what other rules fit her list just as well, and which case would sort them out? I care more about the disagreement between the candidate rules than about the verdict itself. And please be explicit about which bits of this are settled among people who work on Boltzmann brains and which are live disagreements — dont hand me a contested line on what counts as an observer as if it were the consensus.
