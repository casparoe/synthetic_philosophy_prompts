I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on ethics and global poverty, and I am drafting the final examination. The examination consists of a single ninety-minute essay question, and I would like you to compose that question together with a model answer and a marking scheme for my graders.

The question must not be answerable by reproducing the standard exposition of Singer's drowning-child case and the familiar objections to it, such as demandingness, causal inefficacy, or the individual-versus-institutional division of responsibility. To force the students past summary, the question will turn on a case I have developed with the seminar over the last three weeks, and which no one can have read about. Here is the case as I present it in class.

The crowded pond. A child is drowning in a shallow pond. Sixty adults stand on the bank. The water is shallow, the child is close to the edge, and any one of the sixty could wade in and pull the child out in under a minute; the only cost to whoever does it is a soaked coat and a ruined phone. But the bank narrows to a single channel at the point nearest the child, and the entry is abrupt: whoever steps off the bank must commit at once, and two or more people entering at the same time collide in the channel, are carried into a side current, and fail, leaving the child to drown. No one has been designated as rescuer, no one has authority to organise the group, and there is no time to do so; each knows all of this. The child will drown in four minutes.

I use this case to press a question that the solitary version of the pond case never has to face: whether the duty of rescue survives when acting is at once potentially necessary, in case no one else goes, and potentially ruinous, in case someone else goes too.

The essay question should ask students to do three things. First, to set out the conditions under which the solitary pond case does generate an obligation, treated as conditions that do argumentative work rather than as a list of features of the example. Second, to determine whether and for whom the crowded pond generates an obligation, and to defend a verdict. Third, to say what follows for the move from the pond to the world, that is, whether global poverty resembles the solitary pond or the crowded one, and what that does to the argument.

Please supply:

1. The essay question itself, phrased so that a student who has memorised a textbook summary of Singer will not know how to begin, but so that a student who has done the reading can answer it in ninety minutes without any further materials.

2. A model answer of about 600 words. It should take a position rather than survey the options, and it should read as an answer a very good student could give in the time, not as a lecture.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth four marks, for a total of twenty, with a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically get it wrong. Add a short closing list of the two or three responses that look impressive but should not earn credit, and say why.

One constraint on the marking scheme: I do not want the marks to reward recall of names or texts. A candidate who never cites anyone but who isolates the right conditions and defends a verdict should be able to score full marks.
