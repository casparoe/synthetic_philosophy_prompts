I'm running a session of our formal epistemology reading group next month on the accuracy-first programme, and I've landed on the idea of using de Finetti as the foil. I'd like you to do the spadework for me.

Bruno de Finetti died in 1985, so none of this existed in his lifetime. Accuracy-first epistemology is entirely posthumous for him: the thought that there is exactly one epistemic good, accuracy; that we measure it with a proper scoring rule; that a credence is rational to the degree that it maximises expected accuracy; and — the bit I find most interesting — that this is supposed to vindicate probabilism *without* the betting machinery, i.e. without Dutch books. All of that is 1990s and later (Joyce and Pettigrew are the names I associate with it, though the programme is bigger than the two of them).

So: what would de Finetti have made of it? Reconstruct the position he'd have taken as sympathetically as you can, and then tell me where I should stop believing you.

Things I already know, so please don't spend words establishing them: he thought rationality about credences just *was* coherence; that the only probabilities worth the name were somebody's subjective degrees of belief; that he spent his career trying to get rid of objective or 'true' probabilities as a metaphysical residue; and that his standard move was the betting set-up where you name a price and your opponent chooses which side to take, so that any incoherent set of prices hands you a sure loss. Fine. The live question is what happens when you bolt a truth-directed norm on top of that.

My freind has a hypothesis I half-believe and would like tested. Hers is that accuracy-first is de Finetti's coherence requirement in a nicer suit — that a scoring rule is only ever a preference ranking wearing a lab coat, so the word 'accuracy' in 'accuracy-first' is doing no independent work, and the whole argument goes through only because we've all already agreed to care about being right. She reckons de Finetti would have spotted that in about four minutes and been rather rude about it. My instinct is that she's too quick, because accuracy-first does appear to assert something coherence can't: not merely that your credences should hang together, but that they should be closer to the truth. Work out what the disagreement actually turns on, and say who's nearer right.

Two further things I want out of this.

First, veritism. The accuracy-first people usually say accuracy is the *only* epistemic good, and that understanding, explanatory power, or asking the right questions are either reducible to accuracy or not epistemic goods at all. Does de Finetti have a stable position here? He's not a natural ally — he doesn't want a single truth-directed good any more than he wants objective chances — but I suspect he also can't say those are merely pragmatic goods without sounding as though he's simply relabelled everything epistemic as pragmatic. What does his subjectivism actually force on him, and is anything left that he could still call an epistemic mistake?

Second, and this is the bit I think is genuinely hard: exchangeability. Coherence alone doesn't require it — you can be perfectly coherent and treat a run of tosses as a fixed pattern you'll never revise — yet he clearly thought some such invariance was a rational constraint on predictive probabilities, or at least the mark of a sane forecaster. So was he ever really coherence-only? And if not, does that make him a closet accuracy-firster, or does it show there's a third option nobody in the current debate is defending? Say which, and say what would have to be true for your answer to be the right one.

Last, be honest about the limits. Tell me which parts of the modern question he couldn't have had a view on at all, because the concepts or the technical apparatus weren't there, as opposed to the parts where he'd have had a view and it would have been a good one. And definately no references or reading lists — I'll track those down myself.
