Question:

The color phi phenomenon is often described as a case in which a subject's experience seems to outrun the stimuli that produce it. (a) Describe the phenomenon and state precisely what puzzle it raises about the timing of experience. (b) Dennett distinguishes Orwellian from Stalinesque explanations of such cases. State what each hypothesis asserts, and explain why Dennett regards the choice between them as not empirically decidable. (c) Identify the error Dennett claims both hypotheses share, and say what his own alternative is meant to avoid.

Student's answer:

(a) In the color phi phenomenon, a subject is shown a dot of one color at one location and then, a short interval later, a dot of a different color at another location. Under suitable timing conditions, the subject reports seeing a single dot travel from the first location to the second and change color partway along its path. The puzzle is that the reported change seems to occur before the second dot has been presented, so the content of the experience appears to include information about a stimulus that has not yet occurred. Dennett uses this to challenge the assumption that there is a single moment at which a given piece of content enters consciousness.

(b) An Orwellian explanation says that the subject first experiences the initial dot on its own, and that only after the second dot arrives is the memory of the earlier experience revised, so that the subject later remembers having seen the color change. The name comes from Orwell's 1984, in which records of the past are rewritten after the fact. A Stalinesque explanation says that the reconciliation happens before the content ever reaches consciousness: the brain resolves the two stimuli in advance and presents the subject with a single, already-unified experience, like a show trial whose verdict is settled before the proceedings begin. Dennett maintains that the Stalinesque hypothesis is the correct one, since the color phi effect is produced by early visual processing that occurs prior to conscious awareness.

(c) Dennett argues that the two hypotheses cannot be distinguished empirically, because they agree about the subject's behavior and about the underlying neural processes; they differ only over whether a particular processing stage is to be counted as before or after the moment of experience. He concedes, though, that in principle finer-grained measurements of neural timing might eventually favor one hypothesis over the other. What both hypotheses supposedly share is the assumption that there is a boundary in the brain at which the order of arrival becomes the order of experience, an assumption Dennett labels Cartesian materialism. His own view is that no such boundary exists, and that the visual system fills in the intervening color so that the subject's report of a continuous color change is simply a faithful record of a constructed experience.
