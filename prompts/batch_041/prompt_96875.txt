I am the mayor of a mid-sized English town, and on Saturday I shall preside over the council's annual citizenship ceremony — the occasion on which new citizens take the oath, collect their certificates, and are applauded by their families. I have to give the short address that opens the proceedings, and I want to use it well. I read philosophy at university and have lately been turning over the old quarrel about what freedom actually is: the difference between being left alone and being one's own master, and then the further thought that neither of those quite captures what is troubling about being merely at the mercy of another's goodwill, even a kindly other's. It strikes me that a citizenship ceremony is the one public occasion on which this abstract dispute has real consequences, and I should like the address to turn on it rather than on the usual platitudes.

The room will be mixed, and that is the difficulty. Present will be the new citizens themselves, several of whom have spent years inside a system that could have refused them at any point; their families, some of whom speak little English; a Home Office representative; assorted councillors; and, unavoidably, two or three guests who are inclined to think that freedom simply means being left in peace and that the whole ceremony is folderol. There will also be a former colleague of mine from the philosophy department, who will notice at once if I muddle the three positions, so please keep them genuinely distinct rather than blurring them into a warm soup of liberty. The address must do real work: it should say what is actually being conferred here, and it must not simply announce that everyone present is now free — because in the sense I care about, that would be false, and a ceremony that tells people something false is worse than no ceremony at all. You may draw on the ideas themselves, but please avoid leaning on the technical labels, since the labels would lose half the room.

Please write the address: roughly five minutes spoken, in British English, in a register that can be sincere without turning mawkish. Where the three conceptions of freedom pull apart — over what the new citizen has gained, and what she may still be exposed to — let the tension show rather than resolving it too neatly, and let the whole thing land for the nervous family in the third row as well as for my sceptical former colleague.
