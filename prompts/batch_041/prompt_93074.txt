I teach a 300-level seminar on practical reason, and I want a single eighty-minute session to make the group feel the hitchhiker's predicament from the inside: the sense that the traveler who cannot bring himself to pay at the moment of arrival is the one who never gets picked up, and that a settled disposition to act against his own interest at that moment can be the rational thing to have chosen beforehand. There are sixteen students, the room has movable chairs and a whiteboard, I can bring printed materials, and no real money changes hands. Design a runnable in-class game, not a lecture.

The activity should have a payoff structure that produces the bind on its own, before anyone names it. A market with two roles and repeated rounds would suit it: travelers who need to reach a destination and drivers with a seat to offer. A traveler banks a fixed number of points on arrival; a driver incurs a small cost for each ride; payment for the ride is promised in advance but made only after arrival, and is not enforceable by anyone. Set the numbers so that a payoff-maximizing traveler reneges at arrival, that drivers who expect reneging refuse to carry anyone, and that the first rounds therefore collapse into no trade. Then let the structure reopen: give travelers a way to adopt a standing disposition (to keep their promises regardless of the cost at the moment of action) or to post a forfeitable bond with the facilitator as an external commitment device, and run enough rounds for reputations to form. Finish with a condition in which every traveler's disposition is public and it is common knowledge that each chose it to maximize total points, so that the honest and the bonded are carried while the unconstrained and transparent are stranded.

Specify the roles, the exact point values, the sequence of conditions, the materials (disposition cards, bond tokens, point ledgers), and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the eighty minutes with at least twenty left for debriefing. The debrief should map each moment of play onto the idea — the collapse onto the failed ride, the disposition condition onto being the sort of person who pays, the bond onto external commitment devices, and the public-transparency condition onto the assumption that rationality is known — and should let the students reach the conclusion that the disposition which looks self-defeating at the moment of action is the one that pays, while the theory that tells you to renege is self-effacing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (players trying to pay upfront or side-deal around the after-arrival rule, drivers giving free rides out of goodwill and muddying the incentives, overuse of the bond so the point about character is lost, anxiety over points read as real stakes) and how to handle each.

Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce. It lets participants choose their disposition as a card, whereas the hitchhiker cannot simply decide to become the kind of person who pays; it cannot capture the opacity of real character or the case in which no third party and no repeat play make a bond unavailable; and it cannot by itself establish why being that kind of person should be valued beyond its payoff. State plainly which of these the facilitator must supply in speech, and where the game should stop short of pretending to have shown it.
