I'm working up an essay on procreative beneficence and I'd like your comments, mainly on the spine of the argument rather than the prose. Here's the gist.

The principle, as it's usually stated, says parents should choose the child — among the children they could have — whose life is expected to go best. Its defenders present it as modest: it just tells you to pick the healthy embryo over the one with the disease. I want to argue it can't stay modest, but not for the usual designer-baby reason. The trouble is that "the life expected to go best" is doing all the work, and it's ambiguous between two readings. Narrow reading: the comparison is confined to the options actually in front of you at a moment of choice — the embryos in the dish, the gamete donor you've already picked. Wide reading: the comparison ranges over everything you could do to bring a different child into being — a different partner, a different year, a different diet before conception. Narrow PB is a principle about which of the children in front of you to bring into being. Wide PB is really a principle about how much expected welfare the next generation should contain, and it can't be confined to the clinic. Once you go wide you owe an argument for why you're allowed to choose a partner for love when you could have chosen one for expected child welfare. The standard move — love is itself a good, so it enters the calculation — proves too much, because then the principle is just a general injunction to maximise welfare subject to whatever else you happen to care about, and it's no longer about procreation at all.

Then the second problem, which is where I think the real bite is. Suppose you stay narrow. You still have to rank lives for the sake of the person living them, and the three standard theories of wellbeing — hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective list — disagree about exactly the cases that matter for selection, temperament being the obvious one. A cheerful, incurious child wins on the hedonist's ranking; a restless, dissatisfied one wins on an objective list that includes knowledge and achievement. So narrow PB isn't modest either — it silently imports one of three contested theories, and the choice of theory is doing more moral work than the selection does. I want to re-scope the principle as a constraint on how a child is brought into being rather than a maximisation over possible children: don't create a child whose life is expected to fall below a threshold, and don't select for a trait on grounds you wouldn't be willing to state to the child. But I can see a threshold view struggling to explain what's wrong with picking the slightly better of two embryos, which is the case the defenders care about most.

So: is that last concession fatal, or can a threshold view absorb it? And is the narrow/wide line really as clean as I've drawn it, given that the option set is partly up to you — you can go get more embryos tested?

Tell me how confident you are in your main criticism, and flag the one point you're least sure of. No headings, bullets, bold, or tables please — just prose, like you'd write in an email.
