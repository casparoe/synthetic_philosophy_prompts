I've got a draft of an essay on the paradox of voting that I'm going to put up on my blog, and I'd like you to tear into it. Not a line-edit — I want to know whether the argument actually works, whether any of the factual claims are off, and where a reader who already knows this literature would say I'm cheating. I'm most worried about section 3, where I try to keep the thing from being trivially dissolved; if that part is wrong the whole essay collapses, so please be blunt about it. If any section is just me re-describing a standard move and pretending it's mine, say so.

Here's the gist. I've stripped the intro and the jokes; this is the argument skeleton.

1. Two puzzles get called 'the paradox of voting' and they aren't the same thing. One is Condorcet's: majority preference can cycle, so there's no Condorcet winner and no clean 'will of the people' to read off a set of pairwise votes. The other is the turnout puzzle: if voting costs something and one vote almost never changes the outcome, why does anyone vote? I want to keep these seperate because people muddle them, and the muddle makes both look deeper than they are.

2. The turnout puzzle, as usually stated, isn't really a paradox — it's a proof that one of the premises is false. The premise doing the work is that the only payoff to voting is the causal effect of your single ballot on the outcome. Multiply the probability of being pivotal by the value of your preferred candidate winning and you get something tiny; subtract the cost (time, weather, the hassle) and you should stay home. Since alot of people don't stay home, something in the setup is wrong. Fine.

3. Here's my move, and the part I want checked. I don't want to retreat to 'people are just irrational' or to 'voting is expressive, so it's a consumption good' — both are true-ish but both give up on the interesting question. Instead I claim the payoff to a vote is non-linear in the pivotality probability, because of thresholds. Democratic legitimacy, the size of a mandate, whether a result gets contested — these don't scale smoothly with the margin, they jump. A result decided by forty points and a result decided by four hundred votes can license completely different behaviour from the losers, and around certain margins a single vote can flip which regime you're in (a recount, a runoff, a confidence vote). If the social value of the outcome is a step function of the margin, then the marginal value of a vote near a step isn't proportional to 1/N, and the standard calculation understates the expected payoff. So the paradox is partly an artifact of assuming a smooth utility function over margins.

4. The obvious objection, which I want you to press hard: this looks like it can rescue any vote by positing a step wherever you happen to need one, so it's ad hoc. My discipline is that the steps have to be publicly identifiable in advance — recount thresholds, runoff rules, quorum rules, supermajority rules — and not invented after the fact to save the conclusion. I also concede that for most elections and most voters the expected value still rounds to zero, so I'm not claiming the puzzle is dissolved for everyone; I'm claiming it's dissolved for a specific class of voter in a specific class of election, and that the real question is why the argument is always stated as if all voters were interchangeable.

Two things I'm not sure are even true, and I don't want to be reassured on them, I want to be corrected if I've got them backwards: is it right that under the standard models the pivotality probability falls off roughly like 1/N (or faster), and that the whole force of the puzzle comes from that? And is the Condorcet-cycle result really independent of the turnout puzzle, or is there a reading on which they're the same problem about aggregating individual preferences, so that separating them is a mistake?

What I want from you: (a) where the argument is sound and where it's sleight of hand, (b) whether my two factual claims in the last paragraph are right, (c) whether section 3's threshold move is doing real work or just relabelling the expressive-voting reply, and (d) the strongest version of the objection that the step-function move is unfalsifiable. Comments on accuracy first, interest second — I care more about not being wrong than about being exciting.
