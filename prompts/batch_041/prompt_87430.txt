Aristotle holds that exchange presupposes a common measure, and that money supplies it: just as a ruler renders lengths commensurable so that they can be set against one another on a single scale, money renders goods commensurable so that they can be exchanged in determinate ratios, and it does this by convention rather than by nature. That picture — money as the instrument of commensuration, the ruler of value — is not confined to the ancient text. It gets invoked whenever someone wants to argue that exchange ratios are objectively determinable, that there is a fact of the matter about a just price, and that the market does not create value but only reports it.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a summary of Aristotle's theory of exchange. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the ruler, the unit marked on it, the magnitude being measured, the act of measuring, and the authority that fixes the standard — and say which elements of the target map cleanly onto which elements of the source. Then identify where the mapping breaks down. Give particular attention to features the ruler has that money may not: that a measuring unit is arbitrary but fixed; that the property measured exists independently of the instrument and is unaffected by the act of measuring it; that measurement is transitive and additive; and that the instrument need not itself be one of the things measured. For each of these, say whether it carries over to money and value, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion above from the ones that are merely incidental.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — whether exchange ratios can be determinate when the unit is itself a good with a price, when the measured magnitude has no existence apart from the exchanges that measure it, and when the standard's authority is conventional in a way the ruler's is not — and, if the ruler picture cannot carry the weight placed on it, whether some other comparison (money as a token or pledge of future need, money as a language) does better, and why.
