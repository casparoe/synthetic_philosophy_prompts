ok so I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — thirty-five years measuring trace gases, retired three years now — and I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group to keep from going soft in the head. We've wandered into epistemology and I've gotten a bit obsessed with one argument: that no condition is luminous, meaning there's no state you can be in where you're always in a position to know that you're in it. The example that hooked me is the temperature one, because honestly it's my old job. You're in a warm room and you know it. Take off one degree and you'd still say you know. Another, another — and somewhere in there the thing has quietly slid out from under you while you'd still swear you knew. And the part that really bugs me is the second story: if knowing has to have a little margin to it, then you can't ever quite stack knowing-on-top-of-knowing, the I-know-that-I-know just keeps needing it's own little margin and never gets it.

I want to read a poem at the group and I'd rather the form do the arguing than the words. So: 12 to 16 lines, free verse is fine, but I want a refrain that comes back every stanza a hair different — same line, one word swapped, or the second I-know quietly dropped — so that by the end the claim has hollowed out without the speaker noticing it happened. I don't want a poem about doubt in general, and please don't have it explain the lesson to me; the erosion in the repeated line should be the whole point, and the last stanza's refrain should land somewhere the first stanza's couldn't have. Someone who knows the argument should definately be able to watch the margin disappear line by line. Don't worry about making me feel better about it.
