I cover science and ideas, and I'm working up a feature on how medicine keeps redrawing the line between treating disease and enhancing ordinary life. Before I commit to a framing, I'd like a consistency check on the position I've been assuming, since I suspect I've absorbed it piecemeal rather than thought it through. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Whether a part or process of a body is dysfunctional is a straightforwardly biological fact about how far it departs from species-typical functioning, not a matter of anyone's values.
(2) Aging is not a disease, however much it raises the risk of one.
(3) A condition is a dysfunction only relative to the environment the organism is actually in: a trait that is harmful in one setting and harmless in another counts as a disease in the first and not in the second.
(4) Medicine's business is disease, so the treatment–enhancement distinction is real and worth defending — and the labs now chasing drugs that slow aging are doing medicine, not cosmetics.
(5) Infertility is a disease whose treatment is medicine, whereas a patient's request for sterilization is not a disease being treated, even though both concern the same reproductive function.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, say for each what would have to give and what that costs, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your answer in American English.
