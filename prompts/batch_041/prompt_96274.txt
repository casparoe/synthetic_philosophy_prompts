I've got a weird background combination. By day I'm a performance nutritionist — mostly endurance athletes, a few clients with chronic fatigue and gut stuff — so I live in energy availability, macro and micronutrient adequacy, glycemic variability, HRV, sleep debt, iron/ferritin, stress load and recovery, and honestly more than anything the psychology of adherence, because the best plan is the one the person will actually still be doing in six months. But I also read a lot of Nietzsche for fun, and I keep having this feeling about his diet-and-health material — the stomach trouble and the hunt for the right climate, the suspicion of alcohol and heavy food, the vegetarian sympathies, and above all the insistence that what nourishes one person is poison for another. It looks to me like proto-personalized nutrition with a bunch of 19th century crankery layered on top. My friends say that's reductive and that I'm missing somehing, but they can't articulate what, and neither can I.

So here's what I want from you. Take Nietzsche's position on diet, health and the body, and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: energy availability and periodization, nutrient adequacy, glycemic variability, individual response variability, cumulative stress and recovery, behavioural economics of habit formation, interventions dosed to the person rather than the average. Then give me the residue report. Which of his claims come through cleanly? Which only come through in a distorted form — and say specifically what gets distorted, don't just wave at it. And which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? The obvious suspects are whatever he's doing when he treats a diet or a whole way of living as a symptom of something like decline, and whatever he's doing when he treats nutrition as a matter of value rather than of phyisology. For each piece of residue, I want your view on whether it's a defect of my framework — something real that nutrition science can't currently say and probably should be able to — or a sign that the original claim was doing rhetorical work rather than stating anything checkable.

If you can manage the reverse direction too, that'd be great: what does my vocabulary let me see about diet and health that his couldn't even pose as a question? And then just tell me straight at the end whether my friends are right, or whether the residue is big enough that calling him a proto-personalized nutritionist is a bad description of what he was up to. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I'm not going to check them, and please paraphrase rather than pretending to quote him — I care about the shape of the views, not the scholarship.
