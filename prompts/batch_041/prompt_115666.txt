I have spent an embarrassing number of years reading the decision-theory and probability corners of the rationalist blogosphere, which is how I ended up in a pub last Friday with two friends and an argument I still cannot break. The occasion was a long-running disagreement about whether expected value can be the whole of rational choice. One friend is a working probabilist who keeps insisting that expected value is a notion with preconditions; the other is a Bayesian of the stubborn sort who thinks those preconditions are technicalities that no real person ever meets. To embarrass the second friend, I invented a game on the spot. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first head arrives on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars — you win $2 on a first-toss head, lose $2 on a second-toss head, win $8/3 on a third-toss head, lose $4 on a fourth-toss head, and so on, magnitudes growing without bound while the signs alternate. I have a vague sense that puzzles of this shape are known, but I have not tracked down any reference and I do not want you to bother with one; the game is stipulated here, and everything I am asking about can be settled from the stipulation.

What follows is the argument exactly as it stood on the napkin.

(1) Rational choice among gambles is the maximization of expected value: the value of a gamble is the average of its payoffs weighted by the probabilities of the outcomes that produce them, and a rational agent takes an option of maximal value among those available.
(2) The game is as described above.
(3) The first head falls on toss n with probability 2^(−n), and the payoff on toss n is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n, so that outcome's contribution to the gamble's value is (−1)^(n−1)/n. The value of the gamble is therefore 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which is ln 2, about sixty-nine cents.
(4) The order in which one lists a gamble's outcomes, or adds up their contributions, is bookkeeping. Two descriptions that differ only in that order describe one and the same gamble, and a gamble cannot have two different values.
(5) The series in (3) is conditionally convergent: its positive terms alone sum to +∞, its negative terms alone sum to −∞, and by Riemann's rearrangement theorem the same terms in another order sum to any real number whatever. Add positive terms until the running total passes 100, then take a single negative term, and repeat: the sum is 100. Mirror the procedure and it is −100.
(6) So the value of this gamble is ln 2, and 100, and −100, according to an ordering that makes no difference to the gamble. It has no value at all, and by (1) decision theory cannot rank it against anything — not even against a sure penny.
(7) But (1) was supposed to be the definition of a gamble's value. If applying it leaves the gamble without a value, the fault is in the definition, not in the gamble. So (1) is false: rational choice is not the maximization of expected value.

My two friends have split, unhelpfully. The probabilist says the trouble is at (6), that a conditionally convergent series is still a series and that all we need is the right summation convention; the Bayesian says the trouble is at (4), that the order of the tosses is part of the structure of the gamble rather than bookkeeping, so that a value which depends on it is no scandal at all. I would rather keep (1) — I am not prepared to give up on expected value as the core of rational choice — and I would rather keep (4) and (5), which look to me like arithmetic rather than decision theory. Tell me which step fails, why it looked so natural that all three of us wrote it down without flinching, and what it costs to give the step up. I want the price itemized: what I am then entitled to say about this game (is it worth sixty-nine cents to play, or is that a category error?), whether I may still say it beats a sure penny, what becomes of each of my friends' repairs, and what a repaired version of (1) would look like — what it would still cover, and what work it would leave undone.
