I'm a retired chemist -- industrial R&D, 34 years -- who has drifted into philosophy in retirement, mostly language and mind, and I've somehow agreed to run an 80-minute session for a friend's undergraduate course on language and mind. About 20 students, no linguistics background, no reading set. I'd rather they leave with an itch than a definition.

Here's the plan I want you to build out properly. I've made up a small language (call it Tamu) and I want them to work as field linguists who have a shoebox of notes and nothing else. Lexicon: nouns talo (dog), mira (child), wena (river), sanu (woman), deko (man), koro (house); verbs sapa (see), nuka (hit), pula (carry), tira (sleep). Word order is subject-verb-object, no case, no agreement, and one little particle, na, that turns up exactly once in every sentence.

Two rules fit every sentence I intend to hand them. Rule 1: na attaches to the subject noun. Rule 2: na attaches to the first word of the sentence. So the twelve lines of the corpus all have to be plain, one-clause, subject-first sentences -- no time words, no fronting, no questions, no embedding, no coordination -- so that both rules fit all twelve and the students can verify that for themselves. Write those twelve lines with glosses.

Then the session itself. Split them into two teams. Team A gets only the corpus, and has to state its rule and predict six new items. Team B gets the corpus plus a sealed envelope with Rule 2 inside and the instruction to defend it as the rule that fits every line. The six test items should include at least a sentence with a fronted time word (yesterday the child saw the dog), one with a fronted object (the river, the child saw), and a question made by putting the verb first (saw the child the dog). On all of those the two rules put na in different places. Score both teams on the twelve corpus lines first -- they tie -- then reveal the predictions on the six new items, which split, and let them try to settle it. They can't; the notes are all there is.

The reveal I want is a show of hands on which rule they themselves thought was obviously right before the envelopes came out. I'm fairly sure the room goes structural. Then the debrief: the data didn't pick the rule, they did; the structure they imposed isn't anywhere in the notes; and the interesting question is why nobody felt they were guessing.

What I need from you: the corpus handout, the envelope card, the test sheet, a timing plan, the scoring, what I say at the reveal, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the real debate about how much of a grammar a child could possibly get out of the data alone. Don't use the terms poverty of the stimulus or universal grammar until the debrief, and don't say structure-dependence at all.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guess: someone will insist that twelve lines is definately too little to learn anything from, and say it as if that settles the matter. And be honest with me about what the activity can't show. I'm a scientist, I know an underdetermination demo is not an unlearnability proof, and I want that said plainly to the students, with what extra premise a person needs to get from the one to the other.
