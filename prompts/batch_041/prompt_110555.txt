I've drafted a short piece arguing that the butterfly passage at the end of the Zhuangzi's second chapter is routinely misread as an anticipation of Cartesian dream skepticism, and I'd like your comments on it. The draft runs roughly as follows.

The standard reading treats the passage as raising the question of how one can know that one is not dreaming, and then either answers it skeptically (one cannot) or notes that Zhuangzi simply declines to answer. I argue that this imports Descartes' asymmetry. In Descartes the doubt runs one way: the waking world is the default, the dream is the hypothesis, and the question is whether waking can be certified from within. In the butterfly passage the doubt is symmetric. Zhuangzi does not ask whether he is dreaming; he asks which of two lives is the dream — whether he is Zhou who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming that it is Zhou. Neither frame is the default, and the question is posed from a position that seems to belong to both.

The consequence I draw is that no criterion can settle the matter. Any test one might offer — coherence, continuity, the capacity for reflection, pinching oneself — is drawn from within one of the two frames, and so begs the question once it is applied across the symmetry. This is why, on my reading, the passage cannot be an epistemic argument awaiting an epistemic fix: there is no frame-neutral standpoint from which to run the test.

What the passage is doing instead, I claim, is showing that the boundary between waking and dreaming is drawn by the states themselves, so that the self is a position internal to a frame rather than a spectator standing outside all frames. The closing move — what translators render as the transformation of things — then treats the two states not as one real and one illusory but as phases of a single process, which makes the question of which is real look less like an unanswered question than a malformed one.

I'd like comments on exactly five points, as a numbered list, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, whether the symmetric/asymmetric distinction is genuinely supported by the passage or whether I am reading Cartesian categories backwards into it; second, whether the symmetry really defeats every criterion, or whether some frame-neutral test could survive; third, whether I have conflated an epistemic claim with a metaphysical one; fourth, whether the process or transformation reading is anachronistic; and fifth, whether my conclusion differs substantively from the standard skeptical reading or only in emphasis.
