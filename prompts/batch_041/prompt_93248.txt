I'm trying to understand the ESP section near the end of Turing's 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence. The gist of the passage, as I read it, is that Turing says the statistical evidence for telepathy is strong enough that he doesn't want to dismiss it, and then he worries that if telepathy is real, a human in the imitation game could get information through a channel the test isn't meant to cover. He floats the idea of a telepathy-proof room as a fix, then seems to shrug it off. What I don't get is the role of this move. Is he seriously treating telepathy as a live scientific possibility, or is he using it as a limit case to show that the imitation game only tests what comes through ordinary channels? If telepathy is real, why is it an unfair advantage rather than just another human ability? And does the telepathy worry undermine his replacement for the question can machines think, or does it actually reinforce it? I want help unpacking what the passage presupposes about intelligence, evidence, and the boundaries of the mental. Also, is there a coherent reading where he's making a joke about parapsychology while also making a serious point about test design?
