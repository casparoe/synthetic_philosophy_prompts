Bit of a weird one, sorry in advance — I'm a backend/ML engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've ended up in an arguement at work that I'm now pretty sure is a philosophy question. I'd like to either lose it properly or win it properly, not just out-shout the other guy.

Context: I work on the perception stack for warehouse robots. We fuse a bunch of noisy signals — camera detections, a bump sensor, a floor-weight reading — into a belief about what's in front of the robot. The way I've always done it: when a signal comes in and it's not certain, you don't throw it away and you don't pretend it's certain. You take your current distribution over the possibilities and reshape it so the totals over the options match your new confidence, while leaving the ratios inside each option alone. So if the camera says "crate in aisle 3, 70% confident," I dont condition on crate-in-aisle-3 like it's a fact; I re-weight so the mass on aisle-3 vs. not-aisle-3 is 70/30, leaving the shape of the distribution within each of those alone. Someone told me this is called Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics, and that it's the principled thing to do. I've basically adopted it as my religion at this point — I use it everywhere, including for updating my own opinions when I read something half-convincing.

So: what would count against it? Not vibes — I want the actual defeaters, sorted by how much they should move me, from "yeah, that kills it" to "technically true, who cares." Things I keep chewing on:

- The "leave the ratios inside each option alone" part. Is that an assumption that can just fail? My gut says it has to fail whenever the signal also tells me something about how trustworthy the signal itself is — a weirdly confident camera reading should also update my model of the camera, and my rule doesn't seem to do that. Same worry when two signals are correlated and I fold them in one after the other and end up absurdly confident.
- Whether the whole thing collapses into ordinary conditioning. If I expand my state space to include what the sensor reported and then condition on it having reported that, don't I get the same answer without needing the special rule? And if so, is my position actually a distinct position, or just a special case of the boring one? If they come apart, where?
- Order. I have a feeling that if I apply two of these re-weightings in a row I get a different answer depending on which I do first, which seems bad, because the robot shouldn't care what order messages arrived in. Is that right? And if it is, is it a real problem or is there a story about why the order is part of the evidence?
- Information that isn't "here's my new confidence in each of these exclusive options." Like a floorplan note that says if it's in aisle 3 it's probably in the back half — that's about a conditional, not about a set of options, and I don't think my rule has anything to say. Does it just go silent there, and does whatever fills the gap disagree with it?
- New options. If a possibility I hadn't considered shows up, my set of options changes and I don't even know what the update is supposed to mean.

Also be straight with me about whether this thing can be wrong at all. It's a rule about how to update, not a claim about the world, so is the only way to attack it a coherence or bookmaking-against-yourself thing — and does that make it unfalsifiable in a way that should actually bother me? What do people who like this rule admit counts against it, as opposed to what critics say? What would move me most is a case where the rule gives an answer that's obviously wrong on its own terms, not just a case where a rival gives a different answer.

No reading lists please, I'm not going to read papers — just tell me what breaks it and how badly.
