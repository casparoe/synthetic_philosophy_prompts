I'm in a seminar on ancient historiography and we've just finished Polybius Book 6, the constitution book, and there's a passage near the end of his account of how constitutions go round that I can't get out of my head. The professor waved at it and moved on. Here's the bit as I have it in front of me (it's from an old English translation I pulled off some website, hence the antique ring):

"Then come tumultuous assemblies, massacres, banishments, redivisions of land; until, after losing all trace of civilisation, it has once more found a master and a despot. This is the regular cycle of constitutional revolutions, and the natural order in which constitutions change, are transformed, and return again to their original stage. If a man have a clear grasp of these principles he may perhaps make a mistake as to the dates at which this or that will happen to a particular constitution; but he will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage of growth or decay at which it has arrived, or as to the point at which it will undergo some revolutionary change. However, it is in the case of the Roman constitution that this method of inquiry will most fully teach us its formation, its growth, and zenith, as well as the changes awaiting it in the future; for this, if any constitution ever did, owed, as I said just now, its original foundation and growth to natural causes, and to natural causes will owe its decay."

Three things are eating at me. (1) What does "return again to their original stage" actually commit him to — a genuinely closed loop (monarchy, kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob-rule, back to monarchy, forever, each turn starting over), or does the earlier stuff about communities rebuilding after floods and other catastrophes mean the "original stage" is really a pre-political state of nature and not monarchy at all, so the loop doesn't quite close? A friend insists the flood material makes him more pessimistic than later cyclical thinkers, because nothing accumulates between turns — the arts and customs get rediscovered from nothing every time. Is that a real feature of his view or am I overreading some scene-setting? (2) How does this sit with Rome? This passage promises the method will tell us the changes awaiting the Roman constitution, yet in the same book he says it's hard to foretell Rome's future because we don't know enough about early Rome — so is the obstacle just missing evidence, or does the mixed constitution genuinely stand outside the cycle for a while? I've heard both that the mixed constitution is the best and most stable arrangement and that it too decays in the end like everything else, and I cant tell which he actually holds or whether the two are consistent. (3) Everyone says his cycle is just Plato's from Republic 8-9 re-tooled, but Plato's runs downhill from the best city to tyranny and is driven by the corruption of the rulers' character, whereas Polybius's goes round and round and restarts at monarchy with the psychology of the masses doing a lot of the work. What does he actually add, where does he improve on Plato and where does he lose something? And is the "rarely mistaken about the stage of growth or decay" line a real claim to predict constitutional change, or just a boast?

Please don't give me reading lists or who-said-what in the scholarship — I want the passage explained and these tensions seperated out properly, on the assumption that I've read the Republic and know roughly who Polybius is but nothing of the secondary literature.

Even if the honest answer has to hedge, finish with a last line in the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: closed, open, or undecided — your single best label for whether Polybius's cycle as stated is a closed loop returning to the same starting point, an open-ended process, or something the text genuinely leaves unsettled.
