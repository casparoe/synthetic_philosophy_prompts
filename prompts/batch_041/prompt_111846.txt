I run a small interview series for our department's ethics reading group, and the guest for the next session is a philosopher who has spent his career defending a capacity-based account of moral status — roughly, the view that what grounds an individual's moral standing is the psychological capacities it actually has or will come to have, rather than the species it belongs to — and who has more recently been arguing that brain size corrected for body size, and the raw number of cortical neurons, are the best empirical proxies we have for those capacities when we compare across species. Please draft eight to ten questions for me to put to him, together with a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The aim is a conversation that stays inside the argument rather than letting him restate the position, and I would rather not have to cite anything on air, so the questions should be intelligible to a listener who has not read his work.

At least one question should bear on each of the following. First, the apparent gap between brain size and cognitive capacity: some large mammals have brains several times the size of ours, while some birds with brains a fraction of the size do strikingly well on problem-solving tasks that primates also pass, so I want to know what this does to the claim that the proxy is a good one. Second, the implications of his view for human beings with atypical brains — infants, and adults with severe cognitive disabilities — where I expect him to retreat to the claim that moral status and moral treatment come apart; please supply that rejoinder and then the question that makes it expensive to use, and press him on whether the relevant standard is species-typical capacity, individual capacity, or potential. Third, the inference from measurements to capacities: how we are supposed to get from cranial volume or neuron counts to a claim about what an individual can do or feel, and whether the checkered history of using such measurements to rank human populations should discredit the practice in general or only its abuses. Fourth, whether his position is really about brains at all, given that what he values are the capacities themselves, which might in principle be realized in other substrates, so that brain size looks evidentially useful but not itself morally significant. I would also like the sequence to build: open with the clarifying questions a sympathetic interlocutor would ask, then move to the pressure points, and close with one question that forces him to say what empirical finding would lead him to abandon the capacity-based account altogether. Wherever the literature has a standard reply to a question, make sure the follow-up is already waiting for it.
