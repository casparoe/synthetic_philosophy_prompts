I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about whether a machine-learning system can be built to "avoid side effects" without ever being told what actually matters. A researcher I've been corresponding with proposes roughly this: equip the agent with a default policy -- what it would do if it simply pursued its given objective -- and then score every candidate action by how far the resulting world diverges from the world that default policy would have produced, with divergence measured in some fixed feature space. The agent maximizes its objective minus a penalty on that divergence. He calls the penalty "baseline-divergence," and his selling point is that it is value-neutral: it never mentions what is good or bad, only how much the world is changed.

Please explain, for a readership that is intelligent but not specialist, the conceptual problem that makes the value-neutrality claim doubtful. I'd like the explanation to get into the mechanics rather than stay at the level of slogans, and to work through several distinct pressures on the idea:

- why "a change to the world" and "a side effect" are not the same thing, and why separating the intended change from the incidental ones seems to require the very value specification the proposal was supposed to avoid;
- what is smuggled in by the choice of baseline (why "what the agent would otherwise do" is itself a substantive, contestable choice rather than a neutral zero point), and how that interacts with the fact that the baseline policy is generated by an agent that may already contain the penalty term -- so the measure appears to depend on a fixed point of the very optimization it is meant to constrain;
- why the feature space in which divergence is measured cannot be read off the world, and how a purely descriptive metric can fail to rank two changes that differ only in which one was wanted;
- and whether the trouble is a fixable engineering gap or a deeper circularity, i.e. whether any impact measure of this shape covertly presupposes a specification of value.

Where a concrete example helps, invent one (a cleaning robot, a trading agent, a hospital-scheduling system, whatever makes the point vivid) rather than gesturing at abstractions.

Close the piece with a single line beginning "One-line takeaway:" that states, in one sentence, your overall verdict on whether side-effect avoidance can be defined without a full specification of value.
