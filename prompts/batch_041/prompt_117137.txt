Had a long argument with my mate Tom last night about the council's plan to widen the bypass and I've been chewing it over since. The bit I couldn't get past: widen it by one lane and a few thousand people save ten minutes a day, and honestly you'd struggle to say the town is any worse off, the extra noise is nothing. Widen it again, same again. But you keep going and eventually it's a fourteen-lane motorway through the middle of town, noise you can hear from three streets away, and Tom and me both agreed thats clearly worse than what we've got now. Neither of us could point at the lane where it flipped, and I'd defend any single step as a small improvement. Does this shape of argument have a name? I'm not after a label for its own sake — I want to know what the rival diagnoses actually are. Tom said maybe 'better than' just isn't transitive, like rock paper scissors, but that doesn't fit either, because theres no cycle here, it's a straight line and the two ends just disagree with each other. And I don't think it's the heap paradox, because I'm not unsure about anything — I'm sure the first road is fine and sure the motorway is a nightmare. There's a cousin of this that keeps coming up too: two flats, one bigger but further out, one smaller but ten minutes from work, and I couldn't say either was better; put a slightly nicer kitchen in the small one and I still couldn't; but keep upgrading the kitchen and at some point it obviously wins. Is that the same phenomenon or a different one? What's it called, and where does the disagreement actually live?
