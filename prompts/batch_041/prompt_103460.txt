ok so i sit on the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital and i'm trying to figure out what our own procedure actually commits us to philosophically, because something about it feels off and i can't articulate why.

Here's how it works. When a family asks us to withdraw life support, we don't take one up-or-down vote on the whole thing. We split it into three separate yes/no questions and vote on each one on its own: (1) is the patient's advance directive clear enough to count, (2) is the family actually in agreement with each other, (3) is continued treatment medically futile. The chair then tallies it up and announces the committee's position on each of the three questions -- whichever side got the majority on that question -- and our rule is we approve withdrawal only if the committee's position is yes on all three. One wrinkle: for the futility question we require a two-thirds majority, but the other two are simple majority, and the final decision just needs all three committee-positions to be yes.

What's bugging me: we've had cases where the committee's position came out yes on all three, but if you'd just asked us to vote straight up on 'should we withdraw', the majority would've said no. And the reverse has happened to. The chair says that's just an artifact and the three-question version is the real committee view, but i can't tell if that's a real position or a dodge.

So unpack this for me. What does a procedure like this actually presuppose? Does it assume the committee has a single mind or beliefs at all, or only that we can report majority preferences? Does it commit us to the idea that the group's view on each sub-question is fixed independently of the others, and that the group ought to be logically consistent -- never 'believing' all three premises and also 'believing' not-withdraw? Or is consistency just something we're importing from how individuals are supposed to be? And is the committee's view on question 2 even the right kind of thing to talk about -- is a majority of members agreeing about family agreement the same as the committee thinking the family agrees?

Also: is the procedure forced to do it this way, or would a straight up-or-down vote on the final question fit just as well, or better? What gets lost if we switch? And tell me which parts of what we do it genuinely requires versus which parts are just one option among several -- i suspect a lot of it is underdetermined. Then, if you think the three-question version is the right one, what are we supposed to do when the three answers come out inconsistent? Do we have to revise something, and which part is the thing that's actually wrong? I keep feeling like the procedure quietly assumes the committee is a person, but i also don't see how we could function without treating it like one. Somebody told me there's a whole literature on this with a name, but i forget it.
