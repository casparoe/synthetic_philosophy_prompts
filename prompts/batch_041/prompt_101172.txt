I'm an undergraduate taking a course on nineteenth-century political thought, and during section last week two of my classmates had an argument about John Stuart Mill that the rest of us couldn't settle. I'd like an adjudication of the specific moves they made, not a general essay on Mill.

The question was whether Mill's late views commit him to socialism. Here is the exchange as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia opened with the texts. She said the later Mill treats the existing distribution of wealth as something chosen rather than given: in the Principles he holds that production is bound by physical limits whereas distribution is a matter of human institution, so the present arrangement could be otherwise. She added that the posthumously published Chapters on Socialism engages the socialist case seriously rather than dismissively. On her reading, Mill was willing to see the present system replaced if a better one could be found and tried, and that willingness is the substance of his socialism.

(2) Ravi said the label is doing no work and the framework is what matters, and that Mill never left the liberal framework. He kept insisting any move toward socialism be voluntary and experimental; he worried about the pressure it would put on individuality and on the freedom to choose one's own life; and his concrete proposals — worker cooperatives, profit-sharing, curbing inheritance, reforming landed property — are all things a market economy can absorb. A socialist in the nineteenth-century sense wanted the means of production socialized, and Mill never asked for that.

(3) Nadia replied that Ravi was treating the proposals as the whole of Mill's view when Mill treats them as way stations. She said Mill distinguishes the institution of property from the form it currently takes, and argues that the existing form recognizes property rights over things people never produced; once you see that, his critique is not reformist tinkering but the claim that the present system has never been justified on its own terms, so his openness to replacing it is not hedged the way Ravi says.

(4) Ravi answered that this proves too much: every liberal from Smith onward has distinguished the idea of property from its abuses, and entertaining an experiment is not endorsing its outcome. He also said Nadia was running together Mill's predictions — that the working class would increasingly demand a share in the product — with his norms, and that Mill's test for any scheme was always whether it could be adopted without sacrificing liberty, which is a liberal test.

(5) Nadia came back with a further point: she said Ravi's criterion makes the label unfalsifiable, since on his account any socialist whose socialism was voluntary gets redescribed as a liberal. The real question, she said, is what Mill thought was wrong with the existing system, and he expected the relation between employers and labourers to be transformed rather than merely regulated.

They went a few more rounds, but those five are the ones I can set down accurately.

Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who is right about which specific point. Keep the textual and interpretive claims about Mill separate from the definitional claims about what socialism means, since I suspect the two are tangled together here. Say where each of them is arguing past the other. Then tell me what both are missing — something neither raised that any good answer would have to include — and finish with who is more right overall, and by how much.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question.
