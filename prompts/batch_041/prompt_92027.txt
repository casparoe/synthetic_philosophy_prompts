I have no training in philosophy or history, but I have spent the better part of a year arguing about Thomas Jefferson and Winston Churchill, and I would rather have a tool than another essay. Please build me a sorting instrument — a taxonomy, or a quiz with a scoring key — that sorts someone who already knows the disputed facts about such figures into a definite position on how far we may judge them by present-day moral standards. The diagnostic questions are the part I care about: each branch should be a concrete question that a person can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and the whole thing should be usable by people who have never read anything on the subject. Four distinctions seem to me to do most of the work: whether blame requires that the person could have known better at the time; whether the verdict is about the person or about the honour still paid to them; whether greatness and goodness sit on a single scale or on two independent ones; and whether conduct that was contested even then differs from conduct that nobody in the person's world questioned. Please make sure the following positions appear as leaves: that present-day judgment is straightforwardly valid; that it is valid of acts but never of persons; that it is valid only where the person's own stated standards already condemned the conduct; that the real question is what our commemorations say now; and that we should refuse verdicts on persons altogether and judge only institutions. Eight to twelve leaves, named in ordinary language, each with a sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where some combination of answers has no occupant, tell me so and say whether the gap looks like incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect, and tell me which leaves the questions cannot pull apart rather than pretending they separate them. Use Jefferson and Churchill throughout, and add one case of your own in which the wrongdoing was uncontested at the time. No reading list, please — I want the instrument itself.
