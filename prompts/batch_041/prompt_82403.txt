I host a small interview podcast on ancient philosophy and its modern uses — one guest, roughly forty-five minutes, and I try to keep the questions concrete rather than letting the conversation drift into "tell us about your work." Next month's guest is a historian of Hellenistic philosophy (please don't assume anything about her books or articles — treat her simply as a defender of the position I describe here) who has argued for years that the signature doctrine of the modern Stoic revival, the so-called dichotomy of control, is a distortion of Epictetus rather than a distillation of him. Her claim is that when Epictetus marks something as "up to us," he is making a claim about ownership or attribution — about what belongs to one's agency and is therefore one's own to answer for — and not a claim about causal control; the popular reading, she says, slides between the two, so that the modern version comes out either false (we manifestly do not control our health, our feelings, or the upshot of our efforts) or trivial (we "control" only our judgments, which nobody ever denied). Her constructive proposal is that the real therapeutic core of Stoic practice is not the dichotomy at all but what she calls the reserve clause: the discipline of attaching a proviso to one's intentions, willing the action rather than the outcome, in the manner of "I will do this, if nothing prevents." I should warn you that she is remarkably nimble at deflecting objections with the line that her critic is confusing the school's therapeutic exercises with its theory of agency, and I would like the interview to make that move progressively harder to use.

So: draft me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Please build in (1) a question that forces her to say precisely what falls under "up to us" and what falls outside, and to specify what work the word "control" is doing in the popular slogan if it is doing none in the original; (2) one on whether the dichotomy is genuinely exhaustive, pressing on items that sit awkwardly in either column — one's own habits, character, or attention, say — and on whether the advice "attend only to what is up to you" survives once the boundary is drawn at a finer grain than the slogan suggests; (3) one on the reserve clause itself: how it operates inside actual deliberation, whether aiming at the action while disclaiming the outcome leaves deliberation idle or self-deceiving, and whether it is a technique or a thesis; (4) one on the standing tension between "up to us" and Stoic determinism — if everything is fated, in what sense is my assent mine, and does her attribution reading make that problem easier or harder than the control reading does; (5) one on the modern revival: whether it can keep the ethics while dropping the physics and the theology, and whether the popular dichotomy is better judged a misreading or a legitimate development of a school that always adapted its doctrines for beginners; and (6) one on the political objection — whether "only your judgments are up to you" licenses quietism about injustice, and how she squares the dichotomy with Stoic cosmopolitanism and the demand to act on others' behalf. I don't want questions that ask her to summarize her book or to recommend further reading, and I'd like the sequence to close with something that puts her on the spot about whether the ownership/control distinction is Epictetus's own or hers imported into the text — that is, whether she is offering a reading of him or a correction of him.
