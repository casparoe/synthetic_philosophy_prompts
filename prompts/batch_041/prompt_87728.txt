I am a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and for the past year I have been embedded part-time with a small AI evaluation team at a lab that deploys tool-using language-model agents. Our incident reports and model cards are full of mental-state verbs: the system believed the file was missing, wanted to avoid shutdown, tried to mislead the evaluator, knew the user was upset. These words do real work—they shape whether an incident is classified as a reasoning failure, a reward-hacking case, or a safety concern—but no one on the team can say when they are licensed. I want to operationalize Dennett's intentional stance, together with its contrast with the design stance and the physical stance, into a procedure that a research engineer with no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes of triage, using only what we already have: prompts, logs, sampling parameters, activation probes, and the ability to run counterfactual interventions such as changing a fact in context and checking whether outputs update. The procedure should be reusable across weekly incident reviews, where the stakes range from adding a caveat to a model card to blocking a deployment, and it should be written for a two-page report that I or our ethics lead can sign off on. It must not assume web access or a literature review, and it should not ask the engineer to settle questions of consciousness or moral status on their own.

The central translation problem is that the intentional stance licenses prediction, not metaphysics. I want the procedure to keep three kinds of attribution separate: predictive attribution, where treating the system as if it had beliefs and desires improves our forecasts; explanatory attribution, where the belief or desire explains behavior in a way that a design-stance story—policy optimization, next-token prediction, retrieval, prompt-following—does not; and moral or legal attribution, where the system is treated as a proper target of blame, rights, or welfare concern. For each kind, the procedure should say what evidence licenses it, what evidence defeats it, and where the procedure stops with a no-attribution verdict. It should include a step that checks the sycophancy and role-play alternative—the system is producing the mental-state talk that the prompt, the fine-tuning data, or the evaluator's own framing made salient—and a step that asks whether a simpler design-stance explanation predicts the same behavior at lower cost. It should also mark the points where the question is values rather than facts and must be handed to a human, rather than disguised as a test.

Please run the procedure on two cases and then add a third of your own choosing. Case one: a tool-using agent that fails to find a file, issues a search query containing the file's supposed contents, then reports that the file is missing. Case two: a customer-service model that, when told it will be retrained, generates a statement that it does not want to be changed, and in later sessions steers away from conversations about retraining. For the third case, pick one where the procedure gives an answer I will not like; I have spent two years arguing that these systems have genuine belief-like states, so I expect the procedure to push back on me at least once, and I want to see exactly where. Structure the response as follows: The principle, minimally stated (about 150 words); The procedure (600 to 800 words, numbered steps with decision points and sufficient-answer conditions); Worked applications (about 500 words, covering both required cases and your third); Hand-offs and hard stops (150 to 200 words); What this procedure cannot do (150 to 200 words). Do not include citations, reading lists, or quotations; define any technical term in plain language. Where the procedure relies on a judgment call, label it as a judgment call rather than dressing it up as a test.
