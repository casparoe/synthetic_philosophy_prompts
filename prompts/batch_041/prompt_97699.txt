I spent thirty years as a chemist before retiring, and the last few I've been trying to teach myself philosophy of social science, mostly by arguing with old colleagues. Here's the wall I keep hitting. Everyone tells me Weber showed that the Protestant ethic helped cause capitalism, and when I ask how a set of beliefs can cause an economic system, I get handed the same old railway image -- ideas are the switchmen working the points, while material interests are what actually drives the train (I'm fairly sure the switchman bit is Weber's, though don't ask me where he wrote it). My old economist friend waves it around as if it settles everything: culture is a genuine cause, because the switchman decides which track you end up on.

Take that image apart for me. Start with the correspondence table -- what plays the locomotive, the fuel, the rails, the points, the switchman, the destination -- then tell me where the mapping breaks. What nags at me: a real switchman dosen't decide anything on his own, he has a timetable and a signal and a dispatcher telling him where the train is meant to go, so once you put all that back in, haven't you just pushed the causal question back a step and turned the ideas into an effect rather than a cause? And does the picture quietly smuggle in the assumption that the tracks already exist, so that ideas can only ever 'select' among options the material base built in advance?

Then the bottom line: does the switchman image actually earn the strong claim that the Protestant ethic caused capitalism, or only the weak one that it picked which flavour of capitalism took root wherever the material preconditions were already in place? If it's only the weak claim, say so plainly, and tell me whether some better analogy can do the work -- or whether I should just throw out the analogies and state the causal claim in plain terms.
