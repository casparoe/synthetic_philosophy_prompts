I'm a novelist at work on a comic novel set in the administrative back offices of science, and its running gag is a fictional body I call the International Bureau of Foundational Standards — an agency whose entire mandate is to classify the epistemic status of foundational claims, stamping each one Definition, Theorem, Empirical Hypothesis, or Heuristic, and issuing compliance deadlines for researchers found to be using the wrong stamp. For the novel's centerpiece I've settled on the Church-Turing thesis, because it strikes me as the ideal specimen: a claim whose status no one can agree on and which no administrative act could ever settle. I'd like you to write the piece the novel quotes at length — a mock press release, roughly 700 words, in the deadpan voice of a mid-level Bureau communications officer, complete with a classification table, an effective date, a public-comment period, and a closing quotation from a "concerned" logician — and I want it to function as sustained irony, legible to any reader while remaining genuinely funny to someone who has thought hard about the thesis.

The humor has to come from the real substance, so that a specialist laughs rather than winces. I want the Bureau to have resolved the matter by rule, assigning the thesis to one category for some purposes and another for others, and I want the document to keep tripping over the actual difficulties without ever naming them: that on one reading the thesis simply fixes what we mean by "effectively computable" and so cannot be false, while on another it is an empirical hypothesis about what the physical world can compute; that the argument for it proceeds by analyzing an informal notion — a person calculating with paper and pencil under fixed rules — which is precisely the sort of thing a formal proof cannot reach; that the supporting evidence is the open-ended, inductive "no counterexamples yet" variety; that a would-be proof would have to presuppose the very formal notion it was supposed to justify; and that what can actually be proved are the equivalences among the various formalisms, which is a different claim wearing the same name. Please don't cite sources, authors, dates, or titles — the narrator is a bureaucrat, not a scholar, and I don't want the piece resting on anything I couldn't plausibly put in his mouth.

Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no editorial asides, and no real people, living or dead. The target is the practice of trying to settle a conceptual-status dispute by administrative fiat, and the pretense that the thesis has a single determinate status to be filed under — so let the officer be scrupulously polite, procedurally thorough, and entirely confident that the matter is now closed.
