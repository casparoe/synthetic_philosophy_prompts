Two researchers in the philosophy of probability have argued for years without converging. One is a committed subjectivist in the de Finetti and Savage line: probabilities just are an agent's coherent degrees of belief, the calculus constrains belief only through coherence and betting considerations, every probability is a real number and any two are comparable, and what is called uncertainty is at most a shallower or more diffuse distribution. The other is a committed Keynesian: probability is an objective logical relation between propositions, only partially determined by anyone's beliefs; the arguments for a probability carry a weight that is not itself a probability; some pairs of probabilities stand in no greater-than or less-than relation at all; and there are practical questions on which no calculable probability exists. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they can both sign.

The statement should have the following parts, in this order.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must be substantive rather than diplomatic throat-clearing. Include what both accept about the probability calculus itself and about which arguments establish what — coherence and Dutch book arguments, representation theorems, long-run frequency considerations, calibration — and about which of these the other side's tradition has shown to be correct. Include what both accept about the risk/uncertainty distinction, about rational choice under uncertainty, and about the status of the principle of indifference or insufficient reason. Where convergence stops, mark it explicitly rather than papering over it.

2. A disagreements section formatted as a table, one row per disagreement, with columns for: a neutral statement of the issue; the subjectivist's formulation of his own position, in wording he would put his name to; the Keynesian's formulation of her own position, in wording she would put her name to; and the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them. The table must cover at least the following:
   - whether every pair of probabilities is comparable, or whether some pairs stand in no greater-than or less-than relation;
   - whether the weight of an argument is a genuine second dimension of evidential support or is reconstructible as a second-order probability, a confidence measure, or a sensitivity analysis;
   - whether rational belief must satisfy the axioms of probability, or whether the axioms constrain only agents who have committed to betting;
   - what can be meant by Keynes's claim that in some matters there is no scientific basis for forming a calculable probability, given that a subjectivist can always assign some distribution to anything;
   - whether two agents who share all their evidence can both be rational while disagreeing about a probability, and if so whether anything obliges them to converge over time.

3. For each crux, the case, experiment, or argument that both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome written down in advance. At least one of these must be a case the two of them construct themselves rather than one drawn from the existing literature, and it must be a case on which the subjectivist and the Keynesian deliver different verdicts about what a rational agent may do.

4. A closing section stating which parts of the dispute are, on the two parties' own assessment, merely verbal — for example, whether the word probability is doing different work on the two sides — and which parts are substantive. Do not manufacture a verbal resolution where the disagreement is real; if the whole dispute turns out to rest on a single crux, say so and say which one.

Write it as a document both could sign, not as a report about them. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or references to the literature; assume a reader who knows both positions and wants to see where exactly they diverge. Where a claim would be empty without an example, give the example.
