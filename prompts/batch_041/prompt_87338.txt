A friend of mine argues that taxation is best understood as a protection racket: the state demands payment under threat, and what it offers in return is protection — protection, on the racketeer's model, chiefly from the consequences of not paying. He takes this to show that taxation is extortion, and that no further argument is needed. I want the analogy audited properly rather than waved away. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the racketeer, the victim, the threat, the payment, the protection — then say which features of a real protection racket the state shares and which it lacks, with particular attention to whether the threat behind the demand is endogenous to the party making it, and to what happens once we notice that the income being taken is income whose ownership is precisely what is in dispute. Then do the same for the rival analogy I keep hearing from the other side, that taxes are like the compulsory premiums of an insurance scheme, or the dues of a club you cannot resign from. Separate the disanalogies that bear on the extortion conclusion from the ones that do not, say what each analogy quietly presupposes about the baseline entitlement, and tell me whether either can carry the conclusion it is being used for — or whether both are restatements of a verdict that has to be argued for on other grounds. If neither holds, what would a better analogy be?
