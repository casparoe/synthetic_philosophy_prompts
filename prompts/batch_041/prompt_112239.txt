You are a professional philosopher working in social epistemology, with particular expertise in how people form and revise beliefs in online information environments. I would like you to act as a demanding, careful reader of my own views rather than as an encouraging one, and to reason step by step before delivering any verdict. I am a high school student preparing a position paper for a philosophy seminar, and before I commit to the paper I want an honest consistency check.

Here is roughly where I have landed, stated as plainly as I can.

(1) There is an important difference between two ways of being badly informed. In the first, a person simply never encounters the other side of a question: the contrary evidence and arguments never reach them at all. In the second, the person does encounter the other side, but has been brought to treat it as not worth taking seriously, so the encounter changes nothing. I think the second is a genuinely different phenomenon from the first, and not merely a more extreme version of it.

(2) I think the first of these two is the more serious problem for people my age, precisely because it is silent. You cannot miss what you never see, so the person who is worst off has the least reason to suspect that anything is wrong.

(3) I think the remedy for both is fundamentally the same: sustained exposure to well-argued opposing views. If you read and listen to intelligent people who disagree with you, in good faith and at length, you will end up better calibrated.

(4) I think people have a right to curate their own information diet. Nobody owes anyone else their attention, and I do not think there is anything wrong with choosing what to read.

(5) I think that a person who has been brought to dismiss outside views is nonetheless responsible for getting out of that condition, because the evidence that they are being manipulated is in principle available to them.

(6) For what it is worth, I do not think I am in either condition, because I deliberately follow a number of people I disagree with.

Please do the following. First, brainstorm before you evaluate: for each pair of commitments, ask whether the usual reasons someone would hold each one are compatible with the usual reasons someone would hold the other, rather than merely checking whether the sentences could be true together. Second, sort the pairs into three piles — genuinely in tension, only apparently in tension, and not in tension at all — and defend your sorting, paying special attention to any pair that looks contradictory but dissolves once the two commitments are read more carefully. Third, for each pair that is genuinely in tension, rate the severity, saying explicitly whether it is fatal, serious, or minor, and state the cheapest revision that would remove it and what that revision would cost me: which of my other commitments it would weaken, or which intuition I would have to surrender. Fourth, tell me which single revision would resolve the most tension at once, and whether that is really the revision I should make or whether it looks cheap only because it is vague.

Do not list the tensions as though they were all equally bad, and do not reassure me that the package is fine as it stands. I suspect that at least one of these commitments is doing work that another one quietly undercuts, and I would rather find out now than in front of the seminar. If a tension turns on a factual assumption about how people actually respond to opposing views, flag that assumption explicitly and say how your verdict would change if it turned out to be false.
