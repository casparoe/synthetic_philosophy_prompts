I've got a half-formed idea about Hume's argument against miracles, and I need to turn it into a two-page research proposal for a small philosophy-of-religion workshop I'm trying to get into.

Everyone knows the maxim — 'no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish'. The usual fight is whether that argument works as a piece of probability reasoning, and its been fought to a standstill: one side says it's a non sequitur, the other says it can be rescued with enough care about priors. I think the standstill is because both sides assume Hume is making a single claim, and I suspect he's making two that come apart. There's a comparative claim — that testimony for a miracle is always outweighed by the evidence for the ordinary course of nature — and a much bolder absolute claim, that no testimony of any kind could ever be sufficient. Only the second is the dramatic one, and I think only the first is really defensible.

But the bit I actually want to develop is this. I want to ask what kind of epistemic thing Hume is saying about the person who hears a miracle report. In contemporary epistemology there's a fairly standard distinction between a rebutting defeater — a reason to think the belief is false — and an undercutting defeater — a reason to think your grounds don't actually support the belief. My hunch is that Hume's argument, at its best, gives you an undercutting defeater and not a rebutting one, and that alot of the deadlock comes from both critics and defenders assuming it has to be the rebutting kind. If that's right, it changes what a successful defence or refutation would even look like.
