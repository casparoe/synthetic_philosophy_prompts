I'm in a third-year seminar on promising this term — practice/convention accounts, the reliance-and-trust line, the version where the promisee acquires a right to demand and the power to release, joint commitment, the lot — and for my essay I want to run a real case through the theories instead of another borrowed-book example. The case I've landed on is Bush Sr. and the taxes. He pledged at the 1988 convention that there'd be no new taxes (read my lips: no new taxes), having already signed one of those outside anti-tax pledges during the primaries, and then in 1990 he agreed to a budget compromise with the Democratic Congress that raised existing taxes. His defence afterwards was basically that the deficit had turned out far worse than the campaign had assumed and that these weren't new taxes anyway, just higher rates on taxes already on the books.

What I want is the analysis in both directions: what each theory says the parties were actually doing, and then which features of the episode each theory can't account for.

The bits I keep going round in circles on:

- Was it even a promise? By all accounts his own advisers warned him the line would handcuff him, and it went in partly to kill the impression that he was weak and vacillating, which makes it sound more like a self-binding device, a Ulysses contract, than a promise made to anyone. On that reading the breach is a failure of nerve, not a wrong done to a person. But then why did it land as betrayal rather than as a bad forecast?
- Who's the promisee? "The American people" can't shake hands or waive anything. Can a dispersed electorate be a promisee at all, or can a group only do that when it acts as one, at an election say? And if no individual holds the right to demand or to release, does the rights-transfer account just break here — or does it show that this is a pledge, a different normative animal from a promise?
- The content question. He took the narrow reading (no brand new taxes) when everyone knew the broad one (no tax increases at all) was meant. How much does a promisor get to fix the content of his own promise after the fact, and if he gets to, isn't that just a self-serving escape hatch that every promise comes with?
- Was losing in 1992 a release by the promisee, or a punishment that only makes sense if the promise still bound?

Then the part I actually care about: does the case do anything to the theories, or does it just illustrate them? My hunch is that it's an anomaly for the rights-transfer and joint-commitment views — nobody holds the correlative right, and the electorate isn't a plural subject except maybe on polling day — and evidence for practice/expectation accounts, but I want that argued rather than asserted, plus an honest note on where the theories are just silent. Like, is there anything in them at all about promises to people who can never act collectively? Don't worry about citing anything, I just want the argument.
