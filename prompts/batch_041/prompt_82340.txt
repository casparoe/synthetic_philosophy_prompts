I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book whose protagonist is a grad student who works on insect pain — she spends a lot of the middle chapters trying to get her seminar to see that the interesting question isn't whether insects are conscious but how much their sheer number counts when we don't know. For a scene in chapter nine I need the handout she gives them: a sorting quiz, ten questions, that lands a reader in one of the actual positions on this. Can you write it?

Ground rules, because the handout has to work for students who cant look anything up. No citations, no reading lists, no names of living researchers, no "as X has argued". But dont make it a quiz about stuff nobody's ever thought either — the positions should be ones a philosopher would nod at, just put in my own words. Where a position has no handy name, invent one. I've been calling a few of them things like 'the Ledger', 'Number-Blind', 'Intensity Cap' and 'Don't Bet Against the Small' in my notes, and I'd like those to show up if they fit; if a leaf really has nobody standing on it, mark it 'unnamed' and say so.

Every question should be a case rather than a doctrine. Not "do you think insects are sentient?" but: the mosquito on your forearm at 2am; the honeybee you could pull out of a spider's web or leave there; the fruit flies in the lab that get frozen when the experiment ends; an ant colony treated as one thing versus fifty thousand things; a cricket being eaten alive by a mantis and whether you're obliged to interrupt; a silk farm; a bumblebee that seems to be playing with a ball for no reason; an octopus, as the case that shows people aren't really reasoning about insects at all but about where the line goes; and one invented case — a startup claims its headset can register a flicker in an insect brain, so now you have to say what, if anything, you'd do differently.

Build the tree from the questions first and the names second, and make the branch labels the things that actually do the splitting: whether numbers aggregate across individuals at all, or whether the right unit is always the single creature; whether multiplying a tiny credence by an astronomical number is a legitimate move or a category error; whether faint experiences sum at full weight or at a discount; whether the threshold for sentience is a fact we're merely ignorant of or a smear we should average over; whether "we can't do anything about wild insect suffering anyway" is a reason to drop the issue or a reason to work on tractability; and whether the whole scale argument should be read as a reductio of something, in which case say which premise has to go.

For each leaf give me the name, one line on what the position holds, and one line on its most awkward commitment. Note which combinations of answers land nowhere, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just ugly, or merely unexplored. And tell me which leaves the tree cant really separate — I'd rather the handout admit that than fake a distinction.

Then a scoring key: which answer patterns map onto which leaf, with a note for readers who land between two. Keep the whole thing under 1200 words so it fits on a handout, and keep the register like a good teacher who isn't precious about it.
