I've spent the last year on the little county heritage comittee that has to sign off on historical markers, and I've come out of it with a pile of gut verdicts about when the archive should win and when the community's memory should win, and I can't get them under one rule. I'd like you to tell me what principle they add up to, if any.

1) A marker said the 1893 mill fire was set by the owner, Halsey, for the insurance. The archive has him in Boston that week, underinsured, and the fire marshal blaming a lamp in the storeroom. I voted to fix the marker and kill the arson line. Paper wins.

2) The reservoir drowned a valley in 1934. The descendants' association says the village had about 400 people; the last census before the flood lists 150, and I can't find anything wrong with that census — taken in June, heads of households and everybody else. I still voted for the monument to say 'some four hundred.' Memory wins, on a number, with no excuse I can name.

3) Local tradition has it that Father Ott named names to the authorities in 1905 and that's why the town emptied out. The diocesan archive puts him in another parish that year and points at the schoolmaster. The old neighborhood's whole account of itself runs through the priest, and their song about him is the song. I voted against printing the correction and against clearing him. Memory wins again, and I honestly can't say what separates it from Halsey, except that nobody's descended from Halsey's insurance company and the priest story is load-bearing.

4) A linguist in our language class pointed out that the 'old name' for sweetflag doesn't show up anywhere before a 1974 newsletter, where a schoolteacher seems to have made it up. I told her to keep teaching it. It's a living language, nobody's harmed. But I'll let a 1974 coinage be taught as the old name and won't let a rumor from the 1890s sit on a marker.

5) The mound. The state archaeologist's report says it's a glacial feature. The tribe says it's a burial place and didn't want the word 'natural' on our sign. I voted for their wording.

6) The oral map of the traditional territory covers about three times what any deed, treaty or survey supports, and they wanted the county's official map redrawn to match. I voted no — the county can't move lines on testimony, even though I'd bet the oral map is closer to where people actually hunted than the survey is. Here I side with paper not because I think it's true but because of what happens if we stop requiring it.

7) Two-column labels. We tried printing the archive's version and the community's version side by side with nothing to mark which is which. On the mill I thought that was a dodge. On the mound I thought it was the only decent option.

The rule I keep reaching for is 'the record wins on what happened, the community wins on what it means,' except 2, 3 and 4 are all about what happened, and I don't think '400,' or the priest, or the plant name is just meaning. My second try was 'the record wins when the memory accuses somebody, the memory wins when it credits somebody,' which dies on 1 versus 3, both accusations. I don't think I'm just siding with whoever's in the room, but I might be.

Is there a principle, or two, that gets all seven, or nearly? Which of my calls has to go if I want a clean rule, and why that one? And what does the surviving principle say about two I haven't hit yet: a family that wants a marker carrying a story the archive can't confirm, and a group whose memory we now know was invented in the 1970s by one person and spread from there?
