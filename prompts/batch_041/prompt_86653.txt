Im building a handout for a second-year grad seminar on cognitive penetration of perception, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than another prose summary. Last year's cohort just memorized the labels (encapsulation, direct content penetration, etc.) and I couldnt tell whether they actually held a view or were parroting, so this time the instrument has to sort them by what they say about cases, not by which name they like.

Start the tree from a commitment I think almost everyone in the room will grant: there are at least some cases where a belief, expectation, desire, or mood of yours seems to make a difference to what you perceptually experience. Take the first branch node from there, and make each subsequent node one of the distinctions that actually separates the positions. I want these axes on the tree, though you can phrase them however reads best:

1. Does the influence change the phenomenal character of the experience itself, or does it only change your judgment or confidence about an experience whose character is fixed? (content vs. judgment)
2. If it does change content: is the change smart, i.e. does it track reasons or normative relations between the penetrating state and what you end up seeing, or is it a brute associative push?
3. Does it work early, in a way that is insensitive to the rest of your beliefs, or does it only run through attention and selection?
4. Does the penetrating state have to be a belief, or can a desire, mood, emotion, or mere expectation do it? And does it have to be occurrent, or can a standing disposition do it?
5. Does the effect threaten the epistemic role of perception (does it stop the experience from justifying belief), or only the architectural claim that perception is encapsulated?
6. Is the effect best redescribed as ordinary Bayesian/predictive weighting (a prior doing its normal job) rather than a distinct penetration at all? And relatedly, is perceptual learning from training a case of penetration or a different phenomenon altogether?

Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, so nothing like do you believe perception is penetrable. Then place the leaves: the encapsulation/no-penetration defender, the direct-content-penetration view, the attention-mediated-only view, the epistemic-penetration view, the brute-association view, the predictive-weighting redescription, the perceptual-learning-is-different view, and one leaf for the everything-is-penetration-and-its-fine view. Name each leaf descriptively (dont bother with theorists names) and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. If your tree cant cleanly separate a pair of those leaves, say so and explain why instead of fudging it, and note any answer combinations that no leaf occupies and diagnose whether thats because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexamined.

Build the nodes out of concrete cases so people have something to answer. Use at least these, and feel free to add: the two lines that still look unequal after youve been told theyre equal; a radiologist who reports a faint nodule a novice reports not seeing; two people looking at the same poster, one hungry and one full, disagreeing about how big the sandwich looks; a friendly dog across the street that registers as a threat to someone whos afraid of dogs; a genuinely orange card that looks red to you because you were told it was red; a grandmaster who sees the mating pattern at a glance; and a wine that tastes more complex once youre told its expensive. Aim for six branch questions and no more than eight leaves. Keep it to one page if you can.
