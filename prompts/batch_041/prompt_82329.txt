Our school's philosophy club has forty-five minutes at its next meeting, and the member responsible for filling them is me: a junior, a whiteboard, and the club's poster of Escher's drawing of two hands. The topic I have chosen is the tangled hierarchy and the level that Hofstadter calls the inviolate level — the thought that a loop in which two levels each draw on the other, a hand drawing the hand that draws it, is possible at all only because some level is not caught up in the loop, so that what looks like a paradox is not one. My audience is eleven classmates who have all seen Escher's prints in the art room and none of whom has read Gödel, Escher, Bach; two of them have met the phrase strange loop on a podcast and are sure it means self-reference, and one will arrive expecting a lesson about optical illusion.

I am not asking you to explain the idea to me. I am asking for the plan of my explanation: which pieces have to be in place before which, and why that order cannot be shuffled; the one example the session should be built on, what it has to contain and what it must not, and where in the sequence it should first appear; the misconceptions my audience will bring or form, and the precise point at which each should be headed off; the question or task I should close with in order to find out whether the idea landed; and what I should deliberately leave out, saying out loud that I am leaving it out.

Two things I would like tested rather than accepted. First, I had intended to carry the session with something from our own club rather than with the picture. We peer-grade one another's short essays against a rubric that the members themselves rewrote at the end of last term, so the people who write the rules are the people the rules are applied to, and next term's rules will be written by those judged under these. I suspect this is either a mere feedback loop or an ordinary hierarchy with our faculty sponsor quietly at the top, and if so I need to know whether it can be repaired into a genuine tangle or whether I should drop it altogether — and if I drop it, why the drawing should carry the session in its place rather than, say, a hall of mirrors or two dictionaries defining each other's words.

Second, I would like the session to end with the members able to state in one sentence of their own what a tangled hierarchy is and what the inviolate level does for it. Tell me whether the sentence I would settle for — a loop of levels that is possible only because one level stands outside the loop — is the right target or a subtly wrong one, and whether I should announce it at the start as the destination or hold it back until the end.

Assume no mathematics and no formal logic on the audience's part, and assume that whatever I leave out they will meet later, out of order.
