I run a small metaethics reading group (four of us, one of whom is a Kantian who thinks the whole debate is confused) and next month we're doing the Frege-Geach problem. Every time we hit this topic we end up re-running the same three or four moves and calling it a day, so what I want instead is a sorting instrument: a five-question quiz that starts from the embedding data and ends by telling you which response to the problem you actually hold — sorted by what you say about cases, not by which label you like.

The data I want to start from, because it's the bit everyone agrees on: 'lying is wrong' seems to keep its sense when it turns up as the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, inside a question, and inside 'she believes that lying is wrong'; and the argument from 'lying is wrong' plus 'if lying is wrong then telling the children to lie is wrong' to 'telling the children to lie is wrong' is valid in whatever sense any argument is valid. Dont build the questions around 'which embedding is hardest' — build them around what you think the sense of 'wrong' is, and let the hard cases fall out as consequences.

Two things I want forced that we always skip. First, the negation case specifically, not just the conditional: if one person says lying is wrong and another says it isn't, where is the conflict supposed to live — in the two attitudes, in the two contents, or nowhere, because one of the two utterances isn't really a commitment at all? I want a question that makes you pick. Second, the cross-domain comparison: the same shape of puzzle turns up for 'might' (why is 'it might be raining and it isn't' out of order?) and for predicates of taste. I want at least one question whose answer tells me whether you think the moral case and the modal case are the same problem, and I want a leaf for the view that the analogy is superficial — that the modal case gets fixed by a parameter on the context or the information state and the moral case can't be, and that this is where expressivism actually breaks.

Positions that have to be reachable as leaves, even by an ugly route: the force-only line, where the antecedent just isn't asserted and the meaning has to be doing something else; quasi-realism / creeping minimalism, where you help yourself to the truth-conditional machinery and then deny you meant anything loaded by it; the view where the state expressed has possible-worlds structure and the logic comes free; the attitude-logic line where validity is a matter of which combinations of attitudes are inconsistent rather than of truth-preservation; the hybrid line where the embedded clause expresses a belief and the unembedded one a desire; inferentialism, where the sense of 'wrong' just is its inferential role and the puzzle evaporates; plain cognitivism; and error theory or pretense-based fictionalism, where the embedded clauses are genuine contents and none of them are true.

Two things to flag inside the relevant item rather than in a summary: any answer pattern the quiz lets you reach that nobody actually holds, with a one-clause diagnosis (incoherent / unattractive / just unexplored), and any two leaves the quiz can't seperate — my suspicion is that quasi-realism and straight realism are indistinguishable by any question about embeddings, and I want to know if you think that's right.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per question, no introduction and no closing summary — each item is the question, the answer options, and where each option lands you, kept to a sentence or two of actual prose apiece. And in whichever of the five you're least sure of yourself, say so and say why; I'd rather see the soft spot than a uniformly confident instrument.
