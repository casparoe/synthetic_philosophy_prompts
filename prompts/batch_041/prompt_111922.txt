I retired a few years ago — thirty-five years of soil chemistry, most of it spent measuring things nobody wanted measured — and since then I've gotten alot more interested in philosophy. I also somehow got asked onto the clinical ethics committee at our hospital, which meets Tuesdays and handles a couple of consults a week. I'm one of the two lay members. The recurring argument in that room is the one philosophers have apparently been having for fifty years: is there a real difference between killing someone and letting them die?

Here's what bugs me about it. A case comes in, the team is uneasy, and half the room says we'd be killing him while the other half says we'd just be letting the disease take its course. Same facts, two descriptions, both defensible, and then we vote on a feeling. In my old job that would have been disqualifying — if you can't say what you'd measure, you don't have a method. There's also some psychology on omission bias that I half remember, which makes me suspicious of my own intuitions here.

So I want the procedure we don't have. You're a philosopher who has worked on this distinction for years — the metaphysics of it (when, if ever, an omission is a cause of anything) and the ethics of it (whether the distinction carries moral weight of its own, and if not, what does the work instead). Those are two seperate questions, if they really are separate, and I'd like them kept that way. Think it through carefully, brainstorm a couple of candidate structures before committing to one, and don't hand me a survey of positions. No citations or reading lists please — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.

My working suspicion, to test rather than assume: every one of these arguments is really an argument about the baseline — what counts as the course of events that would have happened anyway — and the baseline gets set by policy, law and habit rather than by nature. In my old field we'd say the control condition is a choice. If I'm roughly right, the procedure should say exactly where a baseline is being set and mark it as a decision rather than a fact. If I'm wrong, say why.

It has to be runnable in ten minutes by people who aren't philosophers — me, the other lay member, a nurse, the chaplain, whoever the attending sends — and it has to end in a recommendation with reasons that the chair can write into one page for the attending to sign. It should be allowed to stop and hand a case back to the humans. And it mustn't just collapse into weighing consequences, because that's what we already do, and it's the move that lets everyone smuggle their gut feeling back in.

Then run it on three cases, and be specific:

1. A long-unresponsive patient fed through a tube; the family asks us to stop the feeds. Doing or allowing — and does the answer change if the feeds had never been started?

2. A competent patient with a progressive illness who has stopped eating and drinking on their own, understands what that means, and asks only for symptom relief. Some of the nurses think we'd be helping them die. I expect the procedure to struggle here and I want it to say where.

3. A family insisting on aggressive treatment the team now believes is causing net suffering. Watch how we flip descriptions — continuing is doing when the family wants it and allowing when the team wants to stop. Make the procedure say which description is doing the work.

At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like; flag which, and why. Tell me plainly what the procedure can't do and how I'd know I was using the wrong one.
