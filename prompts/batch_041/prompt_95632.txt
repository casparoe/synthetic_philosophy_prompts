A friend in my decision-theory reading group has been circulating an argument that expected value maximization refutes itself, and I can't find the break. The backdrop is the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads comes on toss n, the game pays 2^n dollars, so its expected value is infinite.

(1) A rational agent maximizes expected value.
(2) Now discount every St. Petersburg payoff by a billion dollars, with a floor at zero. The discounted game still has infinite expected value, because once the payoff has outgrown the discount, each toss contributes nearly a dollar to the expectation and there are infinitely many tosses. Yet this game pays a billion dollars less than the original in every outcome, and it pays nothing at all unless the coin first lands heads on the thirtieth toss or later.
(3) Two options with the same expected value give a rational agent no reason to prefer either, so she is indifferent between them.
(4) So she is indifferent between the St. Petersburg game and the discounted game — and since the discount can be made arbitrarily large while the expected value stays infinite, she is indifferent between the St. Petersburg game and lotteries that pay nothing with probability arbitrarily close to 1.
(5) An agent who is indifferent between a fortune and near-certain nothing is not rational, so (1) is false.

He is careful to say the trouble is not that you can't subtract from infinity; he is happy to run the whole thing in terms of the agent's preferences, which he says are perfectly well defined, rather than in terms of arithmetic on infinity. I would like to keep (1), and I would also like to keep the thought that the St. Petersburg game is worth playing. Name the step I should give up, explain why it looked innocent, and price the repairs: what do I pay to keep (1) with an amendment, and what do I lose if I can keep it only as a fragment of some larger theory?
