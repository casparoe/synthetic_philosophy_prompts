I spent thirty-five years as a seismologist, much of it trying to estimate recurrence intervals for faults whose histories I could only ever observe in fragments, and since retiring I have drifted into philosophy; the local philosophy of the social sciences seminar has now asked me to run a single ninety-minute session for about twenty participants — graduate students, two historians, and a pair of colleagues from my old department who come for the arguments. What I want out of the session is that the group should feel the force of the outside view in forecasting, the discipline of locating a case inside a reference class of comparable cases, reading a base rate off that class, and only then adjusting for what is peculiar to the case in hand, and I want them to feel it without my asserting it at them. So please design a runnable activity rather than a lecture: the participants should make consequential forecasts about the fates of polities, and the arithmetic of their own choices should do the teaching. I would also like the design to end by exposing a puzzle that occupied me for years on the other side of the disciplinary fence: once a reference class is fixed, a base rate does not by itself tell you whether a long-surviving member of the class is safer or in more peril than a young one — whether survival is evidence of robustness or only of luck, and whether the hazard in question is memoryless or accumulating. I remember the same split in hazard work, between those who read a long quiet interval as mounting strain and those who read it as carrying no information whatever, and I would like the group to arrive at that split themselves and to notice that it is a disagreement about the reference class rather than about the arithmetic.

The activity should have participants work from a deck of case cards describing polities, each card carrying whatever attributes you judge necessary for the mechanic to bite; tell me precisely what each card must contain, since I will prepare the deck myself. They should choose their own reference classes rather than be handed one, commit to forecasts with stakes that are real but involve no money, and be scored against a benchmark forecaster that uses nothing but base rates, so that the outside view visibly wins or loses against their narratives. I need the setting, the materials, the roles, the rules and payoff scheme, a run of show for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea — the inside/outside contrast, the gerrymandering of reference classes, the memorylessness puzzle — and that is frank about the one feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply myself.

Finally, I have run enough field exercises to expect that a good design fails in instructive ways on its first outing, so tell me what will predictably go wrong with a group of this composition and how I should handle it, and tell me which single feature of the run, if I saw it, should lead me to judge the design wrong — that is, what evidence would make you revise it, and where your reasoning about the mechanic is most likely to fail. Give me the whole thing as exactly five numbered items and nothing else, with no introduction and no closing summary, each item a dense sentence or two: the setup and materials; the rules, roles and stakes; the run of show; the debrief and what the activity cannot reproduce; and the predictable failures together with what would change your mind.
