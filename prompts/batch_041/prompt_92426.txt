I'm a second-year law student, and a seminar on criminal law has left me with a puzzle about the felony murder rule that I can't seem to resolve on my own. As I understand it, in a number of jurisdictions a death that occurs in the course of certain serious felonies is treated as murder even when the defendant neither intended nor foresaw the death — sometimes even when the fatal act was committed by a co-felon, by a police officer, or by the victim. Yet many of those same jurisdictions insist, both in their doctrine and in their rhetoric, that criminal liability requires a guilty mind: they separate murder from manslaughter by intent or recklessness, and they refuse to treat a genuine accident as homicide at all. What I find striking is that the felony murder rule appears to dispense with that requirement: the culpability seems to attach to the felony as a course of conduct, and the resulting death is then simply hung on it.

I'd like you to tell me what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose a retributivist view on which desert can attach to an act together with its foreseeable risks rather than to the death itself, a consequentialist view on which liability for deaths during dangerous crimes is a useful and inexpensive way of deterring those crimes, or some third position — a notion of attributing outcomes to an enterprise or a course of conduct rather than to an individual choice? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of punishment the doctrine seems to require, distinguish carefully what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify which rival theories would equally well license the rule, so that the doctrine turns out to be philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me where the rule is in tension with the mens rea requirement it coexists with, whether that tension amounts to a real inconsistency or only a superficial one, and what would have to change — in doctrine, in sentencing, or merely in how we describe what the defendant did — if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict retributivism that insisted culpability attach to the death itself. If I have misstated how the rule works in any jurisdiction, correct me rather than building on the error.
