I'm in a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and the assignment this week is to *design* a case rather than analyze one, which is proving harder than I expected: every case I build ends up with the same shape as everyone else's. The two positions we're working with are, first, the orthodox doctrine of the moral equality of combatants, in the form Walzer gives it in *Just and Unjust Wars*: soldiers on both sides of a war hold the same licence to kill and are equally liable to be killed, whatever the justice of the cause they serve, with the responsibility for an unjust war resting on the political leaders who chose it rather than on the men who fight it. Second is the revisionist position McMahan develops in *Killing in War*: the licence to kill in war tracks liability to be killed, liability tracks contribution to an unjust threat, and so combatants on the unjust side act impermissibly when they kill, though they may often be excused, while combatants on the just side, in killing those who are liable, do nothing wrong at all. Every case anyone writes runs the same way — the revisionist comes out harsher on the unjust soldier, more culpable and more punishable, and the orthodox doctrine looks like the indulgent one. I want the opposite result, and I want it to be generated by the theories rather than stipulated by me.

So please construct a single case, set in an ordinary modern conflict, in which the orthodox doctrine of moral equality turns out to be the harsher of the two — harsh, specifically, from the standpoint of the victim and the victim's family rather than the killer's — while the revisionist view, usually thought the unforgiving one, leaves those same people with something the orthodox view denies them. Hold everything else fixed: the two killings in the case should match in conduct, threat, numbers, and in the knowledge and voluntariness of the men involved, differing only in the justice of the cause each killer serves; both killings must be of combatants rather than civilians; and the case must not turn on ignorance, conscription, or duress, since those make the disagreement standard, nor may the verdicts be secured by quietly adopting a defective version of either doctrine — the harshness has to fall out of the best formulation of the orthodox view, that is, out of the fact that its licence attaches to the role rather than to any liability of the person killed. No references needed; I know the literature, what I need is the case.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping. State what each view says about (a) whether each killing is permissible, (b) whether either killer is culpable, (c) whether each victim was liable to be killed, and (d) what the victim's family may legitimately complain of; then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, say what happens if that feature is removed or reversed — in particular whether the case collapses back into the standard disagreement, and if so which premise flips — and finish by saying how a defender of the orthodox view would attack the case: what she would claim is smuggled in or misdescribed, and whether her reply costs her anything elsewhere.
