Write a short story of about 1,500 words about a practice of power that works neither by surveillance nor by punishment, but by requiring people to state in advance what they will do. Set it in an unremarkable institution — a hospital ward, a secondary school, a regional claims office — where the practice has arrived as a modest administrative improvement, and follow it far enough that its logic becomes visible: how the demand to predict oneself alters what people say about themselves, what counts as a true statement about a person, and what kinds of selves can survive being described in this way. The mechanism I am after is not being watched but being made to speak, and to speak in a form that can be filed, compared, and scored, so keep the vocabulary of surveillance and the panopticon out of it and treat power as productive rather than repressive. Do not explain any theory or name any theorists, and do not deliver a verdict on the practice; let the paperwork, the meetings, and the ordinary conversations carry the weight, and leave the ending unresolved, perhaps with someone who cannot or will not produce the required prediction. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
