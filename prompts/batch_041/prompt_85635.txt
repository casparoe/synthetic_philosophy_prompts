You are grading the final examination in a graduate course on social choice theory. The question below was worth 20 points, divided as indicated, and a student's answer follows it. Please assign a mark out of 20 and give the comments that justify it. I want a point-by-point assessment of the answer rather than a general impression: for each substantive claim the student makes, say whether it is correct and, where it is not, state precisely what is wrong and what should have been said instead. The comments should be specific enough that the student can see which sentences earned credit and which did not. Assess the answer as written; do not supply a model answer beyond what is needed to explain a defect.

Question (20 points).

Let S = {X, Y, Z} be the set of feasible social states, and suppose there are exactly two individuals, 1 and 2. A collective choice rule assigns to each admissible profile of individual weak orderings over S a social weak ordering of S. Consider the following requirements.

(U) Unrestricted Domain. Every profile of weak orderings over S is admissible.
(WP) Weak Pareto. If every individual strictly prefers s to t, then s is socially strictly preferred to t.
(ML) Minimal Liberalism. For each individual i there is at least one pair {s, t} over which i is decisive, where i is decisive over {s, t} just in case i's strict preference for s over t entails that s is socially strictly preferred to t.

Consider the profile P:

Individual 1 ranks the states Z ≻ X ≻ Y and is decisive over {X, Y}.
Individual 2 ranks the states Y ≻ Z ≻ X and is decisive over {Y, Z}.

(a) Determine what (WP) and (ML) jointly require of the social ordering at the profile P. (6 points)

(b) Say what your answer to (a) shows about the compatibility of (U), (WP) and (ML), and explain whether the difficulty should be traced to the profile P or to the requirements themselves. (8 points)

(c) A classmate proposes the following repair: the trouble lies in the social ordering's being cyclic, so we should require only that the social ordering be acyclic rather than transitive. Assess this proposal. (6 points)

Student's answer.

(a) Individual 1 is decisive over {X, Y} and strictly prefers X to Y, so X is socially strictly preferred to Y. Individual 2 is decisive over {Y, Z} and strictly prefers Y to Z, so Y is socially strictly preferred to Z. Both individuals strictly prefer Z to X, so by (WP) Z is socially strictly preferred to X. The social ordering must therefore contain X ≻ Y, Y ≻ Z and Z ≻ X. Nothing here is left unranked: at each of the three pairs the social ordering delivers a verdict, so the difficulty is not a failure of completeness.

(b) No weak ordering of S can contain all three of those strict preferences, since together they form a cycle and a cycle is inconsistent with transitivity. Hence no collective choice rule satisfies (U), (WP) and (ML) jointly, and the three requirements cannot all be met. The individuals' own preferences are not at fault — Z ≻ X ≻ Y and Y ≻ Z ≻ X are perfectly ordinary weak orderings, each internally consistent — so the difficulty lies in the requirements rather than in the profile. What generates the cycle is the combination of a Pareto condition sensitive to unanimous strict preference with a condition that hands an individual a pair of states to settle on their own. The result is not a new impossibility, however: it is Arrow's theorem in liberal dress. (ML) confers on each individual a decisive pair, and Arrow's argument shows that a decisive coalition generates a dictator, so the liberal paradox is a corollary of Arrow's theorem; in particular, the derivation presupposes the independence of irrelevant alternatives, which is the premise doing the real work in this family of results. Minimal liberalism is, as its name suggests, a very weak condition: it gives each individual the pairs of states that make up their own sphere of choice, and so makes each of them a dictator within that sphere, which is why the result is thought to be troubling.

(c) The proposal fails. Requiring acyclicity instead of transitivity is a genuine weakening in general — over a finite set it is enough to guarantee a maximal element, and hence to make a choice possible — but it cannot help here, because the social ordering required by (WP) and (ML) at P is already cyclic, and acyclicity forbids precisely that. Relaxing transitivity accomplishes nothing until one permits the social relation to contain cycles outright; and if the relation contains the cycle X ≻ Y ≻ Z ≻ X, no state is maximal, so nothing is chosen. The proposal therefore removes the impossibility only by abandoning the idea of a social decision procedure.
