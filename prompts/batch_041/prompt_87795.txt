I'm a fifth-year PhD student in metaphysics, and next month I'm giving a colloquium talk to a department whose composition has been described to me in enough detail to make me nervous. Roughly half of them are Humean best-system theorists about laws — two philosophers of physics and a philosopher of biology who works on mechanisms and mostly wants to know whether any of this touches actual science. The other half is a smaller but much louder group of dispositional essentialists who hold that properties are irreducibly powerful and who seem to regard the entire finks-and-masks literature as a museum of intuitions in search of a theory. I'm also told there will be two or three cognitive-science students who have read a great deal of Gibson and will want to know whether I am just relabeling affordances. I have thirty-five minutes and about twenty-five minutes of Q&A, and I've been workshopping this with a friend who does causal modeling — we met years ago arguing about do-calculus on a forum — who keeps insisting that my central argument is the modularity assumption in disguise. I think he's wrong, but I can't tell whether he's wrong in an interesting way.

Here is the thesis I'll be defending, as unlovely as it is. The standard menu — the glass that would become shatter-proof if struck, the glass packed in foam that doesn't shatter when struck — is usually read as showing that dispositions need a ceteris paribus clause, with the fight being over whether that clause can be specified without circularity. I want to argue that the fight is mislocated. Take any disposition claim and any pair of worlds differing only in some background condition: I claim there is always a relabeling of the stimulus type under which the two worlds agree about which disposition is present, and always another relabeling under which they disagree, and that nothing in the object or in the counterfactuals privileges one relabeling over the other. Call this the shuffle. If it works, the upshot is not that dispositions are unreal but that they are relational — a disposition is a three-place affair between an object, a stimulus type, and a background ensemble — and the question of whether the masking condition belongs to the disposition or to the stimulus has no answer independent of our interests in drawing the line. I call the resulting view circumstantial dispositionalism. I am not defending a conditional analysis, and I am explicitly not defending the intrinsicality of the causal basis; that last part is the one I expect to cost me.

What I need from you is not a defense of the view but a Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections this audience will actually raise, not the ones I'd get from a reading group of sympathetic dispositionalists: ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each objector would actually phrase it (the best-system theorists and the essentialists will say entirely different things about the shuffle, and the mechanist will say something else again), each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't burn my credibility on the wrong hill, and tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion about what it is, and I'd like to be told whether I'm right about that too.
