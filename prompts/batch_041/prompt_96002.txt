Compose a mock instructional handbook — the kind of blandly cheerful, over-formatted pamphlet a consultancy or a licensing body might issue — under a title such as "Standing: A Practical Handbook for the Recently Offended," published by a fictional organization of your own invention (an institute, a bureau, a faculty of "relational accounting"; name it as you please, but do not attribute it to any real body, author, or text).

The handbook's premise is that a reader may select, incident by incident, among three ways of holding their social worth. The first treats worth as a possession held in the eyes of others, damaged by insult and repaired by a proportionate public response. The second treats worth as intrinsic, so that insult cannot reach it and the correct response to a slight is to decline to treat it as one. The third treats worth as claimed through acknowledged injury, so that standing accrues to those whose suffering is recognized — preferably by an institution. Give each track a serviceable in-house name, and make the handbook's central instrument a ledger on which injuries are entered and acknowledgments credited.

Requirements:

- The humor must come from getting each track's internal logic right rather than from inventing absurdities. A reader who knows the scholarship on honor, dignity, and the moral capital of victimhood should see that the handbook understands the positions; a reader who does not should still follow the joke.
- No track may be a straw man. Dignity must not be written as mere repression, passivity, or cowardice; honor must not be mere vanity or violence; the third track must not be mere attention-seeking or fraud. Each must be given its strongest available self-description.
- Include a set of numbered rules for classifying an incident: who may be answered; what counts as an acknowledgment; when an apology creates a debt rather than discharging one; what the ledger does with an injury whose author cannot be answered at all (a firm, a crowd, the dead). The three tracks must issue incompatible instructions, and the incompatibility must be visible from the rules themselves, never announced.
- Include one worked case of your own invention, run through all three tracks, ending in three mutually exclusive recommended responses.
- Include a brief "common errors" section or FAQ.
- Stay deadpan. No narrator's wink, no break in frame to tell the reader which track is correct. The handbook should present all three as products on offer, so that a reader finishes unable to say whether the consumerist framing is the joke or the doctrine.
- Do not quote or cite any real text, author, or work, and put no real person's words in quotation marks; any quoted material should come from the handbook's own fictional authorities.
- About 800 words, laid out as a handbook: title, sections, numbered rules, the worked case, the closing FAQ.
