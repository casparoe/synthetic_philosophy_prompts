I spent 35 years in a corrosion lab and retired two years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read instrument manuals — obsessivly and without much of a plan. I keep running into the fink/mask business about dispositions and it's got under my skin, partly because it's about my old job and partly because I think it's about more than my old job.

Here's what set me off. In my line we said things like "this alloy is ductile" and put it in a report and nobody blinked. But I had a batch once that passed the bend test every time if you bent it slow, and snapped if you bent it fast. Same bar, same afternoon. Was it ductile or not? My honest answer at the time was: it's ductile-at-this-strain-rate, and the rest is bookkeeping. Then there was the sample we were sure was inert until we found the surface had passivated in air — reactive as anything underneath, it just had a coat on. And then the case that actually bothers me: a run where everything "failed" because the furnace thermocouple was reading forty degrees off. Nobody would call that a property of the material, but at the level of "it didn't do the thing when prodded," it looks exactly like the other two.

As I understand it, philosophers have names for some of this — the disposition getting destroyed by the very thing that would have triggered it (a fink, I think) versus something external blocking the manifestation while the disposition sits there intact (a mask). Fine. What I want from you is the most important implications of all this, and I care about two places in particular.

1. What it means for what a lab report is claiming when it says a material "is" something. If every dispositional claim is implicitly indexed to a test setup, in what sense is any of it a claim about the material rather than about the material-plus-world? And is my thermocouple case a mask or just a botched experiment — where exactly is that line, and does anything hang on it?

2. What it means for character. I'd like to think my grandson is honest and that my late colleague was a generous man, and I gather there's a whole literature saying traits don't predict behavior and I'm fooling myself. If masks and finks are apparently everywhere in metals, why wouldn't they be just as common in people — and does that gut trait-talk or only complicate it?

Don't hand me a survey. I want the implications ranked by how much they'd actually change how I think, not by how famous they are. Tell me how confident you are in each one, and flag the single point you're least sure about. Keep it under 600 words — I'm old and my attention span was never good to begin with.
