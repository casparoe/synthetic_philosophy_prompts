For two years a graduate seminar I teach on explanation in the social sciences has been unable to get past a single dispute. The structuralists, in the tradition of Braudel, hold that the proper object of historical explanation is the long run — geography, climate, demography, the slow rhythms of material life — and that events and individuals are surface foam; their critics reply that only events and choices explain anything, that structures are just aggregated events, and that the longue durée quietly turns agency into fate. Each side knows the other's arguments by heart, and neither has moved.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not so much contradicting one another as answering different questions under a single heading — one asking why a pattern proved so durable and so hard to alter, the other asking why this particular thing happened rather than something else — and that the quarrel is at least partly a confusion between a claim about the causal weight of structures and a claim about what deserves explaining. But I suspect something substantive survives the disambiguation: whether long-run structures are causally primary or merely a convenient level of description, and whether robust macro-level regularities exist at all. So tell me which parts of this impasse are verbal, which are a clash of explanatory interests, and which are a real disagreement about causation and levels of explanation; then, on that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result about emergence, or the concession that the question was always two questions.
