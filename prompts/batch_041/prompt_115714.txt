I'm a backend engineer on a persistent multiplayer world — the kind of place people log into for years. I have no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up with a question I can't shake, so I'd like you to write a short story that puts it under pressure instead of answering it.

First, the machinery, because the story has to be honest about it. Our engine does not keep objects. Each region is stored as a seed plus a small state vector, and entities are computed on demand, only for as long as somebody is interacting with them; when the last interaction ends, the entity is discarded, and the next observer gets a freshly computed instance that satisfies the same description. Between observations there is nothing there — not a dormant object, nothing. Separately, we keep an operation log that lets us roll a region back to any earlier point. After a rollback the region is exactly what it was, and no future interaction can distinguish it from the region as it stood before the incident.

Last month two residents collided in a way that turned into an argument in our own team channel. One of them had spent about nine months assembling a large structure out of parts that could only be earned one at a time through separate events, so the thing was as much a record of a sequence of actions as it was a shape. Another resident wrecked it. Support rolled the region back; the shape returned. The builder says that what stands there now is a replacement, that the destruction still happened, and that the platform has never given a straight answer about which of those two claims it actually believes. Our community lead says nothing was destroyed in the first place, since there was never an object there to destroy; what happened was a computation that got interrupted and then recomputed. The builder, who has been sleeping badly since, says that if her distress is the only real thing in the exchange, then the platform is quietly admitting that the world is furniture and she is the only person in it. A third resident — someone who runs a business inside the world, has most of her friends there, and spends more hours there than outside — says they are both missing the point: what the rollback really demonstrates is that anything anyone does in the world can be revoked by the operator at will, which makes the question of what is real beside the point.

Please write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in the third person, that stages this dispute.

Some constraints:

- Three or four characters at most, each holding a position you can make genuinely persuasive. Include the builder, someone who argues from the platform's side, and someone who lives most of her life in the world and disagrees with both.
- Include a scene in which someone tries to settle the matter by demonstrating the emptiness directly — pulling up the tooling and showing a region in which there is nothing to find — and a later scene in which that demonstration clearly settles nothing.
- Make the rollback and the operator's power to revoke count as more than plot: at least one character should draw a conclusion from it about what kind of place the world is.
- Let the story move between the studio and the inside of the world if that helps.
- Do not name or cite philosophers, do not let anyone deliver a lecture, and do not let the narrator take a side. End on a concrete moment of action or speech rather than a summary or a moral.
- Please avoid the cheap resolution in which somebody observes that the world is only data or only signals and everyone realizes the question was confused.
