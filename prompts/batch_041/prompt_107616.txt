I've been working through a seminar on drug policy — we spent a couple of weeks on Portugal's move to decriminalize personal possession and on the state-by-state legalization of cannabis here in the United States — and I keep running into a framing that I can't quite get to sit still. The claim, as I've heard it put, is what people sometimes call the iron law of prohibition: that intensifying enforcement pressure on a drug market does not so much shrink the market as push it toward more potent, more compact, easier-to-conceal forms of the drug, on the theory that potency per unit weight is what best survives interdiction. The stock illustration is alcohol prohibition breeding distilled spirits rather than beer, and the stock modern illustration is the American opioid story — prescription pills giving way to heroin and then to illicitly manufactured fentanyl, with the fatal-overdose curve climbing the whole way. I'm not asking for a reading list, a bibliography, or citations; I want the analysis itself, and I want it done carefully rather than gestured at.

So take the American opioid transition — roughly from the OxyContin era, through the 2010 reformulation of that drug and the subsequent turn to heroin, to the fentanyl wave that now dominates the overdose figures — and analyze it as a case of the iron law of prohibition. Map the case onto the theory with some discipline: who the relevant actors were, what options enforcement pressure actually removed or rearranged for them, and whether the law explains the timing, the sequence, and the geography of the escalation or merely redescribes the outcome after the fact. Then run the inference the other way and let the case test the law. Is it a genuine causal regularity, a rational-actor prediction about the economics of smuggling, or a selection effect that would surface under nearly any enforcement regime? And what do the decriminalization and legalization cases do to it — does Portugal's experience, or the potency trajectory of legal cannabis, count as evidence for the law, evidence against it, or as a case the law never anticipated? Be explicit about where the theory goes silent rather than forcing it to speak to everything, and please write the entire answer in American English.
