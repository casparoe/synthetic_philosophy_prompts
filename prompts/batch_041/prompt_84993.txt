ok so I'm in this third-year constitutional theory course and we've just done the week on precommitment — Ulysses and the mast, constitutions as chains we put on our future selves, all that. Two people in my seminar, Dan and Priya, went at each other for a good twenty minutes and the tutor had to cut them off, and I definately couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you referee? Here's the gist, roughly in order.

Dan: A precommitment is either toothless or it isn't yours. If future-you can undo it, it did nothing. If future-you can't, then it isn't you doing the deciding anymore — you've handed the wheel to a device, a doctor, a court. Either way, "I bound myself" is a confused way of talking. The binding is always done by someone or something other than the self being bound.

Priya: That's a false dilemma. Tying yourself to the mast changes your options, it doesn't decide for you. The later you still chooses; she just chooses from a different menu. That's what acting on a plan across time just is. And the fact that you could technically cut the rope doesn't make the rope pointless — it's the cost of cutting that does the work, same as with any promise.

Dan: Then everything hangs on the rope being expensive to cut. Make it cheap and it's theatre; make it expensive and you've effectively created a second person who rules you. And notice who's been wronged. If I break a promise to you, you have a complaint. If I break a promise to my earlier self — who exactly is complaining? There's nobody home. That's why "self-binding" contracts aren't really binding; there's no second party to hold you to it.

Priya: The later self wrongs the earlier one — or wrongs the person considered as one life rather than as a string of strangers. You keep assuming the victim has to be someone who exists right now. Think of it as a partnership stretched over time: the earlier self sinks capital into a plan, the later self spends it. That's a wrong even if the earlier self isn't around to lodge the complaint.

Dan: Then say what the wrong consists in, in neutral terms. "She spent her own money on what she now values" doesn't sound like a wrong. And if the earlier self is still around, then she's just one party among two, and she doesn't get to outvote the other one. You can't have it both ways.

Priya: Ok, concrete. Four of us in the flat agreed to put a tenner in a jar every time we skip the gym, and the jar goes to a party none of us would ever vote for. Two months in, I want out. Is it wrong for me to just take my money back and stop paying?

Dan: Depends who set it up. If you did it to yourself, take it back, it's yours. If all four of us agreed, then you need the rest of us to let you out — but that isn't a Ulysses contract, that's just an ordinary deal with other people, and what binds you is them, not you.

Priya: But the whole reason we set the thing up is that future-us will want out. A rule that says "you may leave whenever you like" is not a rule. The point of the arrangement is precisely that your later preference doesn't get a veto.

Dan: Right, and look what that does: now the three of us who want to dissolve it can't, because one of us won't agree. So a minority binds a majority, permanently, in the name of a decision the majority no longer endorses. That's not autonomy over time, that's a dead hand.

Priya: Same thing at the level of a constitution, no? A majority entrenches something so that a later majority can't undo it without clearing a higher bar. Call that a dead hand if you want, but the effect is that we protect ourselves collectively from our own worst moments.

Dan: Or the effect is that people who are dead bind people who are alive, and we call it self-government because we like the sound of it.

Priya: And then my cousin's case. She signed one of those psychiatric advance directives when she was well, saying that if she goes off her meds she can be held for a week. She's fine now, off the meds, and wants the thing gone, and the clinic says it stands. Is the clinic binding her, or is she binding her?

Dan: The clinic is binding her. And notice the trap: the only person who can tell you whether she's currently the good judge or the bad judge is her, and the whole point of the directive was to distrust her judgment. So the directive can never be checked against the very state it was written for. It just permanently assumes the worst of her.

So: who's right about what? Go through it move by move — I'd much rather have a verdict on each step than a mush — and then say who has the better of the overall argument, and what both of them are missing. And since our essay question is basically "is a constitution a Ulysses contract or just a supermajority rule?", if you could tell me which bits of this argument survive the translation to the constitutional case and which ones break, that'd help me alot.
