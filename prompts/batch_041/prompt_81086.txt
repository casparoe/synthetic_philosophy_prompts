I'm a high school student, and I'm writing a piece for the humor page of our school literary magazine, something I might also read aloud at our philosophy club. I'd like you to write it for me: a parody product review, about 700 words, in the voice of a mildly disappointed tech reviewer who has spent a week with a consumer device that claims to read the wearer's thoughts in the brain's own language.

The device's pitch is the whole theory. Thinking, on this view, is not done in English or in any spoken language, but in an inner language with its own words and its own grammar, a language every human being thinks in and that nobody is ever taught, because we are born already speaking it. The headset claims to eavesdrop on that inner language and render it into English.

What I want is a review that is funny because the theory is right, not because the gadget is broken. The jokes should come from the hypothesis's own commitments, so that a reader who knows it laughs and a reader who has never heard of it still follows. That means, for instance: because thoughts are supposed to be built out of parts arranged by rules (compositionality), and because any thought can be rebuilt from the same parts in a new arrangement (systematicity), the device should handle a thought it has never encountered but should choke on a genuinely new primitive concept, the way a calculator handles new numbers but not a new operation. Because the inner language is supposed to be the same for everyone, the headset should work as well on me as on a stranger who speaks no English, and should probably work on my little cousin and maybe even on the dog. Because the basic concepts are supposed to be innate and indefinable in terms of anything simpler, the manufacturer's training mode should turn out to be either a con or a quiet admission that the theory is false. And because the device is supposed to be reading a language rather than guessing, it should occasionally hand back a perfectly clear thought for which I have no English word at all, leaving me unable to say what the word meant.

I also want the reviewer to notice that the headset returns thoughts with their logical form showing, subjects and predicates and quantifiers where the reviewer expected a feeling, and to try the rival product, which makes no claim to find a language in the head and simply predicts what you will say next from statistics, and to be unable to settle which one is closer to the truth. When the reviewer calls support to ask what the inner symbols mean, the answer should come out circular in a way that is funny rather than stupid.

Please keep it deadpan throughout: a real reviewer's tone, fair to the product, never winking at the reader, and never letting the reviewer conclude that thinking is just inner speech in English or that the hypothesis is obviously false. By the end the reader should not be able to tell whether the device is a scam, a genuine window into the mind, or both. Don't name any real scientists, don't cite any papers, and don't invent a fake study, because I want it to read as a review and not a report. Formatting: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics for emphasis, no tables, no pros-and-cons box, no star rating. One continuous review in ordinary prose, the way a long review on a website reads.
