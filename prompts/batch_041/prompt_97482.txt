And the student wrote:

"Friedman's thesis is that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to how good the theory is; what matters is only whether the theory's predictions are accurate. His example is the leaves on a tree, which arrange themselves as if each leaf were trying to maximise the sunlight it gets — nobody thinks the leaves are actually calculating anything, and that doesn't stop the hypothesis being useful. So for (b)(i), the fact that not every patient completes their course doesn't matter: as long as the model predicts hospital resistance rates well, the assumption is doing its job. It's just an idealisation, the sort of thing you put in to get the maths going and then relax once you want more precision, like treating a pendulum as a point mass. For (b)(ii), the same reasoning carries over. If the model already predicts resistance accurately at the hospital level, the assumption of full completion is evidently good enough, so there's no reason it should stop us using the model to advise individual doctors; the level at which the prediction is made doesn't change what makes a theory good. For (c), I think on Friedman's view there's basically no legitimate criticism of an assumption as such — you can only ever criticise a theory by testing its predictions, so any objection of the form 'but people don't really do that' is beside the point. That's what the F-twist is, the twist being that the assumption doesn't need to be true. I'd add that Friedman still thinks a theory should be simple, in the sense of explaining a lot with a little, so a model with loads of assumptions would be worse for that reason."
