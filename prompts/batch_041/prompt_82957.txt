A friend of mine in the physics dept and I have a recurring arguement about why there's anything at all. He thinks the question is a leftover from theology, I think there's something to it, and the other night he tried to close the whole thing out with "nothing comes from nothing" — he says it's just obvious, that it's what physics says anyway, and that the question of why there is something rather than nothing is therefore either answered by the fact that stuff has just always been around, or else it was never a real question. That felt to me like one sentence doing about four jobs at once and none of them well, but when I tried to say which of the four he was actually leaning on, I couldn't. So what I want is a menu: take that one sentence and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, not a verdict on which one is right. For each reading, what exactly is being asserted — what "nothing" means in it (a state of affairs? an absence of stuff? a cause? a material?), whether the claim is causal or modal or about materials or about explanation, what it quantifies over, and whether it's a logical truth, a substantive metaphysical thesis, a contingent generalization, a presupposition of doing inquiry at all, or just a bit of Latin that sounds deeper than it is — and then a verdict on each: trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Also tell me which reading the people who actually say this usually have in mind.

Two more things I'd like folded in, because they're what make the argument go in circles. First, he keeps adding that the vacuum isn't nothing and that quantum fields fluctuate, and he treats that as physics confirming the slogan rather than complicating it — I want to know which of the readings survive contact with that move and which get embarrassed by it. Second, the same sentence gets used in the opposite direction too, as a demand for an explanation rather than a refusal to give one ("you can't just say it's a brute fact, nothing comes from nothing"), and if one sentence can be both a demand and a dismissal then something is shifting in what it means depending on who's holding it — I want to see exactly where the shift is. I suspect the reading that's actually doing the work in our argument is one that makes the question unanswerable in principle rather than answered, which would be funny, but I'd rather you show me the menu and let me pick than tell me what I think. Lay it out as a list I can take back to him.
