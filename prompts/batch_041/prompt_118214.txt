I am a high school student, and I have spent the better part of this year — through a theology reading group, a philosophy elective, and a great deal of arguing with anyone who would listen — arriving at a position I now hold and would like you to take seriously as my own. I believe that God has what is usually called middle knowledge. By this I mean that before God decides anything about what to create, He already knows, concerning every possible free creature, what that creature would freely choose in any set of circumstances in which He might place it. The payoff, as I understand it, is that God can arrange the circumstances of a person's life so that the person freely does what God intends, without God causing or compelling the choice, precisely because God knows beforehand what the person would freely do in those circumstances. I take this to be the only account that secures at once a libertarian freedom of the will (the kind on which a person genuinely could have done otherwise) and a genuinely sovereign, providential God who is not merely reacting to His creatures.

What I would like from you is an honest map of the position's exposure — what would have to be true for it to be false. Please separate the objections that would be decisive if they succeeded from those that would be merely awkward, and tell me which of them the position's own defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, not only the ones its critics press. I am especially concerned with the following, though I do not want to be limited to them. First, the counterfactuals on which the whole view rests — the claims about what a creature would freely do — are supposed to be true before the creature has made any choice at all; what, if anything, makes them true, and can a claim be true if nothing makes it true? Second, if a fact about what I would freely choose is already settled before I exist, in what sense is the choice still mine and still free? Third, does the view predict anything, or rule anything out, that a rival account of providence and freedom does not — and if it makes no observable difference, is that a fatal defect in a view of this kind or simply the ordinary condition of theology and metaphysics? Fourth, when its defenders patch these holes, does the view quietly turn into a different one — for instance, into a view on which the freedom in question is not libertarian freedom at all?

I should say plainly that I would be most persuaded by a defeat aimed at the middle knowledge itself rather than at the surrounding theology, since the middle knowledge is the part doing all the work. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and close by telling me whether the position is well defended or merely well insulated — and whether, on your account, I ought to care about the difference.
