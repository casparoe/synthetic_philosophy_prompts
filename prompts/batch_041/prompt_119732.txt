A friend and I have been trying to break an argument we put together after a seminar, and we cannot find the fault line. The conclusion is absurd, so we assume there is one.

(1) Each agent in a sequence chooses the option with the higher expected payoff, given her private evidence and everything she can infer from the choices of those ahead of her.
(2) Once two agents in a row have chosen the same option, a rational agent will follow them: two agreeing pieces of evidence, arrived at independently, outweigh the single piece of evidence she holds herself.
(3) So she chooses the same option whatever her own evidence says.
(4) So her choice is independent of her private evidence.
(5) So her choice tells those behind her nothing about her private evidence, and they remain exactly as confident as she was.
(6) By induction, the same holds for every agent after her.
(7) Hence the outcome of any such sequence is fixed by its first two choices: however many further agents join, and however well informed each of them is, none of their evidence can alter the result.
(8) Therefore a large group of rational agents learning from one another is no more likely to be right than a very small one, and adding better-informed members is worthless.

That proves too much. It would mean that a market cannot aggregate dispersed information and that a crowd is epistemically no better than a pair. I am attached to (1) and to the negation of (8). My friend suspects the induction in (6), but (6) looks to me like the safest step in the argument; and since (1) already builds in full rationality and full inference from others' choices, "the agents are simply being irrational" cannot be the answer either.

Which step should we give up, and what does giving it up cost? Tell us in particular what would have to be true of the options, the evidence, or the situation for the conclusion to come back, and how much of the outcome is genuinely fixed by the order in which agents happen to move.
