Got asked to give my take on whether immoral art can be good art, and I want to make sure the thing isn't a mess before I say it in front of people. Here's the package: (1) the value of a work just is the value of the experience it affords an informed, attentive audience; (2) the moral and the aesthetic are seperate axes — how good something is morally doesn't tell you how good it is as art; (3) a work can be better as art for being morally ugly, sometimes the ugliness is doing real aesthetic work; (4) the artist's life, character and crimes have no bearing on the value of the work; (5) a work is morally defective only when it endorses the bad attitude or invites you to share it — depicting something isn't endorsing it; (6) but I also think a work can be morally defective for what it dosen't see. What put me onto (6): there's a novel I love, gorgeously written, about a colonial administrator in the 1890s — tender with his kids, polite to his wife, and completely incurious about what his job is doing to the people it's being done to. The book never winks, never lectures, never endorses. I still want to say it's morally rotten, and that the rottenness is a flaw in the book, not just in the man. So which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, how bad is each clash, and what's the cheapest thing that has to move — and what does moving it cost me?
