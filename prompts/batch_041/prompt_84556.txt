I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I sit on the programming board of an annual festival run by our press association: three days of panels and keynotes, roughly forty speakers, funded mostly by member dues and one small foundation grant. Last spring we invited a veteran science writer to give the closing keynote. Within a week of the announcement, a campaign circulated screenshots of an essay she had published arguing that a contested claim in her field had been prematurely foreclosed, and argued that a keynote platform was itself an endorsement. We withdrew the invitation, invoking a clause in our own speaker agreement that permits withdrawal when a speaker's participation would materially damage the festival. She has now asked us either to reinstate her next year or to explain publicly why we will not, and two board members have tabled rival motions: one to reinstate with an apology, one to adopt a standing rule that an invitation, once issued, cannot be rescinded except for fraud, harassment, or a criminal charge.

We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand. What I actually need is an argument about whether what we did counts as punishment. If withdrawing a platform is a punishment, it seems to owe what punishments owe: a stated norm, proportionality, a chance for the accused to answer, no penalizing twice for one act. I do not see how this board supplies any of those by Thursday. If it is not punishment but simply a private body declining to amplify a view it rejects, then the campaign did the sanctioning and our only duty is not to serve as its instrument.

Please recommend one of the two motions, with a concrete course of action and a time frame, and do it without treating punishment as a matter of how the disinvited person feels. Take the following test as mine, and tell me whether it survives contact with this case: a platform withdrawal is a sanction only where the withdrawing body is either the only practical route to an audience the person needs, or is enforcing a norm it has never before applied to anyone in the same position — and where neither condition holds, it is an exercise of association, not a penalty. I am especially unsure whether an institution can punish by custom rather than by rule, and whether the same act can be punishment and association at once.

Give the board a recommendation it can vote on, with the reasoning, and give the strongest version of the case against your own recommendation — the member who believes we have handed a veto to whoever can generate the most email — the hearing it would get if that member had written the memo instead. I don't need citations; I need the argument. And tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would move you to the other motion, and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
