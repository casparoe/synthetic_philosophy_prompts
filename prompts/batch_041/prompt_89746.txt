My roommate and her brother got into a fight about this on a long drive, and I got drafted as referee, and I have to admit I couldn't settle it. Here's how it went, as close as I can remember.

Dana (the roommate) has been reading about factory farming and she's ended up somewhere she calls a lexical threshold view. The way she put it: pain and pleasure are not on the same scale. If they were, then some amount of mild pleasure would have to outweigh a day of agony — just keep adding pleasure and eventually the sums flip. She says that's obviously wrong, so there is no common currency between them. She's a negative utilitarian about it, so for her it's specifically the bad stuff that has priority: there's a threshold of intensity, and once an experience is above it, no amount of lesser good can buy it off. Below the threshold, she says, small pains and small pleasures trade off normally. (She said the no-common-currency thing early and then the trade-off thing later, and I think even she noticed those don't sit together.)

Mo (her brother) came at it on four fronts and I couldnt tell how many of them landed.

1) Where's the threshold? Any particular number is arbitrary, and if she says it's vague, then you get cases where a hair's difference in intensity flips which of two worlds is better by an infinite amount. He said the relation she wants isn't Archimedean and that she should just admit that's what she's buying.

2) The galaxy of stubbed toes. Fine, he said, so a world with one minute of torture in it is worse than a world where every being in the galaxy has a mild headache for an hour, no matter how many beings you stack up. He says he'd take the stubbed toes over the torture, and so would she, and that this is a reductio.

3) If severe pain lexically dominates, you can never justify any of it for any benefit. He brought up a surgery that involves brief excruciating pain and buys you fifty good years, and said her view forbids it, which is insane.

4) The self-undermining one: if the two really are incommensurable, then there's no fact of the matter about which world is better, so she can't say her view is the correct one — she's using better and worse while denying the thing that makes them mean anything.

Dana answered all of it. On 1 she said vague doesn't mean arbitrary, there's no sharp line between bald and not bald either and nobody concludes that hair doesn't matter; and she said the discontinuity complaint assumes betterness has to come in amounts proportional to the stuff it's about, whereas you can have an ordering where one thing always comes first and the other only counts on a tie, like alphabetical order but with severity as the first letter. On 2 she mostly bit the bullet, but said he was cheating by making the stubbed-toe world so enormous that it starts to seem horrible in its own right; if each discomfort is genuinely trivial and separate, she says, it never adds up to the agony. On 3 she said he'd smuggled a benefit into the premise, and the whole dispute is whether any benefit can do that. On 4 she said she isn't claiming they're incomparable — she's claiming one ordering with a break in it, not two separate scales, so worse still works, it's just not representable by any number you could add up.

Mo's last shot was that a view where nothing can ever compensate can't guide any real decision, since it tells you to spend unbounded resources preventing the one bad thing; and that once she makes the threshold precise it either collapses back into ordinary adding-up (if the threshold sits at zero) or it turns out she isn't talking about amounts of pain at all but about a different kind of thing, which is a different claim entirely.

They want me to say who's right. Can you go through it move by move — each of the four numbered objections, and each of her replies to them — and rate them from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each? Then say who's more right overall, and what each of them is missing. I'd also really like to know whether their even disagreeing about the same thing in places, or just talking past each other.
