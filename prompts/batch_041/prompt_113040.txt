In three weeks I have to present a paper to a room that is going to go at me hard, and what I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period -- not a summary of my argument, not a pep talk.

Here's what I'm defending. The question "why are some nations rich?", in the grand form the literature poses it -- how much of today's income differences is institutions, how much is geography, how much is culture -- is not just unanswered but unanswerable, and unanswerable in a way thats more interesting than "science is hard". The argument: the deep-cause debate treats the causes as additive shares of the variance; any share claim presupposes a well-defined distribution of counterfactual histories; we have exactly one realization of history, so there is no population to sample from; so the debate is ill-posed rather than merely open, and the local effects we can identify from natural experiments (the two Koreas, the two Germanys, twin cities either side of a border) don't agregate into an answer to the grand question. Gould's replay-the-tape thought experiment, but for economic history. The corollary I'll get hammered on: a great deal of very good work in this area is answering a question that can't be answered.

The room, so you can aim at the right people: about half applied development economists who live and die by identification -- IV, RD, diff-in-diff -- and who will hear "shares of the variance" and reach for their knives; one economic historian of early modern trade who thinks the whole debate is presentist and that everyone is reading the 20th century backwards into the 16th; a political scientist who works on state capacity and will defend institutions as the fundamental cause to the death; a geographer who thinks latitude, disease environment and transport costs do most of the work; and two grad students who will bring up the newest "deep roots" material -- genetic distance between populations, the timing of the Neolithic transition, the rice-vs-wheat psychology stuff -- and ask why none of it is in my taxonomy.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get at a philosophy of social science conference. Order them by how much damage they'd do to me, phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it, and for each give me a two-minute reply plus an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I'm not burning credibility on the wrong fights, which objection I should just grant and move past, and finish with the one I'm hoping nobody asks. Don't worry about citing literature -- I know the literature, I need to know what this room is going to do to me.
