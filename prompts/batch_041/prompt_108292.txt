I picked up a short popular book on voting reform while I was stuck at an airport, and the chapter on what the author calls the impossibility theorems has been nagging at me for weeks. I have no background in any of this — I work in hospital administration, and the last time I wrote out an argument with numbered premises was in secondary school — so I cannot tell where a careful writer would draw the line between compressing a hard result for a general reader and simply getting it wrong. Here is the passage as best I can reconstruct it:

'The theorem that Arrow proved, and that Gibbard and Satterthwaite later sharpened into the version now carrying their names, is the deepest result in the field. It proves that no voting system can be fair. Once there are three or more candidates, every method of adding up votes — ranked, scored, or anything else — can be manipulated: some voter will always have a way to get a better outcome by voting for something other than what she really wants, and this is not an accident of this or that ballot design but a mathematical certainty. The only arrangement that escapes it is one in which a single person decides. That is why the authors concluded that the search for a perfect voting rule is hopeless, and why serious reformers have quietly given up on ranked ballots in favour of approval voting, where you simply tick every candidate you could live with.'

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Claim by claim, what would someone who actually works on this subject accept as roughly right, what is the kind of shorthand a specialist would wince at but let pass in a book for general readers, and what is straightforwardly false? Two claims bother me in particular. The first is that the result is about fairness at all. My impression, from the little I have read, is that it concerns whether a voter has an incentive to misrepresent her preferences, and it seems to me that a rule under which one person's wishes always prevail would do very well on the incentive question while doing terribly on any question of fairness, which makes me suspect the author has run two different results together. The second is the final move, from the truth of a mathematical result to a recommendation about which ballot to adopt; even granting everything before it, I do not see how the one follows from the other, and I would like to know what a person who has genuinely understood the theorem ought to conclude about choosing among voting methods, given that no method comes out clean. Please do not send me to further reading; I want the ideas sorted out, and I would rather be told where my own suspicions are wrong than be told the whole business is above my pay grade.
