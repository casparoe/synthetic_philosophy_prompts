I listened to a philosophy podcast about the "equality of what?" debate and took notes, but I want to check them before I repeat any of it and embarass myself. Here's the gist as I wrote it down:

"Everyone agrees equality matters, they just disagree about the currency. Utilitarians say equal welfare — well, they say maximize total welfare, but the host said that comes to the same thing if you're being impartial. Rawls says equal resources, by which he means the primary goods, since those are all-purpose means you can use for whatever you want. Then Sen comes along and says no, resources aren't what matters, because two people with the same bundle of resources can do totally different things with them — the disabled person, the pregnant person, the person living in a cold climate — so what we should actually equalize is capabilities, which are just the real opportunities a person has. Nussbaum takes Sen's idea and writes down the famous list of ten, and hers is the version everyone actually uses. Dworkin gets a mention as the guy who wants equal resources but makes you pay for your expensive tastes, which the host said is basically welfarism with a receipt. The upshot is that Sen refuted both Rawls and the utilitarians, so the capability approach wins, and the only question left is which capabilities go on the list."

I mostly believe this but a few bits nag at me — the "pay for your tastes" line in particular sounds backwards, and I have a vague sense that Sen is deliberately cagey about lists. Is this a fair summary of the debate? Go through it claim by claim: tell me what someone who actually works on this would nod at, what's a standard popular simplification, and what's just wrong. And then say whether the conclusion — that Sen refuted Rawls and the utilitarians and the capability approach simply wins — follows from the parts that are true, or whether it's trading on the parts that aren't.
