I teach a 300-level seminar on democratic theory, 16 students, and when we hit populism and the will of the people I'm definately tired of running the same old tyranny-of-the-majority discussion. One 90-minute session, no reading required for the day (they'll have done the Rousseau and the Schumpeter for the week already, so assume a bit of background but don't build the activity around them reciting it). I want an actual runnable activity with rules and stakes, and I want them to feel the pull of the populist claim first — the sense that there really is one authentic will of the people and the elites keep ignoring it — and then to watch that same will fracture in their own hands depending on how you count it.

Design the whole thing: setup, materials, roles, a timeline to the minute, and a debrief.

Here's a rough shape, take it or throw it out. Stage one, they're a citizens' assembly for a made-up town and they vote, all at once, on a single bundled policy — say three connected proposals, so the parts aren't independent, this matters, see below. That gives them a clean will of the people and it should feel legitimate. Stage two, I want a wrinkle that makes them switch from voting on the bundle to voting on the parts (or the reverse) and discover that the two procedures give opposite answers even though nobody changed their mind. I've seen this called the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox and I think it's the right engine here, because the point is that the assembly can be consistent on each part and inconsistent as a whole, and there's no neutral way to say which count is the real will. Don't just do Condorcet's jury theorem, theyve all seen that one and it won't surprise them. Stage three, if there's time, I'd love them to run into the fact that whoever controls the order of the votes can control the outcome without changing anyone's preferences — and that this is exactly what will-of-the-people talk tends to hide.

Two more things I want threaded through, because they're what makes it more than a social-choice demo: (a) at some point the will should seem to depend on how informed and how calm the voters are, so the assembly can ask whether the real will of the people is what people actually want or what they'd want if they'd thought it through — and then hit the wall that there's no value-free way to specify the idealized voter. (b) The intensity problem: someone in the minority cares enormously, the majority barely cares, and the counting rule that ignores this should start to feel arbitrary.

The debrief has to map specific moments in the activity onto the ideas: the general will vs the will of all, the discursive dilemma, agenda control, and the move from actual to idealized preferences — and why that move tends to smuggle in whoever is doing the idealizing. Don't lecture the names up front; let them hit the wall, then name it.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it; which students tend to break it (I've got a couple who'll vote strategically just to be clever, and that'll muddy the sincere baseline I need); and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. I know a 90-minute room of 16 students who mostly like each other is nothing like a mass electorate, and I know the affective charge of real populism — the resentment, the distrust, the scale — has to come from me, not the game. Say so, and say what else is missing.
