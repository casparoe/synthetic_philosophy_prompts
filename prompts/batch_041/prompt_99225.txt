my editor keeps telling me to stop worrying that my new novel is so grim. she says a sad novel is like a rollarcoaster: nobody buys a ticket for the flat bits, the whole point is the drop, and the only reason the drop is fun is that the bar is down and you know youre safe. so readers sobbing at the end isnt a flaw, she says, its the ride working, and i should lean into the bleak ending instead of hedging it. i half buy it. can you actually audit the thing for me? start with the mapping, what in a sad novel plays the track, the climb, the drop, the safety bar, the queue, the exit gate, then show me where the comparison comes apart. whats nagging me: a coaster is over in ninety seconds and youve forgotten it by dinner, but people carry a sad ending around for weeks; and they cry at true sad stories too, memoirs and documentaries, where theres no safety bar at all, which the analogy doesnt obviously cover; and on a coaster the feeling is just a jolt in the gut with no object, whereas grief in a novel is grief about someone, so it looks like the safety story is explaining the wrong thing. which of those breaks actually matter for what shes using the analogy for, i.e. that the bleakness is the product so i should stop hedging, and which are just me being precious about my own book? does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the coaster carrying it the whole time? and if theres a better picture of why people go to sad art on purpose, give me that too, id like it before i commit to the ending.
