I host a small interview podcast on philosophy of mind — the audience is a mix of graduate students and curious general listeners, the format is a single forty-five-minute conversation with no editing beyond cutting filler, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent roughly a decade defending a Hofstadterian picture of the self: the "I" is not a thing but a self-symbol, an abbreviation a system constructs of itself, whose peculiarity is that it occurs inside the very system it abbreviates, so that the loop closes through the symbol rather than around it. He adds two theses of his own that I want to get at. The first is that the abbreviation must be lossy — a self-symbol that fully represented the machinery producing it would not be a symbol of anything — so the omission of the machinery is constitutive of the self rather than a gap in our knowledge of it. The second is that the symbol is load-bearing: it is not a retrospective report the system issues about itself but the thing its own predictions and decisions actually run through, which is supposed to make the self real as a cause without making it a substance.

Please draft eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, each with a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and each doing something other than inviting him to restate the view; nothing about how he came to the topic or what he is reading now. I want the sequence to include, at minimum: a question about the reader of the abbreviation — if the symbol is something the system uses, what is it that uses it, and does that generate a regress or a genuine loop — with a follow-up that presses him if he answers that the same process does both the reading and the being read; a question about what separates a self from every other self-monitoring or self-referential pattern I can name (a thermostat with a model of its own set point, a compiler that compiles itself, an immune system that distinguishes self from non-self, a firm whose annual report describes the firm), and what work the load-bearing clause is doing beyond the familiar claim that a system carries a model of itself; a question about individuation — if the self is a pattern, then which pattern, given that neural, psychological, narrative and social patterns can all be read off the same person and do not have the same persistence conditions — with a follow-up on whether the choice among them is a metaphysical discovery or a pragmatic convenience; a question about a large language model trained to predict text, which uses "I" fluently and has absorbed a great deal about how such a thing behaves, asking whether his account makes it a self and, if not, which of the human's ingredients — a body, an unbroken stream of processing, stakes, valence — is doing the excluding; and a closing question about what would count against the two theses, since "the symbol must be lossy" and "the symbol must be load-bearing" both risk sounding like claims no evidence could touch.

Two further constraints. He describes his position as a middle path between illusionism and the self-model theory of subjectivity, so I would like one question that forces him to say precisely where he departs from each and whether the remaining disagreement is empirical or merely verbal. And please keep the last two questions on consequences rather than foundations: one on what the view implies for beings with degraded, interrupted or unusually partitioned self-symbols — infants, advanced dementia, split-brain patients, social animals — and one on whether anything distinctively Hofstadterian survives once he has conceded as much as a thoroughgoing reductionist about personal identity concedes, or whether the self-symbol has quietly become just another name for a bundle of psychological continuity.
