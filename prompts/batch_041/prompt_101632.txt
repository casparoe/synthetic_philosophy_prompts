I'm a retired soil ecologist -- 34 years in the field, mostly wetland and grassland work -- and since I hung up the boots I've been reading my way into anthropology and moral philosophy, which has been a humbling experience. I keep running into a fight I can't adjudicate on my own, and I'd like you to help me write the thing the two sides should have written years ago.

The fight: one camp thinks the shift from foraging to farming was, on the whole, a catastrophe for human welfare -- that hunter-gatherers were relatively well-fed, well-rested and healthy, and that agriculture bought us numbers at the cost of making each individual's life worse. The other camp thinks that's romanticism -- that farming was a genuine improvement, that the "affluent forager" picture leans on a handful of unusually cushy cases and a lot of wishful thinking, and that the long-run gains in security and material life swamp any short-run costs. They've been going at each other for decades and mostly talking past each other, I think.

What I want is a joint statement -- the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce, that both camps could actually sign. Something with a numbered "claims we both accept" section first, then a disagreements table. In the agreed section I'd want them to nail down a common accounting: get them to agree on what the currency of the comparison is (healthy life-years per person? total healthy life-years summed over everyone who ever lives under a regime? something else?), and to admit that the per-person measure and the total measure can point in opposite directions and that neither is obviously the right one. Also get them to agree on the skeleton of the empirical record -- that the shift to farming coincided with things like shorter stature, more dental disease and more signs of nutritional stress in the bodies we dig up, alongside a big rise in population density -- without either side pretending that settles anything.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, I want them to isolate the cruxes properly, each one phrased in wording that its own side would accept for its own position, and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence that BOTH sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

A few things I'd like them to have to chew on, because I think they're where the real action is and I never see them handled cleanly:

- "Who counts." The people who'd exist under farming aren't the same people who'd exist under foraging, so summing welfare across regimes is slippery in a way I don't think either camp has faced squarely. Make them say how they handle it.

- Whether the relevant comparison is the people alive during the transition, the steady-state populations a few centuries later, or the entire downstream lineage. I suspect each side quietly picks a different window and that this is doing alot of the work.

- Lock-in. Here's a stylized case I'd like them to both analyze: two neighbouring valleys at the same moment. Valley F holds 25 foragers who each live on average to 55 in decent health and put in maybe 15 hours a week on subsistence. Valley G holds 120 farmers who each live on average to 40, lose roughly three children in ten before the age of five, and work closer to 30 hours a week -- but who store grain, which means they can field more people, and more people means they expand. Valley G's population eventually spills over and absorbs Valley F; the foragers can't simply stay foragers. So the "choice" between the two ways of life was never a free choice for the people making it. I want both camps to say whether that changes the verdict, and whether "it was a mistake" is even the right shape of claim for a transition nobody could individually opt out of.

- Whether any of the disagreement is really just verbal -- whether one side means by "mistake" something like "worse for those who lived through it" and the other means "not the best available path," and whether "affluent" is about calories, leisure, security, or contentment.

And I want each side to state what would actually make it abandon its position -- what evidence, if it turned up, would flip it -- and to be honest about whether such evidence is even obtainable, or whether the question is permanently underdetermined and they're both just picking a weighting.

Please don't give me reading lists or citations, I'm not after who said what. I want the substance: the formulations that are neither vacuous nor loaded, the cruxes made exact, and the two sides' predictions laid out side by side. Make it a document they'd both put their names to.
