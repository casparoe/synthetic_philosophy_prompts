I am teaching a unit on transhumanism and its critics, and I want to build a session around Lois Lowry's The Giver (1993) rather than the usual Brave New World, because I think the novel is a harder test case for David Pearce's hedonistic imperative than the standard dystopia is. Pearce's position, in the 1995 online manifesto of that name and in his later writing on what he calls the abolitionist project, is that abolishing suffering throughout the living world is a moral obligation and not merely a permissible ambition: pharmacology, genetic engineering, and nanotechnology should be used to eliminate unpleasant experience and replace it with gradients of bliss, and the functions pain currently serves -- warning, protection, repair -- are to be taken over by signals that carry the information without the agony. I want a reading of The Giver that takes both the novel and that position seriously.

The community of the novel has abolished pain through Sameness, and it has paid for that abolition by surrendering colour, desire, memory, and much of the emotional range of a life: Jonas is issued daily pills against the Stirrings; the old, the failing infant, and the offender are released; the Giver's daughter Rosemary, who preceded Jonas as Receiver, could not endure the darker memories and was released at her own request; and the memories themselves have been offloaded onto a single pair of shoulders. A Pearcean can read all of this as an abolitionism done badly -- a technology too crude to cut the capacity for agony away from the capacity for joy -- and can say that the novel's apparent lesson, that depth must be bought with pain, is a false dilemma produced by the community's primitive means. I would like you to test that reading rather than assume it.

Which scenes genuinely support the Pearcean diagnosis, and which resist it? Does the novel have an answer to him that does not depend on making the abolitionists inept? What work do the euphemisms and the affect-management rituals do -- release, the household reporting of feelings and dreams, the insistence on exact speech? Does the community's arrangement count as abolishing suffering, or only as relocating it, and does that distinction tell against Pearce or against the community? And what does the novel see that the philosophy does not: about memory, about who is left holding the pain that has been declared abolished, and about the difference between a world without suffering and a world in which suffering has merely been made someone's private office?

I do not want a plot summary, a survey of themes, or a verdict on whether Lowry anticipated transhumanism. I want the kind of criticism that would hold up in a seminar: close attention to particular scenes, an honest report of where the reading strains, and a closing judgment about what, if anything, the novel contributes to the debate over the hedonistic imperative that argument alone could not.

One request about presentation. Where you are on well-trodden ground -- what the novel plainly depicts, what Pearce plainly claims -- say so plainly. Where you are making a contestable interpretive or philosophical move, mark it as contestable, and do not present it as the settled view of people who work on this. I would rather see the fault lines than have them smoothed over.
