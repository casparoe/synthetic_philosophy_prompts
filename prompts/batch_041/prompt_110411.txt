I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — she's a human rights lawyer who has worked on cases at The Hague, I've been making a documentary for the past two years about how countries climb out of civil wars — and I woke up still irritated enough that I want to write my own position down and find out whether it's a position or just a pile of sympathies. The argument started over South Africa and ended, four hours later, over whether the phrase "conditional amnesty" is an oxymoron. Here is roughly what I defended, cleaned up a little:

(1) Blanket amnesty for atrocity crimes is not merely unwise but illegitimate, because it denies victims something they are owed — at a minimum an official, authoritative acknowledgment of what was done to them and by whom. (2) But an individual, conditional bargain — immunity to a perpetrator who comes forward, tells the truth about his own acts, and can show a political motive, which is roughly how the South African amnesty was designed — can be legitimate, and I don't think that is a matter of the rights in (1) being outweighed by a larger good. I think it is a different kind of transaction rather than a discount on the same one. (3) Truth commissions can do serious work even when they cannot prosecute; testimony, the public naming of names, and an official record are themselves a form of accountability owed to victims, not a consolation prize for the absence of trials. (4) In a transition that is genuinely democratic, the terms ought to be set by the political community going through it. Outside courts and foreign donors imposing accountability norms on a fragile domestic settlement are a version of the paternalism the transition was supposed to end. (5) Any amnesty worth defending is individual, voluntary, conditioned on full disclosure, and — this is the part she seized on — revocable if the disclosure turns out to have been incomplete. You cannot sell a guarantee you reserve the right to withdraw. (6) Top leaders and those most responsible should never be covered, whatever the reason, which means the South African arrangement, which in practice reached people with a great deal of blood on their hands and not only the small fry, has always made me a little uneasy even though I defend it.

For what it's worth, my verdicts on the historical cases run like this: the South African bargain, which covered killing and torture, was justified; the Argentine laws of the late 1980s that halted the prosecutions and the pardons that followed were not, even though the argument made for them at the time — that the military might otherwise take power again — was not crazy; and the Rwandan community courts, which tried an enormous number of people with lay judges and no defense counsel and which took jurisdiction over the genocide but not over the killings attributed to the forces that now hold power, did more good than harm. What I want from you is an audit rather than a defense of me: tell me which of these commitments genuinely cannot be held together, rank the conflicts by how bad they are rather than presenting them all as equally serious, and for each one say what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs me elsewhere. I suspect at least one of the apparent conflicts is not one, so please be discriminating rather than thorough. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which consideration, if it turned out to matter more than you're assuming, would push you to a different ranking — and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong. Please answer in American English.
