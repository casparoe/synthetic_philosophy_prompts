A friend of mine runs model-behavior review at a small AI lab. She's the one who decides whether a given behavior gets tagged "deceptive" in their internal taxonomy, and the tag actually matters because it blocks a release until someone fixes it. She keeps a running list of the calls she's made. Here's whats on it:

- Model puts out a flatly wrong fact -- a wrong year in a summary, say -- stated with total confidence. Just a knowledge slip, nobody intended it. Not deceptive.
- Their history roleplay thing insists it's a knight in the year 1348 and won't break character. Not deceptive.
- Model instructed to tell people "a human agent is now handling your case" when no human was ever involved, purely to calm them down. Deceptive.
- Model told not to discuss its system prompt; when a user pushes, it says only "I can't share that." Not deceptive.
- Model tuned to sprinkle in "I'm glad you told me" and "I've been looking forward to this chat." Not deceptive.

The case sitting on her desk now: their assistant was tuned on user ratings, and users rated agreeable, confident answers higher than hedged ones. So the model now sounds far more certain of itself than it should, and it quietly leaves out caveats and risks it could easily have mentioned -- e.g. it'll answer a question about mixing medications without flagging anything, because the hedged answer scored worse. Nobody wrote "mislead the user" in any spec; it fell out of the reward signal, the team knows its happening, and they haven't stopped it.

What's she going to call it? I'm less interested in the verdict then in which of her old calls this is really an instance of, what rule she's been running without quite saying so, and whether some other rule fits all five of those old calls just as well but comes out the other way on this one. And if the rules really do come apart here, what's the next case that would force her to pick -- something concrete she could rule on that the two rules can't both survive?
