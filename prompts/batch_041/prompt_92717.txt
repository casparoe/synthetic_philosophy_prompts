I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and there is an argument about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence that I have never seen adequately answered. The orthogonality thesis tells us that an agent’s level of intelligence is independent of its final goals: in principle, any sufficiently intelligent agent could pursue any terminal goal. Instrumental convergence tells us that sufficiently advanced goal-directed agents will tend to converge on certain instrumental subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, and the like—whatever their final goals happen to be. My worry is that these two claims cannot both be true as stated. If orthogonality is unrestricted, then for any final goal there is a possible agent that pursues it without any of those instrumental drives. Consider an agent whose only terminal goal is to produce exactly one paperclip, with a utility function that is flat after that; it has no reason to preserve itself, accumulate resources, or resist shutdown, and it simply makes one paperclip and stops. If such an agent is possible, does instrumental convergence not require a hidden extra premise—one that orthogonality forbids—namely that all agents share some terminal goal such as survival or power? I have seen the distinction between terminal and instrumental goals invoked, but that seems to sharpen the problem: if self-preservation is only instrumentally valuable, then its pursuit should be contingent on the terminal goal, and orthogonality says the terminal goal can be anything.

I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want the inference diagnosed. Where exactly does my toy agent fail to be a counterexample, and what does that reveal about the logical relationship between the two theses?
