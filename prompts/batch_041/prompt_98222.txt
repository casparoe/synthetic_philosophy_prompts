I'm teaching a seminar on moral responsibility and I want to spend a session on standing to blame using The Scarlet Letter. The setup I want to press is this: the whole town blames Hester, and by any ordinary measure they've got a point, but the person who most deserves the blame, Dimmesdale, is also the one up there doling out the sermons. So I want a reading of the novel that takes the standing literature seriously instead of using it as a slogan. Concretely: does Dimmesdale lose standing because he's a hypocrite, or did he never have it, or is it something else? The distinctions I actually care about are between losing standing because you've done the same thing, losing it because you don't genuinely accept the norm you're invoking, never having it because you aren't in the right relationship to the person you're blaming, and forfeiting it by blaming selectively, which is basically the whole town. Which of these does the book dramatize, and does Hawthorne keep them apart or just blur them into a mood?

Second thing: Hester's silence. She knows Dimmesdale's secret and says nothing for years. Does that make her complicit, and so strip her standing to blame him? Or does the silence buy her something? And what about Pearl, a child who blames her mother constantly, has no standing on any theory, and yet comes across as the only honest moral voice in the book. Is that the novel telling us the standing framework is wrong, or just that children are outside it?

Then the Custom House narrator, who finds the letter and writes the whole thing up. He's blameless and distant, so on the usual view he has perfect standing to blame everyone, and yet he's the most suspicous figure in the book. Is that a real problem for the standing view or just a novelist's trick?

You won't be able to quote anything, so don't try; work from the plot and the famous scenes. I'd like the reading to be faithful in both directions: don't flatten the novel into an illustration of the theory, and don't bend the theory to make the novel come out right. End by saying what the book adds to the standing debate that the philosophy papers don't.
