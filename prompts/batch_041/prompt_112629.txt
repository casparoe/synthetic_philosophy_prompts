I am midway through an Indian philosophy course and have hit a wall on the doctrine of karma, and rather than read another article I would like to see the problem dramatized rather than argued. Write me a short story set in an afterworld that administers karma literally, as a bureaucracy: a Bureau of Recompense where clerks and auditors process the ledger of each just-finished life — every intention weighed, every act entered, the balance converted by actuarial tables into the circumstances of the next birth — and where the ordinary frictions of any large office apply, in the form of backlogs, appeals, jurisdictional squabbles over which desk owns a case, and clerks who have never met the people whose fates they compute. Give the place real texture and its own procedures and keep them internally consistent; the metaphysics should be as mundane to the characters as tax law is to us. I was arguing about all this with a friend who studies Buddhism, and we could not agree on whether the doctrine is a theory of desert at all, so I would rather the story expose the question than answer it for me.

My protagonist is a junior auditor handed a file she cannot close. Let the difficulty be philosophical rather than merely sentimental, and let it arise from the doctrine's own commitments rather than from an outside objection. Somewhere in the case are the seams I keep tripping over: the series of lives has no first term, so no one is ever originally innocent and every debt recedes into a past that never began; the tradition denies any persisting self, so what the ledger calls one person is only a causal stream, and it becomes unclear who is being made to pay; merit and demerit are treated as a single summable quantity even though they look like different currencies; and a single life's suffering may be said to discharge the debts of selves that no longer exist and that the sufferer cannot remember. I would like the auditor to reach a decision — to sign the file, refuse, or invent a third option — and for the story to leave the reader genuinely unsettled about whether the bureau is just, incoherent, or both.

Please do not end with a lecture or a tidy moral. Let the argument live in the paperwork, in the dialogue with a more senior clerk who has long since stopped finding any of it strange, and in the moment of signing; and let the story's resolution, whatever it is, cast some light back on the ordinary case in which a person is told that their present misfortune is simply what they are owed.
