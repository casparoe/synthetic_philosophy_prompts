I keep running into an explanatory move that I have no name for, and I would like to know whether it corresponds to something already worked out, or, failing that, whether it can be given a name that marks its edges.

The move is this. A long-run outcome is explained by a slow background process — the gradual salinization of irrigated soil, a river shifting its course over centuries, a century-scale drying trend, the slow exhaustion of a forest — and the explanation is offered in the deep, structural register, as though it were more fundamental than any account in terms of decisions, battles, or individuals. What troubles me is that the same slow process, at a comparable measured rate over a comparable interval, is invoked to explain the opposite outcome in a comparable case nearby. In one district, three centuries of accumulating salt are said to have made continued cultivation impossible; in the next district, with similar soil and a similar salt load, cultivation was intensified, and the same accumulation is described as having forced the change. In one valley, a drying trend is given as the reason a settlement was abandoned; a hundred kilometres away, under the same trend, a settlement lasted another two centuries, and the drying is given as the reason its inhabitants reorganized rather than left. The slow variable is never absent from either story; it is only ever doing work in the direction of the outcome the historian already knows.

I can see that something is wrong here, and I can also see that it is not simply wrong, which is why I want the phenomenon named rather than merely dismissed. A slow process can be a perfectly good explanation: it may be independently dated and measured, so that its presence is not inferred from the outcome it is meant to explain; its magnitude may be commensurate with the outcome; and it may be possible to say what would have happened had it been absent or slower. When those conditions hold, the same salinization I complain about elsewhere seems to me entirely legitimate, and the difference between the two cases does not appear to lie in the time-scale of the cause, or in its slowness as such, but in the relation between the variable and the variation it is asked to account for.

I have been trying to make that difference precise with a distinction of my own, and I would like to know whether it is a real one or whether I have simply reinvented something. I distinguish a slow condition used as a constraint from the same condition used as a cause. As a constraint, it says what could not have happened — with the water table at this level, a certain crop was not available — and it does not select among the things that could have happened. As a cause, it is supposed to say why this happened rather than that, and it can do so only if there is variation for it to explain and if the variation is not itself what told us the variable was there. My suspicion is that much of what passes for deep structural explanation in this vein is a constraint quietly promoted to a cause, and that the promotion escapes notice because a constraint stated in slow variables sounds like a cause and cannot be tested on the timescale of the evidence.

So: is there an established name for this failure mode — for a slow variable that accounts equally well for an outcome and for its opposite, and whose apparent depth derives from being unmeasurable within the period it is invoked to explain? I know there are neighbouring things. I am aware of the familiar methodological point that a condition present in every case cannot explain the differences between cases, and of the idea that causes come in packages, with some conditions standing in the background and others triggering the change. I am also aware that a whole tradition takes long-run geographic and climatic structures to be the proper subject of history, and it may be that this pattern is discussed there under some heading I have not encountered. What I want is not the nearest label but the carving: what exactly the failure mode is, how it differs from an ordinary post hoc explanation and from a merely unfalsifiable claim, whether the constraint-and-cause distinction I have been using tracks anything established — enabling versus triggering conditions, say, or necessary versus sufficient ones — and where its edges fall. In particular, is there a version of the move that remains legitimate even though the slow variable is present in cases with opposite outcomes, for instance where a slow process and a fast trigger must be combined and neither alone predicts anything, and if so, what marks it off? And does the same structure have a mirror image, in which a single event is credited with a slow structural transformation that it could not have produced on its own? If nothing established fits, I would like a term and a definition, with a boundary case on each side.
