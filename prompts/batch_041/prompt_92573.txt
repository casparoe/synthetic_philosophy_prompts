I host a modest podcast on philosophy of religion, and next month I'm recording an episode with a philosopher who has spent a career defending Molinism -- roughly, the view that God has a "middle knowledge" of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: true propositions about what any possible free creature would choose in any possible set of circumstances, known logically prior to any divine decree about which world to actualize. He is a gracious guest but has a habit of conceding the stock objections quickly and then simply restating the position rather than letting himself be pushed; I want questions that put him on the back foot. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and make sure the sequence includes at least: one that states the grounding objection squarely and forces him to say which candidate ground for these counterfactuals he actually accepts -- that they are brute, that they are grounded in the creature's essence, that they are grounded in God's ideas of the creature, or something else -- and what each option costs him; one that presses the difference between what a creature would freely do and what it merely might do, asking whether conceding a robust sense of the latter leaves him any strong sense in which God governs the outcome; and one that asks whether the whole apparatus actually delivers the providential control Molinists advertise, or whether it slides either toward the Calvinist picture it was meant to avoid or toward the open theist one.

Beyond those, I'd like at least one question built around a twist of my own that I haven't seen addressed, which I'll describe so you can turn it into a question: middle knowledge is supposed to tell God what I would freely do in circumstance C, so suppose He tells me the content of that counterfactual -- "I know that were you in C, you would freely choose X." I want to ask whether my freedom survives being told the truth about my own counterfactual, and whether a Molinist can offer anything more satisfying here than the familiar foreknowledge-and-freedom reply; my suspicion is that the middle-knowledge framework makes this worse rather than better, because the truth about my choice is supposed to be settled before I so much as exist. Also include a question on transworld depravity and the free-will defense -- what the Molinist needs it to accomplish and whether the very counterfactuals that make the defense work also make God complicit in the evil they explain -- and one that simply asks how he can tell when a proposed counterfactual of freedom is true, given that no creature has ever occupied the circumstances in question and no one has observed the outcome. No softballs about how he came to the view or where he thinks the debate is heading; press where the literature actually presses, and keep every follow-up specific to the position rather than generic.
