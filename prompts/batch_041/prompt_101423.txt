I'm a science-and-ideas reporter and I'm about three drafts into a feature on the great-man question — whether history is made by individuals or by big impersonal forces. The case I keep circling back to is the obvious one: Nazi Germany. Was Hitler load-bearing, or was Weimar so wrecked that some Hitler-shaped figure was basically guaranteed? Every time I read up on this I get the same mush — structure and agency both matter, it's complicated, yada yada. My editor wants 4000 words and a spine, so I want an actual verdict, not a both-sides shrug.

Here's the handle I've been using, and I'd like you to run with it. A historian I've been interviewing (she's a bit of a character) says quit asking "did Hitler cause it" and ask two narrower things instead. First, the plug test: yank him out at a specific moment — after the failed putsch in '23, or in '32 right before he gets the chancellorship — hold the economic and political pressures roughly where they were, and ask whether the same catastrophe arrives by a different road within, I dunno, a generation, or whether the whole thing dissipates. Second, the lightning-rod test: he didn't make the storm, but did the storm need a rod exactly his shape to discharge the way it did? I.e., are the specific features of the regime — the personalist dictatorship, the race war, the drive east — contingent on him, or would a generic authoritarian have produced pretty much the same thing?

So take those two tests seriously and actually run them on the Nazi case. Tell me what they show, be honest about where the counterfactual gets too spongy to be decisive, and then give me your verdict — load-bearing or replaceable — and which of the two tests is doing the real work in your answer. Then flip it: what does this case do to the great-man vs structural debate itself? Does it vindicate one side, or does it expose that the debate was miscast from the start? Dont hedge at the end — commit to something and defend it, even if you flag where you're least sure.
