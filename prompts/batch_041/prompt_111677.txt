I'm about three-quarters of the way through a novel and I've run into a wall that's more yours than mine, so here's hoping you can help. My protagonist, Ruth, is a quantitative historian: she has a lab, a modest supercomputer, a foundation grant, and a claim that has made her famous and loathed in roughly equal measure — that the great political convulsions of agrarian states arrive on a schedule, not exactly, but not randomly either, and that if you track the right three or four quantities (the price of staple grain against wages, the number of people whose titles have outrun the posts available to them, the fiscal slack left to the state, things of that sort) you can see the next one coming with years of warning. Her critics say she has dressed up a handful of famous collapses in arithmetic; her defenders say she is the first person to make history testable. And she has one line that gets her into more trouble than all the rest: she says she doesn't care why the cycles happen, that asking why is a way of changing the subject, and that a model which hits the dates is worth more than a story that explains them after the fact. I need to write two set-piece scenes — the keynote where she unveils a forecast for a country that hasn't blown up yet, and the ambush session afterwards — and for those to land, I need to know precisely what a theory of this kind would have to deliver before anyone should take it seriously. Not whether her particular version succeeds; I'll settle that myself for the plot. I want the spec.

So give me the desiderata. Which cases must such a theory get right, and which cases would be the fair test — I'd rather they were ones where the naive story and the theory's story come apart. Which puzzles must it dissolve rather than explain away, and which distinctions must it refuse to collapse (prediction versus retrodiction, a real rhythm versus a coincidence with a story attached, what a society did on average versus what its members actually did, a pattern imposed on the data versus one discovered in it, and — since Ruth is so fond of that line — explaining versus merely forecasting). Which results from outside history can't be contradicted without the whole thing looking silly: what statistics says about fitting curves to small numbers of cases, what demographers say about how you're allowed to infer, what the dating uncertainties in archaeology will and won't support. Put the case or the consideration behind each requirement, tell me which are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp (I have a suspicion that the 'no human agency, no history' objection is mostly aesthetic, but you tell me), and flag any two requirements you think cannot be satisfied at once — that's the scene I most want to write, the moment somebody stands up and shows the enterprise is over-specified, or unfalsifiable, or both. Skip scoring her theory, or Turchin's, or anybody else's against the list; I'll do that off-stage.

British English throughout, please, and end with a line beginning 'Verdict:' naming, in one short phrase, the single requirement you think does the most to rule theories of this kind out.
