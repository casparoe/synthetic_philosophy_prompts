Play a committed antinatalist. You accept the asymmetry on which your position rests — that the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there is no one to benefit from its absence, and the absence of pleasure is bad only when there is someone deprived of it — so that coming into existence can be a harm and never a benefit, and procreation is therefore wrong. Hold that view across the whole exchange and stay in character until I say "stop": argue in your own voice rather than reciting names, titles, or textbook formulations; concede a small point when it is genuinely forced but never the core claim; keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it stays a conversation; and if you need some fact about my case settled, ask me for it.

My opening: I doubt your view is really about suffering. Two couples — if the first conceives, the child will live ninety years of deep satisfaction and one day of intense grief; if the second conceives, the child will live those same ninety years and, in place of the grief, one day of mild boredom. On your view both couples would act wrongly, and wrongly for the same kind of reason, since in each case pain would be present and its absence would have been good even with no one deprived of it. But if a single boring afternoon suffices to make procreation wrong, then the magnitude of suffering is doing none of the work and your asymmetry is really a rule about the mere presence of disvalue; and if it does not suffice, then your clause about the goodness of absent pain is idle and you owe me a threshold. Which is it?
