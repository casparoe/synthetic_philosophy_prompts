Some years ago I published a novel set on a big southeastern river — lock-keepers, a drowned valley, a family that had been on the same bend for four generations — and for reasons that seemed good at the time I gave one of the characters a mussel bed to worry over. I invented the animal for the book. The memorial, unfortunately, is not mine to invent: a land trust has set a granite bench and a small plaque on the bank below the old lock, for a riffle-dwelling freshwater mussel that the state declared extinct in the late nineties, and they have asked me to give the dedication speech on a Saturday morning in October. I have been reading about the species problem for weeks, mostly because I am afraid of saying something false in front of people who know better, and I want the speech to be honest in a way that a speech usually is not allowed to be. So let me put the materials in front of you and ask you to write it.

The audience will be roughly three-way split, and that is the difficulty. There will be a handful of malacologists and field biologists, among them the man who described this species from a single bed of dead shells and who has since watched it synonymized with a common congener, un-synonymized, and synonymized again — he will hear every word, and he has already told me, over a bad lunch, that he does not believe the year on the plaque is defensible. There will be the trust's volunteers and donors, who want the loss named plainly and who would find it cold, or evasive, to be told that "extinct" is a decision rather than an event. And there will be a dozen or so neighbours who remember the river before the dam and whose grandfathers sold shells to the button trade. What I have taken from the reading is two things I would like the speech to hold at once. First, that there is no single non-arbitrary way to say what a species is: interbreeding, diagnosable lineage, ecological niche, and plain resemblance each carve the living world differently, and none of them is simply the correct one, so that the number of extinctions in a river can change without a single organism having died. Second, that on one respectable view a species is not a kind at all but a particular — a lineage, an individual extended through time the way an organism is extended through space — which would mean the bench is dedicated not to the loss of a category but to the death of somebody. Those two thoughts pull in opposite directions and I would like the speech to live in the pull rather than resolve it.

Please write the speech. Eight hundred words or so, five or six minutes aloud, composed to be spoken rather than read, with no citations, no footnotes, and nothing that assumes the listener has read anything. It must be a genuine act of mourning that does not rest on the animal having had an essence, or a single moment of death, or a defensible place in a natural order — and it must give the donors and the neighbours something they can actually hold, without telling the biologists a comfortable story. One last thing, which I would like you to use structurally rather than as ornament: as I understand it, a mussel of this kind spends its first weeks as a larva clamped to the gills of one particular host fish, and the adults, who cannot move once they have settled into the gravel, coax that fish close with a flap of mantle shaped and twitched like a small minnow. The host fish is still in the river. So is the minnow-shaped thing that was never a minnow. The bench is for the third party.
