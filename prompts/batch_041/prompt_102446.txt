So I've been reading Schrödinger's later stuff — the essays where he stops doing physics and starts going on about the Upanisads, the idea that there's really only one consciousness and that the apparent plurality of minds is a kind of illusion, the many minds being like many images thrown on a single screen, and that this is basically what the Vedantins meant when they identified the innermost self with Brahman, the ground of everything. I don't need you to adjudicate what he actually believed versus what he was just importing from Schopenhauer, and please don't hand me a reading list — I want to take the position seriously on its own terms. And I want to sharpen it, because as it's usually stated it feels slippery. Here's my gloss: the view is the claim that there is exactly one subject of experience, full stop, and that talk of "my pain" and "your pain" stands to that one subject roughly the way "the sun rises" stands to the earth's rotation — a useful way of speaking that encodes a perspective but isn't, strictly, true of the world. So token experiences are many, but the subject they belong to is one, and the "I" you and I each think with refers to that same thing, not to two things that merely resemble each other.

Now — I'm fairly drawn to this, or at least I cant see why it should be dismissed out of hand, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. Not the lazy stuff (it's unfalsifiable, it's just Eastern religion) — I want the real defeaters, sorted by how decisive they'd actually be. What observations or cases should move me: split-brain patients, dissociative identity, thought insertion in schizophrenia, the way anaesthesia seems to switch a subject off and back on, the fact that experiences seem to come in causally isolated streams that can't share information? For each, say whether it's a genuine problem for the one-subject view or whether the view can just absorb it as an illusion at the level of subjects. And I want the arguments and formal results too — does the view collapse into something more familiar, say a claim about a shared phenomenal field, or a mere redescription of the fact that we can't feel each other's pains? What does it predict that a boring many-minds view doesn't? What would its own defenders, the people who hold it for good reasons, concede as counting against them, rather than only what its critics throw at it? And be honest at the end about whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's actually a defect for a claim of this kind or just what you'd expect from a metaphysical thesis about the subject.

That's the ask. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and flag anything that would be personally decisive for me — I think I'd be most moved by a case where the one-subject view has to call something an illusion that we have independent reason to treat as real. One more thing: keep settled and contested apart. If something is genuinely disputed among people who know this material — say, whether Schrödinger's own position was monism about consciousness or something weaker — dont tell me it's the consensus. I'd rather have the disagreements marked.
