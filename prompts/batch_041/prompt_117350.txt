I'm giving a work-in-progress talk at my department's metaphysics reading group next Thursday and I need a Q&A prep sheet, because I'm pretty sure I'm walking into a buzzsaw. My thesis is that composition happens exactly when a bunch of things are locked into a self-maintaining causal loop — they're each doing work to keep the others in the arrangement they're in, and if you remove or disturb one, the rest actively restore the pattern. Call it the loop view. On this view my dog composes, a bacterium composes, and so does a coral colony, which is the case that got me into this in the first place: I started volunteering one morning a week at the marine lab down the road counting coral fragments in a restoration tank, and I could not stop asking whether I was looking at one organism or a little city of them. A rock doesn't compose, my kitchen table doesn't compose, and a candle flame, a hurricane, and a beehive are the cases I keep going back and forth on. The whole appeal of the criterion is that it isn't arbitrary — it says there's a fact of the matter about which composite objects there are, and that it's an empirical fact about causal structure rather than a fact about what's convenient for us to talk about.

The room is going to be hostile in very specific ways, and I want to be ready for the actual people. Three of the regulars are hardcore metaphysicians. One runs the argument that any restriction on composition at all makes the question how many things there are vague and therefore unanswerable, and he will not let it go. One is an organicist who thinks only living things compose, and he'll say I've reinvented his view with extra steps and worse biology. One works on grounding and will want to know whether the loop is supposed to be what composition *is* or merely a reliable indicator of it — and honestly I'm not sure I know. Then there's a philosopher of biology who will object that I've drafted biology to settle a metaphysics question, and that "self-maintaining" is smuggling in teleology through the back door. Plus a visiting ecologist with strong opinions about whether an ecosystem is homeostatic or just a process that happens to persist for a while. And a friend in the back row who will just keep saying "candle flame, candle flame" at me until I answer.

What I want from you is a prep sheet: the objections in the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them rather than the way a textbook would, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Rank them by how much damage they do to the thesis instead of just listing them all as equally bad, and tell me which ones I should concede ground on immediately, which I should hold, and which concession would cost me the least. I don't need a reading list, I've read enough to get myself into this. Just end with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
