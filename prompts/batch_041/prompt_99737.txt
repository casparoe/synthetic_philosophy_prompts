I'm about three quarters of the way through a novel — 1930s coastal town, alot of rain and bad decisions, my heroine keeps almost leaving and then doesn't — and I've managed to get myself into a running argument with a friend from grad school who now teaches philosophy of time. It started over a sentence my narrator keeps reaching for: "she would never see him again." He says that's me tipping my hand, because in a novel a line like that is true or false, and the only reason it has a truth value is that I've already decided how it ends — I'm the one who settles chapter thirty. Which got us onto the old sea battle thing (tomorrow there's either a battle or there isn't, so if one of those is true right now then it was never really open), and now we can't stop. His line is that there being a fact of the matter about what my heroine does is not the same as its being settled or already decided, and that the fact doesn't push her around — it's more like a shadow cast by how things actually go, or like a single thread picked out of a bundle when nothing in the bundle picks it out. My line is that he's smuggled in an author, and that the alternative he keeps nudging me toward — no fact of the matter at all about chapter thirty until she gets there — costs too much, because then my narrator's line isn't false, it's nothing, and I don't know what I've been doing for two hundred pages. He also said one thing I can't tell is a real move or a dodge: that "she would never see him again" isn't even a prediction about the future, it's just the retrospective narrator telling me what happened. And the part that really bothers me is the same words pointed the other way — after it's all over, that she never saw him again is simply true, and nobody thinks that means she was fated not to.

So I want to see this fought out by two people who both know exactly what they're doing. Write it as a dialogue between him (call him Gil) and a colleague of his (call her Ana) who takes the open-future side. Give me exactly five exchanges, where an exchange is one speech from each of them — straight in, no scene-setting, no narration, no intro and no wrap-up at the end. Ana has to be his equal: she should be able to say why an open future isn't just ignorance about a fixed one, and why the "shadow" picture doesn't actually buy him anything, and Gil should come back at her with seperate real answers instead of getting flustered. If one of them ends up looking better, it should be the arguments doing it, not me stacking the deck. Two or three sentences per speech, tops.
