So two people in my reading group, Sam and Rae, got into it after we did Skolem's paradox, and since I was the one who'd brought in the reading they seemed to expect me to referee. I couldnt. I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it. Heres the gist of the exchange, in order.

The setup they were arguing over: first-order ZF proves there's an uncountable set (Cantor's theorem, so it proves e.g. that the thing it calls the power set of ω is uncountable, and that there's an uncountable ordinal). But by Löwenheim–Skolem, ZF, if it has any model at all, has a model whose domain is countable — countable in the plain sense that we can enumerate its elements with the naturals from outside. Call that model M. So M satisfies "there exists an uncountable set," and yet M has only countably many members.

Sam: This shows "uncountable" isn't a real property of sets, it's model-relative. Inside M the thing M calls P(ω) is uncountable; from outside, M's version of it is a countable set like any other. Nothing in the theory tells you which verdict is the true one, because the theory is satisfied either way. So cardinality talk is talk about what a given model can see, and set theory doesn't describe one determinate universe. And you can't patch this by adding more axioms, since Löwenheim–Skolem applies to any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model — whatever you write down, there's a countable model of it. That's a real result about the limits of axiomatization, not a quirk of ZF.

Rae: Way too fast. Countable just means there exists a bijection with ω. In the countable M, there really is such a bijection between ω and the set M calls P(ω) — it exists, it just isn't a member of M. M doesn't contain the function that enumerates its own reals. So "M thinks P(ω) is uncountable" and "P(ω) is really countable" aren't two answers to one question; they're about different things, the model's internal verdict and the actual set. Two claims, no conflict, so the alleged paradox is just a case of different quantifier ranges, and the relativism doesn't follow.

Sam: "The actual set" is exactly whats in dispute. If M satisfies every axiom a set theorist has, what licenses you to say the real bijection is sitting out there? You're helping yourself to a privileged background universe — the very thing the paradox calls into question. All you've actually shown is: relative to a bigger model, the set is countable; relative to M, it isn't. That's relativism with extra steps, and the relativist reading of all this has a long pedigree, it's not just me being confused.

Rae: I'm not assuming a privileged universe, I'm pointing out that first-order logic can't express countability absolutely — not that there's no fact of the matter, but that no first-order theory can pin down its own intended interpretation. So the lesson is about formalization, not about sets. And M isn't simply mistaken about its subject matter; it's a perfectly good model, its internal claim is true when read with its quantifiers. Saying M is "wrong" is like saying a chess position is wrong.

Sam: Then which quantifier range is the right one? If you say "all the sets there are," you owe me an account of that totality that isn't just "a bigger model." And if the answer is that we have to help ourselves to an intended interpretation that the axioms don't determine, you've conceded the main point: the axioms don't fix their own subject matter, and maths on this picture is closer to a game of symbols than a description of anything.

Rae: That last bit is a non sequitur, and I don't accept the framing of the question.
