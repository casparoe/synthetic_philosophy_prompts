"Sen's liberal paradox is a proof that you can't have both individual rights and the Pareto principle. The three conditions are: (U) the rule has to work for any possible set of individual preferences; (P) if everybody prefers A to B then society has to prefer A to B; and (L) minimal liberalism, i.e. everyone gets at least one pair of alternatives — their private sphere — where their own preference decides. Sen proves no rule can satisfy all three, so any society that respects individual rights is stuck with a cyclic, i.e. irrational, collective preference. His original example has two people and one copy of a racy novel, one who'd rather nobody read it and one who'd rather everybody did, each of them allowed to decide for themselves whether they read it, and you get a contradiction straight away. In modern dress: two flatmates, Ada and Ben, both play instruments. Ada decides whether she plays and Ben decides whether he plays. Ada's ranking is both play > only Ada plays > only Ben plays > neither, and Ben's is the same with 'only Ben' and 'only Ada' swapped. Since each of them is decisive about their own instrument, and both rank 'both play' above 'neither', Pareto forces 'both play' above 'neither' while the rights force the opposite direction through the middle options, and there's your cycle. The moral, for Sen, is that Pareto is the casualty: you keep the rights and restrict the Pareto principle. Really it's Arrow's theorem wearing a liberal hat — swap 'non-dictatorship' for 'each individual is decisive over some pair' and you get the same impossibility. And in any case the whole thing is an artefact of formalising rights as 'pairs you're decisive over' — on the game-form approach the paradox doesn't come up at all."
