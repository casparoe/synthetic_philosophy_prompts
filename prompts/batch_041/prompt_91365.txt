Two camps in the debate over space colonization have spent years talking past each other, and I want the document they could jointly sign. The first camp holds that space colonization should be understood as continuous with terrestrial colonialism: the wrongs are the same in kind — imposed governance over people who did not choose it, extractive economics, and the convenient fiction that the relevant space was empty — and that nothing about leaving Earth changes the moral categories. The second camp holds that the overriding consideration is asymmetric risk: the permanent loss of Earth-originating civilization is uniquely irreversible, whereas a flawed settlement is revisable, and so a measure of imposition on future persons can be justified in the way that saving a child's life justifies conditions the child never consented to. They agree on almost no framing, but they have never sat down to see where they actually converge.

Anchor the exercise in the following case, which both camps must treat as the shared object of dispute. The Ashen Charter is a founding document for a Martian settlement. It (a) vests governing authority in a council that is self-selecting and holds power in perpetuity over all descendants; (b) forbids return to Earth on grounds of cost; (c) permits a dissenting faction to depart only if it can fund and build its own vessel, which in practice it cannot; (d) is justified to Earth-based funders chiefly as existential-risk insurance, since the settlement is one of several that might preserve a technological civilization; and (e) rests on a minimum viable population, so that any sizable departure lowers the survival probability for those who remain, a fact both camps accept. The settlement is otherwise benign: no native life is displaced, and living conditions, while austere, are not worse than for many people on Earth.

Produce the joint statement, in this form:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about the case, about what is at stake, and about what the disagreement is not about. Make these substantive rather than truistic; a claim that either camp would find question-begging is disqualified.

2. A disagreements table giving the precise residual disagreements, each written in a formulation its own side would accept as fair to itself.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect at least these to be in play, and you should decide whether each is genuine or partly verbal: whether the wrong of colonialism is fundamentally about dispossession or about imposed social ordering as such; whether the value of a settlement is additive to, or a substitute for, the value already present on Earth; and whether future persons' claims can bind present founders, and if so whether the benefit of coming to exist can waive them.

4. For each crux, a test, case, or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

5. If you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.

I want a committed document, not a survey of positions. Make definite drafting choices: where two formulations are equally defensible, pick one and say why; where you think one camp is simply right about a crux, say so plainly and give the reason; and end by naming the single crux you take to be load-bearing for the rest.
