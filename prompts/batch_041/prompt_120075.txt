Okay, so I'm on the board of a small housing co-op and this futarchy stuff has suddenly gotten very personal. We have about 40 members and a shared fund, and we're trying to decide between two big options: fix the roof now, or buy the vacant lot next door and build a workshop. A member who's really into prediction markets says we should do a futarchy: create conditional markets on how well each option would go, and then pick whichever option the market says has the higher expected welfare. I'm definately skeptical, but I can't quite say why. I want to turn this into a short research proposal for a six-month independent study (maybe a small grant application, 1500-2500 words) in philosophy/social choice.

Here's my half-formed idea: futarchy seems to treat the prediction market as a measuring device. But in a small co-op, the market isn't outside the system. If the market predicts the workshop will be great, that might change the price of the lot, or make people more willing to contribute, or make the roof people feel steamrolled. And if the market predicts the roof is better, maybe the workshop supporters give up and the prediction becomes self-fulfilling in a bad way. I've been calling this the feedback problem, but I don't know if that's a real term. I suspect futarchy only works under some kind of modularity assumption: the forecast has to be causally seperate from the thing it forecasts. In governance, that assumption often fails. So the proposal question would be something like: under what conditions, if any, can a futarchy's decision rule be justified when the prediction market is itself a causal intervention in the system it's trying to measure? Or maybe: does futarchy require a modularity assumption that real governance rarely satisfies, and if so, can we weaken the assumption without losing the justification? State the question so it has a determinate answer, not just something to muse about.
