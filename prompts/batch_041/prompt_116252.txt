I'm assembling materials for the fiction week of an upper-level seminar on the transitivity of 'better than' — the puzzle that if a sufficiently long stretch of mild discomfort can be worse than a short stretch of intense pain, and each small step up that spectrum preserves the verdict, then either the two extremes are comparable in a way that seems absurd, or 'worse than' fails to be transitive, which sits badly with the fact that we must act, and act consistently, on such comparisons. Most fiction that touches this either lectures or dissolves the problem, so I'd like a short story, roughly 1,200–1,800 words, that stages it as a practical predicament rather than a debate.

Here is a setting I had in mind, though you may relocate the story if another setting serves the mechanism better. In a near-future city, consumer products must carry a single published discomfort rating, and the ratings are produced empirically: volunteers undergo trial experiences, and raters decide pair by pair which of two experiences is worse, with no scale assumed in advance. The protagonist is a junior analyst at the office that runs this process. She has spent months eliciting the pairwise verdicts, each of which strikes her as obviously correct — including when she undergoes two of the trial experiences herself, one long and mild, one brief and vicious — and when she tries to assemble the verdicts into the single ranking the law requires, they close into a cycle. The story should include the moment the cycle closes and she sees the last verdict point back to the first option; a scene in which she tries to explain to someone with the power to demand a number anyway why there is no number to publish; and at least two other characters who take opposite sides of the substantive question — one holding that transitivity is simply part of what it means to call one thing better than another, so something must be wrong with her method, and one holding that the pairwise verdicts are the data, and any theory that cannot accommodate them is the thing that is wrong. Neither of these characters may be refuted by the narrator, and the story should not resolve the question.

Keep the narrator's voice free of philosophical jargon and of any named positions or thinkers; let the puzzle emerge from what the characters do, want, and risk — her job, the volunteers, whatever she stands to lose by reporting a cycle instead of a ranking. At the end you may add a brief note, in your own words, describing the philosophical issue the story is meant to raise; do not include citations, reading lists, or attributions to particular authors, and do not make the protagonist a philosopher or the story a seminar-room dialogue.
