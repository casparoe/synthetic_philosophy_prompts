For the past two years I've run a small interview podcast on philosophy of mind — the audience is mostly graduate students and a handful of stubborn undergraduates, and since we record in a seminar room with terrible acoustics, the questions have to carry the episode on their own. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent several years defending an account of self-deception that she calls, somewhat provocatively, the evasion-first account. The gist of it is this. She thinks the phenomenon is real, and that the traditional paradox — that self-deception would require an agent to set out to make herself believe something she already takes to be false, and to do so while knowing it to be false — is a real problem for the traditional formulation but not for hers. On her view, the self-deceiver need not hold contradictory beliefs, and need not intend to acquire any belief at all. What she does instead is manage her own attention and inquiry: she keeps certain questions from being asked, certain evidence from being gathered, certain comparisons from being made. The resulting doxastic state is typically suspended judgment rather than confident false belief. She insists this is not deflationism — she is not one of the people who says that "self-deception" is merely a dramatic name for wishful thinking — because she holds that the attentional pattern is a stable, recognizable syndrome with its own developmental profile, that it comes in degrees, and that the relevant attentional policies are usually acquired long before they operate, which is why she is reluctant to call the self-deceiver blameworthy.

I'd like ten or eleven questions that press on the content of the account rather than on her career or the state of the field, and I don't want any question whose answer would just be a summary of the view. Please include at least one question on how the account escapes the original paradox, given that an agent who steers around the disconfirming evidence seems to need some prior grasp of what that evidence would show; at least one on why this attentional pattern deserves to be called deception at all rather than a species of motivated ignorance, and what would count as evidence that it is one unified syndrome rather than a rag-bag of unrelated biases; at least one on the degree claim and the responsibility claim, built around a case I'd like you to flesh out — a researcher who has for years never quite gotten around to running the single control that would settle her hypothesis, and who is otherwise scrupulous about method — and at least one on whether the account can handle the loud cases, where someone asserts the flattering thing in full voice and would sail through a lie detector. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and let the later questions build on whatever concessions the earlier ones should have extracted. Close with a question that puts two of her own commitments in tension with each other.
