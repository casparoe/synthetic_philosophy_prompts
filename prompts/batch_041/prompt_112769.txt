I'm not a philosopher by training — I run a small medical interpreting service, and I've been reading old moral theology in the evenings because arguments at work about when it is acceptable to let someone die and when it is not kept using words I realised I didn't actually understand. I have gotten stuck in the Summa Theologica, in the Second Part of the Second Part, in the question on homicide, article seven, which asks whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense. The translation I am working from is an old one, and the heart of the answer reads:

Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental as explained above (Q[43], A[3]; FS, Q[12], A[1]). Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in "being," as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful, because according to the jurists [*Cap. Significasti, De Homicid. volunt. vel casual.], "it is lawful to repel force by force, provided one does not exceed the limits of a blameless defense." Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, as stated above (A[3]), it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity.

Here is where I come apart. The whole argument seems to rest on the killing being beside the intention rather than aimed at, and yet the defender plainly knows that what he does will very likely kill the man, is permitted to do it anyway, and is told he need not hold back in order to spare the attacker's life; then the last sentence tells me it is not lawful for a private man to intend the killing at all. I can grasp the rough idea of foreseeing a death without aiming at it — I have read a short encyclopedia entry on something it calls the doctrine of double effect — but the entry adds a condition I cannot square with this passage, namely that the bad result must not be the means by which the good result is achieved. In the case described it looks to me as though the attacker's death just is the means: he ceases to threaten me because he is dead or disabled, not alongside it. So either the passage commits to something stronger or stranger than the encyclopedia's version, or I am misreading what the word intend is picking out here. I am also unsure what work the two closing moves are doing — the claim that one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's, and the carve-out for public authority — and whether the proportionality condition in the middle, which makes lawfulness depend on how much force is used, sits awkwardly with the claim that the moral character of the act is fixed by intention alone.
