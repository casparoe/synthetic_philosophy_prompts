I'm a novelist — fourth book, and this one's about a string quartet that falls apart, so there's music on nearly every other page and I've realised I have absolutely no method for it. At the moment I either over-explain (two paragraphs on what the second movement is doing harmonically) or I cop out with something like 'she played, and everyone in the room felt the same thing at once', which I know its rubbish.

A friend who did philosophy told me Schopenhauer has the strangest and possibly most useful account of music going: that music isn't a picture of anything in the world the way painting is, that it doesn't copy the ideas of things but goes straight at the inner nature of the world itself — the will, or whatever you want to call it — and that what it gives you is the essence of feeling rather than this or that particular feeling, which is why it can wreck you without your being able to say what it's about. I half believe this and I want to use it, but I've no idea how to turn it into something I can actually do at the desk at eleven at night.

So: write me a procedure. Something I can run every time a scene has music in it — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a judgment call rather than something the theory settles. It needs to be about authorial choices: what gets described (the notes? the players' bodies? the room? the listener's memories?), how much the narrator is allowed to interpret, whether characters get to say what the music means and whether I should trust them when they do, when to name the piece and when to keep it abstract, how long the passage should be. Ground it in what Schopenhauer actually says in The World as Will and Idea rather than the vibe of what he says — if the theory really does hold that music doesn't express particular emotions, I need the procedure to tell me what to do with the scene where I need the reader to know that the cellist is grieving.

Then run it on three of my scenes: (1) the quartet rehearsing late at night, one of them starts crying, nobody stops playing; (2) a funeral where a phone goes off during the music and it's almost funny; (3) a character who knows nothing about music hears something on a car radio and it ruins his day, and at the moment I have the narrator explain to the reader what the piece 'means', which even I think is cheating.

Be honest about where it will misfire — where the Schopenhauerian move produces a scene nobody wants to read, or where it tells me to do the opposite of what I want. And if one of the scenes comes out with an answer I won't like, don't soften it; I'd rather know. I've got a fortnight and no intention of becoming a Schopenhauer person, so it has to be usable without that.

Write it in British English, please — my copy-editor is strict and I'm tired of the arguments.
