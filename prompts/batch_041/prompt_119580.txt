I am preparing a session of a graduate seminar on Plato's *Laws*, and I want a written argument I can hand to the students as a foil — a position they will have to attack. I would like you to construct it.

The material is the long discussion of wine in the first two books, which tends to be treated as an eccentric digression. The Athenian stranger declines to follow his Spartan and Cretan companions in simply forbidding drunkenness. Wine, he observes, intensifies pleasure, pain, appetite and anger; it drives out recollection, opinion and knowledge and leaves a man with as little self-command as a child, so that a greybeard in his cups is in his second childhood. Yet he proposes to legislate for drinking rather than against it. Wine, in the Bury translation, supplies "a cheap and comparatively harmless test" of the dangerous states of soul — insolence, lust, anger, covetousness, extravagance — and, beyond testing, practice in them. A drinking party properly conducted, with sober and sedate men set in command of the unsober, becomes an instrument of education; he remarks that to fight drunkenness without such leaders is a harder task than to fight enemies without leaders, and that such a gathering needs a commander who is imperturbable and wise in the ways of social gatherings. In the second book he connects all this to his general account of education as the correct training of pleasure and pain, and to the claim that right education depends on the due regulation of convivial intercourse. He also fixes ages: no wine before eighteen, since that is to pour fire upon fire; moderation, but no intoxication, for the man under thirty; and for those over forty, convivial gatherings at which Dionysus is invoked, whose gift he describes as a medicine against the crabbedness of old age, through which the temper of the soul loses its hardness and becomes soft and ductile, like iron forged in fire, so that the old are made ready again to sing and to be taught.

The thesis I want argued is this: that this material is not a digression but the clearest statement in the *Laws* of Plato's theory of virtue — that virtue is a harmony of pleasure and pain produced by habituation under the direction of someone who already possesses it — and that the supervised drinking party is a miniature of the political relation the whole work is trying to build, the sober commanders standing to the drinkers as the legislator stands to the citizens.

Argue for that thesis as forcefully and as carefully as you can. I am not asking for a bibliography or citations; I know the literature, and what I want is the argument itself, worked out. Where the text resists the thesis, do not smooth it over.

Please use the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths.

**Reconstruction** (~250 words). Set out the proposal in your own words for a reader who has not read the passage, and say precisely which claims about wine you take to be carrying the weight in the argument I have asked you to make. If you think my summary misstates something, say so briefly here.

**The case for** (~600 words). The strongest version of the argument for the thesis: how the testing and practice passages should be read, what work the sober commanders do, and how the whole fits the account of education as training in pleasure and pain.

**The case against** (~450 words). The strongest objections. At a minimum, consider these: that the state wine produces — a childlike soul with intensified appetites and no self-command — is precisely the state in which virtue is absent, so that it can serve as a medium neither for testing nor for training; that the sober commanders must already possess the virtue the party is meant to produce, so that the institution presupposes what it claims to generate; and that the diagnostic use of wine may be defensible while the educational use is a rhetorical flourish, the practice amounting at most to skill at staying decent while drunk, which need not transfer to sober life. Answer these as well as you can, but do not pretend they are weak if they are not.

**Verdict** (~200 words). Whether the thesis survives, in whole or in part, and what the most defensible version of it turns out to be.

Continuous prose, please, no bullet points, and keep the whole response under about 1,600 words.
