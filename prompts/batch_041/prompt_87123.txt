A friend in my metaethics reading group put this up on the board and I can't tell if it's a deep result or a party trick. (1) Take any candidate ground G of the good and ask: is a thing good because G endorses or requires it, or does G endorse or require it because it's already good? (2) If the second, G is doing no work -- the good is fixed independently of G and G merely tracks it. (3) If the first, then whatever G endorses is good by that very fact, and G could have endorsed cruelty instead, so the good is arbitrary. (4) So no candidate G survives: not God's commands, not what a fully informed and fully rational advisor would endorse, not human nature, not the categorical imperative. (5) So the good is ungrounded. (6) But nobody can actually believe (5), since we really do distinguish better from worse. So something in (1)-(5) has to go, and this same argument is supposedly the decisive objection to divine command theory. Tell me which step to drop and what dropping it costs. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (6) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying. And something nags at me: my friend says the dilemma only bites when G is a will -- a decree is either arbitrary or idle, nothing else -- but then 'what a fully informed advisor would endorse' has a will in it and 'what's good for us' doesn't, which seems to get things exactly backwards. Which of these moves are things people actually defend and which am I making up? Keep the settled parts and the genuinely contested parts seperate -- don't hand me a contested line as if it were the consensus.
