I am assembling a specification of adequacy for resolutions of the St. Petersburg paradox, and I want the specification itself before I consider any candidate resolution: the criteria by which a proposed resolution should be judged, not a survey of proposals. The intended use is a reading group in which each candidate will be graded against the list, so every requirement should be stated sharply enough that I can decide whether a given resolution satisfies it.

Fix the target as follows. A fair coin is tossed until the first tail appears; if that happens on toss n, the payout is 2^n. The probability of stopping at n is 2^{-n}, so the expected payout is the sum of 1 over all n, which diverges, while the median payout is 2. What is wanted is an account of what a rational agent should be willing to pay for such a ticket, and of where the argument that no finite price is too high goes wrong.

A resolution that speaks only to the base game is not a resolution. The specification must cover at least the following family of cases, and each case should generate or discipline a requirement rather than merely being listed:

1. The base game above.
2. A biased coin: tails occurs with probability p on each toss and the payout is again 2^n for a first tail at n. The expected payout is 2p/(2p - 1) for p > 1/2 and infinite for p ≤ 1/2. The expectation is therefore 51 at p = 0.51 and 5001 at p = 0.5001, while at p = 1/2 it is infinite; for any finite price there is some p > 1/2 whose expectation exceeds it. Any principle that assigns a finite price to the base game must say what separates that game from games with finite but arbitrarily large expectations, and why the boundary falls exactly where it does rather than somewhere nearby.
3. A capped game: play stops after N tosses at the latest and the payout is 2^n for n ≤ N. The expected payout is N + 1, so a game capped near a million dollars is worth about twenty-one dollars.
4. Two tickets: the payout is the sum of two independent copies of the base game. This payout is at least as large as one copy in every outcome and strictly larger with positive probability, yet both expectations are infinite.
5. The seller's side: the same game from the casino's position, which collects a fixed fee and pays the payout. Buyer and seller are two sides of one bet, so a price the buyer would pay and the seller would refuse at is a consistency failure unless the resolution accounts for the asymmetry.
6. Indefinite repetition: independent plays of the base game. With probability 1 the running average of the payouts tends to infinity, but the growth is carried entirely by rare enormous payoffs and is extremely slow in the number of plays.

I expect the requirements to address at least: which step of the naive argument fails, and whether the failure is local to this game or a defect of expected utility generally; whether a resolution may simply declare the refusal to pay to be irrational, and what that costs elsewhere; the idealizations at work (unbounded payouts, infinite sequences of tosses, an unlimited bankroll) and whether the paradox survives their removal; whether expectation is the right summary of a gamble at all, given what happens to dominance and to ranking when expectations are infinite; the relation between a single play and repeated play; whether the resolution owes a definite price, a price range, or only a diagnosis; and how it separates the question of what people do from the question of what is rational.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the commitment of a particular camp. I especially want any subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: if some pair or triple is inconsistent, state the inconsistency as sharply as you can and say which side of it has to give. Then name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate resolutions, and close with a short paragraph on how each of the following fares against the non-negotiable list: concave utility, probability weighting, the claim that the game cannot be realized, and the view that any finite price is simply too low. No reading list or citations, please; I want the criteria.
