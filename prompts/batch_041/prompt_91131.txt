I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or law, and I've gotten my friend group into an argument I can't settle on my own. For about a year, seven of us have been playing a self-amending rules game — we call it Nomic, though our rulebook has drifted a long way from whatever the published version says. On your turn you propose an amendment to the rulebook itself, the table votes, and if it passes it becomes law and governs everything afterward. The game is a specification that rewrites itself while it runs, and it keeps producing disputes that feel as though they ought to have an answer and don't.

Our standing rules are these. Rule 2: an amendment is adopted by a two-thirds vote of the players present. Rule 3: at least five players must be present to vote. Rule 5: challenges go to Dana, who has refereed every session this year and whose rulings we treat as binding.

Here is the part of the log that matters. In each case Dana ruled, and the table accepted the ruling and played on.

- Session 3, Amendment 6 lowered Rule 2's threshold from two-thirds to a simple majority. Passed 5–2 with seven present. Dana: valid. "It clears the old bar and it clears the new one. Which one I used doesn't arise."
- Session 9, Amendment 11 added Rule 2a: any amendment that changes the majority in Rule 2 requires a unanimous vote of all players present. Passed 7–0. Dana: valid.
- Session 12, Amendment 14 restored Rule 2's threshold to two-thirds. Passed 7–0. Dana: valid.
- Session 15, Amendment 17 said it took effect on adoption by a simple majority of the players present, said it could be repealed only by a unanimous vote, and reset the quorum in Rule 3 to four players. It passed 4–3 with seven present. Dana: void. "Two independent grounds. It did not clear the bar in force that night, and a rule may not set the terms of its own repeal."
- Session 18, Amendment 20 lowered the quorum in Rule 3 from five players to four. Passed 6–1 with seven present. Dana: valid.

Now the case in front of us. At Session 20, Amendment 24 passed 4–3 with seven present — short of the two-thirds Rule 2 requires. It carries the same self-validating clause as Amendment 17, and it does one substantive thing: it reverses the turn order every other lap. Nobody challenged it that night. We have since played two full sessions under the new order, and Amendment 25, which is uncontroversial on its own terms, was proposed and passed in the order Amendment 24 created. Tonight a player on the losing side of Amendment 25 has challenged Amendment 24, and Dana rules on it at our next session.

What I want is a prediction of Dana's ruling, and what, if anything, that does to Amendment 25. More than the prediction, I want the rule or rules she might be running — the ones that fit her five rulings equally well, since it looks to me as though they pin down less than they seem to — what each of those rules predicts about Amendment 24, which one I should bet on and why, and the single further case that would most cleanly tell the surviving rules apart. My instinct is that this is like asking whether a commit is judged against the CI configuration in force when it was committed or against whatever is on main today, except that the game has a wrinkle a repository doesn't: we have been living with the result for two sessions. If the log genuinely underdetermines the ruling, say so, and tell me what principle the table commits itself to by deciding either way. I have a hunch this is a known problem in legal theory; I'm not asking for the literature, only for what our own record commits us to.
