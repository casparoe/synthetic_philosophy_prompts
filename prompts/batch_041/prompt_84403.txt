I have run a graduate seminar in philosophy of science for close to twenty years, and there is one dispute in it that has never once been resolved. It concerns inference to the best explanation, and by now I can predict the entire exchange in advance, which is precisely what makes me suspect that it is a stalemate rather than a merely hard problem.

The first camp holds that explanatory considerations — unification, consilience, the absence of ad hoc accommodation, the fitting of the evidence into a coherent picture — can license belief in a hypothesis, and that inference to the best explanation is a fundamental ampliative rule. They concede that the rule cannot be vindicated by any argument that does not already employ it, and they deny that this is a defect: on their view any fundamental rule of inference, deduction and enumerative induction included, is defended in terms that presuppose it, so the demand for a non-explanatory certificate is simply the wrong demand to make. The second camp does not deny that inquirers reason this way; they deny that the pattern has normative standing of its own. Whatever the explanatory virtues contribute, they say, can and must be represented either as a prior over hypotheses or as a likelihood; the residue that resists such representation is a heuristic for generating candidates, not a rule that could make a hypothesis credible. Against the first camp they press the objection that a rule licensing belief in the best of an unpromising field of candidates licenses belief in something antecedently very unlikely (the seminar's standing example is a hypothesis that fits the evidence better than any rival yet has a vanishingly small prior). Against the second camp the first camp presses the point that a prior over a hypothesis space has to come from somewhere, and explanation is the only candidate on offer. Each side has an answer to the other's answer; neither has moved in two decades; and the graduate students, who ought to be the ones unsettled by this, have learned to pick a side and stop thinking about it.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would rather have your reasoning than a survey of positions. Concretely: is this a verbal dispute that would dissolve if we stopped using the word inference for two different things — a psychological description of how inquirers in fact generate and select hypotheses, and a normative claim about what makes a hypothesis credible? Is it a difference about the aim of inquiry, so that the two camps are answering different questions under one name, one asking what we are entitled to believe and the other asking what we are entitled to do with a hypothesis (use it, act on it, treat it as a working assumption)? Is a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance, whether every explanatory virtue can be represented without loss as a prior or a likelihood, which is a technical question about which formal framework is in play rather than a philosophical question about explanation? Or is it bedrock: two sets of starting intuitions about whether an ampliative rule requires external certification, neither of which can be argued for from the other?

My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the dispute contracts considerably once one distinguishes two roles an explanatory virtue might play: a constraint on which hypotheses are admissible in the first place, so that no candidate from an unpromising field is ever on the table, and a ranking device over an already-admissible space. The objection about unlikely winners needs the second reading; the autonomy defence is most plausible under the first. If that is right, the stall is largely an equivocation, and progress consists in specifying which role is in play in which argument. But I am not confident, and I can see why the suggestion may be too neat: the two roles may not be separable in practice, and if they are not, the impasse is substantive after all.

Tell me which kind of disagreement this is, and why, using what each side actually says rather than what is usually attributed to them. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, I would rather hear that argued than have it softened.
