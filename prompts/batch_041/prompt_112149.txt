At our department's monthly work-in-progress meeting, two of my colleagues — not I — spent the better part of an hour disputing whether a third party had manipulated someone, and I would like a careful adjudication of their exchange rather than a verdict on the third party. Let me first set out the case they were arguing over, then reproduce their exchange as faithfully as I can.

The case. A colleague of ours, whom I will call Dana, supervises doctoral students. A strong applicant, who held two offers, was leaning toward the other one. Over several conversations Dana praised the applicant's fit for her laboratory, arranged for him to meet a string of impressive former students, observed that the rival program's placement record in recent years had been uneven (true, but a selective reading), and remarked that he would be wasted elsewhere. Nothing she said was false, and she never mentioned that she stood to gain from his staying — from his labor, and from the reflected credit of his choice. He stayed.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

Move 1. Colleague A: This was manipulation. Dana did not so much offer the applicant reasons as engineer a mood — she manufactured awe and flattery and let him draw the conclusion himself. Colleague B: But all persuasion traffics in feeling. If making someone sense the pull of a genuine opportunity counts as manipulation, then advising, teaching, and friendship are all manipulation, and the word has eaten everything.

Move 2. A: The distinguishing mark is concealment. Dana hid that she was an interested party, and that her laboratory and her reputation both rise with his choice. Manipulation is influence that works by keeping its own aims out of view. B: Concealment cannot be the whole story. Imagine someone who announces, "I am about to work on your guilt, because I want you to stay," and then does exactly that: fully transparent, still manipulation. And imagine someone who conceals that he is arguing on behalf of a cause he cares about, while offering sound reasons in good faith: covert, yet not manipulation. Transparency, then, is neither necessary nor sufficient.

Move 3. B: What actually marks manipulation is that it bypasses the target's rational agency — it works on non-rational levers, exploiting a fear, a vanity, or a bias, rather than on the target's assessment of reasons. A: Then tell me which levers are non-rational. The applicant's wish for a good career and for the regard of senior people is not a bias; it is a reason. Had Dana cited placement figures, you would call it rational. She cited people and standing — the same considerations, a warmer channel. Either everything that moves a person through feeling is irrational, or the line is being drawn to secure the verdict you wanted.

Move 4. A: The real issue is whose judgment was operative. The applicant decided, but Dana supplied the shape of the decision — she framed the options so that the conclusion arrived on its own and was experienced as his. That is what it is to substitute your will for another's: not to force a choice, but to make it seem to issue from the chooser. B: By that standard every teacher, parent, and well-made argument substitutes its will, since every influence helps constitute the judgment it produces. If a good argument changes your mind and you endorse the change, nothing has been substituted. The test cannot be that influence shaped him — that is simply what influence is.

Move 5. A: Then take seriously the test I think you are groping toward: would Dana's method still have worked had the applicant seen all of it — the interest, the flattery, the selective statistics? If it collapses under that light, then what did the work was his not-seeing, and that is manipulation. B: Much legitimate persuasion is like that; the Socratic question works partly because the student cannot see where it leads. And some manipulation survives full disclosure — a skilled guilt-trip can work even when named aloud. So your test detects when an influence is fragile, not when it is manipulative.

Move 6. B: In any case, Dana meant well; she sincerely believed her laboratory was the better place for him. Benevolence of intent should count for something. A: Intent is beside the point. A benevolent manipulator is still a manipulator; what matters is the mechanism and whether it respected the applicant's agency, not the warmth of the hand on the lever.

I do not want a bare declaration of a winner. Please adjudicate the exchange as it was actually conducted. Structure your response in exactly the following sections, in this order, and label each:

1. Statement of the issue (~100 words). Say what is genuinely at stake in the dispute, as distinct from what the two of them take themselves to be disputing.
2. The competing criteria (~200 words). Reconstruct, and state as sympathetically as you can, the criterion each party is implicitly relying on, so that each could recognize his own position in your rendering.
3. Move-by-move adjudication (roughly 100 words per move; six moves). For each numbered move, say who has the better of it and why, rating each contribution on a scale from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and flag any move that is correct but beside the point, or that shifts the question without acknowledging it.
4. The cruxes (~150 words). Identify the sub-questions on which the dispute actually turns — the ones such that a settled answer would move at least one party.
5. What both parties miss (~150 words). Name the considerations that neither side has raised and that bear on the case.
6. Verdict (~150 words). Say who is more right overall, and on which specific points, without pretending the matter is fully one-sided.
