I spent 35 years as a geochemist and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I'm re-inventing something here. I keep bumping into the complaint that historians of science write "whig" history — telling the story as a march toward the present and grading old scientists by what we happen to believe now. Honestly this never bothered me in my old field, because there does seem to be a real direction: independent lines of evidence keep converging on the same answers, and that feels different from a room full of people just agreeing with each other. So what I'd like you to do is make the argument that whig history is basically fine — maybe even correct — for the natural sciences, but a genuine vice in moral and political history, and that the line between the two can be drawn by pointing to convergence among independent methods rather than mere consensus. Argue it as strongly as you can, then tell me where you think it breaks down. Please don't give me a survey of what everyone has said — I want you to actually stake out and defend the position, and I'd rather you spend the words on the hard cases than on throat-clearing.
