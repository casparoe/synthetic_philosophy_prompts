I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy and the head TA for a large upper-level lecture course on population ethics and procreative ethics; I wrote this term's midterm myself and I'm now working through a stack of scripts on a marking deadline that leaves me less time per answer than I'd like. One question was built around a case of my own rather than the familiar examples from the literature, because I wanted students to reconstruct the structure of the problem instead of reciting a standard case, and in lecture I gave them a piece of terminology I expect them to use — the same-subject constraint, by which I mean the requirement that a claim of harm be grounded in a comparison between two states of the same person. The question read: "Question 3 (25 points; parts (a), (b), and (c) are worth 10, 5, and 10 respectively). A and B plan to have a child. Because of a temporary environmental exposure, a child conceived in the next few months would have a mild but permanent impairment that will make her life go somewhat less well than it otherwise would; if A and B wait until next year, the exposure will have cleared and their child will not be impaired. Assume that a child conceived next year would be a numerically different person from any child the couple could conceive now. (a) Explain why, on a view according to which an action is wrong only if it makes some determinate person worse off than they would otherwise have been, the couple's decision to conceive now cannot be criticized as harmful. (b) Say precisely what work the assumption about numerical difference is doing in your answer to (a), and what would follow if it were dropped. (c) Evaluate the following proposed resolution: 'The couple's choice is permissible, since the child who results has a life worth living and would not have existed at all otherwise, so she has no complaint.'"

The script I'm looking at reads: "(a) The couple's choice cannot be criticized as harmful because the only alternative open to the child they actually conceive is that she never exists at all. Since the child conceived next year would be a numerically different individual, there is no possible future in which this child has a better life; her impairment is not something inflicted on her in preference to something better for her. Applying the same-subject constraint, the comparison a harm-claim requires cannot be constructed, and on the view that an act is wrong only if it makes a determinate person worse off, the decision falls outside the prohibition. I take it that this difficulty is specific to consequentialism, which is the family of views that assesses acts by their outcomes; a theory grounded in rights can simply observe that no one's rights have been violated and consider the matter closed. (b) The assumption about numerical difference is doing less work than it appears to. What blocks the harm-claim is that no one can be worse off for having been brought into existence, and that holds whether or not the two possible children are the same individual. If we dropped the assumption and supposed that the couple's child would be the very same person either way, the analysis would be unchanged: she would still owe her existence to the choice, and she would still lack a baseline of non-existence against which to measure the impairment. (c) The proposed resolution is right, and I would go further: the couple has not merely failed to harm their child, they have benefited her, since a life with a mild impairment is still a life worth living and she would have had nothing otherwise. Once we see that the child has no complaint, the only remaining basis for saying the couple should have waited would be a judgment about total well-being in the world — an impersonal judgment that has nothing to do with her interests. So the person-affecting approach that makes (a) come out as it does also shows that the choice is permissible."

Where a claim is defensible rather than simply wrong, please say so instead of treating it as an error, and keep errors distinct from points that are merely undeveloped, so that the total I report is one I can defend if the student comes to office hours.
