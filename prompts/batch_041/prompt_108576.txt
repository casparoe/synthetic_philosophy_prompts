I've spent enough time in decision-theory and modality discussions online to have absorbed the vocabulary of possible worlds without ever getting the metaphysics straight, and I keep encountering a particular summary of counterpart theory that I suspect is subtly off but cannot diagnose on my own. Here is the passage, reproduced from memory from a blog post I can no longer locate; I have tried to keep it faithful.

"Counterpart theory is David Lewis's way of doing modal metaphysics without identity across worlds. On Lewis's picture, possible worlds are other concrete universes on a par with our own, and every individual exists in exactly one of them—we are 'world-bound.' So there is no such thing as one and the same person turning up in two worlds; strictly speaking, transworld identity is a fiction. When we say that you could have been a banker, then, we are not saying that you—this very individual—exist in some world where you are a banker. We are saying that a counterpart of yours, a distinct individual who resembles you closely enough, is a banker there. Counterparts are just similar individuals, so the counterpart relation is simply the ordinary similarity relation, though it is vague and context-dependent. Because of this, Lewis's view is that talk about what you could have been is never really about you; it is about someone else who happens to be like you. The familiar objection—that when I say I could have been a banker I mean me, not a stand-in—is generally taken to have refuted the theory, or at least to show that it is a stipulative redescription of modal talk rather than an analysis of what we mean by it. And since counterpart theory comes bundled with Lewis's belief in other concrete worlds, anyone who rejects that picture can set the whole thing aside."

What I want is an audit, not a general introduction to the topic. Take the paragraph claim by claim and sort each one into one of four bins: something a careful defender of the view—not a caricature of it—would accept as stated; a simplification that is defensible but needs a specific qualification, which you should supply; something Lewis would explicitly deny; or a live objection presented as a settled result. I would especially like the seams marked in two places. First, where does the summary slide from a claim about what the semantics does to a claim about what the sentences are about? Second, where does it use a technical term as though it carried its everyday sense? While you are at it, tell me whether the passage is conflating transworld identity with identity over time, and whether the commitment to concrete worlds is genuinely part of counterpart theory or a separate commitment that the summary has welded on.

Then the bottom line. The passage's closing verdict is that counterpart theory is a stipulative redescription that changes the subject, and can therefore be bracketed by anyone who is not antecedently a modal realist. Does that verdict survive your corrections, or does it inherit the errors you flagged? I will admit my priors: the objection always struck me as the one obviously right thing in the paragraph, so if you think it fails, I want to know precisely what a defender says in reply and whether the reply amounts to more than a face-saving stipulation. Where the paragraph is in fact right in a way that is easy to miss, say so as well; I would rather over-correct than keep a false picture.
