I'm recording a long-form interview with a decision theorist who defends causal decision theory and argues that the familiar counterexamples are either misdescribed or less damaging than they appear. The centrepiece will be the Death in Damascus case: you meet Death in the market, learn that your appointment is tomorrow, and must choose whether to stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, knowing that Death has already predicted where you will be and will be waiting there. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, that make her work through the mechanics of the case rather than recite the idea that rational choice tracks causation rather than correlation. At least one question should force her to state what the causal expected values of staying and of fleeing actually are, and what follows about whether either act is required, permitted, or forbidden. Another should press the deliberative instability of the case: if she says the agent should simply pick one option and commit to it, ask what makes that choice rational rather than arbitrary, and whether the same move rescues causal decision theory in the psychopath button case. A third should ask whether she accepts ratifiability as a constraint on rational choice, and if so how her theory satisfies it here. Include a question about whether the scenario is coherent at all, given that the predictor's reliability seems to make deliberation self-undermining, and a question about whether adding a small causal sweetener to staying — dying at home rather than in a strange city — shifts the verdict in a way she can defend. Close with a question about what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon causal decision theory.
