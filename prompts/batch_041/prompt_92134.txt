You are a philosopher of mind who also understands distributed systems and concurrent computing well enough to use its concepts precisely. I want you to act as a careful translator between those two vocabularies, and I want the residue to be the point of the exercise. Before you write anything, spend some time thinking the problem through: sketch for yourself, in notes, the main positions on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, then the main concepts of distributed systems that might correspond to them, and only then attempt the translation. Do not rush the mapping; the honest failures are more valuable to me than a smooth one.

Here is who I am. I am a site-reliability engineer with no formal philosophy background. My mental furniture is the vocabulary of distributed systems: nodes and replicas, network partitions, leader election and quorums, consensus protocols, replication and state-machine replication, consistency models running from linearizability down to eventual consistency, single-system images, fencing tokens, and the trade-offs that show up when a cluster splits and each side believes it is still the whole. I am fluent in that vocabulary and in the engineering judgments that go with it; I am a beginner in philosophy of mind.

What I want from you is a systematic translation of the philosophical debate about split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness into my vocabulary, one position at a time. Take the main interpretations of the commissurotomy findings — the idea that a divided patient harbors two streams of consciousness, the idea that there is one subject whose access to itself is divided, and the idea that the unity of consciousness is in some way an illusion or an achievement rather than a given — and restate each as faithfully as you can in the terms I actually use. For each, tell me what carries over cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Keep the translation honest: do not quietly assume that a partitioned cluster simply is two systems and present that as the translation, when the philosophical question is precisely whether partition at that level settles anything about how many subjects there are.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims about the unity of consciousness translate cleanly into something like a consistency guarantee, a single-system image, or a quorum, and which translate only by stretching my terms past what they mean? What in the philosophers' debate has no counterpart in how I think about a cluster that has split — is there anything in the notion of phenomenal unity, or in what it is like to be one subject rather than two, that my framework cannot even state? And when something fails to carry over, tell me which way to read the failure: is my vocabulary missing something real, or is the original claim less clear than it looked and trading on a picture rather than a distinction?

I am especially interested in whether the engineering notion of a system being one thing for practical purposes can bear the weight the philosophers put on being one thing for consciousness, and where exactly that analogy breaks rather than merely strains. End by telling me what a good engineer should conclude they cannot answer with their own tools, and why.
