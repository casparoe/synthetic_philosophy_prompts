ok so i'm the person who writes the behavior spec for our consumer AI assistant -- the doc that says what the thing may and may not say to people. we ship to a few million users, and every week i get escalations: it stated something flatly and it was wrong, or it hedged on something it should've just answered, or it said "studies suggest" when there's really one study. right now we handle these ad hoc and legal keeps overruling me at the last minute, so i want an actual procedure.

the part i need real help with is the philosophical spine. there's this whole literature on what you're allowed to assert -- one camp says you may assert only what you know, another says truth is enough, another says sincere belief is enough, another says reasonable belief. i know none of that maps onto a language model cleanly, because the model doesn't believe or know things in the normal sense, and nobody here can inspect its insides anyway -- we only ever see outputs and whatever sources it had. so part of the job is figuring out the operational stand-in for each of those views (calibration? source reliability? whether a careful person with the same sources could defend the claim?) and being explicit that its a substitution, plus where it breaks.

what i want: a decision procedure my policy team can run on a single flagged output in under twenty minutes, without me in the room. the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the case to a human, and -- the part i care about most -- where it hands off because the question is really about values (how much we care about being wrong here) rather than about evidence. tiers, not yes/no: assert plainly, assert with a hedge or an attribution, present as one view among others, don't assert at all.

three things it has to survive. (1) the move where the model dodges commitment by saying "some people believe..." or "it's debated" while still planting the claim in the reader's head. my instinct is that's the worst of both worlds, but i can't defend that in a way legal accepts, and i suspect the literature has something to say about whether that even counts as asserting and whether it's worse than just saying the thing outright. (2) stakes -- a false claim about which pasta shape is better is a different animal from a false claim about a drug interaction, but i don't know whether stakes change what the model is allowed to assert or only how bad it is when it goes wrong, and i gather philosophers fight about that too. (3) claims about the future or about probabilities, where there's no fact of the matter yet to be right about.

then run it on three cases: the assistant flatly stating a well-evidenced but politically contested empirical claim; the "some people say" hedge; and a case where it's pulling from a source it trusts something that goes against its own leanings, and the question is whether it may assert it anyway. i expect the procedure to hand me at least one answer i won't like -- the flat-assertion case probably comes out licensing exactly what our comms team wants banned -- so dont sand it down to make me happy. and end with what the procedure can't decide and how a person should break the tie. no reading list please, i wont read it.
