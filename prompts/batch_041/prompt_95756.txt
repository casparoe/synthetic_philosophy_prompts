I have been trying to work out what the law of armed conflict must believe about moral responsibility, and I keep colliding with a pattern I cannot make sense of.

A soldier in a regular army who kills an enemy soldier in battle commits no crime. He is not a murderer, he cannot be tried for it, and if he is captured he is a prisoner of war rather than a criminal — and this holds whichever side he is on. The conscript serving an aggressor enjoys exactly the same legal privilege to kill as the soldier defending his own country against that aggression. Yet the same body of law is not neutral about the war itself. The international crime of aggression is a leadership crime: by definition only those who control or direct a state's political or military action can commit it. The ministers and generals who launch the war are criminals; the men who fight it are not, and cannot be, however unjust the war. And the privilege does not reach everyone who kills a soldier. A fighter who does not satisfy the conditions the law sets for lawful belligerency — who is not under responsible command, bears no fixed distinctive sign, carries arms openly only intermittently — can be prosecuted in an ordinary criminal court for murdering a soldier whom a uniformed soldier standing beside him could have killed with complete impunity. Meanwhile, the very law that treats the justice of the war as beyond a soldier's competence insists that he must disobey a manifestly unlawful order and that superior orders are no defence to atrocity. He is incompetent to judge the ends he serves and competent, indeed obligated, to judge the means.

Reconstruct the theory of moral responsibility this practice presupposes. Is killing enemy combatants not wrong at all, a permission attaching to a role and therefore available to whoever occupies it? Or is it ordinarily wrong and merely excused here, by non-culpable ignorance, duress, or the impossibility of refusal — and if so, does the excuse hold equally for the aggressor's conscript and for the man defending his home? Does the practice strictly require the moral equality of combatants, or does it need only a symmetric rule, justified by reciprocity, discipline, and the hopelessness of asking soldiers to adjudicate their own cause, beneath which a thoroughly asymmetric morality could sit unnoticed? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — in particular the picture of war as a police action, on which one side's soldiers are doing something like police work and the other side's are doing something like murder? Does the whole arrangement hang together, given that it deems the soldier unable to judge the justice of the war but obligated to judge the lawfulness of an order? And where is it merely underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license it exactly as it stands?

Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the privilege itself, in prisoner-of-war status, in war crimes prosecution, in veterans' benefits, in the way the dead of both sides are honoured — if we replaced the presupposed view with the view that unjust combatants do something seriously wrong and are at most excused for it. Argue it out in your own words; I do not want a reading list or citations.
