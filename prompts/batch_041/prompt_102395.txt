I want to do a case analysis of the Jahi McMath affair through a lens I've been trying to build myself, and I'd like you to stress-test the lens on the case. Quick refresher on the facts, since you can't look them up: Jahi was a 13-year-old in Oakland who went in for a tonsillectomy in 2013, had a bad bleed and a cardiac arrest, and her hospital declared her brain dead by neurological criteria a few days later. Her family refused the finding on religious grounds and fought it in court; California issued a death certificate, but the family eventually moved her to New Jersey, which is the one state that lets families reject the neurological standard for religious reasons, and she stayed on a ventilator there until her heart stopped in 2018. So you had one body, one set of physiology, and two states legally disagreeing about whether she was alive, plus a family that never accepted it.

Here's the lens, which I've made up, so don't go looking for it in the literature — just tell me if it earns its keep. I want to split any definition of death into three levels: the state level (what condition of the organism death actually is), the test level (what bedside findings we treat as proving that condition), and the authority level (who gets to decide when the state has been reached, and whose values settle it). Most disputes that look like fights about the state are really fights about the authority level wearing the state level's clothes — I've been calling that criteria-laundering. Second piece: the distinction between irreversibility and permanence for circulatory death. Irreversible means the function can't come back even if we tried; permanent means it won't come back because nobody is going to try. Those come apart, and I think the whole thing turns on which one a criterion is really asserting.

So: map the McMath case onto the three levels. At which level was the hospital actually operating, and at which level was the family? Does criteria-laundering describe what the courts did, or does it misdescribe it? Then bring in the irreversibility/permanence distinction — does it explain why the circulatory standard and the neurological standard came apart so badly in her case, or is it a distinction that does no work here? And the payoff I actually care about: does the episode show the whole brain-death settlement is unstable, or does it show the settlement is fine and the instability was really about institutional authority all along? Be honest about where the lens runs out and I'm just forcing the case into it.
