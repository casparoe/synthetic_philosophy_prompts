I direct a college program inside a medium-security prison — sixty students, three adjuncts borrowed from the state university, no internet, no laptops, everything argued out over photocopies — and in three weeks I'm giving the commencement address at our first graduation. The audience is genuinely mixed, and I have to write for all of it: about twenty graduates, most of them serving long sentences, some in for violent offenses; their families, who have driven a long way and are dressed up; the warden and a dozen correctional officers who have to sit through the whole thing and who have told me more than once that they will be listening for any hint that we in the program think the people in front of them were victims of circumstance; four volunteer faculty from the philosophy department, two of whom will catch everything; and a reporter from the local paper. The warden speaks first and will keep it short. Then it's me, at a lectern next to a card table with a sheet cake on it, for about seven minutes — call it a thousand words.

Here is my difficulty. I've spent the past year teaching a seminar on free will, and I have come to think that Galen Strawson's Basic Argument is probably right. As I understand it: you do what you do, in any given situation, because of the way you are; to be ultimately responsible for what you do you would have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects; but you cannot be ultimately responsible for the way you are, since anything you did to shape yourself would trace back to a self you did not make; so you cannot be ultimately responsible for what you do. The argument never mentions determinism, and Strawson seems to think it goes through whether or not determinism is true, which is part of what unsettles me. The standard commencement speech is a machine for doing precisely what the argument says cannot be done: it credits the graduates with having made themselves. I don't want to stand up in front of these people and their families and say something I believe to be false. But I don't want the opposite either — a speech that dissolves four years of work into luck, or that hands anyone in the room a sentence to repeat at a parole hearing. And I've noticed that if the argument kills blame it appears to kill praise in exactly the same way, which I find harder to live with than I expected to. I also can't tell whether Strawson means to leave us with some weaker, non-ultimate notion of responsibility that could do honest work at a lectern, or whether that is only a verbal retreat; I suspect the speech has to take a side on it. One more thing, which is really what set this off: at the rehearsal dinner, a graduate who learned to read at forty and is now the sharpest close reader in the seminar asked me, half joking, that if nobody made himself, what exactly is he supposed to have done these last four years.

Please write the speech. It has to work out loud — concrete, short sentences, plain words, no jargon, and at least one passage addressed as squarely to the warden and the officers as to the graduates. It should be honest about the argument without becoming a lecture on it, and I would rather not quote Strawson at all, since at the podium I will be working from memory and I don't want to put words in his mouth. After the speech, in two or three sentences, tell me what it refuses to say and which single line is carrying the most weight, so that I know what to protect if I have to cut it down to five minutes.
