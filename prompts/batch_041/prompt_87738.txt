Last night I spent two hours in a hotel bar arguing with an old friend of mine — a philosopher, and one of the groom's colleagues — about the toast I have to give this afternoon, and I am no closer to knowing what to say than I was when we sat down. I would like you to write the toast.

The occasion. My oldest friend, Daniel, is marrying Ana. Daniel teaches moral philosophy; most of his department will be in the room, and they will hear every structural move a speech makes whether or not it means to make one. Ana plays cello in a regional orchestra and has no interest in any of it. Her father drove trains for thirty-one years and told me at dinner last night that he was afraid the toast would be too clever for the rest of us.

My friend's objection, which I will try to state fairly, because it is the whole difficulty. My draft contained a sentence of this shape: they are good for each other, because each of them is steadier, and braver, and less frightened, in the other's company. He said that sentence is fatal, and he had an argument, not merely a taste. Any clause of that shape, he said, turns two people into the satisfiers of a description. If what is loved is the steadiness and the bravery, then the steadiness and the bravery are what is loved, and a stranger who supplied them would serve as well. He has a test he calls the recommendation test: if a sentence in the toast could be used to recommend Ana to somebody who had never met her, cut it. He then brought in Williams's man who saves his wife rather than the stranger, and the thought that she is his wife arriving as a justification rather than as a reminder — the point usually called one thought too many — and said that by that standard my draft was one thought too many, repeated for three minutes.

I said the obvious things back. That the guests are not choosing anyone's spouse, so nothing said in a toast can function as a recommendation in the sense he means. That a speech which refuses to say anything about the two of them is an empty glass and a joke. That a reminder is all very well until you ask what it reminds us of. He said the couple are the ones listening, and that a public inventory of their merits is the one thing a marriage cannot be told. We did not settle it, and the speech is at four o'clock.

So: the speech itself, three minutes or so, delivered standing, glass in hand, to a room that is half philosophers and half Ana's family. Requirements, all of which I take seriously.

- Not one sentence may give a reason why Daniel and Ana are right for each other, why the marriage is a good thing, or what makes them work. Every sentence has to survive the recommendation test.
- It must still be a real toast: specific to these two, not a set of warm noises that could be given at any wedding. My friend objects to reasons; he has no patience for vagueness either.
- It may not mention philosophy, Williams, my friend, this argument, or the fact that it is being careful. Ana's father should hear a warm speech about two people he loves. Daniel's colleagues should hear what the speech is doing, and think it was done without showing off.
- Somewhere it must decline to explain something. The refusal has to land as confidence or as affection, not as a hole and not as a trick.
- The only facts you have are these: they met in a laundrette on New Year's Eve, the only two people there, and neither has ever given a satisfying account of what they talked about; Daniel has carried a photograph of Ana in his wallet for six years, taken from behind, so that you cannot see her face; Ana is a cellist and Daniel cannot carry a tune, and hums along anyway; the two of them argue about the thermostat with a seriousness they bring to nothing else; Ana's father calls Daniel the professor; Daniel's mother is in the front row and has asked me not to make her cry. Please do not invent other facts, and do not give them children, or houses, or a history I have not mentioned.

Write it as something I can actually say out loud, in my own voice, to that room.
