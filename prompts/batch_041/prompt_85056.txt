A friend of mine writes popular philosophy, and she recently summarized Avicenna's flying man argument for me as follows. Avicenna asks us to imagine a man created all at once, fully grown, suspended in midair, blindfolded, his limbs held apart so that he receives no sensation and touches nothing, not even himself. Avicenna says that such a man would still affirm that he exists. Since he is sure of his self while knowing nothing of his body, the self he knows cannot be the body; hence the soul is an immaterial substance, independent of the body and able to survive its death, which makes Avicenna the original substance dualist. The argument is essentially Descartes's cogito four centuries early, an argument from doubt: strip away everything that can be doubted — the senses, the body, the world — and what remains is the certainty of the "I." Avicenna's real point, she says, is that self-awareness is not something the soul acquires from the body; it has it always, though the body ordinarily obscures it.

Please audit this claim by claim rather than giving me a general account of the argument. For each sentence, tell me what a specialist in Avicenna would accept, what they would treat as a forgivable simplification, and what Avicenna denied or never claimed. I am especially interested in how much of the conclusion about immateriality and survival is carried by the thought experiment itself and how much has to come from elsewhere in his system, and whether the Descartes comparison illuminates anything or simply misleads. I suspect the last sentence is basically right and the Descartes line is just a cliché, but I cannot tell where the real mistakes are. Please write in American English.
