I am a novelist, and for some months I have been arguing with a friend who writes speculative fiction and reads a good deal of theology. His position is that the world we live in is like a novel, and that a novelist's experience shows why a good creator could not have made a world without evil in it. On his account the correspondence runs like this: a story with no conflict is unreadable, so a world without suffering would be worthless; the villain and the disaster are not blemishes but load-bearing parts of the design; and the reader who wishes the author had spared a character is wishing for a duller book. He has a term of his own for the quantity of suffering a satisfying plot requires — narrative overhead — and he says the existence of evil is simply that overhead, paid by the characters for the sake of a whole that is better for having it. He takes this to be the serious version of what is usually called, after Leibniz, the best of all possible worlds.

I would rather have the audit than the verdict. Please do the following. First, set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the novelist's picture plays the role of the creator, the characters, the plot, the reader, the suffering, and the goodness that is supposed to be served — and mark the places where the picture has no counterpart at all. Second, tell me where it breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the use he is making of it from the ones that are merely picturesque. Three concern me in particular, and I want to know whether you judge them fatal or survivable: that a novelist can write a genuinely good story in which no one is harmed, so that conflict being necessary does not entail suffering being necessary; that the satisfaction the analogy points to is felt by an audience outside the story, whereas in the case at issue there may be no audience whose enjoyment could compensate anyone for anything; and that the best of all possible worlds is ambiguous between best overall and best for those who live in it, with the analogy seeming to support only the first. Third, tell me whether the conclusion he wants — that evil is justified as a condition of the whole's excellence — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If you think a better analogy would do the work he wants, or that none can, say so.

Finally, be explicit about the confidence you attach to your own verdict and about what would move you off it: name the argument or consideration that would lead you to a different answer, and identify where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
