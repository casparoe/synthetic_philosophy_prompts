I chair the defence committee of our parliament, and before next week's debate on the popular initiative we are calling Service for All I have to circulate the committee's written recommendation. Please draft it for me.

The initiative would abolish our present standing force of some four thousand volunteers and put in its place a universal obligation: every resident, on reaching twenty, owes twelve months of service, discharged either in the militia or in a civilian service corps working in hospitals, mountain rescue, and care of the elderly. There is no way to buy an exemption and no general waiver save medical unfitness. Its drafters insist that what they propose is not conscription. Conscription, they argue, is the state seizing particular bodies for its own military ends; their scheme, by contrast, is a duty each person owes to the polity as such and may discharge in whichever form best serves the common good. They lean on a clause in our constitution that forbids forced labour "save under a general civic obligation," a phrase, as everyone knows, that no court has ever had to define. The general staff says a militia cannot man the high passes or the new coastal radar in time and wants the professional force preserved. The finance ministry says the civilian corps will cost more than it saves. The civil-liberties groups say that routing refusers into unpaid hospital work merely launders conscription, and that the "general civic obligation" clause was meant for jury duty and flood relief, not for a standing levy on a year of a young person's life.

I want a recommendation the members can vote on without further drafting: adopt, reject, or adopt with named amendments. If you recommend adoption, say what should become of the volunteers already enlisted and over what period. Above all, engage the drafters' distinction between conscription and universal service on the merits; do not wave it away as a word game, and do not grant it as a truism either. If it survives scrutiny, tell me what follows for the rules governing conscientious objectors; if it collapses, tell me which of the drafters' claims falls with it. Give the strongest case for whichever side you decide against its full weight rather than a straw version.

Write it as continuous prose, in the form of a letter to the committee: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
