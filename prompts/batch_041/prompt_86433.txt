I have come to hold a position, and I want you to map its exposure rather than restate it. The position is this. When many roughly likeminded agents are each capable, on their own authority, of bringing about the same consequential outcome, and each decides whether to do so on the basis of a private and fallible assessment, then the probability that at least one of them imposes that outcome against the group's better judgment rises toward certainty as the number of agents grows. The individual error rate can be very small and the aggregate risk still be large, because the risk is compounded across many independent chances to act. From this I conclude that the default in such settings should be a standing presumption against unilateral action: refrain unless one can show that the collective assessment, and not merely one's own, favors acting.

What I want from you is the anatomy of this position's exposure. Which assumptions are actually load-bearing, what would count against them, and which candidate defeaters are real rather than merely rhetorical? In particular I want your judgment on the following, and I do not want them treated as equally serious:

(a) Whether the argument is well formed as stated. It counts the errors that produce action and ignores the errors that produce inaction, so the mirror-image quantity — the probability that no one acts when the group would have wanted someone to — may be just as large. If a conformity rule merely trades one aggregate risk for its mirror image, is anything gained?

(b) Whether the aggregate risk really scales with the number of agents once the assumption of independent, individually small error probabilities is relaxed. Correlated errors, heterogeneous error rates, or a single agent who is simply better informed than the rest may break the derivation; I want to know how much of the result is an artifact of the probabilistic model rather than a feature of the situation.

(c) Whether the argument presupposes what it needs. It takes the pooled judgment of the group as the benchmark against which the acting agent is judged fallible. But the agent who acts is often the one holding information the group lacks, and the group's own errors may be correlated rather than cancelled. What would have to be true for the group's judgment to deserve that privileged status?

(d) Whether the conclusion survives the case of the lone dissenter whose unauthorized act averts a collective disaster, and whether such cases can be dismissed as rare exceptions without circularity.

(e) Whether the benchmark is well defined at all. With genuinely heterogeneous values, "acting against the group's interest" may not be a determinate description, in which case the argument may be about disagreement rather than about error.

(f) Whether the prescription recurses rather than dissolving the problem: a group that adopts a norm of conformity is then itself a single agent capable of acting unilaterally with respect to other groups, so the same structure may reappear one level up.

(g) Whether the position is really about unanimity and multiplicity at all, or whether the work is being done by something else — irreversibility, say, or the asymmetry between harms and benefits — in which case the interesting version of the view may be narrower than the one I stated and largely a restatement of a mundane point about evidential thresholds for actions that cannot be undone.

I would also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not, so that I can see whether it risks anything at all or has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of a probabilistic argument whose parameters are unmeasured.

Rank the defeaters by how much they should move me: which are fatal, which force a genuine qualification of the position, and which are noise. Take a stand. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me. Tell me whether the core claim survives in any form worth holding, what the surviving form is, and whether the thing I end up with is still recognizably the position I began with or something thinner wearing its name. The consideration I would personally find most persuasive against myself is a demonstration that once the view is restricted to the cases it actually handles, it has quietly become a claim about irreversibility rather than about unilateralism; if you think that is what happens, say so plainly, and say whether it matters.
