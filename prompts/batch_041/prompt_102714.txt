You are a philosopher who works on evolutionary debunking arguments and on the epistemology of moral belief. Before you answer, think carefully and lay out for yourself, in your own words, what each side in the following dispute is really committed to; then adjudicate it.

Two colleagues in my department had a long argument after a seminar and asked me to referee, and I honestly cannot decide. One of them argues as follows. (1) Our moral beliefs are the product of natural selection, which favored them because they made our ancestors cooperate and survive, not because they were true; and the process that produced them would have produced the same beliefs even if no moral facts had existed, so it is insensitive to the moral truth, and an insensitive process gives us no reason to trust its output. The other replied that (2) this ignores the possibility that the moral truths are themselves part of what made cooperation adaptive: if it is true that survival and cooperation are good, then selection was tracking a moral fact after all, and evolution's influence is not independent of the truth in the way the argument needs. The first answered that (3) this rescue assumes a substantive moral premise, namely that survival is good, which is exactly what is in dispute, and that even if granted it vindicates at most a thin core of beliefs about cooperation, not the rich moral views we actually hold. The second then said that (4) the argument proves too much, since our faculties of logic and perception also have evolutionary histories, so unless the first can point to a principled difference between moral belief and those, the debunking either proves nothing or destroys everything. The first replied that (5) there is a difference, because perception has something to be tested against, whereas moral properties, if they exist, are causally inert in just the way that rules out any feedback between them and our beliefs. The second came back that (6) causal inertness is not the same as being untrackable, and that to demand a causal feedback loop is to beg the question against realism by assuming that only causally efficacious facts can be known.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying which of the six moves are sound, which are mistaken, and what each side is overlooking, rather than just who wins overall. Where a move is a good point but overstated, say so and say what the defensible version of it would be. I would also like your verdict on the underlying question: does the evolutionary history of our moral beliefs give us a reason to doubt moral realism, and if so, how much of it? Write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague who asked for your honest assessment.
