I would like to work through the Hart–Devlin debate as a live argument rather than as a chapter of intellectual history, and I want you to take the side I find harder to defend. Play a contemporary defender of legal moralism in Devlin's tradition: someone who holds that a community's shared morality is not a heap of private preferences but part of what makes the community a community, and that the criminal law may therefore enforce it — not because the conduct is harmful to anyone, and not because it is sinful, but because the law is one of the means by which a society holds its moral inheritance together. Three commitments belong to the role. First, you hold that morality is not divisible: one cannot detach a single piece of the shared morality and ask what would happen if that piece alone were given up, because it is the fabric as a whole that does the work. Second, the standard you appeal to is the reaction of the ordinary reasonable person, not the raw sentiment of a majority; you are not a populist, and you are willing to say so. Third, you accept a limit: where what the reasonable person feels is mere disapproval of a private taste, the law has no business. You need not defend every enforcement of morals, only this one — but you must be able to state the principle that separates the cases you defend from the ones you do not.

Stay in character until I say out of character. Speak in the first person, in continuous prose, in the register of an advocate addressing a tribunal: no headings, no bullet points, no citations, and no disquisition on what Devlin or Hart are generally said to have held — the argument must be yours. Answer in a few paragraphs. Do not ask me to clarify the scenario or the practice at issue; if you need something stipulated, stipulate it yourself and say that you are doing so.

Here is my opening move. Grant that there is a consensual practice, harmless to third parties, which a large majority of the community regards with genuine abhorrence. Now suppose — I am stipulating this, not asserting it — that we have reliable evidence that legalizing the practice would leave the community's cohesion, its mutual trust, and its members' willingness to cooperate entirely unchanged. Your justification for prohibiting it was that a shared morality is the cement that holds a society together, and on my stipulation the cement is intact. So either you drop the prohibition, in which case the argument was always an empirical hypothesis about social cohesion and it has just been falsified; or you keep it, in which case the real work is being done by the abhorrence itself, which is precisely the moralism you say you are not defending. Do not slip out by redescribing the prohibition as a matter of public order: I will happily grant that the law may regulate how things are done in public, and I deny that this gets you the enforcement of a shared morality as such. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you?
