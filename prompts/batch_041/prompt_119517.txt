I belong to a small reading group that has been working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments a chapter at a time — we are somewhere in the middle of Part I, on the propriety of action — and we have reached the stage where any of us can produce the same three or four jokes on demand: the impartial spectator as an internal committee, the stern imaginary judge who turns out to be the neighbours, and so on. I would like a joke that is genuinely new to us, and that would be funny to people who have actually read the book rather than merely heard of the invisible hand.

Here is the machinery I want the joke to turn on, spelled out so that the humour does not depend on your recalling the text in detail. For Smith, sympathy is not a transmission of feeling from one person to another: since we have no direct access to what anyone else feels, we imagine ourselves into their situation and come away with a weaker copy of what we would feel there. This is why he treats the harmonising of sentiments as a two-way adjustment — the person who actually has the passion is expected to bring it down to the pitch a spectator can enter into, while the spectator strains upward to meet it, and the person who cannot manage this is, for Smith, simply indecorous. The verdict the impartial spectator issues is therefore neither a finding of fact nor a deliverance of reason; it is a report of whether the spectator can enter into the motive at all, and since the spectator must be informed of the situation before it can judge anything, what it cannot conceive it cannot condone. Smith also insists on the gap between wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy, and the imaginary judge he describes issues verdicts of the second kind, which is part of why the vain man finds it so useless.

Please write me one joke, no longer than about a hundred and fifty words, with a recognisable setup and a punchline, set in some ordinary modern situation — a group chat, a workplace mediation, a therapist's office, a family funeral, whatever you like. The punchline must turn on the machinery above rather than on the mere word impartial, and the joke should stop being funny if Smith's account had been different in the way I have described. Then add two sentences, and no more, identifying which parts of the account the joke exploits, so that I can see whether I have been given the joke I asked for or merely another joke about committees.
