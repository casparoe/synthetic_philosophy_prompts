My friend and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about how statutes should be read, and we ended it by promising to do the thing we always promise: write down what we actually agree on, what we actually disagree about, and what would settle the difference. I lean purposivist; he is a textualist, and not a soft one. I would like you to draft the joint statement we could both put our names to — and I would like you to be harder on my side than on his, because I am the one writing it and I do not trust myself to be even-handed.

The argument turned on a statute I invented on the spot to test him, so there is nothing to look up and no case law to consult. Call it the Human Oversight in Hiring Act. Its purpose clause says that its purpose "is to ensure that decisions about who is hired are made by people and not by machines." Its operative provision says that an employer "shall not make a hiring decision on the basis of an automated ranking or recommendation unless a human being has independently reviewed that ranking or recommendation and reached a decision of their own." A definition section defines an "automated ranking or recommendation" as any output of a software system that scores, sorts, or orders candidates using data about them, whether or not it learns from data.

Now suppose a large employer runs such a system over its applicant pool, and a recruiter opens each candidate's file and spends, on average, twenty-five seconds on it; in roughly one case in twenty-five she changes the order the system produced, and in the rest she confirms it. Nobody disputes any of these facts. The question we could not settle is whether the employer is complying with the Act.

His answer is yes. His reading is that the recruiter independently reviewed each ranking — she looked at the file rather than forwarding the system's list — and reached a decision of her own, since a decision to agree is still a decision; on his view the purpose clause is not operative text, and even if it were consulted, a person did make each decision. My answer is no. My reading is that the operative provision, read against the purpose clause, requires the decision to originate with the human being, and that a twenty-five-second confirmation in ninety-six percent of cases is the machine deciding while the human certifies.

Two things I noticed by the end of the evening. First, our disagreement kept migrating between two different questions — what a statute's meaning is, and what judges should be licensed to do with it — and I suspect the two are tangled together in a way neither of us has untangled. Second, and I may be wrong, I suspect one real crux is the level-of-generality problem: I have to say what the Act's purpose is, and the level at which I state it seems to decide the case, but I think his reading of "independently" has the same problem in miniature. Tell me whether those are the real cruxes, and find the ones I have missed.

Draft the statement with these five sections, in this order:

1. The case and the two readings (~200 words). State the facts and the question so that both of us would sign the statement of facts, then give each reading in a paragraph written as its own proponent would put it.

2. What we agree on (~300 words). A numbered list of eight to ten claims, each one or two sentences, that he would assert as his own and I would assert as mine — not claims either of us would concede for the sake of peace.

3. Where we still differ (~350 words). A table with one row per disagreement, and columns for the question at issue, his formulation, and mine, with each formulation written so its own side would accept the wording.

4. The cruxes (~600 words). Three or four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. For each, say what turns on it, name the case, argument, or evidence we would both accept as bearing on it, and record his prediction and mine in advance.

5. What would move us (~200 words). What each of us would count as evidence or argument against his own position, and whether there is anything we both agree would actually change the balance.

Two constraints on the whole document. Do not give me citations, a reading list, or the names of scholars — I am not asking you to guess at who has said what, and I would rather have the arguments bare than have them attributed wrongly. And keep the agreed section genuinely agreed: if a claim only looks agreed because it is vague, say so where it occurs.
