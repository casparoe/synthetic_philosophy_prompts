I'm a novelist, and I've somehow agreed to run a one-off session at a summer fiction workshop next month: 18 adults, 75 minutes, one room, no homework, and a few of them will be quietly skeptical that any of this is real philosophy. What I want them to actually feel is consequentialist cluelessness -- not the easy version where you just don't have the facts yet and more digging would settle it, but the harder thing where even with the facts in hand the chain of effects runs off past the horizon, the total keeps flipping sign every time you extend it a step, and it starts to look like no amount of cleverness makes one option better than another. Part of what I want them to bump into is that on a total-view sort of consequentialism the far future swamps everything nearby, so the choice right in front of you gets judged almost entirely on effects nobody could ever know. Novelists are professional consequence-tracers, so I figure they'll walk straight into it.

Design me the activity. Rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't need money or prizes worth anything. I want them to hit the wall themselves before anyone says a word about the theory, so no framing talk up front and no naming the idea until the debrief. The debrief should point back at specific moments in what just happened and tie each one to a piece of the problem. Also tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity just can't deliver and I'll have to say out loud myself.

And if this is a muddle -- if a game can't propperly produce the harder version of the feeling and I'm fooling myself -- just tell me straight instead of humoring me. American English, please.
