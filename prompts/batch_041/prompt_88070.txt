I run grantmaking at a mid-sized foundation that funds public-interest work in fast-moving technical fields, and I have a recurring problem with our own process. A large share of the proposals that reach my desk are justified, in their central paragraph, by some version of the same argument: the field is a race, our side cannot afford to fall behind, and therefore this work must be funded now on the terms proposed. My program officers have taken to calling these "Moloch-shaped" proposals, and I have come to suspect that we are less choosing among them than being pulled along by them — that our own grantmaking is one of the things setting the pace. What I want from you is a decision procedure my officers can actually run, not an essay about collective action. It must be usable by generalists who have not read the underlying literature; it must fit in about forty minutes per proposal (we see sixty or so a year, mostly between $200k and $2M, and the board meets quarterly and signs off on anything above $500k); and it must survive the fact that we cannot demand confidential information about a grantee's competitors. The output should be a short written verdict that a program officer can defend in front of a board that includes two economists and one trustee who thinks the answer is always more coordination.

The procedure should take the form of questions asked in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. A few things I would like it to sort out, because my officers currently cannot: how to tell a genuine multipolar trap from a coordination problem that has a focal solution, from a bargaining problem among a small identifiable set of parties, from a waiting game in which everyone would rather someone else moved first, from ordinary competitive pressure that is nobody's trap at all. It should force the question of who "the others" are — a closed set of known rivals, an open population, or future entrants who are only attracted because there is now money in the race, since I suspect that funding a race tends to add runners to it. It should ask whether the dynamic has a natural finishing line (a standard gets adopted, a rule changes) or no terminus at all; I have noticed that some of these races end and others simply continue at increasing cost. It should ask whether any unilateral or small-coalition move exists — a transparency commitment, or the Schelling-style move of making one's own defection impossible — because if one does, the thing is not a trap in the strong sense. It should ask whether the prize is positional (a ranking, a first-mover advantage) or a genuine public good, and who outside the race is helped or hurt by our entry into it. And it should require a red-team step in which the officer writing the verdict states the strongest case that the dynamic is not a trap at all. Where a step is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, say so.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work. First, a proposal to fund a nonprofit that will build a "responsible deployment" toolkit for firms in a fast-moving commercial field, on the argument that the grantees' competitors will not wait for them. Second, a proposal to build a public-interest alternative to a piece of entrenched infrastructure that is entrenched mainly because everyone already uses it — I expect this one may not be a trap at all. Third, and I want this one played straight, a proposal for a rapid-response legal fund in an area where we have already made eight similar grants in four years; if the procedure says our own money is what keeps that fight going, say so plainly. For each case, tell me which question decided it, what verdict the procedure returns, and where it reaches the limit of what it can do — including whether it can ever detect a trap that the foundation is inside of and cannot see.
