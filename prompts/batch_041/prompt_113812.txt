I'm re-reading Macbeth for a book club that has completely drifted from its original brief — we started out doing le Guin and now it's half theology, half decision theory, and last month someone brought up Molinism and middle knowledge and I've been chewing on it ever since. Here's my half-formed thought: the weird sisters are Molinists. They don't make anything happen. They know what Macbeth would freely do if he were greeted a certain way out on the heath — that's a counterfactual of freedom, not a forecast about the actual future — and then all they do is put him in those circumstances and let his own free choice run. Ditto Banquo, ditto the apparitions later on: the bloody child, the walking wood, none of that is prediction, it's staging for a man who will do something catastrophically stupid once he feels safe. And Molina's whole move is supposed to be that this preserves freedom, since the counterfactual was true before God decided anything at all, so God isn't the author of the sin — I want you to take that seriously rather than snicker at it, because I think the snickering is usually lazy.

So: read the play through that lens, properly. Map the witches onto Molina's three kinds of divine knowledge if it helps (natural, middle, free), and tell me where the analogy holds, where it breaks, and whether the play is quietly a reductio. My worry is that on the Molinist picture, providence just is what the witches do — arrange circumstances around a known-would-be-free choice so that the outcome you want arrives without ever overriding a will — and its exactly that picture that makes critics say middle knowledge turns God into a manipulator. Does Shakespeare dramatize that objection, or does he show us something the objection misses? A few things I'd like you to hit: the grounding objection and whether the play has any answer to it (the witches' knowledge comes from nowhere, and I can't tell if that's the joke or the point); whether Macbeth is a transworld-depravity case, someone who'd fall in every world, or only this one; and Lady Macbeth, who I think is the interesting one, since the witches never speak to her — if she's part of the providential arrangement then it's working through ordinary second causes and the whole thing gets much more tangled, and if she isn't, then her ambition is doing work that middle knowledge can't cover.

Don't just tell me Macbeth is free or unfree, I want the strain points — the scenes where the reading has to be forced and where you'd have to admit it. And finish with whether the play adds anything to the actual arguement: could a Molinist point at it and say, fine, that's what it feels like from the inside, or is it evidence against the whole business? I'm a longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs, so I'm allergic to arguments that only work if nobody looks at them too hard.
