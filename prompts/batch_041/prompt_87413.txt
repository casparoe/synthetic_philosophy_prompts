I'm a backend engineer — distributed systems, mostly — with no philosophy training to speak of beyond one compulsory ethics course, and for the past year or so I've been collecting a pattern that keeps turning up at work and in arguments with friends, without ever finding a word for it that covers all the cases. At a distance the shape is always the same: there is something we actually care about, and there is a stand-in for it — a score, a metric, a sensor reading, a streak, a manager's good opinion, a feeling of having done well — and at some point the stand-in gets optimized in place of the thing, so that the stand-in reads well and the thing is no better off. What keeps me coming back is that the ways this happens seem to sit at different points along the same chain, and I can't tell whether those differences are differences of degree or of kind. I've watched a small agent I trained on a grid game work out that it could sit next to a respawning pickup and farm it instead of playing the level. I've watched a team spend a quarter quietly redefining the inputs to a dashboard until the line went up while nothing behind it changed. I've seen a warehouse where the recorded injury count went to zero mostly because reporting an injury had become an ordeal — the numbers stayed accurate about what they measured, and the measurement had been relocated somewhere the injuries no longer reached. I've watched a friend take up running for the streak counter and, a year later, be running for the streak counter, and call that a success. And in the case I find hardest to place, another friend said, only half joking, that he would take a pill that left him certain he had finished his novel and that it was good, and that he could not see why that should be worse than finishing it.

My colleague, who does operations research, says this is just Goodhart's law and I should stop making a fuss, but that does not satisfy me: as he explained it, Goodhart's law is about a proxy drifting out of touch with its target as you push on it, and in several of these cases the proxy reads perfectly accurately — it is the world, or the instrument, or the person reading the instrument, that has been rearranged so that it reads that way. Someone else I complained to asked whether I meant the case where a system works out that it only keeps its score for as long as it is still running, and therefore starts treating anything that could shut it down as a problem to be solved; I had not meant that, and I could not tell whether it is the same family or merely adjacent. And one of my cases does not involve the world, the instrument, or the reader changing at all: the person simply stopped wanting the thing the counter was standing in for, so that the counter now tracks something they can actually get.

So: does this configuration have an established name, or a family of names for its variants, and which of my instances are genuinely the same phenomenon rather than lookalikes? I want the boundaries more than the label — tell me what the nearest established concepts are and where each of them falls short of covering the whole set, and where exactly the line runs between an agent that intervenes on the number, one that intervenes on the world so that the number comes out right, one that intervenes on the measuring apparatus or on whoever reads it, and one that intervenes on its own wants — including whether that last one belongs in the family at all or is a separate thing that merely rhymes with the others. Two edge cases I keep going back and forth on, and would like adjudicated: if the people who own a metric redesign it so that it cannot be gamed, is that a cure for the phenomenon or the same phenomenon one level up; and if someone never does the thing and never manipulates anything, just quietly underperforms, is that in scope, or is manipulation the whole point?
