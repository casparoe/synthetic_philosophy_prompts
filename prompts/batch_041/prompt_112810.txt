I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on formal epistemology, and I'm in the middle of a seminar paper on diachronic norms of credence. I've been trying to get clear on the Reflection principle — the constraint that my present credence in some proposition A, conditional on my future credence in A being x, should itself be x — and I've hit a snag that I suspect is really a snag about how I'm reading the principle rather than about the principle itself, though I can't see my way out of it on my own. My working picture has been that Reflection is a *deference* principle: it instructs me to hand epistemic authority to my future self, on the grounds that my future self will be better placed to judge — better informed, or at least better positioned. Read that way it belongs in the same family as the Principal Principle (deference to objective chance) and as the ordinary norm of deferring to someone who knows more than you do. The more I press on the analogy, though, the more it seems to come apart, and I'd like help diagnosing where my reading goes wrong.

Here is the case that bothers me. Suppose I know I'll spend tomorrow in a state that degrades my judgment: badly sleep-deprived, and I know from experience that in that state my credence in a certain proposition — say, that a proof strategy I've been developing actually works — falls well below where it stands now, not because I'll have discovered an error but because I simply won't be able to hold the whole structure in mind. I can predict this about myself with reasonable confidence. If Reflection is a deference principle, it looks like it's telling me to treat my exhausted self as an authority on my own proof, which strikes me as plainly wrong; surely the right response is to keep my present credence and discount the future one. But if I'm permitted to discount it, then Reflection isn't a requirement at all — it's a heuristic that happens to hold when the future self is more reliable. So either Reflection is false or it isn't the deference principle I took it to be. What I want is a clear statement of what the principle actually asserts: what it does and does not claim about my future self, and what the conditional structure is doing, since I suspect that is where I've gone astray. Then, with the better-informed-future-self picture set aside, I'd like an account of what remains of the underlying debate — in particular, whether the objection from anticipated irrationality (or anticipated decrepitude, as in my case) is best understood as showing that Reflection needs a defeater clause, or as showing that the whole principle is a non-starter.

Please be self-critical about this. If my diagnosis of my own confusion is itself confused, tell me so rather than working around it, and say which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about how the principle should be read — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
