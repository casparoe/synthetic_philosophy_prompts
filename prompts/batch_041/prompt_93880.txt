I chair the inquiry panel that our community convenes whenever a recovery is put forward as a miraculous sign. Under our charter we may recognize such a recovery only if, after investigation, we judge that it exceeds the reach of natural causes and is attributable to the intercession of the figure whose cause it is meant to serve. The case before us: a woman with a biopsy-confirmed malignancy, re-imaged a year later, shows no detectable disease; the three physicians who reviewed the file, none of them members of our community, agree that they cannot account for the recovery on the basis of the treatment she received, though two of them note that spontaneous regression of this tumor, while rare, is documented. One panel member has circulated a memo invoking the argument usually associated with Hume — that testimony can never render a miracle credible, because where an event would amount to a violation of a law of nature the falsehood of the testimony is always more probable than the event itself — and urges us to decline. Another member replies that this reasoning begs the question and that our task is merely to compare two hypotheses on the evidence.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition — recognize, decline, or defer pending specified further steps — with a time frame; state plainly the standard we are in fact applying; engage the dissenting member's argument at its strongest rather than dismissing it as prejudice; and separate what in our decision is a question of evidence from what is a question about the meaning that an act of recognition carries for the community. I would like the opinion to be candid about where the classical argument is strongest and where it is vulnerable — in particular, whether it is really one argument or several, and what work the phrase about natural causes is doing given how much of nature we do not yet understand — and to engage, rather than wave away, the standard grounds for distrusting reports of the marvelous: the credulity and interest of witnesses, the tendency of such reports to flourish where scrutiny is weak, the mutual cancellation of rival claims, and the difficulty of ruling out causes we have not yet discovered. Please also say what, if anything, would change the recommendation, and address the asymmetry between the cost of a mistaken recognition and the cost of a mistaken refusal.
