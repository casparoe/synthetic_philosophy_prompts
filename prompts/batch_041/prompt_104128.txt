I teach the mind-and-machine segment of an introductory philosophy of mind course, and I have decided to close it with Blade Runner. I want a reading of the film through Dennett's three stances and his account of real patterns: the replicants are engineered artifacts (design stance), biological systems (physical stance), and beings we cannot help but treat as believers and desirers (intentional stance), and the film's drama turns on whether that last stance latches onto something real or is merely a convenient projection. Please take the philosophy seriously in both directions. Where does the film dramatize the idea that a pattern counts as real just when the stance that posits it compresses and predicts better than its rivals, and where does it instead lean on the intuition that there must be an inner fact of the matter, a ghost in the machine, that the stance either captures or misses? Say what the Voight-Kampff test is actually testing for in these terms, whether the long-running suggestion (stronger in some cuts) that Deckard may himself be a replicant strengthens or weakens the Dennettian reading, and which scenes I should anchor the discussion on. If the film resists the reading at some point, if it wants a fact of the matter that Dennett denies, say so rather than forcing the fit, and tell me what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone does not.
