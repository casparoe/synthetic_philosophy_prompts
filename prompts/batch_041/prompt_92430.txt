So I've been reading way too much higher-order evidence stuff lately — epistemic akrasia, the level-splitting move, the whole "I believe P and I also believe I shouldn't believe P" shape — and I want a short story that makes it land for someone who's never read a word of the literature. Here's the premise, and I want you to actually use it instead of swapping in your own: a woman has a personal ledger, a well-calibrated statistical model of her own track record that she helped build and trusts the math on. Not an oracle, not somebody's opinion — it just tells her, for the kind of belief she's about to settle into, what fraction of her own past beliefs of that shape survived contact with the world. Her own record, not some population base rate. Tonight the first-order evidence is strong and it points one way: her brother-in-law did the thing. The ledger says that beliefs she forms this way, about people she loves, have held up about one time in five. She's still pretty sure she's right. Write it so both pieces of evidence are real and neither gets debunked by the end — no hacked machine, no reveal that he's innocent, no late phone call that settles it. She can notice the contradiction she's in, but don't let her solve it. She does have to do something before the story ends, make a call or say a thing, and the action should sit equally well with either resolution so I can't tell where you come out. Third person close on her, one night, 1200-1800 words. Don't have anybody in the story explain the concept out loud, no character saying "thats epistemic akrasia" — I want the reader to feel the shape of it, not get lectured. Start with the story itself, no preamble or commentary before it.
