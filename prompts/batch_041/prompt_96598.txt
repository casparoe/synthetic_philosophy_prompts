I'm a novelist, and I chair the grant committee of a small foundation set up by a writer who died in the nineties — the will says only that the money should go to work he would have approved of, which worked fine for twenty years because we all knew what he liked. Thursday we vote on a proposal to fund a serialized thriller, written with heavy help from an LLM, by a young writer who is openly building a brand. He would of loathed it: he was a stylist, he thought plot was for children and self-promotion was for hacks. But two trustees keep saying that's the wrong question, that we should fund what he'd have wanted if he'd known then what we know now and had actually thought it through, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's deep or whether it's just us putting our taste in a dead man's mouth. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: a concrete recommendation with a deadline, criteria we can apply to this one and to the next one, and give the other side its best hearing — especially on how anyone is supposed to know what an idealized version of him wants, and whether that idealized man is still him or just a character we wrote. Don't bother looking up his books, we've all got them memorized.
