I'm putting together a handout for a reading group that just finished Turing's 1950 paper in Mind, the one where he replaces the question of whether machines can think with the imitation game. We spent most of the session on the objection from extrasensory perception, and I'd like to close with a poem that does more than gesture at it. Please write a villanelle — nineteen lines, the usual two rhymes and two refrains — in the voice of Turing at his desk working the problem through. The two refrains should carry the two halves of the argument that will not stop coming back. The first should state the objection at full strength: if telepathy is real, then the human respondent has a channel to the interrogator that the digital computer lacks, and the game is rigged before it begins. The second should carry the concession that keeps the objection from being fatal: whether any such channel exists is an empirical question about minds rather than a conceptual truth about machines, and the experiment can be made fair by shielding, with the interrogator sealed in a room no thought can enter and everything passing through the teleprinter.

Let the turn arrive in the middle tercets, at the moment he notices the shield cuts both ways. If mind can reach mind without a wire, then nothing in the very idea of a digital computer rules out a machine built to receive, or one fed answers through a human medium, or one given a random element so that its replies become unpredictable in the way the objection insists only a person can be. The poem shouldn't resolve this into a verdict; Turing didn't, and I'd rather the ending leave a reader holding both the fairness of the room and the strangeness of needing one. Formally, the refrains should do the philosophical work: they return, as refrains do, because the objection and the concession genuinely keep alternating, and the closing couplet should set them side by side in a way that shows why neither one settles anything.

Accuracy matters more to me here than beauty. Someone who knows that section should recognize the specific moves — the objection itself, the telepathy-proof room, the willingness to treat the matter as empirical, the thought that a machine might turn out to be telepathic too — and someone who doesn't should come away with the argument rather than a mood. Please don't put anything in quotation marks or attribute any wording to Turing; I would much rather have the reasoning in your own words than a misremembered line. No bibliography or reading list either, since we have the paper in front of us.
