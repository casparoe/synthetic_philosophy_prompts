I'm co-teaching a first-year interdisciplinary seminar on the rise and fall of civilizations, and next week I have the one session that deals explicitly with the concept of collapse itself, before we turn to any particular case — the Bronze Age, the Maya, and the rest come later. My students are bright but they arrive with a very confident, very undifferentiated picture: "collapse" means a society that was there and then wasn't, brought down by some blend of hubris, resource exhaustion, and catastrophe, and the moral is always that we could be next. I want the session to leave them with a sharper instrument rather than a list of fallen empires. The idea I most want to get across is that what we call collapse is really several distinct processes that can come apart — a demographic collapse (people die or leave), an institutional collapse (the political order stops being able to do what it used to do), and a collapse in social complexity (the society sheds the layers of organization it had accumulated) — and that the genuinely interesting historical questions live in the cases where these come apart: where the state falls but the population and its way of life carry on, or where complexity unwinds without anyone dying. I would also like to fold in an idea I have been borrowing from ecology, the adaptive cycle — growth, conservation, release, reorganization — on which "release" looks like collapse from the inside but is only a phase of a longer loop, and to use it to press the question of whether collapse is something that happens to a society or a name we give to a transition we are standing too close to see whole.

Please don't lecture me on the concept; plan how I should teach it in eighty minutes. What has to be in place before the three-sense distinction will register as a clarification rather than a trick — what must come first, and what depends on what? Which single case should carry the whole session, given that Easter Island is the one everyone already half-knows and that I would rather not lean on it; tell me what that case has to contain and what it must not, and why it earns its place over the obvious alternatives. Then map the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to head each off — I am fairly sure that "collapse = disappearance," that "collapse is always sudden," and that "the moral of the story is always ecological overshoot" will surface, and I want to know which of these the three-sense distinction repairs by itself and which I have to kill earlier or they will corrupt everything that follows. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so openly, and give me one question to ask in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they have actually grasped the distinction or are merely nodding along.
