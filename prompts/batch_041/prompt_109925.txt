I'm setting the final take-home for my upper-level seminar on self-reference in law. The last two weeks we've been building Nomic-style self-amending rulesets and arguing about whether a rule can legitimately govern the amendment of the very rules that fix its own adoption threshold. Last year's question was a bust: everyone recited the standard can-a-rule-change-itself back-and-forth and then sat on the fence. I want something that makes them pick a horn and live with it, and that they can't answer by summarising.

Here's the ruleset they'll be handed (I made it up, so dont worry about matching it to anything real):

- R1: R1 and R2 may be amended only by a unanimous vote of all eligible players.
- R2: Any rule other than R1 and R2 may be amended by a simple majority.
- R3: A proposal whose adoption would change the threshold required for its own adoption is out of order.

And the facts: the players are split 5-4 on a proposal P that would replace R1 with "R1 may be amended by a simple majority." A separate proposal Q would simply repeal R3. Q is not entrenched, so under R2 it needs only a simple majority, and it has one.

Write me: (1) the exam question, answerable in about 60 minutes with no notes and no internet, that makes them say which rule decides P's fate, under which reading of the ruleset, and defend that reading against the other; (2) a model answer of roughly 600-700 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20, with five or six must-hit points, the marks for each, and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates that point.

Two things. The question has to turn on something they must decide, not something they can look up, so build no marks around names, movements or citations -- I don't care whether they've heard of any particular legal theorist and they can't cite anything anyway. And the move I most want rewarded is noticing whether passing Q first actually changes anything for P, and why the answer to that comes out differently under the two readings -- including what each reading implies about how R1 and R2 could ever have gotten adopted in the first place.
