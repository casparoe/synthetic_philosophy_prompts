In three weeks I present to a behavioral economics seminar a thesis I expect to be received badly, and what I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not reassurance. I come to this from the rationality-blog side rather than from the field, so I know what I believe and not yet what this room will say back to me; my instinct, after years of that reading, is to care about what a bounded agent should do to win rather than about what an idealized agent's preferences imply, and I would rather have the objection that stings than the one that flatters.

The thesis: honoring sunk costs is not always a mistake, and the textbook treatment of it as a fallacy rests on a framing of the decision problem that does more work than anyone acknowledges. Three legs. First, for a class of goods — vocations, friendships, research programs, institutions — the value of continuing is partly constituted by the history of investment: the good is not a fixed prize at the end of the project but a historical object whose identity includes what has already been spent, so the marginal principle does not apply to it, and applying it is not neutrality but a rival substantive view. Second, expenditure is often the cheapest evidence a resource-poor agent has about a project's type and about her own competence, so escalating with investment can be a rational second-best under scarcity of information rather than a bias. Third, honoring sunk costs is a commitment device: expenditure is the bond, and the bond is credible only because quitting is costly, so an agent who expects her future self to want to quit may be right to cultivate the disposition. My claim is that the fallacy label presupposes that an agent can costlessly re-plan from a fixed, history-independent preference ordering, and that this presupposition, not the psychology, is what the classic vignettes actually test. I concede those vignettes — money already sunk into a failing project, tickets for a rained-out event — and I need to know what that concession costs me.

The room: mostly experimental and behavioral economists who teach this as settled and care about identification and predictive models; one game theorist working on precommitment and reputation, who will think the third leg is old news; one decision theorist who will want the formal object pinned down; one philosopher of economics who will ask where the normativity comes from; and a historian of economic thought who probably knows the older marginalist debate better than I do.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each in the vocabulary the person who raises it would use, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objection I should concede ground on in my opening remarks and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Begin with one sentence naming the objection most likely to end the discussion if I fumble it, then go straight into the sheet: no preamble, no restating of the question.
