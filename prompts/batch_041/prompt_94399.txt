Question: Explain Aristotle's account of money and exchange. Why does exchange need money at all, and what makes usury a particularly bad form of wealth-getting? Be sure to distinguish the kind of acquisition that belongs to household management from the kind he calls chrematistics.

Student's answer:

Aristotle starts from the fact that exchange is necessary for any community, since households and cities need goods they don't produce themselves. But exchange presupposes that the things exchanged can be compared, and a house and a shoe are so different that they can't be compared directly. Money solves this: it is a single standard that measures all goods and makes them commensurable, and it also acts as a guarantee, so that if I don't need anything right now I can still accept payment and get what I need later. Money is not natural in the way food or shoes are; it exists by convention, which is why its value can shift or be abolished by agreement.

Because money is the measure of value, Aristotle thinks exchange value is ultimately grounded in the labour that went into producing the goods, with money serving as a conventional proxy for that labour. That's why exchange has to be proportionate: a builder and a shoemaker don't exchange equal amounts of goods but goods in the ratio of their labour, so neither ends up with a surplus at the other's expense.

He then distinguishes household management from chrematistics. Household management uses money only as a tool for getting the goods a household actually needs, and in that role money and exchange are natural and necessary. Chrematistics, by contrast, aims at money itself, without limit, and that's what he criticises. Usury is the worst case, because the lender's gain comes from money itself rather than from any productive use of it: money is meant for exchange, not for generating more money, so making it breed is contrary to its function. Hence his claim that usury is the most unnatural way of making a living.

So for Aristotle money is fine as a medium of exchange but corrupting when it becomes an end in itself.
