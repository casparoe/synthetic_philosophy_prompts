I have no academic training in history or philosophy, and what follows is a question I have been turning over on my own for some time, so I would rather have the framing corrected than politely accepted. It concerns the night of 9 November 1989 in Berlin. The East German government's press spokesman, Günter Schabowski, gave a televised briefing at which he announced that the new travel regulations took effect at once; they were in fact meant to begin the following day and to require applications, and he had not been fully briefed. Crowds went to the crossings, above all at Bornholmer Strasse, where the guards telephoned their superiors, found that nobody in the government was willing to take responsibility for using force, and in the end raised the barrier on their own authority, so that some twenty thousand people had walked through to West Berlin well before midnight. What interests me is not the sequence of events but how we are meant to explain it, because the standard answer is that the fall of the Wall was overdetermined. The Soviet Union had signalled that it would not intervene, the economy was hollow, tens of thousands had already left through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, the Monday demonstrations in Leipzig had grown, and Gorbachev's visit for the republic's fortieth anniversary in October had made plain that no help was coming, so that even if the briefing had gone smoothly, or the guards had held the line that night, the Wall would have come down soon enough anyway.

I want that claim treated as a piece of causal reasoning and then tested against the episode, using the notion of overdetermination as it is used in discussions of historical explanation: an outcome produced by more than one cause, where each of those causes would by itself have been enough to bring it about. Please do five things. First, sort the factors I have listed into long-run causes, conditions and triggers, say what it would even mean for one of them to be sufficient on its own, and tell me whether Schabowski's briefing and the guards' decision count as causes at all or only as occasions. Second, say what the overdetermination story explains well, and which question it is the right answer to. Third, say what it misses, especially the question of why the Wall opened on that night and in that manner, and whether that is a separate question rather than a gap in the account. Fourth, put the notion under pressure: tell me whether any single factor really was sufficient, and whether the episode is better described as conjunctive, with every factor necessary and all of them jointly enough, which would be the opposite of overdetermination, and what that implies about whether overdetermination ever genuinely occurs in history. Fifth, say whether calling an event overdetermined does explanatory work or excusing work here, with respect to Schabowski, the border guards and the Politburo, and what evidence could distinguish an outcome that was inevitable from one that was merely redundant.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
