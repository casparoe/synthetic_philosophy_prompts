I am assembling the agenda for a small, three-year working group — a handful of philosophers plus two game theorists — on what one might call the theory of moral compromise: the idea, modeled loosely on exchange, that two parties with divergent value systems can sometimes reach arrangements that each regards as better than the status quo, so that there are gains from trade in morality itself. No one in the group does empirical work, and I would rather not have the agenda collapse into a literature survey. I need you to tell me which open questions most deserve the group's effort and to defend the ordering.

Take the following as the candidate questions, though you should feel free to add, drop, re-cut, or recombine them:

1. Does the exchange analogy survive incommensurability? If two parties' values cannot be placed on a common scale, can a contract curve — the set of arrangements neither party would veto — even be defined, or is the appearance of mutual improvement an artifact of the representation we choose?
2. Is there a principled line between compromising on a value and compromising on a policy that merely expresses it — and if so, which trades fall on the first side of it?
3. Can a sincere commitment be traded without hypocrisy? Each party continues to regard the other's position as wrong; what, if anything, makes it coherent to exchange forbearance on one issue for forbearance on another?
4. Do limits of consent bind? If a party freely agrees to weaken a commitment, does the agreement make the trade permissible, or are some commitments of a kind that cannot licitly be alienated at all?
5. How are absent parties — future people, children, non-human animals — represented in a bargain struck only among present, consenting parties?
6. What constrains the distribution of the gains? Is a morally acceptable compromise one that satisfies some fairness condition on how the surplus is split, and does the analogy to a difference principle or to a bargaining solution do real work here?
7. Does the framework presuppose that each party can rank whole bundles, and what becomes of it when a single agent's own values conflict — compromise within one person rather than between persons?

Two of us have floated reframings I would like you to weigh. One wants to recast the whole enterprise as an Edgeworth box for values and to ask whether the contract curve remains well-defined once preferences are incomplete or the value space is non-Archimedean. The other insists that most real political compromise is not issue-trading at all but splitting the difference on a single issue, which need not be a Pareto improvement and may leave both parties worse off than some untried alternative — so that a theory built on the exchange model may be solving a problem that rarely arises.

Please give me a prioritized agenda rather than a survey. For each item you place near the top, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people with philosophical and game-theoretic tools but no new data, how neglected it is, and what result or argument over the next few years would move it up or down the list. Where two candidate questions are really one question in disguise, say so; where the group should deliberately not spend time, say that too. End with a single machine-readable line giving your ranking, in the form Priority order: Q3 > Q1 > ... , using the numbers above and adding Q8, Q9, ... for any questions you introduce.
