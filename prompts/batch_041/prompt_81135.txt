A year into a reading group on machine minds and we're stuck in the same loop, and I want a diagnosis instead of another round. Here's the shape of it. Everyone at the table agrees on the physics and the behavior: we can write down the exact dynamics, we agree on every input-output fact, we agree the brain does whatever the brain does. What we can't agree on is which description of the system is the one that carries the moral weight. One camp says it's the computation -- the functional organization, the pattern of transitions, whatever realizes it. Get the causal profile right and the substrate is irrelevant; neurons, silicon, a big table, doesn't matter, it's the same computation and so the same moral status. The other camp says it's the causal machinery itself -- a computation only counts as really implemented when the physical parts are doing the work in the right way, and alot of what the first camp calls implementing is just us drawing a mapping from physical states onto formal ones and calling it done.

Then someone brings up the old point that any physical system can be seen as implementing any finite-state machine if you're allowed to pick the mapping freely, so 'implements' comes cheap, and someone else replies that you block that by requiring the mapping to respect the causal structure of the system, and we circle.

Here's the wrinkle I threw in last time that I want you to take seriously, because I think it sharpens things. Suppose we add a third criterion, robustness: a system only really implements a computation, in the sense that could matter morally, if it would still implement it under small perturbations -- nudge the physics a little and the same abstract transition structure survives. On that criterion a lookup table that just stores the answer for every input is out, a system that computes it the way a brain does is in. Now the fight splits into two possible fights: (a) a verbal fight about whether robustness is part of what 'implements' means, in which case camp two is just defining 'implements' more narrowly than camp one and there's no real disagreement, or (b) a substantive fight about whether robustness is a seperate, independent criterion that tracks something morally relevant -- i.e., whether moral status attaches to the computation or to the robust causal structure that happens to support it, which would be a real metaphysical disagreement dressed up as a definitional one.

My guess is it's mostly (b), but I keep talking myself out of it. So diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this actually -- verbal, a clash of hidden metaphysics (is a computation attributed to a system or discovered in it?), two different questions wearing one name (a descriptive question about what a system computes vs a normative question about what deserves moral concern), or a hidden empirical question that we're all pretending is conceptual? Say what each side would have to give up for it to be a genuine disagreement, and what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a changed question -- or argue that none should be expected and why. Please don't survey the positions, I know them; I want the diagnosis and your reasons for it.
