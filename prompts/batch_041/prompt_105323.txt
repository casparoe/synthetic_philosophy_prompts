I run trust-and-safety policy for a platform with a large American user base and a substantial European one, and I need the two legal traditions we sit between turned into something my moderators can actually execute. They make on the order of forty thousand keep-or-remove calls a day from user reports, most of them inside ninety seconds, and the escalation queue that reaches me is where the genuinely hard ones land. Outside counsel has given us the legal floor in each jurisdiction and nothing above it, and my moderators keep asking the same question in different words: is this the kind of thing we have to leave up, or only the kind of thing we are permitted to take down? I want a decision procedure that answers that in the order they encounter an item, and that keeps two layers visibly separate — a jurisdiction-specific legal floor, and a discretionary policy layer above it that we own rather than the law.

Please write that procedure. It should give the questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what counts as no answer at all, state where the procedure stops with leave it up, and mark the points where it must hand the item to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Two asymmetries need to survive the simplification. First, the American floor is very high: general vituperation against a group is ordinarily protected, and even advocacy of violence is reachable only through a narrow incitement test requiring imminence and likelihood, with a separate and equally narrow category for words aimed at a particular person that are likely to provoke an immediate fight — whereas the European floor is lower and proportionality-shaped, allowing restrictions on hate speech that a US court would strike down, with states given real latitude in how they draw the line. Second, the risks of error are not symmetric: leaving up unlawful content in one jurisdiction is a legal exposure for us, while taking down protected speech in the other is a policy and legitimacy failure rather than a legal one, and the procedure should say which way it is designed to err and why. It also has to tell a moderator what to do with context they cannot verify — reclaimed use by members of the targeted group, journalism and quotation, counterspeech, satire, art — without pretending that every moderator can adjudicate all of that in ninety seconds. Say plainly where the procedure will misfire: mixed audiences, items whose jurisdiction cannot be determined, and the fact that the weight given to a group's vulnerability is a judgment call dressed up as a threshold.

Then run it on three items and show your work. First, a video of a comedian repeating a racial slur many times while discussing the word itself. Second, a public post that names a minority group and says, in effect, that someone ought to do something about them, with no plan, place, or time. Third, a post aimed at a named individual, using a slur, telling them to get out of the neighbourhood, apparently from someone who lives nearby. At least one of the three should come out as leave it up in a way I will find distasteful, and at least one should come out differently in the two jurisdictions; if none does, say so and explain why. Finish with a short section on what the procedure cannot do and which decisions no procedure can absorb.
