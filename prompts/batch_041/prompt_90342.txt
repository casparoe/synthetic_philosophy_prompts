I keep returning to Dewey's experimentalism — the idea that inquiry is the business of converting a confused, unsettled situation into a settled one, and that this experimental attitude ought to be extended from physics to morals and politics, with democracy understood as a mode of associated living rather than a mere voting mechanism. A friend of mine from the rationalist forums has picked this up and pushed it further than I think it will bear. His line is that democracy simply is science applied to politics: a policy is a hypothesis, an election or a pilot scheme is the experiment, public criticism is peer review, the next cycle is replication, and the whole arrangement is self-correcting. From this he draws a practical conclusion — that we should treat governance as continuous experimentation (reversible pilots, published results, public updating, a standing willingness to abandon what fails), and that we should therefore be much less exercised about the ways real democracies fall short of ideal deliberation, because the machinery corrects itself over time.

I want the analogy audited rather than applauded. Give me the correspondence table first: what in political life plays the role of the hypothesis, the experimental intervention, the control condition, the replication, the peer review, the laboratory, the anomaly, and the research community. Then tell me where the mapping breaks — and I care about the disanalogies that bear on his conclusion, not merely any difference you can find. Among the candidates I want weighed: that there is no control condition because the whole polity is treated at once; that nothing replicates because the system mutates underneath the experiment; that the experimenter is also the subject and bears the cost; that the outcome measure is itself one of the things in dispute; that reversibility is often fictitious because the people who lost the experiment are dead, ruined, or exiled; that there is no external standpoint from which the result can be read off; and that a public, on Dewey's own account, is constituted by those caught up in the indirect consequences of others' actions, which makes the boundary of the experimental population a political artefact rather than a given. Tell me which of these do real work against his conclusion and which are decorative.

Then say what the analogy quietly imports from the source side: a neutral measure of success, cheap failure, a consensus that tracks the truth rather than power, a community already sharing a problem and a vocabulary, and the pragmatist substitution of warranted assertibility for truth — does that last move help the analogy or quietly dissolve what made science the model in the first place? Say whether Dewey's own version of the comparison avoids these imports or commits them too, and whether it matters that his experimentalism was as much a claim about the structure of inquiry as a recommendation about institutions.

Finally, the verdict I actually want: does his conclusion — treat policy as experiment, and be relaxed about democratic deficits because it self-corrects — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along? If a different comparison would do the work he wants, offer it and say what it would change. And tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if it came out one way rather than the other, would flip your assessment of how far the analogy holds, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in British English.
