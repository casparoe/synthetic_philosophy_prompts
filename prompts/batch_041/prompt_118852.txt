I'm a science-and-ideas reporter working on a feature about whether the old socialist calculation debate has been quietly settled by computing power, and I'd like your help framing the central question I plan to put to the economists I'm interviewing. My working assumption is roughly this: the Austrians eventually abandoned the claim that a socialist economy was logically or mathematically impossible and retreated to an epistemic complaint — that the information a planner would need is dispersed, partly tacit, and constantly changing — which is essentially a bandwidth problem of the sort that cheap computation and modern machine learning are built to solve. On that reading, Oskar Lange's design was correct from the start: a Central Planning Board announces prices for producer goods, watches for shortages and surpluses, and ratchets prices up or down until the system clears, which is just trial-and-error equilibrium-finding, and the only reason it looked impractical in the 1930s was the absence of hardware. So my question is this: now that we can aggregate and process far more dispersed data than Lange or Hayek could have imagined, what remains of the Austrian objection, and why shouldn't we report that the debate has been decided in Lange's favor? Since I'll be writing for a general readership, I'd especially like you to explain what is wrong with the way I've set the question up — including whether Mises's original objection and Hayek's later argument are really the same objection at all, whether either of them ever conceded feasibility in principle, and what market prices are supposed to be doing that a sufficiently powerful central processor could not do by other means. I'd also like to know how you would handle the awkward fact that Lange's planners are not really setting prices for capital goods in the way a market does, since I keep hearing that this is where the whole dispute actually lives and not in the computing.
