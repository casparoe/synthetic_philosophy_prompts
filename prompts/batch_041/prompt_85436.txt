Longtime lurker here — LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias days, a couple of rat-adjacent Discords I'm definately not going to name. I have a pattern I keep noticing and I want to know if it's a thing, or if I'm just pattern-matching on vibes.

Here's the shape of it. Two sides in some ongoing dispute both see a fight coming. Before it arrives, each side does something that makes its future response automatic: a law, a public promise, a clause in a contract, a piece of automation, a rule with no discretion left in it. Neither side wants the fight. Both would prefer a deal. But each thinks: if I don't wire my response in now, they'll wire theirs in, and then when the moment comes I'm the one who has to fold. So both wire it in. Now the two wired-in responses are incompatible, neither can be quietly withdrawn, and the deal both sides preferred is gone — not because anyone's preferences changed, but because each side's commitment is only worth anything if it isn't taken back. The commitment itself is usually free to make. A press release, a policy memo, a line in the terms of service. The cost only appears when it's tested, at which point you either honor it or eat the credibility loss on every future commitment you ever make.

Three places I've watched it happen:

A pair of countries each pass a statute saying sanctions trigger automatically if the other does X. Both pass, because passing is free and not passing looks weak. Now the leaders who want a deal have to repeal a law, which reads as surrender, so the deal dies.

A company sets up a filter that auto-rejects any request matching a pattern. The counterparty can't negotiate with a filter, so it escalates, and now the company would have to publicly override its own system to de-escalate — a much bigger move than never having built the filter.

A union and a firm each announce a red line well before bargaining starts, mostly to look resolute to their own members. Both mean it less than they sounded. Neither can move first.

The part that really bugs me: the waste seems to scale with how *cheap* it is to commit. When locking in costs a lot — actually burning the bridge — people only do it when they mean it, and the mechanism self-limits. When locking in is free, everybody does it, and you land in the worst outcome having done none of the deliberation. That's backwards from the usual "credible commitments have to be costly" story. And the race element is strange too: the outcome seems to hinge on who the other side believes will still be committed when the moment comes, which means the winner is often just whoever has the simpler, more legible decision procedure. Rewarding legibility over resolve seems like a bad property for a mechanism to have.

I've already tried the obvious candidates and none of them fit. War of attrition, no — nobody's paying a running cost to stay in. Arms race, no — it isn't about capability. Brinkmanship is close, but that's about making things slip out of your control on purpose, and this is about making them automatic. Cheap talk is wrong in the other direction: these are cheap to say and expensive to unsay. Commitment device is one-sided, self-control, not a two-player race.

A friend called it a commitment race. When I went looking, that phrase seems to be doing duty for at least three different things: two labs racing to ship first, two bargainers each trying to be the one whose threat is treated as fixed, and this. Maybe they're all one thing and I'm being dense. Maybe they're three things wearing one coat.

So: is this named? If it is, which name, and which of the neighbors is it *not*? If it isn't, coin something and give me a definition plus the boundary cases — what looks like it and isn't, what is it and doesn't look like it. And tell me whether I've described one phenomenon or a bundle; if it's a bundle, where are the joints.

You're a game theorist with good taste in conceptual hygiene, and you've read enough of the rationalist canon to know the difference between a real distinction and a blog handle. Think it through before committing: spend a few lines on the candidate concepts and where each one breaks, then pick. I want a committed answer. If you think it's already named, say so and defend that against the alternatives. If you think it's new, name it, define it, and tell me what would make you drop the claim. Don't hand me a menu of five possible names and "it depends what you mean." And no reading list, please — I'm offline and can't check citations, so argue from the structure of the thing instead.
