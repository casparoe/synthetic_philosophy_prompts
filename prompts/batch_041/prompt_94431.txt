I have been thinking about M. C. Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands — the sheet of paper from which two hands rise, each in the act of drawing the other into existence — and about the general puzzle of self-reference it illustrates. In the course of these discussions a piece of jargon keeps surfacing, what is sometimes called the inviolate level: the level in a tangled hierarchy that is not itself caught in the loop, the level that quietly supplies the rules the loop runs on — whatever it is, in the case of the drawing, that guarantees there is a sheet of paper and a pen at all, rather than two hands conjuring each other out of nothing. One person in our group insists that some such inviolate level is unavoidable: that any system which refers to itself — a self-amending legal order, a program that rewrites its own source code, a mind that represents itself — must bottom out somewhere in something that is not itself part of the tangle, on pain of never getting off the ground. Another insists this is an illusion produced by our habit of describing such systems in levels: that a sufficiently rich tangle can be genuinely closed, grounding itself in nothing beyond its own operation, and that the inviolate level is simply wherever we happen to stop drawing the diagram.

To make the dispute concrete, consider a small online community governed by a single written rule that specifies how it may be amended, and which further provides that this very amendment procedure may itself be amended by that same procedure — so that the rule governing changes to the rules is changeable under the very rules it governs. Is there an inviolate level here, or is this a genuine strange loop that grounds itself? If there is one, is it to be located in the brute fact of what the members actually do when they disagree, in the physical substrate that stores the document, or somewhere else entirely? And if there is not, what exactly does the system rest on when the procedure it uses to revise itself is the thing being revised?

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Set out the strongest version of the case that every tangled hierarchy requires an inviolate level, then the strongest version of the case that it does not, and then commit to a verdict rather than splitting the difference: say whether the inviolate level is genuinely necessary, and if it is, whether it must lie outside the system in any metaphysically interesting sense or whether it is merely an artifact of how we describe the tangle from a given vantage point. Be as concrete as the subject allows, and develop at least one further example of your own beyond the amendment case.
