I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I would be grateful if you kept technical vocabulary to a minimum and explained any term you do introduce. I have been reading around Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, and I keep running into two readings of the central idea that one's humanity is owed to others. I would like a careful comparison of them.

The first reading is radical (I have seen it called radical communitarianism): being human and being a person are not the same thing. Personhood is a status to be attained, through participation in communal life and through morally decent conduct, and some human beings never attain it or attain it only partially; on this reading it is the community that confers personhood. The second reading is moderate: personhood belongs to every human being from the outset, as something intrinsic, and communal relations are what develop, express and sustain it rather than what create it; on this reading the community is indispensable to a person's flourishing but is not the source of their standing.

Please compare the two on the following points. First, what is the disagreement really about — is it a disagreement about the metaphysics of personhood, about the conditions of full moral standing, or about which of two senses of the word person deserves priority? Second, does either reading allow that personhood comes in degrees, or that it can be lost, and does either reading need to say so in order to make sense of its own claims? Third, which reading copes better with a human being who cannot take part in communal life — someone with a profound cognitive disability from birth, say, or someone raised in complete isolation — and what does the other reading have to say for itself?

I am most interested in a problem I suspect only one of the two can handle. Suppose a community spends two generations treating one group of its own members as not fully persons, excluding them from the ceremonies, councils and marriages through which, on the radical reading, personhood is conferred. The practice is then abandoned and everyone now regards it as a disgrace. On the radical reading, were the excluded people persons during those two generations, or was the community merely operating different rules? I want to know whether the radical reading can call the community mistaken, and if it can, what it must appeal to in order to do so — a standard of personhood that is not simply whatever the community happens to say — and what that appeal costs it. If the moderate reading wins this particular argument, say whether it wins it too cheaply, by building the answer in at the start.

Finally, tell me which of the two readings you would defend, and whether the relational conception of personhood is best understood as a rival to the Western idea that human beings have an intrinsic and inalienable moral status, or as an answer to a different question altogether.

Please write your answer in British English.
