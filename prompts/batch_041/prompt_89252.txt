I am a novelist — my last book was set in a mill town in the 1890s, which is how I ended up as the lay member of the interpretive planning committee at our regional museum — and I have to write the committee's formal opinion before we vote on the 14th, so I would like you to draft it. The case is this. We are rebuilding the permanent gallery on the city's industrial past, and the grant that pays for half of it requires the museum to submit a finalized master narrative to the funder within six weeks; if we miss that date we lose the money and the gallery opens two years late, if at all. Two drafts are on the table. The curator's draft, 'The Long Ascent,' runs the story as a single arc: the cholera years, the sanitary campaign, the municipal waterworks, the clearance of the courts and wynds, the new hospital — and ends in a final room with panels on present-day public-health statistics and a wall of photographs of current residents, on the theory that visitors need a payoff and that the reformers deserve to be shown as having won. The community historian's draft, 'Crossroads,' covers the same events but treats each as a contingent choice with beneficiaries and losers: the clearance panel names the families displaced and the compensation they did not get; the strike panel records a defeat that stayed a defeat for forty years; one reformer is shown as also holding views we now find repellent, and the draft says so plainly rather than in a footnote. Our director objects that 'Crossroads' is one damned thing after another, that school groups will wander out unmoved, and that a permanent gallery which refuses to say whether anything was gained is not being neutral but merely coy; a major donor, whose family name is on the 1878 waterworks, has let it be known that his gift is contingent on the ascent framing surviving in the final room. My own view is that the curator's draft is exactly the thing historians complain about when they complain about Whig history, and that the community historian's draft is not innocent either — its insistence on contingency and on the cost of every gain is itself a present-day judgment about how history ought to feel, and its refusal to say that the waterworks were an improvement strikes me as a kind of cowardice dressed as rigour. What I need from you is a written opinion I can circulate to the committee, recommending one concrete course of action — adopt one draft, or adopt one with named modifications, or commission a third — with a deadline and a division of labour that will actually get a document to the funder in six weeks, and with reasons the curator, the community historian, and the director could each respond to rather than simply reject. The philosophical work I want done in it is a criterion, stated sharply enough to be applied panel by panel, for when a progress arc in a museum narrative is legitimate and when it is wishful retrospection; I would like the criterion tested against at least three specific panels — the cholera years, the clearance of the courts, and the final room — and against the question the committee has not yet faced, namely whether a permanent gallery must end in the present at all, or whether it may end in 1911 and let visitors draw the moral themselves. Please be honest about the strongest case for the ascent framing, including the director's point about coyness and the uncomfortable fact that the museum exists because of the very reforms it would be celebrating; if the criterion you propose forces me to give up something I have said I believe, say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over. Write it as a document we can act on at the meeting — under two thousand words, no citations or reading lists (I will add references myself if we publish it), and a bottom line in the first paragraph.
