I am a retired statistical physicist who has recently become interested in philosophy, and I have been trying to think through the modern debate over Boltzmann brains. The core idea, as I understand it, is that if the universe is a sufficiently long-lived thermal fluctuation, then typical observers should be disembodied brains that briefly fluctuate into existence rather than evolved beings like us, and this is often treated as a reductio of cosmological models that predict such a universe. A common reply is that the reasoning is self-undermining: if Boltzmann brains dominate, then our own inferences are unreliable, so we cannot trust the argument that leads to the conclusion that Boltzmann brains dominate. What I would like from you is a prediction of what Ludwig Boltzmann himself would have said about this dialectic. Please reconstruct his likely view from his actual commitments about statistical mechanics, probability, and the second law, rather than from the way his name is used in contemporary cosmology. Would he have accepted the Boltzmann brain conclusion as a genuine consequence of his own fluctuation hypothesis, or would he have regarded it as a confusion about probability, typicality, or the nature of time? How would he have responded to the self-undermining objection? And what, if anything, does this imagined confrontation reveal about the philosophical foundations of the modern problem?
