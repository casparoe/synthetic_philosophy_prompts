The argument, roughly: "corruption" as the indices actually measure it bundles two things with different structures and, I want to claim, roughly opposite moral valences. Call them extraction and diversion. Extraction is the arms-length one — an official takes money from someone who's basically a stranger to them in order to hand over a service they were already supposed to provide: the bribe for the permit, the envelope to the nurse, the cut to get your file moved up the pile. It's thin, it's market-like, it's a tax on access. Diversion is the thick one — you steer the contract or the job or the fertilizer to your cousin, your co-villager, your patron's nephew, because that's what you owe them, and in a lot of places the local moral norm doesn't merely tolerate this, it demands it; the guy who *doesn't* take care of his people is the one who gets blamed. My claim is that the entire anti-corruption apparatus — the perception indices, the conditionality, the good-governance reform template — is calibrated to extraction as its paradigm case, and that what it's really tracking underneath is impersonality, the Weberian ideal of the bureau that treats everyone the same because nobody there is anyone's kin. Which is a specifically modern Northern European ideal, not a universal. But here's the part I think is actually the interesting one, and the part that'll get me in trouble with my own side: I don't think this ends in "so leave them alone." I think the moral valence is roughly backwards. Extraction is *worse* than the discourse admits — it's a regressive levy on exactly the people with the least access, and the only thing that can be said for it (the "grease the wheels" story, that it's how the poor get anything at all out of a state that otherwise ignores them) is a defense of a broken system, not of the practice. And diversion is *better* than the discourse admits, because where the state doesn't reach, the thick obligations are often the only functioning welfare system there is. So the reason the two get conflated isn't that they're morally similar; it's that the metric is secretly measuring distance from impersonality rather than harm.
