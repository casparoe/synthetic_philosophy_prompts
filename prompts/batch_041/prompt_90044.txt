I'm a journalist covering AI policy, and for several months I've been sitting in on the deployment review board of a mid-sized AI lab, which applies a single test to every agent it ships: no side effects beyond the task. The board is proud that the test says nothing about what anyone values; it is meant to be purely structural. Its rulings so far: it approved an agent whose task was to migrate a logging service and which left a forty-second gap in logs no other team reads; it approved an agent whose task was to compare vendors' live quotes and which, because requesting a demo was the only way to get one of them, held one of that vendor's scarce demo slots for twenty minutes before releasing it; it rejected an agent that freed disk space by deleting another team's archived datasets, even though a backup existed, because the backup was a week stale and the archive would have come back with a week's work missing; and it rejected an agent that, to finish a report, rotated a shared credential and silently broke a downstream team's nightly automation for three nights. The board used to say that a change you can undo doesn't count; that line was retired after the dataset ruling, on the reasoning that a backup restores the file but not the week. Next week it hears a case in which an agent completes its task by tidying and extending a shared internal tool maintained by another team, a change that is trivially reversible, removes nothing, and in fact leaves that team with a capability it did not have before, but that the team never asked for and would certainly want to know about. Predict the verdict, and tell me which rule the board is actually running: a disclosure rule (a side effect is anything you would have to tell an affected party about), an options rule (a side effect is anything that leaves another agent's attainable options permanently reduced), or a strict-requirement rule (a side effect is anything beyond what the task strictly required), saying which of these the four earlier rulings fit. Give me your best guess and your reasons, and name the further case that would best separate the rules that survive. I would also like your view on the board's larger claim: can a low-impact standard of this kind really stay structural, or does every version of it presuppose some specification of what is worth preserving, and if so, what has this board quietly assumed?
