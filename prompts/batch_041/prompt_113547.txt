I'm a graduate student in philosophy putting together a problem set for a seminar on practical rationality, and I've gotten stuck designing one of the problems. We spent a week on Kavka's toxin puzzle — the setup in which an eccentric stranger offers you a million dollars tonight if you now intend to drink, tomorrow, a toxin that will make you painfully ill for a day but leave no lasting harm; the money is in your account before the drinking hour arrives, so once you have it there is nothing left to be gained by drinking, and Kavka's claim is that this is precisely why the intention cannot be formed in the first place, since you cannot intend an act that you can already see you will have no reason to perform. I want a problem that runs the structure the other way, and I want to give the students a case rather than a question about a case. So construct a scenario in which an agent does perform the act, and performing it is plainly the rational thing to do at the time, and yet the corresponding intention could not have been formed in advance.

Some constraints, because the easy versions of this are worthless. The impossibility of the prior intention has to be structural rather than psychological: not weakness of will, not self-deception or confusion, not a change of heart between the two moments, not mere squeamishness about committing, and not a trick that works only because this particular agent is unusual. It has to follow from what the case is. In particular, the reason to act must come into existence only at the moment of action — if the agent already had that reason beforehand, she could simply have formed the intention then, and the problem evaporates. The only candidates I've come up with are things like being moved to tears by a piece of music or laughing at a joke, and those don't count, because nothing in them is an act; I need something unambiguously an action, with a definite cost or benefit, that an ordinary person could face. Keep the transactional frame of the original: someone offers the agent a payoff for the intention itself and not for the act, and you may stipulate whatever method of verification you like so long as it reads the agent's state at the time of the offer rather than her later conduct; tell me whether she takes the deal and why. Setting and stakes should be ordinary — family, friendship, workplace, neighborhood — with no billionaires, no poisons, no mind-reading, and no science fiction. Give me the scenario itself in a single paragraph.

Then do the work on it. Walk through the agent's reasoning at both moments, showing why the intention cannot be formed at the first and why the act is rational at the second, and then tell me what the case is actually about. Specifically: which features of the original puzzle does your case keep, which does it drop, and which does it invert — the payoff attached to a mental state rather than to conduct, the gap in time between intending and acting, or the fact that the act becomes pointless once the payoff is banked? My own suspicion is that once the reason to act exists only at the moment of action, the case stops being a mirror of Kavka's and becomes a different phenomenon wearing its clothes — something about how planning corrodes the very thing it aims at, or about the fragility of spontaneity — and I would rather hear that plainly than be handed a case that only looks like the inverse. So say whether it is a genuine mirror image, and if it isn't, name the disanalogy that kills it. Finally, give me the strongest reply available to someone who thinks Kavka's original conclusion survives your case untouched — for instance, that the agent could still have formed a conditional or higher-order intention, or that she had the relevant intention all along and merely doubted she would act on it — and tell me whether that reply actually works. Please write in American English.
