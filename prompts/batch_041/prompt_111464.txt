Q3. Let {E, ¬E} be a partition of your possibility space and let P be your credence function.
(a) State the rule of Jeffrey conditionalization for this case, and explain how strict conditionalization comes out as a special case of it. (4)
(b) Give a case in which Jeffrey conditionalization is the right rule to use and strict conditionalization is not, and explain why. (4)
(c) Explain the rigidity condition and why it matters for Jeffrey conditionalization. (6)
(d) Assess the following objection: "Jeffrey conditionalization is doing no work, since when your evidence is uncertain you can always simply conditionalize on the higher-order proposition that your new credence in E is q." (6)

Her answer:

(a) Let q be my new credence in E, where 0 ≤ q ≤ 1. Then for any H,
P'(H) = q·P(H | E) + (1 − q)·P(H | ¬E).
Strict conditionalization is the case q = 1: the second term vanishes and we get P'(H) = P(H | E). So Jeffrey conditionalization is what you get when you relax the demand that the evidence be learned with certainty and only require that the new credences in the cells of the partition be specified.

(b) I look at a piece of cloth under a streetlamp at night and it looks blue-ish to me. Before looking, my credence that the cloth is blue is 0.5; after looking I should shift to about 0.9. But I don't know that the cloth is blue — the lamp could be tinting it — so strict conditionalization isn't available here. Conditionalizing on E would drive my credence in E to 1 and make me certain of something I'm not certain of. Jeffrey conditionalization is the right rule: I set q = 0.9 for E = "the cloth is blue," and the remaining 0.1 goes to ¬E, with the alternatives inside ¬E keeping their relative proportions. This is the kind of case the rule was built for — the experience bears on E without settling it.

(c) Rigidity is the constraint that the conditional probabilities of any H given each cell of the partition stay fixed across the update:
P'(H | E) = P(H | E) and P'(H | ¬E) = P(H | ¬E).
All the update is allowed to change is the credence in the cells of the partition themselves. I don't think this is really an extra assumption laid on top of the probability axioms. P(H | E) is defined as P(H ∧ E)/P(E), and the axioms already dictate how those quantities have to be related to one another, so a Jeffrey update can't move the conditional probability around without breaking the calculus — the conditional probability is fixed by the numbers rather than being some further thing you could decide to preserve or not. This is also what makes Jeffrey conditionalization behave like strict conditionalization in another respect: because the conditional probabilities given the cells are pinned down, it doesn't matter in what order you take two pieces of uncertain evidence. If I first get uncertain evidence bearing on E and then uncertain evidence bearing on F, I end up in the same place as if I had taken them the other way round — just as with strict conditionalization, where updating on E and then F is the same as updating on F and then E.

(d) The objection has something to it, but I don't think it's decisive. It's true that you can always enrich the algebra with a proposition to the effect that your credence in E is now q, and if you are certain of that proposition you can conditionalize on it, so there is a description under which the uncertain-evidence case is a case of ordinary conditionalization. But it's contested whether the higher-order proposition really captures what the experience gives you rather than just redescribing it, and the move requires you to be certain about your own credence, which is a strong assumption in its own right — you would be as certain of the higher-order fact as the strict conditionalizer is of the evidence, so it isn't obvious that anything has been gained. So I'd say the objection is a real challenge to the idea that Jeffrey conditionalization is a primitive rule, but not to the idea that it is a correct rule.
