I sit on the deployment review board at the AI lab where I work (mid-size, we train our own frontier-ish models, no household names), and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's 9am meeting on a case that has split us 4-3, and I still havent landed anywhere. Model Atlas-2 came up for full certification last month. The process side is a mess: during a two-week crunch in March someone switched off the training-data provenance filter for about a 30-hour window, the run log for that window is missing, and the engineer who flipped it has since left the company. Under our standing rule 4.3, an unlogged run is presumed to have included the worst plausible slice of our excluded-data list unless the team can show otherwise — and they can't show otherwise, they've tried. The outcome side is the opposite story: Atlas-2 is the best model we've ever put through red-teaming, under our harm thresholds on every pre-registered eval, the first checkpoint that mostly refuses the bio-uplift probes, and the external auditors (who didn't know about the gap) called it the safest thing they'd tested all year. So: bad process, great outcomes.

Here's the fight. Three of us say the process record is the only thing we actually control, that eval numbers are gameable and unrepresentative of deployment, and that rule 4.3 exists precisely for the case where the missing evidence would have been damning — if we waive it now we've admitted the rule is decorative. The other three say certifying is the right call anyway and that blocking would be rule-worship: 4.3 was adopted *because* evals were treated as unreliable proxies for harm, and here the harm evidence is unusually strong, so insisting on the proxy when you have the thing itself is backwards. They also make a sharper point that I can't shake: if the same facts had come in with terrible evals we'd all agree to block, which means our real decision rule is outcome-based and always was, and the process talk is just how we dress it up. I'm the only person on this board with any philosophy background, which is why they keep handing me these things, and honestly this feels like deontology vs. consequentialism wearing a lab coat.

So please draft the written opinion I'll circulate Thursday. It should recommend one concrete course — certify, certify with conditions, or block — with conditions spelled out line by line so we can vote on them separately, and a time frame attached (e.g., what happens in 30 or 60 days, who re-audits, what triggers revocation). Engage the strongest version of the side you come out against rather than the sloppy version; the two members I most need to convince are the ones with the best arguments. And say explicitly whether your recommendation would flip if the eval results had come out badly — if yes, explain why that isn't just outcome-based supervision with extra steps, and if no, explain what work rule 4.3 is actually doing that a harm estimate couldn't do. American English, and keep it under about 800 words, nobody reads past that before a 9am meeting.
