Consider a proposal for testing whether the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism — the charge that aggregative calculation treats many lives as though they were one life — is doing any independent work. The test: take a case in which the objection is supposed to bite (one person's ruin traded against a million barely noticeable gains), and try to state the complaint without any distributive or deontic vocabulary at all — no fairness, no desert, no rights, no using-someone-as-a-means. If every honest restatement smuggles one of those terms back in, then the objection is not an independent objection to aggregation; it is a distributive intuition in disguise, and a utilitarian may simply deny the intuition and be done.

Give me your credence that the test, applied honestly, vindicates that deflationary verdict. Decompose it into at least these sub-questions, each with a rough probability and one line of justification: (i) whether the separateness of persons is a metaphysical fact about persons or itself a normative premise; (ii) whether the standard replies — that a person is no more unified across time than a population is at a time, and that the objection may only tell against aggregating ex post while leaving ex ante aggregation untouched — engage the objection or change the subject; (iii) whether any formulation of the objection survives the vocabulary test; (iv) whether the dispute is partly verbal, in that the objection targets a version of utilitarianism its defenders need not hold.

Then show how the terms combine into your number, name the term the number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or case would move it most.
