I'm a first-year PhD student in archaeology, and I've been sitting in a seminar on long-term social change where the word "collapse" gets used constantly and, it seems to me, carelessly — always about the disappearance of palaces, dynasties, monumental building, and long-distance exchange, almost never directly about what happened to the people living under them. I've started sketching a distinction to discipline the word, and before I try it out on my seminar I'd like you to stress-test it rather than cheer for it. The distinction is between two ledgers. The first is a welfare ledger: survival, health, population, the continuity of ordinary life — farming, households, language. The second is an order ledger: the fate of a political or institutional order and its signature outputs — royal monuments, dated inscriptions, tribute systems, interregional trade. My suspicion is that what scholars call a "collapse" is defined almost entirely on the second ledger but borrows its emotional force from the first, so that a claim about the disappearance of an elite order gets heard as a claim about the death of a people. I want to know whether that diagnosis survives contact with a real case, or whether I am the one being careless.

Take the Late Bronze Age crisis in the eastern Mediterranean, roughly 1200 to 1150 BCE, as the primary test, and don't spend effort narrating it back to me — I know the shape: palace-centered economies running from Mycenaean Greece through the Hittite empire to the Syrian coast, a cluster of destructions and abandonments at the major centers, Hattusa given up, the Mycenaean palaces burned, the administrative script of the palaces vanishing with them, long-distance exchange contracting sharply, and Egypt surviving in a diminished form while its neighbors' order did not. What I want is the analysis. Run the two ledgers over this episode explicitly: populate each one, and for each entry say whether it is actually recoverable from the material record or whether we are inferring it from the fate of elites. Then tell me where the distinction does real work and, more important, where it breaks. My own hunch is that it breaks precisely in the cases we care about most — that where there is genuine demographic collapse the two ledgers converge and the framework has nothing left to distinguish, so it would be useless exactly when it matters. Tell me if that is right and what it implies for the framework, rather than rescuing it.

Three further things, and please resist the urge to make the concept speak where it is silent. First, is there a reading on which "collapse" names a real, non-evaluative process rather than an observer's verdict — a cascade of network failure through a densely interconnected system, say — and does the Bronze Age case support that reading, or is "cascade" just one more frame imposed after the fact? Second, turn the framework on itself: does the word "welfare" or the decision to treat order as a merely elite artifact smuggle in a normative judgment of the kind I am accusing others of making? Third, say whether the analysis carries over to the Terminal Classic in the Maya lowlands, where the southern cities stop raising dated monuments and are largely abandoned around 800 to 1000 CE while the northern centers carry on and the Maya themselves plainly do not end, and to Rapa Nui, where — I'm aware — it is disputed whether the pre-contact population crashed at all, so I'm less interested in who is right about the island than in what the concept of collapse is even doing when the demographic fact is contested. Close with a verdict: is collapse a natural kind, a useful shorthand, or a category mistake, and if my distinction holds up, what should we say instead. No citations or reading list — I want the argument.
