You are a philosopher of epistemology with a specialty in the ethics of belief and a close working knowledge of the classic exchange between William James and W. K. Clifford over whether it is ever permissible to believe something on insufficient evidence. I am applying for a two-page summer research fellowship and need you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proposal. Please think carefully before drafting: begin by sketching three or four ways the project could be framed, say briefly what each would commit me to, and then choose the sharpest framing and write the proposal around it. Do not show me the discarded options in the final document.

The half-formed idea is this. The James–Clifford dispute is almost always read as a single disagreement about one question — whether belief without sufficient evidence is ever permitted — after which each side is graded on its verdicts about religious belief and about the careless shipowner. But the two writers may not be disagreeing about one question at all; they may be operating under two different kinds of norm. Clifford's norm looks like a norm of public accountability: because beliefs feed into action and testimony, a believer owes the other members of an epistemic community a certain standard of care. James's norm looks like a norm of personal possibility: some beliefs are preconditions of acting, or of a life going well, and a demand for evidence-first can be self-undermining. If that is right, the productive question is not who is right but whether these two normative systems can conflict, how a conflict between them is to be resolved, and whether the conflict is genuine or merely an artifact of applying both norms to the same belief at the same time.

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following:

- States the research question so that it has a determinate answer rather than naming a topic.
- Explains why the standard readings of the debate leave that question open — that is, why neither the usual evidentialist position nor the usual pragmatist position already settles it.
- Sets out the method: what I would actually do, in order, across the fellowship, and why a conceptual approach is the right one here rather than a historical or empirical one.
- Describes what a successful result would look like — what would count as showing the two norms are genuinely in tension and what would count as showing the tension dissolves on inspection — and is honest about what would count as the project failing or ending inconclusive.
- Tests the framework against at least one case in which the two norms appear to pull in opposite directions. You may choose the case, but justify the choice and say exactly what the framework predicts about it.
- Scopes the work realistically for a summer and says explicitly what I would have to leave out.

Keep it to roughly two pages. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by reference; refer to positions and arguments by description and I will add the sources myself. Where you are unsure of a title, a date, or an attribution, describe the work rather than naming it.
