I'm a second-year sociology PhD and I've been roped into running one of the theory seminars next term — ninety minutes, eight first-year PhD students, and about half of them are from econ or quant backgrounds and have already privately decided that the whole Weber thing is either trivially true or unfalsifiable. The seminar is on "can culture be a cause?", i.e. the Protestant ethic thesis plus the general problem of explaining action by the values people hold. I dont want you to explain the thesis to me — I want you to plan how I should teach it, so that by the end they can see what kind of claim it actually is. The first thing I need is the order. My instinct is to start with the problem of explaining-with-values in the abstract and only then bring in the historical case, because if I open on Calvin and predestination they'll file the whole thing under history of ideas and never feel the methodological knife. Tell me whether that's right, and what has to come before what for it to land — in particular where the reasons-versus-causes question has to sit relative to the switchmen point (you know, Weber's image of world-images as railway points that set the track along which interests push conduct) and relative to the elective-affinity idea. And I want a fork in there that I always fumble: whether the ethic is meant to do its work by motivating individuals or by selecting the groups and firms that behave that way — the selection reading keeps tempting me because it makes the thesis cleaner, but I suspect it quietly guts the causal claim. Decide which reading I should teach them and why.

Second, the carrying case. Please don't use the Franklin time-is-money example — every one of them has seen it and it smuggles in the very economic motive we're supposed to be explaining. Pick one case to run the whole seminar on, and tell me what it has to have and what it mustnt have: it needs a value that plausibly bites on behaviour (ledgers, saving, deferral of consumption, whatever) without already being a profit motive, and it mustn't be so exotic that they can't judge it for themselves. I've been toying with a small dissenting congregation whose members kept unusually scrupulous accounts — is that the right shape, or is there something better? Build in the objection I can never answer well, namely the one where they say: fine, so thrift and deferral helped, but then the religious content is doing no work at all and you could tell the same story about any belief system that produced the same habits. I want that objection to land as a genuine threat, not to be waved away.

Then the misconceptions, which is really why I'm asking. They'll arrive with some and the sequence will breed others. Off the top of my head: "Weber says Protestantism caused capitalism" (rather than a much thinner claim about the spirit); "if values explain anything then interests explain nothing"; "explaining by reasons isn't real explaining because reasons aren't causes"; and the econ crowd's own "so it's unfalsifiable, therefore worthless". Map the rest, put each one at the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and tell me which of them I should let them hold for twenty minutes and then dismantle rather than pre-empt. I also want the one closing question I can ask to check they've got the shape of the claim rather than just the story, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so (I'm assuming the predestination-theology apparatus and the later secularisation argument, but you tell me). Be committed — give me one plan with real choices and your reasons for them, not three options and a shrug; where you're genuinely torn, say so, but still decide. British English, and give it to me as: "Opening move" (~100 words), "The order of ideas" (~400), "The carrying case" (~250), "Misconception map" (~400), "Check for understanding" (~100), "What I'm leaving out" (~100).
