I am a novelist, three years into a book set among the Cambridge circle that gathered around Henry Sidgwick in the 1880s and 1890s, and I have been asked to run a single session next month in a joint philosophy-and-literature seminar. Sixteen graduate students, ninety minutes, and I would rather they did something than listen to me talk about the period.

Here is the material I want the activity built from, and it is all the material you need; assume the participants know none of it in advance. Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics ends not in a settled verdict but in a dualism of practical reason: rational egoism and rational benevolence each hold their ground, and reason, as he presents it, cannot adjudicate between them. He was a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and he spent the last two decades of his life sitting with mediums and sifting testimony, holding himself to evidential standards he had himself made severe, in the hope that evidence of survival would underwrite a moral order in which his dualism might be resolved. He died an agnostic, without the evidence he wanted. The Society's subsequent history preserves the shape of his problem twice over: a sealed letter that Frederic Myers left to be opened after his death, which the Society had to report as a failure; and the scripts later attributed to the dead researchers themselves, fragments that meant nothing one by one and everything in combination, provided one was willing to do the combining.

Design a staged sitting that makes the participants feel the bind rather than hear it named. Concretely, I want: a cast with roles and written instructions (a medium working from a sealed packet and instructed to cold-read honestly, with deliberate misses and no peeking; two or three sitters, one of whom holds private information; a Recorder who may transcribe but not interpret; and one participant designated to write the Society's verdict for its Proceedings, carrying both the institutional stake in a positive result and the strongest personal interest in one). A pre-registration mechanic that forces every member of the council, before anything happens, to write down on a sealed card a specific outcome that would move their credence, the number it would move to, and a kill criterion stating what would end their participation. A limited budget of sittings or guineas that must be partly committed in advance. Three rounds: one in which the target information is loose in the room and leakage is possible, one in which it is sealed before the medium enters, and one that yields nothing at all, followed by the medium's explanation that the conditions were hostile. Then the fragment round, in which each participant is handed pieces of a correspondence and must decide individually whether to assemble them into a message, and only afterwards compares. The sealed cards are opened at the moment of the final vote, not before.

Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular features of Sidgwick's position, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and is honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to supply myself. I am aware of at least two things it cannot reproduce, and I want your list to be candid about them and about anything I have not thought of: the decades-long attrition of a hope that is never refuted but never vindicated, and the fact that in the real case the judges were the friends of the judged.
