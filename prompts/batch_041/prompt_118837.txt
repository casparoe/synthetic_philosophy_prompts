I'm leading a seventy-five-minute session for a mixed group of biology and economics graduate students who have all heard of the handicap principle, can all recite that costly signals are honest, and none of whom can say what does the work in that claim. Don't explain it to me — plan the explanation. What has to be established first for the argument to be more than a slogan; at what point the thought that honesty requires a cost differential rather than merely a cost has to enter, and why; and why the receiver's response must be on the table before the handicap itself can even be defined. Choose one carrying example — not the peacock's tail, not human conspicuous consumption, and not one in which the signaller is courting — and say what it must have and what it must lack for the structure to show. Then map the misconceptions in the order this particular group will produce them: that any costly display is thereby a signal; that the cost makes the signal a waste, so that some collective benefit must be doing the real work; and that a signal is honest because low-quality signallers simply cannot produce it, which is a different mechanism altogether (some call these indices rather than handicaps) and has to be kept distinct rather than merged. Say exactly where in the sequence I head each of these off. Finish with one question for the room that separates a costly trait from a costly signal, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
