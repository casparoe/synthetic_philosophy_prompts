For the past three years I have sat on the steering committee of the participatory budgeting process in my city, and I have been trying, without much success, to work out what we are actually committed to when we run it. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have read a fair amount on my own, and I suspect the process assumes something about democracy that no one has ever stated out loud. I would like you to help me pin it down.

Here is how the process works. Each year the city sets aside a fixed share of its capital budget — money for physical projects, not services — and divides the city into districts. In each district, residents meet in open assemblies over several weeks, hear from city engineers about what is technically possible, propose projects, argue them through, and then vote on a ranked list. The winning projects, up to each district's allocation, are binding on the city: they go into the capital plan and are built. Before anything reaches the ballot, a staff office screens each proposal for cost and feasibility, and anything the engineers judge infeasible or beyond the allocation is struck. Once a winning project is built, there is no mechanism for revisiting it and no requirement that the city report back on whether it worked. Separately, the same city holds ordinary up-or-down referendums on other questions, where residents simply vote yes or no on a proposal the council has already drafted.

What puzzles me is this. During the assemblies, everything is arranged as if people's preferences are not yet settled — as if the point of meeting, hearing the engineers, arguing with neighbours, and revising is to work out what the district actually needs, and as if a good proposal is a kind of hypothesis about what will help. But the moment voting starts, the whole thing treats those preferences as finished and simply counts them, exactly like the referendum. And once a project wins, the process treats it as settled — built, done, never examined again — even though the discussion that produced it was all about uncertainty and revision.

A friend who studied philosophy says this sounds like John Dewey: that for him democracy was less a matter of elections than a way of living and inquiring together, and that a policy was the sort of thing you try and then learn from. But I do not know whether our process actually commits us to anything like that, or whether it merely borrows the vocabulary while resting on something far more ordinary.

Please tell me what view of democracy, and of inquiry, the process as I have described it actually presupposes. Be specific about which parts of it strictly require a particular view and which merely happen to fit several. Tell me which rival pictures of democracy the process quietly rules out — for instance, that voting is simply the aggregation of preferences people already have, or that governing is a matter for experts who consult the public rather than for a public that decides. Say whether the pieces of the process are consistent with one another or whether, as I suspect, the assembly phase and the voting-and-building phase pull in different directions. And finally, tell me what would have to change about the process if the view you identify were replaced by its main rival: what we would have to stop doing, and what we would have to start doing, for the process to be honest about what it assumes.
