In 1990 Terri Schiavo, then twenty-six, went into cardiac arrest at her home and suffered severe brain damage from oxygen deprivation; after two and a half months without improvement she was diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state. Her husband, as her legal guardian, concluded that she would not have wanted to be kept alive in that condition and in 1998 petitioned to have her feeding tube removed; her parents disputed both the prognosis and his account of her wishes, and over the following seven years the case ran through the Florida courts, an act of the Florida legislature, an act of Congress signed by a sitting president, and repeated refusals of certiorari from the Supreme Court. The tube was removed for the last time on March 18, 2005, and she died thirteen days later; the autopsy reported severe, irreversible brain damage. Notice how the contest was conducted: in behavioral and testimonial terms — what she could track or respond to, what she would have wanted, who had authority to decide — with the causal structure of what remained of her brain entering only as contested scan evidence.

I want that episode re-run through Integrated Information Theory. Take the following as given, since I don't want the answer spent rehearsing it: IIT identifies a system's consciousness with its intrinsic, maximally irreducible cause–effect structure rather than with behavior or report; it treats integrated information, φ, as the quantity of consciousness, so that consciousness comes in degrees; its exclusion postulate says that only the maximally integrated complex is the subject of experience, which is how the theory can hold that the cerebellum, with several times the cortex's neurons, contributes almost nothing to experience; and it has a clinical arm, the perturbational complexity index, which combines transcranial magnetic stimulation with EEG to gauge how complex and differentiated a brain's response to a perturbation is, and which has been used to estimate consciousness in patients who cannot report anything at all. Tell me what IIT says the parties were actually disagreeing about, what it would have wanted to know that nobody asked, and whether it vindicates, overturns, or simply bypasses the diagnosis — on what evidence, and with what confidence that evidence could have been had at the time.

Then run the analysis the other way and be hard on the theory. IIT's central quantity cannot be computed for a human brain, and its clinical proxy is a coarse instrument that could not have been applied to the question that mattered here; say whether a theory that cannot adjudicate the case it seems tailor-made for is thereby weakened, or whether that is a complaint about measurement rather than about truth. IIT has also been charged with being unfalsifiable — say whether this episode supports that charge or is a case where the theory could have staked a risky prediction and did. Ask, finally, what the theory is silent about: if what matters is intrinsic causal structure rather than the capacity to suffer or to have interests, does the ethical dispute the parties were actually having survive translation into IIT's vocabulary, or does it get replaced by a different question — and does IIT owe us a bridge principle from φ to moral standing that it has not supplied? Note where the theory's verdict, if it had one, might have cut against both sides.
