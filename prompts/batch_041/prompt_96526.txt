I help run a small grant-making circle that funds animal welfare work, and for the better part of two years we have been going round in circles on the same question: whether insects ought to be anywhere near our list of priorities. The scale argument, as it is usually put to us, runs roughly as follows. The number of insects is astronomically larger than the number of vertebrates — orders of magnitude, not percentages — and if there is even a modest probability that an insect has experiences that matter, then the expected amount of welfare at stake in insect populations may dwarf everything else we could hope to accomplish for animals, so that on any broadly consequentialist accounting insect welfare deserves serious attention. The sceptics in our group do not dispute the entomology or the arithmetic. They say instead that the probability is very small; or that we have no business assigning a probability at all to something so far outside our evidence; or that the expected-value machinery is being driven in a way that would license absurd conclusions elsewhere, which is the familiar fanaticism worry; or that even if insects are sentient, the intensity and variety of good and bad experience of which they are capable is so narrow that the vast numbers simply wash out. The proponents reply that each of these moves is either question-begging or else rests on assumptions no less contestable than the conclusion it is meant to defeat. Both sides argue in apparent good faith from premises the other side accepts, and in two years nobody has shifted an inch.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My own hypothesis — and I offer it only as a hypothesis, to be tested against what the two sides actually say — is that this is not really a disagreement about entomology or about the numbers at all, but about whether sentience is a threshold property or a matter of degree, and therefore about whether the expected number of sentient insects is even a well-formed quantity. The scale argument seems to require that sentience comes in degrees, since it multiplies a probability by a count and then compares the product across species; the sceptical position, when one presses it, tends to treat sentience as all-or-nothing, so that an insect either is or is not a subject of welfare and there is nothing in between to be uncertain about. If that is right, the impasse is metaphysical and no amount of further work on insect neurobiology will touch it. But I may well be wrong, and I would like you to tell me so if I am.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A substantive disagreement about the facts of insect minds; a disagreement about which decision rule is appropriate under deep uncertainty; a disagreement about the metaphysics of mind dressed up as a disagreement about priorities; or two groups answering different questions under a single heading — one asking what we are permitted to believe about insect experience, the other asking what we ought to do with our money? Diagnose it with reasons drawn from the positions as I have described them, say what evidence, argument or formal result would be needed to settle it, and then give me an honest answer about what would count as progress here given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, a decision-theoretic constraint, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please write your answer in British English.
