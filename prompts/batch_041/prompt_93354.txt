I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and in the welfare-economics corners of the internet for years, and a friend in my reading group has handed me an argument I can't shake. He's a formal type — the sort who thinks in representation theorems and in the distinction between ordinal and cardinal structure — and he built this to show that a certain everyday class of claim is incoherent. I suspect it proves far too much, but each premise looks individually defensible, and I can't decide whether the fault lies in a slide between steps or whether I simply have to pay one of the premises' price. Here it is, laid out roughly as he wrote it.

(1) To say that A is better off than B is to say that A's welfare is greater than B's. (2) For one quantity to be greater than another, both must lie on a common scale — the same unit and the same zero point. (3) Whether such a common scale exists is a question about what the world and our measurement practices actually supply. (4) Everything we can genuinely measure — choices, revealed preferences, self-reports, whatever neural correlates one cares to name — is calibrated person by person; nothing in it fixes a unit or a zero shared across persons. (5) So there is no fact of the matter as to whether A's welfare is greater than B's. (6) Hence claims of the form 'A is better off than B' are neither true nor false, and any policy defended by appeal to a net improvement in welfare is defended by appeal to nothing.

I'd like help diagnosing this. If it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon, and be candid about the bill — I'm attached to (1) and (2) and would sooner surrender (6), but I don't want to be handed that conclusion for free. If it isn't valid, name the step where it slips, explain why that step looked innocent, and say what work the missing distinction is doing. I'd also like a clear-eyed sense of how much of this is actually contested: please separate the parts that people who work on this would treat as settled from the parts where there is genuine disagreement, and don't dress a contested position up as the consensus. Concretely, I want to know whether the argument's real target is measurability or comparability; whether anything survives as a mere ordering even where no metric scale does; and what, if anything, in the reasoning blocks the thought that the same considerations would strip comparisons of a single person across time of their truth — a question I raise because I can't see what stops it, not because I'm confident it follows.
