I was in a reading group on population ethics, and a friend offered the following argument, which I have not been able to shake. We were comparing two outcomes. In T, one person is tortured for fifty years. In D_n, n distinct people each get a single dust speck in the eye—uncomfortable for a second, no lasting damage, and no one else is affected. The argument runs:

(1) T is worse than D_1.
(2) For every n, if T is worse than D_n, then T is worse than D_{n+1}. That is, no single additional person’s dust speck can be the difference between the torture being worse and the dust specks being worse; each speck is too trivial to be decisive.
(3) Therefore, by induction, T is worse than D_n for every finite n.
(4) Therefore, no finite number of dust specks, however large, is worse than one torture.

The conclusion seems absurd. If n is enormous—say, a number with a hundred digits—we should not think that preventing one torture is more important than preventing that many people from each getting a speck in the eye. Yet each premise has a pull: (1) is obvious; (2) seems to follow from the sheer smallness of a single speck; and (3) is just induction. My friend says the lesson is that we must accept (4) and stop pretending that aggregating tiny harms can ever outweigh a severe one. I suspect something has gone wrong, but I cannot decide whether the culprit is (2), the induction, or the assumption that “worse than” is a total order over these outcomes.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I give up (2), what do I have to say about where the flip happens and whether that threshold is arbitrary? If I keep (2) and reject (4), what does that commit me to about the transitivity of “worse than” or the possibility of incommensurable values? I would like to keep (1) and the idea that tiny harms genuinely matter, but I am willing to give up whatever is least costly. Please diagnose the argument rather than simply asserting that the torture is worse, and make clear what each available response has to pay.
