Legal systems seem to me to hold two commitments about vagueness that cannot comfortably sit together, and I would like to know which of them is doing the real work. On one side, courts in the jurisdictions I know best treat indeterminacy in a criminal prohibition as a defect serious enough to void the statute: if an ordinary person cannot tell what conduct is forbidden, or if the prohibition leaves police and juries to enforce it as they please, the law is struck down even when the conduct it targets is plainly worth prohibiting. On the other side, the same systems run on predicates that are graded through and through — negligence, reasonableness, materiality, competence, undue influence, obscenity — and on a definition of death by neurological criteria, and nobody supposes these are void. Here the line is left to a jury.

I want the implicit theory of vagueness reconstructed. Take the sorites seriously: neurons do not cease together, impairment is a continuum, and the age of majority falls at midnight on a birthday with no physiological significance whatever. When the law stipulates such a line, does it suppose it is discovering a boundary that was there all along, or openly creating one? When it delegates reasonableness to twelve jurors, has it decided that there is no fact of the matter, or that there is one and jurors are as reliable at finding it as anyone? I suspect two devices carry different commitments and ought to be separated: stipulation — the authoritative arbitrary line, defended on grounds of notice, administrability, and equal treatment, with the maxim that the law does not concern itself with trifles, grandfather clauses, and limitation periods as its cousins — and delegation, where no line is drawn at all and the case is handed to a decision-maker.

So reconstruct the theory, or theories, and say whether the practice is unified. Which accounts does it fit: that vague predicates have determinate but unknowable extensions; that they have gaps, so that a legal line is a permissible precisification of a predicate the law had left unsettled; that vagueness is a matter of degrees of truth; that the extension shifts with context and with the purpose of the inquiry? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely tolerates — I expect that a legislature's power to draw a line somewhere is compatible with almost every view, and that the interesting question is what the voidness doctrine rules out. Then tell me what would actually change in court if we adopted a rival account: under a degree-theoretic picture, should verdicts themselves be graded, so that partial negligence and partial liability become the honest outcome? And if extensions shift with context, is the voidness doctrine confused about the problem it takes itself to be solving?
