I have no training in philosophy or psychology — I read popular science and listen to podcasts, and I've been chewing on this on my own for a while. There's a chapter in a popular book on moral psychology (the author is a journalist rather than a researcher, and I'm paraphrasing, not quoting) that summarizes the cross-cultural "WEIRD" findings roughly as follows:

"For most of the twentieth century, psychology studied only Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic people and then called the results human nature. The cross-cultural work has shown that this was a mistake, because moral intuitions are not universal at all but vary from society to society; the WEIRD pattern turns out to be the outlier. In the West, morality is a matter of rules, individual rights, and principles that apply to everyone equally. In more communal societies, it is a matter of roles, relationships, and duties that differ depending on who you are. The decisive evidence is that when you give people from different societies the same dilemma, they reach opposite verdicts, and each side finds the other's answer not merely mistaken but unintelligible. This is why the older belief in a single true morality has been quietly abandoned: if two cultures disagree about what is right and neither can appeal to anything outside its own intuitions, then morality is simply whatever a culture says it is. The empirical study of moral psychology has thus settled a question that philosophers had argued about for centuries."

I suspect most of this is right, but the last part makes me uneasy, and I can't tell whether my unease is well-founded or just sentimentality about morality. So I want an audit. Which claims would someone who actually does this research accept, which would they wince at as a simplification, and which are straightforwardly false? I especially want the gap, if there is one, between the claim that people's moral intuitions differ across cultures and the claim that there is no fact of the matter about right and wrong: does the second really follow from the first, or is the book sliding from one kind of claim to another? If the inference is bad, what would actually have to be shown for cross-cultural variation to count as evidence against objectivity, as opposed to evidence only about how moral beliefs happen to be formed? Take the summary's picture of the East/West contrast seriously for a moment and tell me whether that contrast is itself well supported or is one of the simplifications. And is there anything cross-cultural evidence could in principle show that would bear on the objectivity question at all, or is the whole dispute simply not the kind of thing that this evidence can touch? Finally, if the book is wrong, tell me what a defensible version of its point would look like — and whether any part of its philosophical conclusion survives once the empirical part is stated accurately.
