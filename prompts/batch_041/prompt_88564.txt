Question: In "Politics and the English Language," Orwell argues that language and political thought are connected. What exactly does he think the relationship is? Is it one-way or reciprocal? How do his examples and his proposed rules support his view? Write a short essay.

Student answer: Orwell thinks that bad language leads to bad politics. He says that when language becomes vague and full of dying metaphors, it becomes easier for people to have foolish thoughts. He gives examples of political writing that uses euphemism and pretentious diction to obscure meaning, like calling the bombing of villages pacification. He then offers six rules for clear writing, such as never using a metaphor you're used to seeing in print, and never using a long word where a short one will do. The point of these rules is to force writers to think clearly, because if you can't say something clearly, you probably don't understand it. Orwell's view is essentially that political language is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable, and that this is a one-way street: corrupt language corrupts thought, but clear language can't be corrupted by bad thought. He thinks that if we all followed his rules, we could fix politics. However, he also admits that language is a natural growth, not something we can fully control. So his view is that we should try to improve language anyway, because it's the best we can do.
