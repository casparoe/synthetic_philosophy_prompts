I am a graduate student in philosophy of science, and for the past several weeks a friend and I have been working through the literature on self-locating belief — in particular Bostrom's distinction between the self-sampling assumption, on which you treat yourself as a random draw from the observers who actually exist in your reference class, and the self-indication assumption, on which you treat yourself as a random draw from all possible observers weighted by the probability of their existing, so that a hypothesis is favored in proportion to how many observers it contains. We have now stalled on a variant we constructed ourselves, and I would like your help. A machine flips a fair coin. On heads it creates a single observer, and that observer is you. On tails it creates a very large number of observers — call it a billion — and you are one of them, chosen uniformly at random. The wrinkle is that on tails all billion observers are created as exact duplicates of one another and of the lone observer in the heads case: identical bodies, identical memories, identical momentary experiences, and at the instant of creation nothing about the world distinguishes any of them from any other. They diverge only a moment later, when each begins to receive a different stream of experience. You wake at the instant of creation, before any divergence. The self-indication assumption appears to entail that your credence that the coin landed tails should be essentially 1, since your existence is a billion times more likely under tails; the self-sampling assumption appears to entail 1/2, since you were going to exist either way. My friend holds that the duplication is a red herring and that the self-indication verdict survives untouched; I suspect that once the observers are exact duplicates, the self-indication assumption's method of counting has either broken down or must be recast in terms of centered worlds or observer-moments rather than individuals, and that this may well change the answer.

What I want is your own credence in one reasonably precise claim: that in this duplication variant, an agent who accepts the rest of the setup and reasons correctly should have a credence of approximately 1, rather than approximately 1/2, that the coin landed tails — that is, that exact duplication makes no difference to the self-indication verdict. If forced to commit, my friend puts his credence in that claim near 0.9 and I put mine near 0.4. Please do not simply hand me a number. Decompose the claim: how much of your credence rides on whether the self-indication assumption or the self-sampling assumption is the correct framework for self-locating belief in the first place; how much on whether exact duplicates at a single time should be counted as one observer or as many for anthropic purposes; how much on whether the proper unit is the individual, the observer-moment, or the centered world; and how much on whether the question has a determinate answer at all, rather than being underdetermined by the constraints of rationality or settling into a mere bookkeeping convention. Give me a rough probability for each of those, explain how they combine into your final number, and tell me which single sub-question your answer is most sensitive to.

Then tell me which sub-question my friend and I most plausibly diverge on, and what argument, counterexample, or formal result would move your number the most in either direction. I should say that I have no stake in the self-indication side of this; I would be just as interested to hear that the whole dichotomy is a mistake and that neither assumption is the right way to frame the case. Please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just sentences, with the numbers embedded in the prose where they belong.
