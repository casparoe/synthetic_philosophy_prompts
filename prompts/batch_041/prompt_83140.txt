For the past few months I have been sitting in on a reading group that has been working through the long-run history of violence, and last week two of the regulars — a demographer by training and a historian — had one of those arguments that keeps resurfacing without ever resolving. I stayed out of it, but I would like a dispassionate referee's report, because I suspect each of them is right about something and that neither will concede the point.

The demographer argued, in order, that (1) the claim that violence is declining is well-supported, since the homicide rate in medieval and early-modern Europe appears to have been many times the modern rate, and a fall of that magnitude and consistency is too large to be an artifact of better record-keeping; (2) the correct measure is violence per capita, because what the question asks about is the risk that any given person will be killed or assaulted, whereas absolute body counts are largely a reflection of population growth; and (3) the decline is a brute empirical fact once one fixes a definition of 'violence', the value-ladenness of the word notwithstanding. The historian replied, in order, that (1) the comparison sets a measured rate against a reconstructed one, since the medieval figures are extrapolated from fragmentary records, so the apparent decline may be an artifact of the very state-building that improved the records — and that in any case the fall in private violence coincided with a rise in the state's own violence, so we may have relocated violence rather than reduced it; (2) even granting a per-capita decline, per capita is not obviously the morally relevant measure, since if what matters is the total quantity of suffering then the growth in absolute numbers is what counts, and the question of whether the world is less violent is ambiguous between the two; (3) the category itself has shifted over time, so that acts once tolerated — wife-beating, dueling, corporal punishment of children, killing in war — are now counted as violence, which means that part of the apparent decline may be an expansion of the definition rather than an improvement in behavior; and (4) if one widens the definition still further to include what is sometimes called structural violence — deaths from preventable deprivation, for instance — the trend might reverse, and because the choice of definition is driven by values rather than by nature, there may be no fact of the matter about whether the world is really becoming less violent.

The demographer's only reply to that last point was that widening the definition until the trend disappears is a kind of gerrymandering: a category broad enough to guarantee the answer is not an empirical claim at all, and value-ladenness does not by itself make the question indeterminate, since once a definition is fixed the question becomes an ordinary empirical one. I would like you to adjudicate this move by move rather than declare a winner. For each of the claims above, say whether it is true, false, or dependent on something the speaker has left unspecified, and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification. Where the two of them are talking past each other, identify the source of the confusion; and at the end, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about what it would take to answer the question at all.
