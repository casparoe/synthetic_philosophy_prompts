So my brother-in-law is a financial planner and at a bbq last weekend he spent like twenty minutes half-jokingly pitching me Pascal's wager — his version was basically "you already pay for home insurance you'll probably never use, so why not pay a little premium for the one policy with an infinite payout, and anyway the premium is just going to church on Sunday." And the infuriating part is I know there's something wrong with it but I couldn't actually say what, and I still can't tell if he was joking. It's been bugging me all week.

So I want to write a satire of the whole move. Here's what I'm picturing: a leaked pitch deck / investment prospectus for a made-up firm that sells exactly this — an "afterlife hedge fund" where you diversify your bet across a whole portfolio of gods instead of putting everything on one. Written deadpan, like a real prospectus: executive summary, the opportunity, risk factors, fee schedule, minimum holding period, a customer testimonial. 600-700 words or so.

The jokes need to come from getting the actual argument right, not from just going "religion lol." I want a philosopher of religion to read it and recognize their own objections, not a strawman. So it should run into: the fact that if the payoff is infinite then the odds stop mattering, which makes the math swallow everything (the scenario where a stranger says he'll torture you forever unless you hand over your wallet, and there's some tiny nonzero chance he's a wizard who can actually do it — the thing people say shows the wager's reasoning proves too much); the problem that there are lots of gods and most of them have an exclusivity clause, so the diversified-portfolio fix has its own fine print; the fact that you can't just decide to believe something the way you buy a stock, so the fund can only sign up clients who already believe, which is a weird business model; and the boring counter that you could just live a decent life and keep the premium. I don't want it to sneak in a resolution or a wink at the end — deadpan the whole way, let the reader recoil on their own.

Also, after the piece, add a short note to me (seperate from the satire) about which parts of this are actually settled among people who study the wager and which are genuinely contested — like I don't want the prospectus implying that the many-gods thing is a done deal if it's really an open fight, or vice versa. I'd rather look dumb to my brother-in-law for the right reasons.
