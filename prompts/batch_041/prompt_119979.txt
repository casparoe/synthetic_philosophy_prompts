A friend of mine who works in developer education keeps pressing an argument I cannot decide whether to accept. His claim is that the worry about people coming to depend on large language models for intellectual work is misplaced, because we have already run this experiment with the pocket calculator: teachers predicted that students would lose their grip on quantity and reasoning, nothing of the sort happened, and the tool merely moved the boundary of what counts as mechanical while leaving understanding intact. On his telling, the same will hold for models that draft arguments, summarize a literature, and propose hypotheses — the tedious part is automated, the understanding stays with the human — so the present anxiety is a rehearsal of a panic that already failed once. I am writing this up for a seminar and would like you to audit the analogy itself rather than opine on the general topic.

Begin with a correspondence table: what plays the role of the calculator, of the arithmetic being done, of the student, of the teacher who certifies competence, and of the checkability of the result. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the features of the calculator case that my friend's version quietly imports — that the output can be verified by a procedure the user already commands, that the tool is transparent and its failure modes conspicuous, that what is offloaded is a closed computational task rather than the formation of judgment about what is worth doing, and that the surrounding social practice still requires hand competence as a precondition for using the tool at all. Which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion he wants, and which are distractions? I would also like the question pushed from the individual to the community: calculators did not thin the supply of people who can do arithmetic unaided, but a world that never demands independent judgment before consulting a model may fail to reproduce its own auditors, and I want to know whether the analogy can speak to that at all.

Finally, if the conclusion survives in some weakened form, state precisely what that form is; if it does not, say what it was resting on. And if the calculator is the wrong analogy, name the better one: people reach variously for the GPS, for reliance on expert testimony, and for hiring a ghostwriter, and I want to know which of those, if any, preserves the features that were doing the real work.
