I have been reading rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent blogs for the better part of a decade, and the argument I keep failing to settle in my own head is the arithmetic-versus-individual problem in animal welfare: whether the thing that matters is deaths per calorie — which is what makes the beef-versus-chicken comparison, the grass-fed-beef-versus-crops argument, and the field animals killed by crop cultivation all so uncomfortable — or whether that whole style of reasoning is a category error when applied to beings whose lives are precisely what is at stake. I have been asked to open a film club discussion of Bong Joon-ho's Okja, and I would like you to read the film through that lens. The super-pig programme is, on its face, the efficiency argument made flesh: twenty-six enormous animals, one crowned the winner and the rest sent to slaughter, a corporation pitching a single creature as feeding a great many people on less land and less feed. I want a reading that takes that seriously rather than treating it as mere satire, and that says where the film's own arithmetic actually lands.

Please work through the scenes that carry the weight rather than the film's general vibe. What does the contest structure — twenty-six competitors, one survivor, the winner's prize being simply that she is not eaten — do to the idea that welfare is a matter of numbers? What are we to make of the ALF implanting a recording device in Okja and letting her be recaptured, treating one particular animal as an instrument for a general good and deceiving the person who loves her about it? What about the ending, where a gold figurine buys exactly one life, a pair of pigs push a single newborn through the electric fence, and Mija then sits down to dinner while Okja looks on? I have taken to calling the film's apparent stance the arithmetic of affection — particular love winning a single round against an aggregate it cannot touch — and I want to know whether that is a genuine moral position the film dramatises, a failure of nerve, or a diagnosis of why the aggregate always wins. Where the film resists this reading, say so plainly; I would rather have the honest critic's concession than a tidy interpretation, and I would rather you not flatter either the film or the position.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I have no web access when I use this, so argue from the film and the ideas rather than pointing me at literature. Open with a single sentence stating what you think the film's position, if it has one, actually is, and then give me the reading. Hard limit: 700 words. Tell me how confident you are in the reading as a whole, and single out the one claim within it that you are least sure of.
