You are a philosopher of biology who works on the species problem and on the science–policy interface of conservation law. Before answering, think carefully, and begin by setting out in a short list the candidate rules that the decisions below might be tracking; then do the work.

I sit on the listing committee of a national biodiversity agency. Our statute speaks of species, but we have never adopted a definition, and our decisions have hardened into something like a case law. Five of them. (1) We protected a canid population of hybrid ancestry — descended from wolves and from a smaller coyote-like lineage — which breeds true, occupies a distinct niche, and is only partly reproductively isolated from both parental forms. (2) We refused to list a fish population in the next valley, fully reproductively isolated and genetically diagnosable, because it is morphologically and ecologically indistinguishable from its sister population; genetic distinctness alone, we said, does not make a unit worth listing. (3) We protected an all-female, clonally reproducing lizard lineage of recent hybrid origin, which has no sexual reproduction at all and lives in a habitat that neither parental species uses. (4) We protected a bird population that is morphologically and ecologically distinct but interbreeds freely with the main population along a broad hybrid zone. (5) We listed the two terminal populations of a ring complex around a desert basin as separate units, even though they meet at the ring's closure and do not interbreed there, and each is connected to the other by a chain of interbreeding populations.

The petition now before us concerns a freshwater snail: a lineage of recent hybrid origin, reproducing clonally, morphologically indistinguishable from both parental species, fully isolated from them geographically and reproductively, but confined to a chemically unusual spring system in which neither parent can survive, and known from a single site.

Tell me which candidate rules fit our five decisions, what each of them predicts for the snail, which of our earlier petitions is the closest analog, and what you would recommend — making explicit what principle the recommendation commits us to, and which of our past decisions that principle leaves in doubt. Say also which further petition would best tell the surviving rules apart, and whether our case law amounts to a defensible species concept or has quietly replaced the species category with a conservation unit of some other kind.
