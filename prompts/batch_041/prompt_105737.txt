I am trying to work through a difficult argument in John von Neumann's posthumously published lectures on computation and the brain, and I would like help understanding it. Because I do not want to risk misquoting, I have set out below my own paraphrase of the argument rather than the original wording; please treat it as a reconstruction and reason about the position it describes, not about the fidelity of my phrasing.

Von Neumann's position, as I reconstruct it, runs roughly as follows. The nervous system is a computing machine, and its elementary signals are all-or-nothing, so at the level of the single neuron the system is digital in character. Yet the brain does not work like a digital computer. A digital computer is built to execute long chains of precisely specified logical operations, each accurate to many decimal places. The brain's components are slow, imprecise, and unreliable, and the number of serial steps in any brain process appears small. So the brain must be doing something else: relying on redundancy, on the statistical properties of large populations of neurons, and on a code whose messages are less like discrete logical propositions than like statistical aggregates. From this he draws the conclusion that the language in which the nervous system represents and processes information is not the language of mathematics. He also notes that the brain's memory does not seem to be addressed the way a computer's is, and that its economy of storage and access is different.

What I cannot get straight is how to hold three claims together: that the brain is a computing machine; that its elementary signals are digital; and that its language is not the language of mathematics. Each sounds plausible alone, but together they threaten either to collapse into triviality or to trade on an ambiguity.

Please help me as follows. First, distinguish at least three senses in which one might speak of the language of mathematics — the notation of formal logic, the language of exact arithmetic, and the language of probability and statistics — and say, for each, whether von Neumann's claim comes out substantive and what it would commit him to. Second, say whether he is best read as holding that the brain is an analog computer, a digital computer, or a hybrid, and how the reliability-and-redundancy argument bears on that question. Third, consider whether the claim is about the brain, about the limits of our present mathematics, or about a mismatch between the notation of a theory and the internal representations of the system that theory describes. I have been calling this last possibility the notation-mismatch reading; assess whether it is a fair reading of him, and whether it requires positing a mathematics we do not yet have. Finally, say whether his treatment of memory supports or undercuts that reading.
