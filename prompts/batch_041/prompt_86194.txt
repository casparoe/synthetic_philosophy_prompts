I am the mayor of Harborview, a mid-sized city that has become a flashpoint over a referendum scheduled for next week. The measure would extend voting rights in municipal elections to residents who have lived here for at least five years but are not citizens, including many people who will be sworn in as citizens tomorrow at the naturalization ceremony where I am to give the keynote address. The ceremony is tomorrow morning, and the audience will be a genuinely mixed room: newly naturalized citizens and their families, long-time residents who already vote, a contingent of activists who support the referendum, a contingent who oppose it, and a few local officials who would rather I say nothing controversial at all. I was a philosophy major in college, and the thing I cannot stop thinking about is the boundary problem: any vote on who belongs to the demos seems to presuppose an answer to who belongs to the demos, since the electorate that decides the question is itself the body whose boundaries are in question. In Harborview the circle is especially tight, because the referendum would decide whether a group of residents may vote on future referendums, and some of those residents are in the room tomorrow being welcomed into a different kind of membership altogether.

Please write the speech I should give. It should run about seven to nine minutes, be suitable for reading aloud, and be addressed to the whole room rather than to any one faction. I want the boundary problem to do real structural work in the speech, not to be a decorative allusion: it should shape what I can and cannot say, including why I cannot simply declare that we are all one people already and why I cannot pretend the referendum's legitimacy is obvious. At the same time, the speech has to be a genuine welcome to the people being naturalized; it cannot leave them feeling that their new citizenship is being used as a pawn in a local fight. You may give me a way of framing the circle that is honest about its difficulty without turning the ceremony into a seminar, and you may end with a position on the referendum if you think a mayor can responsibly take one, but if you do, the position must follow from the structure of the problem rather than from a generic appeal to inclusion or exclusion. Do not include citations or a reading list; I need prose I can deliver.
