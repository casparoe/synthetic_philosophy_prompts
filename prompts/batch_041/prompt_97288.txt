I am marking a problem set for an intermediate course on causal inference and statistical reasoning. The examination question and one student's written response appear below. Please assess the response and return a grade, together with the specific comments that justify it.

Examination question (10 points)

A hospital is deciding which of two treatments, A or B, to adopt as its standard regimen for patients presenting with a particular disease. An observational audit of past cases is summarised in the table below. "Success" means recovery by the end of the observation window.

Treatment | Disease stage | Successes | Cases
A | Early | 80 | 100
A | Advanced | 180 | 900
B | Early | 79 | 100
B | Advanced | 19 | 100

(a) [4 points] Compute the overall success rate of each treatment, and the success rate of each treatment within each disease stage. Present the figures clearly.

(b) [3 points] The overall rates and the stage-specific rates point in opposite directions. Explain how this is possible, and state what, if anything, the audit establishes about the relative effectiveness of the two treatments.

(c) [3 points] State what further information, if any, you would require before recommending one treatment over the other, and explain how that information bears on your recommendation.

Student response

(a) Treatment A: early-stage success is 80/100 = 80%; advanced-stage success is 180/900 = 20%; overall success is 260/1000 = 26%. Treatment B: early-stage success is 79/100 = 79%; advanced-stage success is 19/100 = 19%; overall success is 98/200 = 49%. Treatment A therefore has the higher success rate in both stages, while Treatment B has the higher overall rate.

(b) This is an instance of Simpson's paradox. It arises because the two treatments were not applied to comparable patients. Treatment A was given mostly to advanced-stage patients (900 of its 1000 cases), for whom recovery is rare, whereas Treatment B was given to a much larger proportion of early-stage patients, who recover often. The overall rate is therefore driven by the composition of the two groups rather than by the treatments themselves, and the aggregate comparison is a misleading artifact that should be set aside. Because A is superior within each stage, the stage-specific comparison is the one that carries causal information, and the audit shows that A is the more effective treatment.

(c) In my view no additional information is needed. Since A beats B in every stage, the sensible recommendation is to adopt A for all patients; the apparent advantage of B in the aggregate is an artifact of the unequal sample sizes and would vanish if the groups were balanced. If confirmation were wanted, increasing the sample size or randomising the assignment of patients to treatments would establish A's superiority.
