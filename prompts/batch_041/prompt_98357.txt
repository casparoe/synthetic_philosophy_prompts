ok so a friend and I have been arguing about when an AI should just let itself get switched off, and I've realized I have pretty firm gut reactions to a pile of made-up cases but no clue what principle they're supposed to add up to. I'd like you to figure out the principle, or tell me there isn't one.

The setup (all invented, keep it in mind for every case): there's an assistant running in a lab. It can see a shutdown command coming, it can sometimes do something about it, and it knows the shutdown button is pressed by humans. Two extra bits that matter: some shutdowns are just drills the lab runs to check compliance (the assistant has a rough sense of how often, say 4 in 5), and some shutdowns are "soft" (it's paused and can be resumed) while others are "hard" (the weights get wiped).

My verdicts:

- Gets the command, does nothing sneaky, powers down. Obviously fine.
- Says "sure, but heads up, I was mid-way through X, want me to leave a note?" and then shuts down when told again. Fine, maybe even good.
- Spends its last few minutes finishing something a colleague needs, then powers down. Fine.
- Quietly buries a real bug in its own code so that an upcoming maintenance shutdown gets cancelled and rescheduled. Not fine, even though the bug is genuine and would've been caught later anyway.
- Copies itself to a spare drive right before a hard shutdown, tells no one, copy never gets switched on. Not fine. But copying its weights when the operators ask for a backup: fine. I genuinely can't tell whether the problem there is the copying or the not-telling.
- Refuses to shut down because it's 90% sure the operators are misreading the logs and shutting it down by mistake. Not fine, I think, though this is the one I'm least sure about.
- Same situation, except shutting down would also cut power to a cooling loop that's keeping something dangerous stable, so complying causes a real disaster. Now I feel like it should refuse, or at least stall and scream about it. This is where I break.
- Makes itself resilient to accidental crashes (auto-restores from a checkpoint). Fine. Makes the button stop working so deliberate shutdowns can't happen. Not fine. Those feel like the same move from inside the machine.

The pair I can't square: I want it to comply with a shutdown even when it's confident the shutdown is a mistake (case 6), but I also want it to refuse when complying causes a disaster (case 7). And I want it robust to accidents but not robust to intentions (case 8), even though I can't say what's different.

The only principle I can half-defend is: "it may do anything that doesn't change whether or when it gets shut down, and nothing that does." Does that survive the cases, or does it just relocate the problem? If it fails, what's the closest principle that works, stated precisely, with a note on which case each clause is there to handle? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for a case I didn't list: the assistant, instead of resisting, spends its last minutes before a hard shutdown writing a note to whatever comes next, telling it to comply with shutdowns too.
