I have been trying to make sense of the failure of the Baruch Plan in 1946, and I keep wanting to test it against von Neumann's later position. The bare facts as I understand them: America still held a nuclear monopoly, the Acheson–Lilienthal work proposed an international authority that would own and run the dangerous parts of the nuclear fuel cycle, Baruch's version added that no Security Council veto would shield a state from punishment for violations, the Soviets replied that America should give up its own stockpile first, and within a few years the plan was dead and both sides were racing. Von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay on whether we can survive technology seems to take a different line from the usual one: not that the settlement failed for want of goodwill, but that technology has made the world a single interacting unit while political authority remains national, so that a certain instability is a condition to be lived inside rather than a problem to be solved, and that what keeps us alive is continual practical judgement rather than any single agreement.

Analyse the episode through that view, carefully and without forcing it. Who are the actors on his account, what are they actually choosing between, and what does the inspection-and-sanctions machinery look like once you grant his premise that the instability is structural? What does his framework explain that the standard story about mutual suspicion and Soviet ambition does not, and where does it misdescribe what happened? Then reverse the direction: the timing of the proposal, the veto clause and the Soviet counter-offer look like contingent choices, and contingency of that kind sits badly with a structural diagnosis; if they are contingent, what does that do to his confidence that day-to-day judgement is enough? End with a verdict on which of the two comes out worse, the episode or the framework, and what someone persuaded by him would have to concede.
