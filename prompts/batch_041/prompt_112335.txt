For the past term I have been sitting in on a reading group devoted to transitional justice — we have spent most of our time on the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the Argentine amnesties of the 1980s and their subsequent invalidation, and the Rwandan gacaca courts — and I have come away with the strong impression that the central argument, the one about whether amnesty is a betrayal of victims or the necessary price of peace, has simply stopped moving. The two camps are by now entirely familiar to one another. One side, which I shall call the realists, holds that a negotiated transition is possible only if the outgoing regime is offered a way to leave the table without a rope round its neck; that amnesty is therefore a tragic necessity; and that the moral cost, though real, is outweighed by the killings that would otherwise occur. On this view amnesty is a wrong we choose in order to avert a greater one, and the honest thing is to say so plainly rather than dress it up. The other side, the justice camp, holds that amnesty entrenches impunity and so compounds the wrong already done to victims by denying them the one thing the state now owes them; and, crucially, that the empirical premise doing the work here is far weaker than the realists suppose, since some transitions have prosecuted afterwards and survived, and the existence of international tribunals has altered what is and is not negotiable. When pressed, each side treats the other's favourite case as an awkward exception to be explained away — the realists discount Argentina, the justice camp discounts South Africa — and nobody's position has shifted since October.

My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that this is neither an empirical disagreement about the causal relation between accountability and the termination of conflict nor a straightforward moral disagreement about how to weigh a certain number of victims against a certain number of lives, but a disagreement about what the word 'justice' is doing in the sentence. One side appears to use it to name something owed, retrospectively, to identifiable individuals, in which case amnesty is plainly a wrong and the only live question is whether we may commit it; the other appears to use it to name a property of a political order going forward, in which case amnesty may be not a cost paid out of some moral budget but something closer to a constitutive act — part of what makes the new order's claim to authority valid at all. If that is right, then the two sides are not disagreeing about the facts or the values so much as answering two different questions under a single heading, rather as people who ask what we owe the dead and people who ask what we owe the living might talk past one another indefinitely. A related possibility, perhaps the same point in different clothes, is that the whole debate trades on a conflation of amnesty (a juridical act, a status the state confers) with impunity (a social and political condition), so that a society might grant the former while still securing something recognisable as accountability by other means — truth-telling, reparations, lustration, public memory — and, conversely, might prosecute a few scapegoats while leaving the powerful untouched.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, working from what each side actually maintains rather than from a survey of the literature: a hidden empirical question that one side has settled prematurely; a divergence in the standards of evidence each side demands; a verbal dispute about the words 'justice' and 'amnesty'; or two groups asking different questions under one name. If it is the last, say precisely which two questions, and whether either side could be brought to see that it is answering only one of them. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a case both sides must accommodate, a formal result, or nothing at all, because the disagreement rests on something neither side can argue for, such as what a political community is ultimately for. I would far rather have a clear negative answer than an optimistic one. Please write your reply in British English.
