My brother, a software engineer, has been reading a popular book on Gödel and the mind ever since our father was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's, and it has become something of a consolation to him. He now summarises the Lucas–Penrose argument as follows: 'Gödel proved that in any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic there is a true sentence the system cannot prove. A human mathematician, however, can always see that this sentence is true by stepping outside the system. So the human mind cannot be any such formal system. Since a computer is just a formal system, no computer can match the mind; and because Penrose showed that this gap requires quantum processes in the brain, artificial intelligence of the kind people dream about is impossible.'

He is not a philosopher and neither am I, but something about this seems too quick, and I should like to know how much of it a philosopher of logic or mind would accept. Please go through it claim by claim: what is a fair statement of the theorem, what is a standard simplification, and what is actually wrong or at least unsupported? In particular, I want to understand whether the conclusion that the mind is not a machine follows from the parts that are true, what work the phrase 'can always see' is doing, whether the argument needs only the first incompleteness theorem or also the second, and whether the appeal to quantum mechanics is part of the argument or a separate speculation. Please also say what a defender of the Lucas–Penrose line would reply, and whether the final claim about AI is an exaggeration of what the argument at its best establishes. Please answer in American English.
