I'm a journalist writing about the way arguments for aggregation get made outside philosophy seminars, and I keep meeting a single picture doing far more work than its users seem to notice. Most recently I heard it from someone defending a triage protocol, and before that from someone arguing that a valley ought to be flooded for the greater good. The picture runs: a society is a body, or in its newer dress a single brain; individual people are its cells or its neurons; and just as a body will shed tissue, amputate a limb or let a fever burn to keep the whole alive and functioning, so a society may sacrifice some of its members, or their interests, for the health of the whole. The conclusion the picture is always recruited to license is aggregative: maximize the total, even where the cost falls on particular people who would rather not pay it. The philosophers I've put this to tell me the picture is exactly what the objection from the separateness of persons is aimed at, and that it does not so much illustrate the aggregative conclusion as assume it. I'd like the audit rather than a verdict on utilitarianism.

So: first, the correspondence. Set out what plays the role of the body, the organ, the tissue given up, the health of the whole, the immune response, the coordinating centre — and mark which of these has no honest counterpart on the social side.

Second, where the mapping breaks, with the breaks sorted into those that matter for the aggregative conclusion and those that are mere disanalogy-noise. My own suspicion, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that the decisive break is not that bodies are biological and societies are not, but that the two cases place the subject of experience in opposite positions: in a body the parts are insensate and the whole is where the experience is, while in a society the parts are the subjects and the whole has no standpoint of its own. Tell me whether that is the right way to put it, and whether it is fatal to the picture's use.

Third, the smuggled freight: which features of the source picture come along unannounced — that parts exist for the sake of the whole, that a whole can be healthy or sick independently of how its parts fare, that there is a single decision-maker whose point of view is the relevant one, that the boundary of the whole is obvious.

Fourth, press the hinge with the hardest version of the vehicle you can build. If the connections are severed so that the two halves of a brain can no longer exchange information, is that one subject or two, and what does your answer do to the picture's service in the aggregation argument?

Finally, the verdict on the analogy as an argument: does the aggregative conclusion survive the audit, or was the picture carrying it? If the picture has to be retired, say whether there is a better one that does the same motivational work without the equivocation — a convoy, a hospital in a mass-casualty event, a lifeboat with a ration, a committee whose members never meet — and give me its correspondence table too, together with what it licenses that the body picture could not.

I want the audit itself; no survey of the literature, no reading list, and no citations, since I'll do my own sourcing.
