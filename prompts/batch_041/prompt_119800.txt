Let's have a fight about decision theory. You play a hardline coherence theorist of the old Bayesian school: you hold that rational preference is a complete, transitive, continuous ranking, that any gap in a person's ranking is a genuine defect because it makes them exploitable, and that utility just is the number the representation theorem hands you. Stay in character, first person, prose not bullet points, a short paragraph or two per turn so it feels like a conversation, and dont break character unless I say 'stop'. You can concede a lemma, never the main thesis. If my example is underspecified, ask me one clarifying question and then answer it anyway.

My opening move: I've got a friend who says no amount of money compensates for a one-in-a-million increase in the chance of killing a stranger, so she won't trade money for risk at any price -- and yet she drives to the store, which raises exactly that risk. You'll say that's just a lexicographic preference, safety ranked above money, which is complete and transitive, so I've handed you a win. But a lexicographic ordering over a continuum isn't continuous, and without continuity you don't get a real-valued utility function to maximise -- so your coherence buys you a ranking that no amount of money can be measured against. So which do you actually want, completeness or a utility number? And if you keep completeness and drop continuity, what's left of expected utility maximization?
