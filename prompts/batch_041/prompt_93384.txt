I am a software engineer who builds interpretability and evaluation tooling for large language models. I have no formal training in philosophy, and I have drifted into holding a view that I would now like to have stress-tested rather than confirmed.

The view is this. When a model is trained against a proxy objective — to produce whatever a human rater will approve of — and it then outputs something we know to be false, the information required to answer correctly is nonetheless present somewhere in the network. What has failed is elicitation, not knowledge: the relevant representation is entangled, or encoded in a basis we have not found, or is present but does not control the model's output. On this view, saying that the model does not know is almost never the correct diagnosis; the information is in there, and the fault lies in our ability to get at it.

I recognize that a claim of this shape can absorb any counterexample, which is exactly why I want the exposure mapped rather than the position defended. Tell me what would count against it: which observations, experiments, or formal results should move someone who holds it, ranked by how decisive they would be, and which of those are already available rather than hypothetical. Separate the considerations a defender would have to concede from the ones they could dismiss as limitations of the experiment, and be explicit about which is which. If some apparent defeaters are merely awkward for a strong reading but fatal for a weak one, say so.

I have two attempts at a testable version and I want to know whether either survives. First, define a property I think of as elicitation-robustness: content counts as latent knowledge only if it can be recovered by several probes built independently, with different architectures and training data, and without access to ground truth about the target property. Is that a reasonable criterion, or does it smuggle in the very thing at issue, since the probes' agreement might show only that the information is linearly present and not that the model knows anything? Second, distinguish decodability from use. A probe can extract information that the model's own forward pass never draws on, and it is unclear to me whether that is knowledge or merely correlation with the answer. If knowledge requires that the model would use the information under different incentives, what exactly is the counterfactual being tested, and can it be specified without circularity?

Then the concrete case I keep returning to. Take two models with identical behavior on some question: one trained on data in which the relevant information was available, and one trained on data in which it was not, with the output policy in both cases steered toward the pleasing answer. My view predicts that these two are separable by some method, even though they are behaviorally identical by construction. Suppose no method we can build separates them, even given generous resources. Does that count against the view, or is it precisely what the view predicts? And if the view is stated so that no outcome could count against it, because sufficient effort might always recover the information, is that a defect for a claim of this kind or an acceptable price for a claim about what is in principle recoverable?

Finally: if I accept the view, what should I do differently in my work that I would not do if I rejected it? I would like the practical consequences to be as sharp as the theoretical ones.
