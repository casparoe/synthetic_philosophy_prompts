Last night a friend and I argued about testimony until neither of us could restate the other's position fairly, and I would rather end with a number than another round. The claim I want your credence in is this: for ordinary adult human hearers, anti-reductionism is the correct account of testimonial justification, meaning that a hearer is prima facie justified in accepting what a speaker asserts, absent specific grounds for doubt, without needing positive non-testimonial reasons to think that speaker, or speakers of her kind, reliable. My credence is about 0.35; my friend's is about 0.85, largely on the strength of the argument that the reductionist standard would leave nearly everything we believe unjustified, since no one can audit the testimonial chain behind even a fraction of their beliefs. I replied that this shows only that we have tacit grounds, not that none are required, and we went in circles from there.

Please give me your own figure, and give it as a decomposition rather than a verdict: the sub-questions the number actually depends on, a rough probability for each, how they combine, and which one the final figure is most sensitive to. The sub-questions I take to be doing the work — though tell me if I have misidentified them — are these. First, whether the two views are genuine contraries at all, or whether the reductionist is answering a question about the structure of justification while the anti-reductionist is answering one about the default attitude a hearer may take, in which case the question of which account is correct may be underdetermined rather than merely disputed. Second, whether entitlement, warrant without evidence, is a genuine epistemic status or a redescription of the fact that we cannot help believing what we are told. Third, whether ordinary hearers in fact possess the tacit inductive grounds the reductionist requires, and, if they do, whether that vindicates reductionism or merely shows that anti-reductionism's conditions happen to be met in the ordinary case. Fourth, what weight to give the sheer scale of testimonial dependence: nearly all of what any of us believes rests on testimony we could not have checked, which may tell in favor of a default warrant, or may be a psychological fact with no epistemic force. Fifth, scope: whether a default warrant extends to informants of a kind the hearer has never tested — the first machine, the first member of a community whose reliability no one has had occasion to assess — and, if it does not, whether anti-reductionism needs a restriction that quietly returns it to reductionism for precisely the cases where the two views seem to differ. Sixth, whether the dispute survives being recast in probabilistic terms at all, as a disagreement about the prior a hearer assigns to a speaker's reliability rather than about the structure of justification.

Then tell me which of those sub-questions you are least confident about, what evidence or argument would move it, and — since my friend and I are the ones who need it — which sub-question our 0.35 and 0.85 most likely differ over.
