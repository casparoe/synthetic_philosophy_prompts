I am a doctoral candidate working on algorithmic accountability, and I am trying to use the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision of AI systems as an analytic frame rather than as a slogan. For a chapter on the limits of that distinction, I want to work through one real episode in detail: the Australian automated welfare-debt scheme commonly known as Robodebt.

Treat the following as stipulated, so that the analysis does not turn on contested details. From around 2015, Centrelink, later Services Australia, used an automated process to compare income that welfare recipients had reported with annual income data held by the tax office. Annual income was averaged to yield an estimate of fortnightly income; where the estimate exceeded what the recipient had reported, a debt was raised automatically. The recipient, not the agency, bore the burden of showing the estimate was wrong. The method was later held to be unlawful; the scheme was abandoned, a large class action was settled, and a royal commission subsequently found the scheme unlawful and condemned the conduct of those responsible. The scheme was rule-based automation, not machine learning, and I want you to keep that in mind and tell me at the end whether it matters.

What I want is a two-directional analysis.

First, theory onto case. Distinguish process-based supervision, meaning oversight of the design, legal basis, testing, documentation, sign-off, and change-control pipeline through which a system is built and deployed, from outcome-based supervision, meaning monitoring of the measured results of the system in operation: error rates, appeals and reversals, distributional impact, recovered revenue, harm reports. Then say precisely which of the two actually operated in this episode, at which points, and what each was monitoring. I am interested in whether the process machinery was formally complete but substantively empty; whether the operative outcome metric was the agency's own throughput rather than the system's correctness; and what the scheme would have had to measure in order to supervise itself by outcomes, in particular where ground truth about wrongly raised debts would have had to come from and who in fact supplied it. Note the selection problem: which errors generated a signal at all, and which were structurally invisible.

Second, case onto theory. What does the episode show about the distinction itself? I am inclined to think it exposes a third mode that the standard dichotomy obscures, in which error discovery is delegated to the supervised population at that population's own cost, but I want you to test that, and to say whether the more fundamental axis is process versus outcome at all, or rather the independence and information access of whoever supervises, together with the allocation of the burden of proof. Address these specifically: whether process compliance could ever have caught the failure, given that the process and the metric were designed by the same institution; whether outcome-based supervision collapses into self-reporting when the deployer selects the outcome; what role external adversarial channels such as courts, the ombudsman, journalism, and a royal commission played, and whether any equivalent channel exists for AI systems; and whether after-the-fact blame allocation is a form of supervision or a substitute for it.

Finally, the disanalogy. Robodebt involved transparent deterministic rules, no learned generalization, no distribution shift in the machine-learning sense, and a statutory welfare power over a captive population. Say honestly which parts of your analysis survive the transfer to systems that are opaque, learned, and privately deployed, and which parts depend on features this case does not have. Where the process-versus-outcome frame is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
