I'm a fourth-year PhD student in early modern history, and for the past year I've spent two days a week at a small political-risk firm. My dissertation is on the longevity of composite monarchies, which has led the firm to treat me as its house expert on historical base rates — flattering, and probably a mistake.

The firm sells written assessments: about a dozen a month, each answering a client question of the form "what is the probability that X happens to polity Y within Z years" — a violent leadership transition, a constitutional rupture, a secession, a default that triggers one of the above. Each assessment goes out with a point probability and a stated range; my supervisor, a former intelligence analyst, signs off; clients allocate capital on the strength of these, so the difference between 15% and 40% is real money. Turnaround is three days, and the analyst who does most of the work has a history MA and one regression course.

The wall I keep hitting is the reference class. Here is the draft I've been running, which I'd like you either to repair into something a junior analyst can run unsupervised or to tell me can't be repaired:

1. Restate the client's question as a dated, resolvable proposition, and fix the outcome coding before looking at any cases.
2. Code the target polity on a fixed set of dimensions (regime form, succession mechanism, revenue base, elite fragmentation, recent instability).
3. Pull the subset of our in-house dataset — a few hundred hand-coded polity-spells, roughly half pre-1900 empires and half post-1945 states — that matches the target on as many dimensions as possible.
4. Report the frequency of the outcome in that subset as the base rate.
5. Widen the class, dropping dimensions in a fixed order, until n reaches thirty.
6. Adjust the result by the inside view and report the adjusted figure with a range.

The failure modes I've already noticed, in case they matter to your repair: the nearest class is often tiny; widening restores n but destroys similarity, and I can't tell whether the dimensions I'm dropping last are the ones that carry the causal weight; the historical half of the dataset includes entities held together by dynastic accident, and I don't know whether those are exchangeable with a modern rentier state or belong to a different class entirely; the chronicle record is itself selected on the outcome, since disruptions get written about and quiet centuries don't; and clients only ever ask about places already in the news, which I suspect makes our whole forecast record a biased sample. Finally, the client always wants a number, and sometimes the honest output seems to be that there is no forecast to give.

What I want from you is a procedure, not a lecture on the reference class problem. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a refusal to forecast, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts — in particular, how much of the client's decision the firm is entitled to make on the client's behalf. Then run it on three cases: (a) a personalist monarchy in a rentier economy facing its first contested succession in fifty years, with no informative precedent; (b) a parliamentary democracy where a coup attempt recently failed and the outside view and the inside view point in opposite directions; and (c) a case whose nearest reference class has eleven members, seven of which are the same empire at seven different dates. At least one of those should come out as a refusal to forecast, and I expect to dislike which one.

Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic. And please push back rather than perform: if the honest conclusion is that base rates cannot do this work and the exercise mostly launders narrative judgment into the appearance of a number, say so plainly and tell me what the firm should do instead — including, if that's your view, that we should stop selling point probabilities.
