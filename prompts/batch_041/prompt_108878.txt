A colleague on our foundation's grant-allocation committee has a favourite analogy, and I would like it audited rather than either endorsed or waved away. Our committee cannot agree on the moral framework that should govern our allocations: some of us reason in a broadly consequentialist way about lives improved per dollar, others hold that certain ways of treating people are off the table no matter how the totals come out, and a third group suspects that at least one of these frameworks is not so much false as mis-framed. My colleague regards this disagreement as a distraction from the real work. His analogy is the engineer's: two instruments of unequal reliability are returning different readings of the same physical quantity. Nobody supposes the engineer must first settle which instrument is the true one; she weights the readings by their reliabilities and takes the weighted value. So, he says, with us: assign credences to the rival moral theories, weight their recommendations accordingly, and act on the weighted result. He takes the analogy to license four things — that we should maximize expected choiceworthiness; that the question of which theory is correct can be set aside as irrelevant to what we do; that no further work is required on how the theories' verdicts are to be compared with one another; and that the only genuinely open question is the calibration of our credences. Being an actuary by training, he adds that our case is one of parameter risk rather than model risk: we know the shape of the problem and are merely uncertain about a number, whereas model risk would be not knowing which family of models generates the numbers at all. When pressed, he retreats to a second analogy, that of a portfolio hedged across assets whose returns cannot be placed on a common scale.

What I want is an audit of the first analogy. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the instrument, the reading, the quantity measured, the reliability, the weighting rule — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Separate the breaks that matter for the four conclusions he draws from the ones that are merely decorative. In particular, I want to know what the engineer's situation supplies that ours may lack: whether the weighting of readings is a theorem or a stipulation; what must be true of the two instruments for it to be justified; whether the quantity being measured exists independently of the engineer's decision about what to do, and whether anything in our case corresponds to that independence; and whether any moral theory actually satisfies the conditions the analogy needs. Then tell me whether he is right that ours is parameter risk and not model risk, and what turns on the answer. Finally, say whether the hedge analogy is a genuine improvement or merely relocates the difficulty, and whether his four conclusions survive the disanalogies at all — if some survive in weakened form, say which, and in what form. If neither comparison can carry the weight, say what a better one would look like.

I would rather not have a tour of the literature on moral uncertainty; I want to know what this comparison can carry, what it cannot, and where the weight of his conclusion is actually resting.
