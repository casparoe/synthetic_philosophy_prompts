I've spent enough time in the alignment-adjacent parts of the internet to notice something philosophically loaded in how process-based and outcome-based supervision actually get used, and I'd like you to reconstruct the view they presuppose. Here is the cluster of practices as I understand it. A lab training a model on a math or coding task will sometimes score it on the final answer alone and sometimes reward it step by step, giving credit for each intermediate move that a separate reward model judges sound. The community treats a model that lands the right answer through a sloppy, luck-dependent, or plainly bad chain as a defect to be corrected, yet treats a model that reasons beautifully and then fumbles the last line as a near-miss that the process mostly got right. The same labs decide whether to ship a model almost entirely on outcomes -- benchmark scores, win rates -- and treat the model's written chain of thought as a window onto its reasoning that may or may not faithfully represent whatever computation actually produced the answer, so that a clean-looking trace can be penalized even when it isn't the trace the model truly used.

What conception of evaluation and agency does this presuppose? More sharply: is the practice committed to the view that the reasoning is where the model's agency really lives and the answer is mere downstream evidence, or is it fully consistent with a hard instrumentalism on which the process is only a more sample-efficient proxy for the outcomes we actually care about? Which of these does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Is the cluster internally coherent, given that it trains on process while deploying on outcomes, and given that scoring a step as "sound" seems to smuggle in an outcome judgment one level down? And if you swapped the implicit view for a thoroughgoing outcome-based theory of credit -- blame and credit attach only to results -- what in the practice would have to change?
