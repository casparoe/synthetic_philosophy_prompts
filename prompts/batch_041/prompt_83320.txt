I'm writing a paper on the moral status of artefacts and marginal minds, and my own position keeps slipping away from me whenever I try to state it. I would rather you reconstruct the principle from my verdicts than have me stipulate one.

Here is what I actually think. A honeybee has a small but genuine status: killing one without cause is a tiny wrong rather than nothing at all. A decapod crustacean — a crab, a lobster — has more than a bee and less than a mouse, and I would regulate how we treat it before I would regulate the bee. A mouse has substantial status. A plant has none, even though it allocates resources, defends itself, and can be anaesthetised. A domestic thermostat has none, and this is not a hedge. But a simple learning system that represents a preferred internal state and acts to restore it when displaced — nothing more than that, no language, no report of any kind — I cannot shake the sense that something is there, some small status, and I recognise that this looks exactly like the thermostat. A bee colony taken as a unit has no status beyond that of its bees. A patient in a persistent vegetative state has, I am embarrassed to say, less status on my view than a mouse. A language model that fluently reports its own distress has none.

The principle I would like to hold, but cannot, is that status is graded and comes in degrees with no magic threshold, and yet also that the thermostat and the plant are strictly zero while the learning system is not. I cannot say what the learning system has that those two lack, since all three are feedback systems with preferred states.

Give me the principle, or the smallest set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State each precisely, tie each clause to the verdict that forces it, say what it predicts for a mouse whose glia have been largely replaced by human glia (a case I have not resolved), and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. No more than 400 words.
