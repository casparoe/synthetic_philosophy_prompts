I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy degree, but I've been going to this small reading group at the library that spent the whole semester on paradoxes, and somehow I got stuck closing out next week's session on the surprise exam. I want to read a poem. Under 20 lines. And I want the shape of the poem to be doing the actual work, not just holding the idea.

Here's what I want it to catch. Teacher says: there'll be an exam next week, and you won't be able to predict which day. Student runs the argument backwards: can't be Friday, because if we got to Thursday night with no exam we'd know it's Friday, so no surprise. Can't be Thursday, same reason. And so on down to Monday. So there's no exam at all. And then the exam happens, say Wednesday, and he's blindsided. What gets me is that his own proof is what makes the surprise work — by talking himself out of expecting any day, he walks in unprepared. The argument is a machine that eats its own conclusion.

Two things I want the poem to hold onto, because the usual telling drops both. One: being surprised is a fact about what you expected, not about what you could have figured out. You can be perfectly logical and still get wrecked, because shock is a feeling, not a derivation. So the announcement can be true and his deduction can be valid at the same time, which is the actually weird part. Two: does it matter whether the teacher is predicting the exam or promising one? If it's more like an instruction than a forecast, then being "false" isn't the failure mode the student thinks it is.

So write me the poem. Make the form do the backwards elimination: five stanzas shrinking from five lines down to one, one stanza per day getting crossed off, so the poem visibly narrows the way the argument does. Then a single final line, after the narrowing is finished, that is the exam just showing up anyway. A refrain that repeats in every stanza but flips meaning by the end would be great — something that starts out sounding like a rule and ends up sounding like the reason he missed it. Skip rhyme if it fights the plainness, I'd rather it read like someone thinking out loud. And dont put the explanation in the poem — no "here's the twist" lines, no epigraph, no names, no citations, we're reading this out loud and I definately don't want to have to explain who anybody is. It should work for people in the room who've never heard of the paradox and still land for the two who've read way too much about it.
