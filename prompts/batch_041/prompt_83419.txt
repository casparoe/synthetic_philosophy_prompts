I am trying to locate the real disagreement in a long-running argument about Malthus. My own credence that his central prediction is best classified as right but early — rather than as falsified, or as never having been a genuine prediction in the first place — is about 0.25. A colleague who works on long-run economic growth puts the same claim at about 0.7. I want your number, and more importantly the decomposition that produces it.

For concreteness, take as the candidate precisification of the prediction this: where food production is not being transformed by sustained technological or institutional change, population growth outruns the growth of subsistence, and the resulting shortfall is absorbed by rising mortality rather than by anticipatory restraint on fertility. Revise it if you think it is unfaithful to the substance of the argument, and say so explicitly, since the whole question turns on whether a faithful precisification exists.

State the proposition you are assigning your number to precisely enough that it could come out false, then decompose it into the sub-questions it rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each: whether a faithful precisification exists rather than a retreat to safety; whether the demographic and agricultural record of the past two centuries falsifies it or leaves it merely untested; whether the mechanism Malthus actually named has been operating, as against a mechanism that only resembles it after the fact; whether the resource constraint he presupposed is better treated as a moving technological frontier than as a fixed ceiling, and what that does to the prediction's content; and the meta-question of whether right but early is ever a legitimate epistemic status for a long-run prediction, or always a rescue in disguise. Then give the combination.

Two further things. First, I have a proposed test to separate legitimate deferral from rescue, and I want to know how much of your final number depends on accepting it: a deferral is legitimate only if the theory's other commitments are currently at risk — that is, only if it forbids something we could observe now, and we have not observed it. Assess whether Malthus's theory passes this test, and whether the test is too strong, since a theory whose only predictions are long-run may fail it without being unfalsifiable in principle. Second, tell me which single sub-question my 0.25 and my colleague's 0.7 most plausibly diverge over, and what evidence or argument would move your own number the most.
