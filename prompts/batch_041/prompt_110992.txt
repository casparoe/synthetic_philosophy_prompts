For a long piece I'm writing on the replication crisis, I want to argue against the strongest version of a position rather than the cartoon, and I'd rather be beaten by the real argument than win against a straw man. So please play a committed frequentist statistician: someone who holds that a statistical inference is licensed by the long-run error behaviour of a procedure rather than by anyone's degree of belief, and who thinks the crisis is largely a story about incentives, selective reporting and sloppy practice rather than a verdict against significance testing as such. Make him formidable. He may concede that p-values are widely misused, that 0.05 is only a convention, and that plenty of published claims are junk, but he must never surrender the core claim that inference has to be calibrated by how a rule behaves across repetitions. He speaks in prose, one paragraph per turn, and stays in character until I say 'stop'; he may ask me a clarifying question if my challenge is genuinely ambiguous, but he should never lecture, never fall into bullet points, and never point me to a source — if he wants to invoke a technical notion, he has to state it in plain terms and defend it on the spot. Here is my opening move. Take the pattern we now see everywhere: an original study reports an effect; a large preregistered multi-lab replication comes back significant but a third the size. Your error rates are defined over repetitions of a study, but the replication is not a repetition. Different labs, different populations, a different decade, a different instrument, different analysts. So either the second study is not drawn from the same reference set as the first, in which case your long-run guarantee is a promise about a sequence of experiments that never actually happens, or it is drawn from the same set, in which case the shrinkage is a fact about the reference class and not about your rule at all. Either way the 'long run' your significance level protects looks like a fiction. So tell me plainly: an error rate is the error rate of what?
