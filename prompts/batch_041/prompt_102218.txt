I write about biology and the philosophy of science for a monthly magazine, and for the past few years I have also taught a small upper-level seminar at a university nearby — the kind of course where the students are strong enough that they will happily recite a position back at you without ever having thought about a case. The session I am currently preparing is on the concept of biological function, and I want to set them a final essay question that the standard secondary literature cannot answer for them. They already know the two families of view: the etiological account, on which a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, so that function is a matter of history and a trait can malfunction; and the causal-role or dispositional account, on which a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system, so that function is a matter of present organisation and history is beside the point. What I do not want is a question they can pass by summarising those two sentences and listing the usual objections. I want them to have to decide what each account is actually committed to when the cases pull it in an inconvenient direction.

To that end I have written three short cases, and I want the question built on them. Case one: in a lineage of sediment bacteria, a protein we can call P37 was, in the ancestral line, a structural component of the flagellar motor, and was plainly under strong selection for that role; the descendant lineage has lost the flagellum altogether, but still expresses P37, and the only detectable effect of the protein now is that it loosely binds and sequesters a mildly toxic metabolic byproduct. There is no measured fitness difference when P37 is deleted, in the lab or in the field, and the current best judgment is that the gene simply has not been lost yet. Case two: a soil bacterium was engineered to grow on a synthetic carbon source and then passaged in the lab for some two thousand generations on that substrate, during which an existing enzyme we can call EgtA acquired a secondary activity that is now required for growth on the synthetic substrate; the selection here is real and strong, but it is artificial, a few years old, and confined to a population that would not persist outside the flask. Case three: a group of chemists has built a small artificial vesicle containing a designed catalyst and a designed membrane pump that together hold a pH gradient across the membrane; the system does what it does reliably, but it has no ancestry at all and does not reproduce. I would like the students to work out, for each case, whether the etiological account assigns a function, whether the causal-role account does, and what happens to each account's handling of malfunction — and then to say whether the disagreement between the two accounts is a substantive disagreement about nature or merely a recommendation about how to use a word.

Please write me the essay question itself, pitched so that a good student can answer it in sixty minutes and a weak one will be exposed within the first paragraph; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves a top answer must make — including at least one move about whether the etiological account requires natural selection specifically or any selection at all, and one about whether either account can be stretched to cover all three cases without quietly giving up its distinguishing claim. For each must-hit point give the marks it carries and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. No reading lists or citations are needed; I want the question to stand on its own.
