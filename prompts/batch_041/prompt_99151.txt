I am a graduate student in philosophy, and I have been drafted to lead the discussion in a seminar on population ethics, which has turned out to be a poor decision for my peace of mind: ever since I started reading in this area, the question of whether a much larger world of lives that are each barely worth living could be better than a smaller world of much better lives has followed me around, and I keep changing my mind about it. I want to walk into that session with one carefully built case rather than a dozen hand-waving ones, so I would like you to construct it for me. I need a two-option case, and I need the disagreement among the standard views to fall out of the numbers rather than being stipulated, because the entire point of the exercise is to show the students that the theories themselves do the work. Here is the specification, and I believe it is jointly satisfiable, though not easily: the two options must be related by mere addition in the strict sense, that is, the smaller population must be a subset of the larger one, so that no one who exists in the smaller option is made worse off in the larger one and no one is harmed by being brought in; the extra lives in the larger option must be clearly worth living, so that someone defending the larger option can say without strain that those additional people are glad to exist; total utilitarianism must rank the larger option strictly better; average utilitarianism must rank the smaller option strictly better; and a prior-existence or person-affecting view must treat the two options as on a par, in the sense that the larger is not worse than the smaller. I also want the case to be finite, to use ordinary human welfare levels rather than any exotic psychology, and to keep the arithmetic simple enough that I can put it on a single slide.

Beyond the four verdicts above, I want the case to do a second job. Once you have the scenario, run a critical-level view across a range of critical levels and show me how its verdict moves: at one end it should collapse into plain total utilitarianism, at the other it should agree with the average view, and you should identify the critical level at which it flips from one camp to the other, with the arithmetic that shows why. Then give me the full demonstration. For each of the four views, state the verdict and the reason, and make clear that the verdict is generated by the numbers rather than assumed. Identify the single feature of the case that is actually doing the work and explain why that feature, rather than the population sizes or anything else in the setup, is responsible for the split. Tell me what happens if I remove or reverse that feature, and in particular which of the views collapse into agreement once it is gone. Finally, tell me how the strongest defender of whichever view comes out looking worst would try to defuse the case, so that I can field that objection in the seminar rather than be blindsided by it.

One more constraint, because I have seen versions of this go wrong: I do not want the disagreement to be carried by inequality aversion or by any distributive grievance, and I do not want it to depend on the added people being harmed or wronged. The added lives must be ones the person-affecting view can honestly decline to count, not ones it can accuse the larger option of ruining. If the case only works because someone is treated unfairly or badly, it will not isolate what I am after, and I would rather you tell me that the specification cannot be met cleanly than hand me a case that smuggles in a second issue.
