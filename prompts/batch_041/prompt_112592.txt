I'm a novelist — third book under contract, first two did okay — and for reasons I no longer remember I sit on the reading committee of a small foundation that gives an annual prize for formally adventurous fiction. We meet a week from Thursday to settle the shortlist and pick a winner, and I've been handed the job of drafting the committee's written opinion on one entry, because I'm the one who flagged it and now everyone expects me to have a view. The entry is a novel that claims to write itself: there's a chapter that is essentially a program, and the author's note says that if you type it out and run it, it prints the entire book — title page to last line — and the program is what you've just read, so the novel contains, in plain text, the instructions for producing itself. She calls it a self-portrait of the text. Half the committee thinks it's a stunt, or worse: our rules require every entry to be the original work of the submitting author, and they say a book that writes itself isn't authored by anyone — she wrote the little machine that spits it out, which is a different and much smaller thing then writing a novel — and besides, none of us can verify the thing even runs without sitting down and running it, which is not what we signed up for. The other half thinks that's the whole point, that it's the most honest book on the list, and that a text whose subject is its own coming-into-being is exactly what the prize exists to reward.

Here's the part I keep circling and can't get past. There's that Escher lithograph where two hands are each drawing the other into existence, and the thing people always say about it is that the loop can't be the whole story — somewhere off the page there has to be a level that isn't itself being drawn, an artist outside the frame, or nothing would ever get started. Something Hofstadter calls the inviolate level, I think. And I can't tell which way that cuts for us. Does that outside level mean the self-writing book is a fraud, because the real author is always standing outside the frame doing the actual work? Or does it mean her position is just Escher's — that being outside the loop is precisely what it is to make one, so there's nothing counterfeit about the hands drawing each other and nothing disqualifying about the program? Please write the opinion I'll circulate before the meeting: a concrete recommendation on what we do with this entry and by when, the reasoning behind it, and an honest statement of the case against my recommendation — the strongest version of it, not a straw man I can knock over in the room. I'd also like you to tell me how confident you actually are in the recommendation you're giving, and to single out the one step in the argument you're least sure about, since I'd rather know where the soft spot is now than discover it in front of the committee. And please don't just tell me it's a rich question with merit on both sides; I need something I can actually defend at the table.
