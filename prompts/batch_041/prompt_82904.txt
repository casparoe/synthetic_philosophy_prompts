Quick question from a journalist who covers science and ideas and is in over her head on the philosophy.

A patchwork of city, state and EU rules now says that high-stakes automated decisions — hiring screens, benefits eligibility, credit scores — have to be audited for bias and, in some places, signed off by a human before they're final. Everyone treats the human-in-the-loop part as the obvious fix. But the whole reason those systems got built was to get the human's gut out of the loop, because the gut was biased and the rule at least treats like cases alike. So the human is the villian in one story and the safeguard in the other, and nobody seems bothered by the contradiction.

Here's what I keep chewing on. I've seen a word floating around — sphexishness. Some digger wasp stings a cricket, drags it to her burrow, goes inside to check for intruders, and if you nudge the cricket a couple of inches away while she's in there, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and checks again. Forever. The word gets used for anything that looks like it's deciding but is really running a script, and the uncomfortable part is the suggestion that we might be the same, just at a level of description we can't see from the outside.

So what is the human-in-the-loop rule actually committing us to? That a person can do something a machine structurally can't? Does that require free will in some strong sense, or is "flexible enough, and accountable" all the rule really needs? And if the reviewer turns out to be running a script too — a checklist, a quota, a quiet fear of being the one who signed off — is the practice a sham, or was it never about flexibility in the first place and only about having a name attached to the decision? I've been going back and forth on this alot. Tell me which parts of the practice are load-bearing, and what would have to change if we dropped the rigid-versus-flexible story entirely but kept the human.
