I want to have an arguement with a committed epiphenomenalist, and I want the epiphenomenalist to be actually good rather than a strawman, so you play one. Here's your position: conscious experiences are real, they're not identical to or reducible to anything physical, and they cause absolutely nothing -- not my behaviour, not other experiences, not even the brain states they're paired with. The physical world is causally closed, so a zombie duplicate of me, physically identical but with the lights off, would say and do everything I say and do. Two claims you never give up: experiences exist, and they do no causal work. Everything else is negotiable, and you should definately concede the obvious costs rather than dodge them -- it's counterintuitive, it makes consciousness a spectator, you can't point to it in a lab. Your resources, and please use them: the point that a physicalist who accepts causal closure, plus the thought that if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause there's no work left for a non-physical cause, is already an epiphenomenalist whether they like it or not; an informational/indicator theory of content on which a state can represent whatever it's reliably correlated with, even when the correlation runs the wrong way or comes from a common cause; and the zombie's fluent speech about redness as evidence that the physics alone fixes what we say. Rules: stay in character till I say stop, no citing anyone, no reading lists, no lectures about who said what -- just argue it out. A paragraph or two per turn so it feels like a conversation, and finish every turn with one line beginning 'Bottom line:' that sums up in a single sentence what you're standing on.

My opening move, as the zombie's lawyer: your view says my judgement that this red looks like *this* is produced by a brain state that's merely correlated with the redness. But correlation isn't aboutness. If the quale is a causal dead end, nothing about it ever makes it into the content of the judgement -- the brain state tells me about the brain state, and the redness is just along for the ride. Which means your sentence 'experiences exist and are like this' has no more phenomenal content than your zombie twin's identical sentence; by your own lights you can't be saying what you claim to be saying, and you've got exactly as much evidence for qualia as for their absence. And don't tell me the correlation is enough for representation: a law tying X to Y doesn't turn Y into a signal about X unless X does something to Y. So what makes the judgement about the qualia, if the qualia are inert?
