I am preparing a graduate seminar on visual epistemology and the unity of science, and I keep returning to Otto Neurath's picture-language program—the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics, later called Isotype, and its development in his book on the international picture language. I want to stage a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors who disagree about how far that program can be pushed. The first is a fictional former collaborator of Neurath's at the Social and Economic Museum: a designer-statistician who believes the picture language can be extended from a mere helping language for popular education into a genuine auxiliary language for the unified-science encyclopedia, one that could represent scientific claims without the ambiguities of ordinary verbal language. The second is a fictional philosopher of language who admires Neurath's fallibilism and anti-foundationalism but argues that pictures can at best serve as mnemonics for propositions that must already be stated in words; on this view, the stronger epistemological ambitions of the picture-language program are incoherent, because negation, quantification, modality, and hypothetical reasoning cannot be pictured without covertly reintroducing verbal syntax.

The dialogue should work through concrete cases rather than trading generalities. Let them argue over what happens in the transformation of a statistical table into repeated pictograms: does the transformation preserve inferential content or merely produce a memorable display? Let them test the system against a negative existential claim (for example, that a certain district has no cholera cases this month), a modal or dispositional claim (for example, that a proposed housing policy could reduce overcrowding by a certain percentage), and a claim involving vague or contested social categories of the sort Neurath treated as conglomerations. I would also like them to address whether Neurath's fallibilism about science is in tension with the standardization of a fixed symbol system, and whether the picture language can handle the kind of conceptual revision that the encyclopedia is supposed to accommodate. Each character should respond to the other's actual arguments, and neither should be a strawman: the designer should have real resources for handling some of the objections, and the philosopher should acknowledge where the picture language genuinely outperforms prose.

Please write the dialogue in a polished, formal register, and make it end with a definite verdict rather than leaving the issue open. I want one of the characters—or, if you prefer, a short closing exchange—to state clearly which position survives the exchange and why. If the dialogue has to take liberties with the historical Neurath's actual views or with the actual expressive capacities of Isotype, add a brief note at the end identifying those liberties. I am asking for a committed answer, not a neutral survey: make a choice about which side gets the better of the argument and defend it through the dialogue itself.
