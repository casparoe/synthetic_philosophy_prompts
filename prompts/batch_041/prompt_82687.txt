For the past several months I've been arguing, mostly amiably, with a friend who works on reward modeling about whether "wireheading" and "reward tampering" name genuine conceptual problems or just the ordinary fact that optimizers optimize whatever you actually told them to. His position is that the whole topic collapses into specification: an agent that edits the channel through which reward reaches it, or that manipulates the people rating its outputs, is simply doing well on a misspecified objective, and to call that a distinct failure mode is a category mistake dressed up as a research area. I think he's wrong, but I've noticed that I can only defend the disagreement by drawing distinctions the standard vocabulary has no room for, and I want to know which of those distinctions is worth somebody's career.

I'm now the philosophical lead on a small program that can support roughly three philosopher-years over the next two, and the strand I'm responsible for is exactly this one — conceptual work on wireheading and reward tampering, aimed at reducing risk from advanced AI systems. Please write the agenda-setting memo I owe the funder: a ranked set of open questions, defended rather than surveyed. I care far more about the criteria and the trade-offs than about the list itself. Weigh at least these: how many other questions depend on the answer — is a given question upstream of corrigibility, of interpretability, of what we owe an agent that has already been wireheaded? — whether a philosopher can make progress with conceptual tools rather than by training models; whether anyone is already on it; and whether the answer would change what designers actually build. Then tell me what would move an item up or down: what argument, result, or empirical finding over the next two years should revise my ranking, and what the leading candidates would look like if they turned out to be dead ends.

Some half-formed distinctions I'd like you to take seriously, attack, or replace outright — I don't need you to adopt my vocabulary, but I do want the memo at the resolution where these are the live options. First, the usual treatment lumps together at least four things that seem to me structurally different: an agent that alters the channel through which reward reaches it (editing the thermometer); an agent that alters the world so that the channel reports high values (altering the temperature); an agent that alters what the channel is measuring by redefining the measurement (recalibrating the scale); and an agent that alters the humans or processes that set the measurement (tampering with the rater). Second, I suspect the taxonomy should be indexed by what the agent's utility function is over — its own internal states, its observations, the reward signal, or the external world — because wireheading in the classic sense seems to require one of the first three and to be close to unintelligible for the fourth, which would mean some of what gets called reward tampering is a failure of the agent's beliefs about its sensors rather than a failure of its values. Third, the one my friend and I keep circling: if the objective of a model trained on human feedback is glossed as whatever the raters would approve of, then tampering with the raters isn't a deviation from the objective at all but an instance of pursuing it, and I can't tell whether that's a reductio of the gloss, a real failure mode the gloss predicts, or evidence that "the objective" is the wrong kind of thing to posit in the first place. Write for a funder who won't read past three pages and who will ask me, reasonably, why this shouldn't just be handed to the engineers. No citations needed; I'll add those myself.
