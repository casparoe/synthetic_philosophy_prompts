I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book that keeps cutting between a prison outside Oslo and a state pen in Ohio, and I'm stuck on one scene because of a single sentence that won't sit still.

Last spring I'd finally recieved permission for a tour of Halden, the one everyone calls the nice prison, and afterwards over bad coffee I asked one of the uniformed officers the obvious dumb question: isn't this a bit soft on people who've done terrible things? And he said, like it settled the matter, "the punishment is the sentence." Meaning, I think, that the sentence is the whole of the punishment and everything after it is just life. I wrote it down. I've been thinking about it more then I should.

Then a couple months later I'm talking to an assistant DA in Ohio about a unit I'd visited — no AC, an hour out of the cell a day, the whole picture — and he said something with the exact same shape: they're already being punished, being locked up is the punishment, so what happens in there is logistics. Same sentence, doing opposite work. One guy using it to say don't add misery, the other using it to say the misery isn't his department.

So here's what I want. I need the precise claims hiding inside that one line, laid out as a menu, from the flatly trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: true, false, plausible but contested, or just a way of changing the subject. Some joints I'd like it cut along, and I'm sure there are others:

- Is it a claim about what punishment *is*, a claim about what prisoners *feel*, or a claim about what the state is *allowed* to do? Those seem to come apart.
- Is the loss of liberty the punishment itself, or only the occasion for it — is confinement the hard treatment, or just the setting where hard treatment happens?
- Does it function as a limiting principle (nothing beyond the deprivation may be imposed on purpose) or as a licence (whatever isn't the sentence isn't punishment, so nothing inside counts)?
- Is it a claim about equality — two people given the same sentence should come out with comparable damage, so conditions are part of the punishment after all?
- Is it about standing: the offender keeps everything except liberty?
- Someone told me the deprivation only registers as a loss relative to whatever life the guy would have had on the outside. Is that buried in there somewhere?
- Does it commit to any purpose? Does it rule out rehab as an aim, or only as a justification for adding suffering?

Then tell me which reading my officer was actually using, which one the DA was using, and which reading, if it were true, would make the slogan sound false. And which reading I can put in a character's mouth without making her sound like an idiot — she's decent and she's not stupid, and I don't want her to be a mouthpiece for a platitude she can't defend.

You're a philosopher of punishment who's also read far too much penology; think it through carefully, brainstorm the readings before you start sorting them, and skip the citations and reading lists, I'm not writing a dissertation. And if it turns out the line is mostly ambiguous in a way that lets people end arguments without saying anything, say so — that's a scene too.
