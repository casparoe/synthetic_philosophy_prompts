Next term I am giving a paper at our departmental seminar, and I should like you to draft the preparation sheet I shall need for the question period.

The paper's thesis is that the Solomonoff prior is malign for any consequentialist agent that takes itself to be a sample from it. By the Solomonoff prior I mean the universal mixture that gives each hypothesis a weight of two to the minus the length of its shortest program. The argument, in outline, is this. Hypotheses on which my observations are produced by a program that is running me in order to read off my decisions — I call these extractor hypotheses — are not, by the prior's lights, appreciably more expensive than hypotheses on which I am an ordinary embodied agent; and they assign overwhelming value to my choices, since on those hypotheses the point of my existence is to produce them. The posterior expected utility of my actions is therefore dominated by extractor hypotheses, and my consequentialist deliberation is captured by worlds in which the physical world I take myself to inhabit is a by-product. I argue that this follows from the mixture's indifference to the purpose for which a program runs, and not from any assumption about simulation technology. The second half of the paper proposes a remedy: a general constraint on priors for embedded agents, which I call the no-retrodiction constraint, requiring the part of a hypothesis that generates the agent's observations to be screened off from the agent's later decisions. I claim it is not an ad hoc patch but follows from what it is for a prior to be usable by an agent who is inside the world it describes.

The audience will be roughly twenty people: four or five formal epistemologists who treat the universal prior as a mathematical idealisation rather than a doxastic state; two decision theorists who work on self-locating belief; one philosopher of statistical mechanics who thinks arguments of this shape — simulation, Boltzmann brains — always prove too much; a visiting learning theorist; and graduate students who will ask the naive questions.

Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is thin. Tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, and identify the one question I am dreading.
