In the jurisdictions that permit it, medically assisted dying is governed by an unusually dense procedural apparatus, and I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical theory that this apparatus presupposes — not the arguments its supporters offer in public, but the commitments built into the machinery itself.

Consider the shape of the regime. Eligibility turns on a request made by a competent patient, in the present tense, together with a finding of suffering that the patient reports and that two clinicians independently certify as unbearable and without prospect of relief. There is a waiting period; there is a second opinion; the act is reported afterwards to a body that reviews whether the statutory criteria were satisfied; and in some jurisdictions the patient must be able to take the final step unaided, while in others a clinician may administer. Set against that apparatus three practices that produce much the same result with a fraction of the oversight: the withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment, which may be decided on behalf of a patient who never had capacity and whose wishes can only be inferred; palliative sedation to unconsciousness, which is largely unregulated and is routinely described as an ordinary clinical option; and the refusal of food and fluids by a competent adult, which no jurisdiction I know of submits to review.

The pattern is not obviously incoherent, but I want to know what it takes for granted. Does the two-track structure presuppose a morally significant difference between killing and letting die — or between intending death and accepting it as a foreseen side effect — or can the whole regime be reconstructed on a view that recognizes no such difference? Does the insistence that the request be contemporaneous and first-person presuppose that the value at stake is a species of authorisation that cannot be delegated or exercised in advance, and does that sit comfortably with the way advance refusals of treatment are honoured? What does the mandatory consultation presuppose about the epistemic status of a competent request — that it is a datum to be respected, or a symptom to be worked up? The consultation is required but the treatment it recommends remains refusable; say whether that combination is coherent and what view of the requesting subject it implies. And does a reporting regime that treats rising numbers as an alarm presuppose a substantive view about how often the practice ought to occur, one that may be in tension with the autonomy the regime exists to protect?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates: where several incompatible theories would license the same machinery, say so, and say what would distinguish them. Identify the points at which different parts of the apparatus pull in opposite directions. Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by its principal rival — if the killing/letting-die distinction were abandoned, or if autonomy were displaced by an objective account of a life worth living. Which practices would survive unchanged, which would have to be redesigned, and which would turn out to have been optional all along?
