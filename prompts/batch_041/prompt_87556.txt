In 399 BCE an Athenian jury convicted Socrates of impiety and of corrupting the young, and then, in a second vote, chose death over the penalty his accusers had proposed. Take the following mechanics as given, and correct me if I have any of it wrong. The jurors — several hundred of them, drawn by lot from a standing pool of citizens who had sworn to judge according to the laws and, where no law spoke, according to their own sense of justice, paid for the day, and voting by secret ballot — heard the speeches for the prosecution and the defense and then voted at once, since jurors were forbidden to discuss the case among themselves; a simple majority sufficed to convict; and after conviction the two sides each proposed a penalty and the same jurors chose between the two proposals, again without discussion. Plato's Apology is the principal surviving account, and it is a speech for the defense rather than a transcript.

Analyze this as a case for the Condorcet jury theorem, and run the analysis in both directions. Begin by setting out exactly what the theorem assumes — a determinate right answer, a common probability above one half that any given juror reaches it, independence of the jurors' errors, and decision by majority — and what it delivers. Then do the mapping, and tell me where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Four things interest me in particular.

First, whether the theorem applies to the question the jury actually decided. The charge was evaluative rather than factual, and the jurors had sworn to fall back on their own sense of justice where no law spoke; say whether the theorem has any content when the correct verdict is itself contested, and if you need to fix a ground truth in order to run the analysis, fix it openly instead of smuggling it in.

Second, independence. The jurors were forbidden to deliberate, which on its face makes them more independent than a modern jury; but they all heard the same two speeches in the same political atmosphere, shortly after the restoration of the democracy. I have been calling this inherited correlation: no juror influenced another, yet their judgments share a common source. Say how much damage this does relative to the ordinary kind that deliberation produces, whether the theorem's conclusion survives it, and whether the distinction is real or merely verbal.

Third, selection. A lottery over those who chose to present themselves is not a random sample of the citizen body, and competence on a question of this kind is surely not uniform across jurors. Say what that does to the competence assumption, and what becomes of the theorem when competence varies rather than being a single number.

Fourth, the two votes. Does the second vote corroborate the first, or is it the same body answering a different question? Say what that implies for the common habit of treating a sequence of votes by one body as accumulating reliability.

Finally, turn it around: what does the episode show about the theorem? Is it a counterexample, a scope failure, or a confirmation? My own inclination is that it is a scope failure rather than a refutation, and I want to know whether that is too easy an escape.
