I'm 71, retired six years now after 34 years as an analytical chemist — soils, water, the occasional contaminated-site job — and since I stopped working I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university, mostly so I'd have somewhere to take these questions. The one that's actually bothering me isn't abstract at all.

My father died in 2019. He was 84, emphysema, on oxygen at night, in and out of hospital that whole year. One night he got up, the bulb on the landing had been out for a week and nobody had gotten around to changing it, he missed the last step, broke his hip. Surgery, then pneumonia, and he was gone three weeks later. My sister says the fall killed him. My brother says no, the emphysema killed him — a man with lungs like that doesn't survive a hip fracture and pneumonia, and he was a year from the end anyway. We've been going in circles about it for four years now and it's definately starting to poison the holidays.

What I notice is that when I argue my sister's side I'm using a rule I picked up in the lab: I count something as a cause if there's a knob you could have turned that would have changed how it came out. The bulb is a knob. His lungs aren't, so I wave the emphysema away — but my brother's point is that the emphysema was doing just as much of the work, and that I'm really only counting the things that could have been prevented, which I'd of said was a different question. And then there's the part we both skip over: I don't actually think he'd have lived forever without the fall, only that he'd have had another year or two. My brother says you can't have caused something that was going to happen anyway. So is there a principled answer here, or is this one of those cases where the answer depends on which question you're really asking? And is my lab rule a real criterion for what caused something, or just a habit I've dressed up? I'd like to have something to say at Christmas that isn't just "we're both right."
