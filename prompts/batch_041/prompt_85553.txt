I'm a fourth-year PhD student in statistics and the graduate member of my university's Learning Analytics Advisory Committee, which votes on Thursday on whether to switch on an early-alert model the provost's office has piloted: it scores every first-year student's probability of failing the introductory statistics sequence, and everyone above the threshold is assigned to an intrusive advising program with roughly 500 seats. The vote is therefore about who gets a scarce benefit rather than who gets punished, which I suspect changes which fairness criteria are even the right ones, since nearly everything I've read assumes the decision imposes a burden; and because the seat count is fixed, any group-specific threshold is also a decision about how many seats each group gets. The vendor sells two modes of the same model and refuses to combine them: one whose probabilities are calibrated within each of the eight colleges that feed the course, and one tuned so that the true-positive rate is equal for first-generation and continuing-generation students at whatever threshold we set. Our chair treats the impossibility results as settling this in favor of the second. The student member argues that the labels are just historical grades, and so encode every inequity the institution has produced, and that no mode is legitimate; she wants a no vote and the money spent on the writing center instead. Some of us also worry that being labeled at-risk does harm of its own, which complicates the thought that a benefit should simply be allocated by maximizing true positives. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the vote: a concrete recommendation, namely which mode, what threshold, what conditions, and what review date, with the reasoning for it and a serious engagement with the student member's objection rather than a dismissal of it. Include a brief section on what we should measure over the first two terms to find out whether we were wrong, and what pattern of results would establish that we were.
