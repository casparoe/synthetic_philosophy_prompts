I am assembling the final examination for a graduate seminar in decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and I would like you to draft a single long essay question for it, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The examination is closed-book and ninety minutes long, and the students will have no access to the literature. Nothing in the question may therefore require a citation, and the marking scheme should not reward the bare naming of published discussions. Assume that students have seen enough real analysis to be at ease with the convergence and divergence of series, but do not assume that they have encountered the particular games below. The question must be self-contained: every game it uses must be stated in full, with its payoff rule, inside the question itself.

Build the question on the following three games.

Game A. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads. If heads first appears on toss n, the payoff is +2^n/n when n is odd and −2^n/n when n is even.

Game B. Identical to Game A, except that the payoff when heads first appears on toss n is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n/n^2.

Game C. Identical to Game A, except that the coin is biased: each toss comes up tails with probability q, independently. The payoff rule is that of Game A. For your own reference when drafting the marking scheme, the expectation of Game C is (1−q) · ln(1+2q)/q wherever it is well defined.

I also want a fourth, comparison case, which you should construct in the question: the same coin-tossing structure as Game A, with payoff 2^n/n on toss n and no change of sign. Call it Game D.

Divide the question into parts, and design it so that students must:

(i) compute the expectation of each game when the terms are summed in the natural order by toss number, and classify each resulting series by convergence type;

(ii) draw out what that classification implies for whether the game has a well-defined expected value, giving an explicit recipe for reordering the outcomes of Game A so that the partial sums converge to a target you specify, and explaining why no analogous reordering can rescue Game D;

(iii) explain why Game B has a well-defined expectation even though its payoffs are unbounded, so that students cannot treat the boundedness of the stakes as the crux of the matter;

(iv) confront the fact that Game D pays at least as much as Game A on every single outcome, so that dominance reasoning appears to deliver advice exactly where expected-utility maximisation falls silent, and defend a position on which of the two is the better guide in this case;

(v) assess the response that if utility is bounded the difficulty evaporates, and say whether that response resolves the problem or merely relocates it;

(vi) reach a verdict on whether these games show expected utility theory to be refuted, incomplete, or simply inapplicable, and justify that verdict rather than leaving it hanging.

The question must not be answerable by summarising the St. Petersburg paradox; a student who does little more than that should score poorly.

Then supply a model answer of roughly 900 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks that identifies the specific moves an A answer must make, the marks each move is worth, and the specific errors that should cost marks — for example, treating an undefined expectation as though it were merely a very large one, or reading off a value of ln 2 from the fact that the partial sums in toss order converge without addressing whether that ordering is privileged.
