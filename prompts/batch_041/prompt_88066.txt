I have been working through an idea on my own, without any training in philosophy, and I would like you to help me pin down what a good account of it would have to look like rather than to argue for one.

The idea is this: a great many of the things I am most certain of are not things I could give a reason for, and not things I could seriously doubt. That my memory is broadly reliable; that other people have minds; that the world will not simply dissolve between one moment and the next; that my own reasoning is not systematically rigged against me. I hold these as fast as anything I know, and yet if I were asked to justify them I would end up going in a circle. They seem to be the frame within which I doubt and reason rather than one more item inside it. I understand this is roughly the ground Wittgenstein was working over in the notes he left at the end of his life, and that the image he reached for was of convictions that have hardened into the bed of a river, so that the current of our thinking runs through them, though in time they can be churned up and carried along as part of the current themselves.

What I want from you is the specification, not a verdict: what would any account of these frame convictions have to get right in order to be adequate? Give me the requirements, and for each one the case or consideration that forces it, rather than a survey of who has said what.

To keep the list concrete, I have been worrying at a handful of cases. For each, say whether an adequate account must handle it and, if so, what it must deliver:

(a) someone raised in a community where it is held fast that the dead speak through dreams — must the account treat this on all fours with my certainty that my memory is reliable, or may it distinguish them, and on what basis that is not simply my own conviction talking?

(b) a conviction that was once an ordinary belief, hardened into a frame, and later came loose again and rejoined the current — how does the account describe the transition, and must it say when such a change is an improvement rather than a mere drift?

(c) certainties about my own capacities, where doubting them would have to use the very faculty under doubt;

(d) a man who says he does not believe the bridge will hold and crosses it anyway — does the account have to locate these certainties in what we do rather than in what we believe, and what follows if it does?

(e) two people whose frames are incompatible and neither of whom can be argued out of his, as when one holds that testimony from authorities is generally trustworthy and the other that authority is mostly a mask for power;

(f) a child acquiring one of these certainties, by training rather than by evidence if that is right, and whether the account can explain the acquisition without going in a circle.

Then, please:

- mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp;
- flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which has to give — this is the part I most want to see, since a list with an impossibility buried in it is itself a result;
- name the single requirement that does the most to rule out the most popular accounts, and say what it rules out;
- distinguish, if the distinction is worth making, between a frame conviction being unjustified and its being unjustifiable, and say which one the account is really committed to.

The purpose I have in mind is practical: I want an account good enough to tell me when it is appropriate to try to shake someone else's frame conviction and when doing so would be a kind of violence, and whether calling such a conviction irrational is ever more than name-calling. Keep the scoring of particular theories brief; the requirements are what I am after.
