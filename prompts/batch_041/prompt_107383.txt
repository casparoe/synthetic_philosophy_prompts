I am preparing to interview a philosopher who has spent years defending Kant's 1797 reply to Benjamin Constant on the supposed right to lie out of philanthropy — the essay in which Kant denies that even a lie told to a would-be murderer asking where his intended victim has gone can be justified, on the ground that truthfulness is an unconditional duty. My guest is polished at deflecting the obvious objections with two moves: that the essay belongs to the doctrine of right rather than to ethics, so the only live question is whether the murderer has a right to the truth; and that the duty in question is truthfulness rather than truth, so the apparent paradox dissolves. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that make those two moves progressively harder to sustain, with a follow-up for each question keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. The questions should press where the scholarly literature actually presses, and should not be the sort of thing that lets her simply restate the essay's conclusion.

Please make sure the sequence includes, in whatever order you judge best:

1. A question about how the essay's unconditional prohibition squares with the lecture-notes discussions of a lie of necessity — for instance the treatment of lying to save a friend from a murderer in the Collins notes — and a follow-up that presses whichever reconciliation the guest offers, rather than letting her dismiss the notes as mere pedagogy.

2. A question about Kant's argument that the liar becomes the author of the consequences, and whether that argument quietly imports a consequentialist notion of responsibility into a position that is supposed to be deontological.

3. A question about the self-regarding dimension of the duty: whether lying is primarily a wrong to oneself, or to humanity in one's own person, rather than a wrong to the person deceived — and what follows from the guest's answer for the murderer's desert.

4. A question about where the prohibition stops: misleading truths, evasion, strategic silence, and the servant's not-at-home formula. Follow it up by asking whether the position the guest ends up with can be distinguished from Jesuit mental reservation, or whether it is a more austere version of the same manoeuvre.

5. A question about a case in which the person asking is not a private murderer but an agent of a persecuting state hunting a fugitive — the Anne Frank or slave-catcher variant — and a follow-up asking whether the guest accepts the implication or reaches for an exception.

6. A question about whether an unconditional duty that cannot be met under some circumstances violates the principle that ought implies can.

The remaining questions are yours to choose, provided they stay at this level of specificity and do not ask for bibliographic detail.
