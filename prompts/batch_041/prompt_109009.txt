So I got my first jury summons last week — supposed to show up on the 14th — and its got me thinking way too hard about the Condorcet jury theorem. You know the setup: if every juror is independently more likely than not to get the call right, then the probability that the majority is right climbs toward certainty as the jury gets bigger; but it only works if the errors really are independent and each person is above 1/2, and if the average competence drops below 1/2 then piling on more jurors makes things worse, not better. Fine. Here's what I want.

Write me the juror orientation pamphlet for a county court that has decided to take the theorem literally. Around 600-700 words, deadpan, laid out like a real court handout — a welcome letter, a few numbered sections, a FAQ. No winking at the reader. It should read like something written in total good faith by a clerk who is genuinely proud of the new system, and it should never once say out loud that any of this is silly.

Work all of this in, in the same bureaucratic voice: jurors are forbidden from discussing the case with one another and are housed in separate rooms during breaks, since deliberation would wreck the independence assumption; jurors have to pass a short certification quiz before they're seated, because the whole design only holds if everyone is above 1/2; in districts where the measured competence of the jury pool falls under 1/2, the judge is instructed to enter the opposite of whatever the jury decides; the county now impanels enormous juries, with a section apologizing for the parking and the wait; and the stuff the theorem doesn't actually guarantee — correlated errors, jurors marinating in the same news and the same biases, the awkward fact that nobody knows anyone's real p — shows up only in fine print or a footnote, in a tone implying these are minor technicalities. Let it slip somewhere that the county stopped drawing juries from a cross-section of the community in favor of higher-competence pools, and don't comment on that. A joke at the expense of the court's consulting statistician would be welcome.

Don't explain the theorem in the piece and don't lecture me about it. The funny has to come from the pamphlet being completely sincere.
