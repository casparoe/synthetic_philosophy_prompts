Im putting together the final for a grad seminar on idealization and the will, and I want one essay question on coherent extrapolated volition. The trouble is my students have heard the slogan enough to recite it half asleep -- something about wanting what we'd want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were -- and then they stop thinking. So I need a question where the slogan gets them nowhere.

Here's the setup I want to use. A student in the seminar proposes an amendment she calls the uptake condition: an extrapolated preference counts as your volition only if your present, un-idealized self, shown that preference and the reasoning leading to it, would own it -- not merely admit it's what you'd want if you were smarter, but recognize it as yours. She says this does two jobs at once. First, it stops the whole thing from being paternalism in disguise. Second, it tells you where to stop extrapolating, becuase you extrapolate only until present-you says "yes, thats me." She also thinks it dissolves the indeterminacy worry, since the right extrapolation is just the one your actual self ratifies.

I want the exam question to make them stress-test that. It should force them to say (a) whether the uptake condition really pins down a unique extrapolation or only moves the indeterminacy one level down -- if present-you could ratify different extrapolations, or none, what then? (b) what happens under aggregation, since CEV is supposed to be over everyone and not one person, so whose present self does the ratifying when they disagree, and does the amendment make that worse or better? and (c) whether the whole thing is deference to a deeper self or transformation into someone else, and whether her amendment quietly presupposes an answer. I also want them to confront whether the target should be a fixed point at all -- an idealized self that, shown its own idealization, endorses it -- or whether demanding a fixed point begs the question, since a genuinely transformed agent might reject the very self that produced it.

Give me the question itself (phrased so it can't be answered by summarizing the proposal), a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 with five must-hit points, where for each point you say what a solid B answer would do instead of the A move. Keep the model answer honest about what's genuinely unresolved rather than pretending there's a clean winner.
