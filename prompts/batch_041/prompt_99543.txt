In my philosophy of biology seminar I am expected to present my own position on adaptationism so that the group can test whether it holds together, and I would like a careful audit before I do. Here is where I have landed. (1) Natural selection is the only known source of adaptive complexity, so when we encounter a trait that looks engineered, an adaptationist hypothesis deserves to be the default research strategy. (2) Gould and Lewontin were substantially right that many traits we treat as adaptations are instead byproducts of developmental or architectural constraint, and that this possibility is routinely underweighted in practice. (3) The charge of panglossianism is best understood as an objection to treating optimality models as literal descriptions of organisms rather than as devices for generating testable hypotheses. (4) I think the spandrels analogy itself is overrated, since the triangular spaces in San Marco are entailed by the structural solution of setting a dome on arches rather than being idle leftovers. (5) Appeals to drift, constraint, or phylogenetic inertia are usually unfalsifiable as actually deployed, and therefore do not amount to a genuine rival to an adaptationist account.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each? I would rather not be reassured that the package is fine; I suspect it is not, and I cannot see where the fault lines run. Please rank the conflicts by severity, state for each what would have to be surrendered, and identify the single revision that would resolve the greatest number of them.
