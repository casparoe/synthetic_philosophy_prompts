I am presenting a paper next month to a reading group that mixes philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and Wittgenstein scholarship, and what I want from you is not a defence of the paper but a question-period preparation sheet. The paper defends a reading of Wittgenstein's private language argument on which the argument is not aimed at the possibility of a private language at all, but at a specific picture of how linguistic meaning gets fixed: the picture on which a sign acquires its meaning through an act of inner ostension, a private baptism in which the speaker correlates the sign with an occurrent sensation. On my reading, the long-running dispute over whether Wittgenstein has shown private languages to be impossible is largely beside the point; the argument is a reductio on the inner-ostension picture, and whether some other private language might be possible is a question it leaves open. I then argue that the picture the argument dismantles is not a historical curiosity but is still doing quiet work in some contemporary theories of mental content — in particular, in views that treat thought as conducted in an inner representational code whose symbols are supposed to acquire their content by being correlated, one by one, with the items they represent. My claim is that the private language argument, read as I read it, transfers to that setting, though not in the way the usual critics of such theories suppose.

The audience is mixed, and I need the objections as they will actually be voiced by these particular people. Roughly half are Wittgenstein scholars, broadly sympathetic to the later work and inclined to think that the whole industry devoted to whether Wittgenstein refuted private languages is a misreading — but they will be suspicious of my move to computational theories of content, and will suspect that I am modernising Wittgenstein into a thesis he never held. Two or three are committed Kripkeans who read the argument as a sceptical paradox about rule-following and meaning, and who will want to know why my reading is not simply a less interesting version of theirs. The rest are empirically minded cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind who work on representational content; they do not know the secondary literature, and they will come at me with functionalist and information-theoretic accounts of content, and with the charge that inner ostension is a strawman — that no serious theory of content says a symbol gets its meaning by a private correlation of the kind I attack.

Give me the prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get at a Wittgenstein conference — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that part of the room would phrase it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft, together with a note on what I should concede up front rather than try to defend. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
