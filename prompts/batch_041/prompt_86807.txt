I am preparing a position paper on freedom of expression and would like an audit of my commitments before I settle the framing. Here is roughly where I have landed.

1. I hold the harm principle strictly: the only admissible ground for coercing an adult — whether by law or by the organised pressure of a majority — is the prevention of harm to others. Offence, disgust and the agent's own good are excluded.

2. I defend free expression chiefly on the fallibility argument I take from Mill: since we can never be certain that a suppressed opinion is false, and since even a true opinion loses its living force unless it is contested, the free collision of opinions is the best truth-finding arrangement available to us.

3. I do not accept the usual corollary that the remedy for bad speech is always more speech. Where a coordinated campaign manufactures the appearance of a live expert controversy — where the aim is not to defend a proposition but to corrode any shared standard of evidence — I think restriction is permissible.

4. I would nonetheless keep hate speech lawful. The state's judgment about which contemptuous speech falls beyond the pale is less reliable than the public's eventual response to it, and the people such speech targets are not made safer by its criminalisation.

5. On grounding, I take the harm principle to rest on autonomy rather than on utility. The reason the state may not coerce me for my own good is that my life is mine to lead, and that claim is prior to any aggregation of welfare.

6. Methodologically, I want a test for when expression may be regulated that is robust enough to be applied by a judge who dislikes the speaker — not a standard that only a sympathetic interpreter could be trusted to apply.

7. I would criminalise incitement to violence, and I would prohibit outright deception in commercial advertising.

8. I would have the state fund independent public broadcasting and teach critical reasoning in schools.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and for each genuine tension how severe it is and what the least costly revision would be. Rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; tell me which single revision would clear up the most and what it would cost me elsewhere; and be specific about which commitment has to give.

I do not know where the trouble is, and I would rather be told that one of these commitments is not so much in conflict as confused, or that the audit as I have posed it cannot be carried out cleanly, than be handed a tidy list that flatters my framing. If two of the tensions I have handed you are really one, or if something I have offered as a commitment is not a commitment at all, say so plainly instead of manufacturing symmetry. No reading list, please — work from the positions themselves. Write in British English throughout.
