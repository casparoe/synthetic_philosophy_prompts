Ok so me and a friend got into it about program equilibrium and I wrote his side up as a numbered arguement, and now I can't tell if it's a good reductio or if he smuggled a step in somewhere.

Quick setup so we're on the same page. Two players play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. Before anyone moves, each one writes a computer program and hands it to a referee. Each program is allowed to read the other program's source code and even simulate it before choosing cooperate or defect. A "program equilibrium" is just a Nash equilibrium of this whole thing -- a pair of programs where neither player wants to swap their program out for some other one. There's a classic construction where both programs cooperate, roughly: cooperate if the other program, run against me, would cooperate, otherwise defect. And defect-always vs defect-always is also an equilibrium, which I bring up because I think it matters later.

His arguement:

(1) In the one-shot PD with mutually readable programs, there's an equilibrium where both players cooperate.

(2) Nothing in that construction uses repeated play, reputation, punishment, or any concern for the other player. The only ingredients are mutual transparency of the programs, plus each player best-responding.

(3) So mutual transparency of decision procedures is, all by itself, enough to make rational agents cooperate in a one-shot PD.

(4) If transparency alone does that, then even when the other program is transparently "defect no matter what", I should still cooperate -- transparency is perfect in that case too, so (3) still applies.

(5) But against a guaranteed defector, cooperating is strictly worse than defecting, and that can't be the rational move.

(6) So (3) is false: transparency isn't enough by itself, and program equilibrium doesn't show what he says it shows.

I think he's onto something at (6), but I can't tell whether the reductio is definately legit or whether he snuck something in earlier. Where does it actually break, and whatever I have to give up, what does it cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) if I can -- those are the parts I'm pretty confident are true.
