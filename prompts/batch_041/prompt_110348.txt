ok so I have to write a dissertation prospectus — 15 pages, and then I'll be living with the thing for about a year — and I have a half-formed idea I want you to turn into something real. The topic is the old "are dreams experiences?" mess: Malcolm's book and Dennett's paper, the two things everyone hands you when you ask about this.

Here's what bugs me about how it's usually run. It gets set up as a question about whether anything was going on for you while you were asleep, and then it either turns into a fight about the concept of an experience or it gets handed off to the sleep lab (wake people up, ask them, watch their eyes). I dont have lab access, and I'm not convinced the lab would settle it anyway.

My hunch is that the crux is in the dream report rather than in the sleep. A report isn't a list of stuff, it's a story with a clock in it and a past tense. People dream that they've been walking for hours, that they did something earlier in the dream, that they just now realized something. I have a recurring one where I "remember" an earlier stretch of the dream that I'm fairly sure never happened, and if you look back at a page of text in a dream it has changed. And there's the false awakening thing, where the dream contains you waking up. So here's the fork as I see it: if the report is put together around the time you wake, then whatever does the putting-together has to manufacture not just scenes but a fake history — apparent memories of earlier phases of a dream that never had phases. My first instinct was that this is bad news for that view. Now I think it might be fine, and that the other side has the mirror-image problem: if there really was an experience with its own timeline, why is the report so bad at preserving it, and why do dreams feel like they lasted much longer than the sleep they could have occupied?

Turn this into a proposal. I need the question stated so it could actually come out either way (not "explore the relationship between dreaming and experience"), why the received positions leave it open instead of settling it, and a method I can really execute at a desk. I can read empirical work, I can't run studies, and I don't want to hang the whole thing on my own dream diary. What I was picturing: pull apart what a report says happened, what it claims was experienced, and the memory claims inside it — remembering an earlier part of the dream, as opposed to remembering something from waking life — and then see whether the structure of those inner memory claims can do any evidential work, maybe by hand-coding a few hundred dream reports from one of the big public collections (I'll track down the collection, you don't need to name it). Then say why that's a better bet than straight conceptual analysis of the concept of experience, or than waiting for better sleep science.

Also: what an actual result would look like, what would count as the project failing — I want the honest version, including the possibility that neither view predicts any difference in the reports and the whole question is underdetermined — and a realistic month-by-month shape for a year, with the first three months spelled out, since that's what the committee will grill me on. And tell me straight whether the thing I'm calling the crux is just the old debate in new clothes.
