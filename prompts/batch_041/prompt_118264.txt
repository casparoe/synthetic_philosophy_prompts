For the past several years I have taught an upper-level seminar that pairs cross-cultural pragmatics with moral philosophy, and until recently the unit on politeness and deception ran smoothly on the standard contrast between Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness. Last year I relocated from Amsterdam to Kyoto for a two-year posting, and the move has unsettled the way I teach the material: what I used to present as a tidy descriptive contrast — that Dutch speakers say the blunt thing while Japanese speakers wrap it — now strikes me as concealing the interesting question rather than answering it. In daily life here I have found that the wrapped remark can be more wounding than the blunt one, that a Dutch colleague's plainness can read as respect rather than rudeness, and that a great deal of what I would once have filed under white lies is not a false statement at all but a hint that everyone present is expected to decode. I have concluded that my old final let students off far too easily, and I am rebuilding it around this unease.

Please write me one essay question for that final, pitched at a final-year undergraduate seminar whose students have already read the standard material on politeness and on the ethics of lying but are not specialists. The question should be unanswerable by reciting the directness-versus-indirectness contrast, and equally unanswerable by reciting either that lying is always wrong or that morality is simply relative to culture. What I want it to force students to confront is the gap between the descriptive pragmatics and the normative ethics: whether the fact that indirectness functions as a politeness strategy has any bearing at all on whether a white lie is wrong, and whether the moral analysis changes once we notice that much of what we casually call a white lie is not a false assertion but a misleading implication — that the speaker never actually said anything untrue. I would like the question to require them to say what would have to be true of a polite evasion for it to be genuinely innocent, and to identify at least one case in which the culturally fluent response and the morally defensible one come apart.

Along with the question, please supply a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth out of twenty and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates that point. The marking scheme should be sharp enough to separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer must make — for instance, distinguishing what is asserted from what is merely implicated, or refusing to let the descriptive claim about face-saving do normative work on its own. Assume the students have seventy-five minutes for the essay and no access to texts, so the model answer must stand on its own reasoning rather than on any citation.
