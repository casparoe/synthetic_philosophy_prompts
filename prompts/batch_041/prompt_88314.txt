Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student in history — early modern Europe, mostly, but my committee keeps dragging me into the quantitative stuff and now I'm stuck in the "is violence declining?" literature for a seminar. The frustrating part is that everyone seems to be talking past each other, and I want to see the argument actually done properly. Could you write a dialogue between two people who are both really good at this: one of them is convinced human violence has declined over the long run, the other thinks the whole trend is a artifact of how we define and measure the thing. Neither should be a pushover — I don't want the skeptic to be "well you can't measure anything" and I don't want the declinist to be a walking press release. They should be answering each other's actual points, not giving speeches past one another.

Here's what I'd want them to fight about, because these are the bits I genuinely can't sort out. First, the denominator: count violent deaths per 100,000 living people and you get one story, count them as a share of all deaths and you get another, because once people stop dying of cholera a violent death is a bigger slice of a smaller pie even if the rate fell — and if the population is growing, the absolute body count can climb while the rate drops, so "more or less violence" depends on whether you're counting bodies or odds. Which one is *the* rate of violence, and does anything change if you divide by births instead, or by person-years lived? Second, the sort of thing I've been calling slow violence — harm smeared over decades with no single perpetrator, a famine engineered by a grain policy, a watershed poisoned by a plant that finally closes twenty years later — is that violence at all, or does calling it violence just stretch the word until it covers everything and stops doing work? Third, I want them to chew on a case I invented: two neighbouring provinces in the 1690s, one loses 30,000 people to a war over four years, the other loses 30,000 to a famine over fifteen, and the war dead are mostly men in their twenties while the famine dead are mostly old. Does one of these count as more violent than the other, and on what principle — raw numbers, years of life lost, intent, or the mere fact that one of them has an agent you can point a finger at?

And I'd really like it if they don't just trade measures but get down to whether there's a fact of the matter here at all. Is "violence has declined" something true or false once you've fixed a definition, or is the choice of definition the whole ballgame — i.e., is the declinist discovering something about the world or just making a decision about words and dressing it up as a discovery? Let them press each other hard on that, make the skeptic say what she thinks would actually settle it, and make the declinist say what would make him drop the trend claim. A page or so, and end it wherever the argument honestly arrives — a real standoff is better than a fake handshake, but if one of them lands a good hit the other should concede it and adjust rather than just repeating herself.
