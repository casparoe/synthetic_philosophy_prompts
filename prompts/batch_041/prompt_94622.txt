I'm a second-year PhD student working on structured propositions, and a colleague in our metaphysics reading group has handed me a reconstruction of the old regress about relations — the one Bradley sets out in Appearance and Reality — that I have not been able to shake. His version is deliberately tightened so as to close off what he calls the cheap exits, and I cannot decide whether it succeeds or where the trick lies. I would like you to take the role of an expert in metaphysics and the philosophy of language, think the matter through carefully before committing to a diagnosis, and give me a considered reply rather than a survey of the literature. It may help to begin by asking what a proposition would have to be for the argument's third step to be satisfied at all.

Here is the argument as he states it. (1) A proposition is a single thing with a content: it is the sort of thing that can be true or false, and its constituents — objects, properties and relations — are combined into one content rather than merely heaped together. (2) Were they merely heaped together we should have a plurality, not a proposition. (3) So there must be something about the proposition, over and above its constituents, that makes them one content rather than many. (4) The only candidate for that something is a relation that holds among the constituents and thereby binds them; call it the unifying relation. (5) But the unifying relation is itself one more item alongside the constituents, so it too must be bound to them; that binding is a further relation, which is itself one more item, and so on. (6) Either the series is infinite, or it terminates in something that is not a relation, or at some stage the binding is not a further item at all. (7) It cannot terminate in something that is not a relation, since to bind is to relate, and only a relation relates. (8) Nor can the binding at any stage fail to be a further item, because if nothing further is doing the binding then the constituents are bound by nothing, which is simply the heap of (2) over again. (9) The series is therefore infinite, and this regress is vicious: at no finite stage is the unity achieved. (10) So no proposition is ever a unity; strictly speaking there are no propositions, nothing is thought or said, and nothing is either true or false. Now (10) is plainly false, and if I have to choose I would rather keep both the structuredness of propositions and the reality of their unity than accept it.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong: which step I ought to reject, why that step looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it would cost me. If several steps turn out to be load-bearing, I want to know which rejection is cheapest and what the alternatives would force me to give up elsewhere in the theory of content — I am not interested in being told that unity is simply primitive unless you also price that refusal to explain, and I am suspicious of the same answer dressed up as a distinction of types or orders. My colleague insists his version is immune to the usual replies, on the grounds that the question of what makes this one thing can be asked again at every level, so that any answer either restarts the regress or declines to answer it; I should like to know whether that is a real objection and, if it is, precisely what it is an objection to. Please write this as continuous prose, the way one would write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists of your own, no bold text and no tables.
