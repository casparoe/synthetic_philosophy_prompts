I'm a novelist — three books out, no philosophy training past a first-year logic course I scraped through — but I've been reading analytic epistemology on the side for six or seven years now, and through a series of small social errors I've ended up with a slot at the epistemology work-in-progress seminar at my local university, three weeks from now. The paper is on Fitch's paradox of knowability. What I'm defending: Fitch's proof doesn't refute the anti-realist, it refutes one careless way of stating the knowability principle. The proof runs off the unknown truth — p, and nobody knows that p. But look at what that sentence is. Its content includes the fact that it isn't known, and that's why it can't be known: not because there's some fact tucked away in a corner of the universe, but because the sentence defeats itself in the knowing, the same way the sentence p-and-I-don't-believe-p defeats itself in the asserting. Nobody thinks the Moore sentence shows that belief is incoherent, or that there are facts we're forbidden to believe. So my line is that the unknown truth is the Moore sentence of this debate, and the anti-realist only needs a knowability principle restricted to sentences that aren't about their own not-being-known. Tennant does something in this neighbourhood — he restricts it to propositions whose being known isn't already contradictory, Cartesian propositions, he calls them — but that restriction is written in the very machinery that's under dispute, so it looks built to order to me, and I want to state the restriction in plain terms instead. Whether I can is genuinely up in the air, and whether the plain version survives contact with a logician is worse than up in the air. Also: the Moorean bit is load-bearing. It's carrying the whole paper. If it goes, I've got a book report.

There's a five-minute stretch at the end where I claim fiction is the test case for all this, on the grounds that a novel is a place where truths get made rather than found and I think an anti-realist owes an account of "made" that doesn't just collapse into "whatever a reader can verify." Keep that part short in the prep. I mostly need to survive it.
