ok so my history teacher is making us do this assignment where you take a real event and run it through one thinker's theory, and I got Marx (other options were Rousseau and some econ thing, no thanks). I picked the Luddites, the machine breakers in England around 1811-1816, cause it seems like the most obvious test case for what Marx says about machines and automation.

stuff I already know so you dont have to guess at it: they were framework knitters, croppers and weavers in Nottinghamshire and Yorkshire, mostly working at home or in small workshops. The frames that got smashed were the ones that let owners put cheap unskilled labour (kids included) on the job and pay way less - the wide frames, the shearing frames. They didn't wreck every machine, they picked specific ones, and mostly they picked owners who were cutting the agreed rates. Oaths, night raids, threatening letters signed Ned Ludd. Parliament passed a law in 1812 making frame breaking a hanging offence and sent in the troops, Byron of all people gave his first speech in the House of Lords against it, and people got hanged and shipped to Australia over it.

what I need:

1. What does Marx's machinery chapter in Capital (and the Manifesto bit where workers first lash out at the machines instead of the system that runs them) say these people were really up against, and what does it say they should have done instead?

2. Is his distinction between the machine and the capitalist use of the machine doing real work here, or is it a cop out? Because they attacked both at once - they wrecked frames, but only frames belonging to specific guys who were cheating them.

3. The progress thing bugs me. He thinks developing the productive forces is what makes a better society possible, so smashing machines is fighting the future. But he obviously doesn't think the workers were just being dumb. Is that a real contradiction or does he have a way out? Whats the way out?

4. The compensation debate - do machines create as many jobs as they kill? Does the Luddite case actually bear on that or is it beside the point?

5. The bit in the Grundrisse where he imagines knowledge and machinery doing the work and labour time stopping being the measure of wealth. The Luddites were decades early to something like that and got hanged for it. Is that a case his theory anticipated, or one it never really accounted for?

6. Where does the theory just go quiet? Like the oaths, the local organising, and the fact that alot of them wanted the old agreed prices honored more than they wanted no machines at all.

Then end with a verdict: was Marx right that breaking the machines was a mistake? What would they have had to attack to be doing it right by his lights? And does the case make the machine vs its use distinction look solid or like something he made up after the fact? Don't force the theory to explain everything, tell me where it runs out.
