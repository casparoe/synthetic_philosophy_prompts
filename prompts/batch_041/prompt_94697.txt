I'm a graduate student in political theory, and this term I'm working slowly through Thucydides' account of the Mytilene affair — the revolt of Lesbos, the assembly's first vote to put the adult men to death and enslave the rest, the reversal the following day, and the paired speeches of Cleon and Diodotus. I've read the episode several times, so please don't walk me through the plot; what I want is help with a single passage in Diodotus' speech that I keep circling without ever feeling I've got it. Here is the passage, in the English translation I've been using: "All, states and individuals, are alike prone to err, and there is no law that will prevent it; or why should men have exhausted the list of punishments in search of enactments to protect them from evil-doers? It is probable that in early times the penalties for the greatest offences were less severe, and that, as these were disregarded, the penalty of death has been by degrees in most cases arrived at, which is itself disregarded in like manner. Either then some means of terror more terrible than this must be discovered, or it must be owned that this restraint is useless; and that as long as poverty gives men the courage of necessity, or plenty fills them with the ambition which belongs to insolence and pride, and the other conditions of life remain each under the thraldom of some fatal and master passion, so long will the impulse never be wanting to drive men into danger. Hope also and cupidity, the one leading and the other following, the one conceiving the attempt, the other suggesting the facility of succeeding, cause the widest ruin, and, although invisible agents, are far stronger than the dangers that are seen. Fortune, too, powerfully helps the delusion, and by the unexpected aid that she sometimes lends, tempts men to venture with inferior means; and this is especially the case with communities, because the stakes played for are the highest, freedom or empire, and, when all are acting together, each man irrationally magnifies his own capacity. In fine, it is impossible to prevent, and only great simplicity can hope to prevent, human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon, by force of law or by any other deterrent force whatsoever."

My difficulty is not lexical. Diodotus is arguing against the decree that would kill the whole adult male population, and this looks like the heart of his case: severity cannot deter, because hope and desire and fortune will always outrun the fear of punishment, and human nature cannot be restrained by law. But if that is the argument, I cannot see how it reaches a practical conclusion at all. If no penalty deters, the reasoning seems to cut against punishing the ringleaders exactly as much as against punishing the whole city, and yet Diodotus plainly does want some punishment — he is not urging clemency on principle. A friend in my seminar has pushed me further. She claims the passage is not really about deterrence but about the terms of the question, and that Diodotus has quietly conceded the Mytilenians deserved to die while moving the assembly onto a different footing altogether — deciding, as he puts it elsewhere, that "the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens." I find her reading either impressively modern or quietly sinister, and I cannot tell whether the text actually bears it out or whether she is reading her own politics into a fifth-century speech.

You are a historian of Greek political thought who has taught this text for years and is used to students arriving with exactly this kind of half-formed reading. Before you commit to an interpretation, think carefully and sketch to yourself the two or three rival ways this passage could be made coherent, then decide which the text best supports. Please give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion; the items should together reconstruct what the argument in the passage is actually doing, say how the piece I've quoted connects to the practical recommendation Diodotus goes on to make, and tell me how far my friend's reading can be sustained and where it overreaches.
