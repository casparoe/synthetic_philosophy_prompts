I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this term and the final paper can be on a film instead of one of the standard cases from the literature, which I thought would be a nice break until I realized every movie I actually like is about brains in vats or androids. I want to do Inside Out, plus the sequel since it adds the belief system and the Sense of Self, and read the two of them as a test case for higher-order theories of consciousness. The versions I've got from the readings are roughly: the higher-order thought line where a mental state is conscious only if there's an actual occurrent thought about it (Rosenthal-ish), the inner sense version where it's more like a perception of your own mind rather than a thought (Lycan-ish), and the dispositionalist version where you don't need any occurrent higher-order state, just the standing ability to apply the right concept to the state you're in (Carruthers-ish). At first the movies look almost engineered to illustrate this: there's a console, there's a screen showing what Riley sees, and when Joy and Sadness are stranded outside Headquarters the film has Riley run on autopilot, nobody at the console, nobody home, which is a very clean picture of the difference between conscious and nonconscious processing. The dream studio that literally films Riley's dreams is my favorite bit if it's any use to you.

What I want isn't a summary of the films, it's the reading: where do they genuinely fit a higher-order account, and where do they cheat? Push on the places it gets awkward rather than smoothing them over. The emotions are themselves subjects — they see, feel, want, get embarrassed, have their own little arcs — so if the console is just a representation of Riley's states we need somebody to read it, and if the console is the subject then the emotions aren't conscious and we're back to a small person in the head. The sequel seems to make this worse or better, I can't tell: the Sense of Self and the beliefs are built out of memories and then decide which first-order stuff matters, which sounds like higher-order representations doing the work, but Anxiety generates a sense of self made of self-doubt and inferiority, i.e. a higher-order representation that's flat-out false about the states it's supposed to make conscious — so does the film accidentally make consciousness cheap and fallible, or does it have some resource for saying the false one isn't really the higher-order state that counts? And the old objection about how an unconscious higher-order state can do any work at all — the films seem to answer that by making the watcher a full-blown conscious agent, which looks less like the theory and more like a denial of it.

So: give me the strongest version of the film's implicit theory first, then say where it collapses, and be honest about the scenes that resist the whole reading — I'd rather have a reading that admits the movies are more committed to emotions-as-little-people than to any theory of consciousness than one that pretends everything lines up. If the films dramatize something the papers miss, say what.
