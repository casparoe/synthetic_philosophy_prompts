So I retired a few years back after a whole career in chemistry (never cracked a philosophy book till I hit 65, now I can't stop) and I've fallen down a rabbit hole I need help getting out of. It's this old perception experiment with two dots. You flash a blue dot up top, then a moment later a red dot lower down, and if the gap between the two flashes is in the right window you don't see two blinks, you see one dot glide from the top spot to the bottom spot. Ok fine, that's just how cartoons work. But here's the part that's got me: the dot you see gliding seems to start out blue and turn red about halfway along the path, so you seem to see it go red before the red dot has even flashed. Which definately sounds impossible, because how can what you experience depend on something that hasn't happened yet at the moment you're experiencing it? I gather Dennett leans on this experiment to force a choice: either the brain lets you have the experience and then quietly rewrites your memory of it after the fact (he calls that Orwellian), or the brain sits on the experience and stages the whole thing late so it comes out looking right (Stalinesque), and he says you could never tell the difference from the inside, so the whole distinction may be a dud.

Here's what I want. William James died ages before anyone ran this experiment, but he wrote alot about consciousness being a stream and about the present moment not being a razor-thin instant. What would James have actually said about this dot? Would he have come down as an Orwellian or a Stalinesque, or would he say that way of posing it is confused from the start? And would he say you genuinely do experience the dot moving and changing color, or that that's a story told after the fact? Really try to get inside his head and reason it out from what he actually believed rather than handing me the safe textbook answer. Get specific about the timing part especially. American English please.
