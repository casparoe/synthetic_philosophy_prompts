Some friends and I have been working through Hofstadter's imagined talking ant colony — Aunt Hillary, the one who insists she is conscious and can hold up her end of a conversation — and the discussion after our last session turned into a long argument between two members of the group that I honestly could not referee. Nadia's position was that the colony is a mind, and that the ants stand to it roughly as neurons stand to us: no single ant knows where the better nest site is or when the colony should move, yet the colony compares options, commits by something like a vote of the crowd, and afterwards behaves in ways that look like a memory of the choice. She said the alternative is a size chauvinism that nobody can state as a principle — we happily let a hundred billion non-thinking cells compose a thinker, so on what ground do we refuse to let a few hundred thousand small thinkers compose one? Ravi's reply was that the analogy dies at exactly that point, because neurons are not subjects and ants are: the colony, if it is anything, is a mind made out of minds, and he does not see what the "as a whole" talk buys, since you can always redescribe the colony's deliberation as ants following local rules and its memory as pheromone left on the ground. Nadia answered that this proves too much, since you can describe a brain the same way and get cells following local rules and memory as synaptic weights. Ravi said that is the point: at the end of the brain story there is someone for whom the remembering is happening, and at the end of the colony story there isn't. He called his constraint, half-jokingly, the no-minds-inside rule — a system can be a subject only if its proper parts are not — and Nadia said that rule is simply asserted, and that if it were true it would rule out any mind we might one day build, since we would have to build it out of parts that are themselves agents of a sort.

It got worse from there. She raised what she took to be a decisive case: the colony's temperament — how readily it explores, how aggressively it treats strangers, how it allocates foragers — persists across the complete replacement of its workers and even a change of queen, so whatever we are calling the subject there is not any ant or any collection of ants but a standing organization; Ravi said this shows only that a practice can persist, and that a practice has no point of view, which was precisely his complaint. Then a third person, trying to help, offered a hypothetical: take a single colony, split it so that the two halves spend a season under very different conditions, and then let the halves meet again — if the reunited colony behaves in a way that is neither half's but a stable third thing, is that a mind changing its mind, or just averaging? Both of them claimed the case supported them and neither gave an inch. I would like a verdict rather than a referee's shrug.

Please work through the exchange move by move, rating each of the main moves from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating — this should include the neuron analogy and Ravi's disanalogy, Nadia's size-chauvinism point, the no-minds-inside rule, the colony-temperament case, and the split-and-reunion hypothetical. Then say explicitly what each of them is missing, and whether they are actually disagreeing about the same question or trading on an ambiguity; my own suspicion is that "mind" is doing two jobs in the argument, one about organization and information processing and one about there being something it is like to be the thing, but I would rather you tell me whether that is the real fault line or a red herring. Finish with a verdict on who is more right overall, and what each of them would have to concede in order to settle the matter.
