I help run a small skeptics' society that offers to design and pay for a controlled test of anyone who claims an extraordinary ability — a standing challenge, though ours is funded at the scale of a few hundred dollars rather than a million. Six or so claims reach our monthly meeting, and my job is to turn each into a decision: either we agree on a written protocol with the claimant, or we decline. We have been improvising this, and I would like a real procedure.

I am not asking for the textbook Popper checklist. I have read enough rationalist blogs to know that falsifiable-or-not is not a single switch, and I know the standard objections to treating it as one. What I want is a procedure that survives them. It has to handle four things in particular. First, a claim is almost never tested alone: you test the claim together with a bundle of auxiliary assumptions about the apparatus, the statistics, and the claimant's honesty, so a failure does not by itself tell you which part was false. Second, the rescue move Popper warned about, in which a claimant saves a refuted claim by reinterpreting a term or tacking on a clause, so that no result could count against it. Third, the difference between a claim that has merely been tested and one that has been tested severely, where the test would very likely have produced a discordant result had the claim been false — the severity idea associated with Deborah Mayo. Fourth, the Lakatosian thought that you judge a line of inquiry by whether its amendments predict new things or only absorb failures.

The constraints are real. We have about twenty minutes per claim at the meeting, a couple of weekends and a few hundred dollars per test we agree to run, and the protocol has to be written and signed by the claimant before we spend anything. It has to be defensible both to our own members, who are quick to notice when we have bought a vague claim, and to the claimant, who will call us unfair if we smuggle in a criterion they never agreed to.

Please give me the procedure as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, each item stating one step and, where it bears on a claim below, naming which claim it decides and how. It should say what to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a decline, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is really one of values or of feasibility rather than of testability. Run it on three claims: a man who says he can locate underground water more reliably than a geologist can; a woman who says she can, by intention alone, make a random-number generator produce more ones than chance, but only when no hostile observer is present; and one I will admit I want to accept because it would be fun and would draw a crowd — a claim that the stone blocks of a certain ancient monument were moved by sound, an ability no one can reproduce now. I expect the procedure to give me at least one answer I will not like.

Tell me where the procedure is most likely to fail — which kind of claim it will misclassify, and which of the four objections above it handles worst — and what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to redraw one of the five steps. American English, please.
