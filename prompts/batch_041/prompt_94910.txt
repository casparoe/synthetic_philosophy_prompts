I have recently begun acting with a small amateur company, and a friend who works on the eighteenth century has taken to pressing Rousseau's attack on the theatre on me: the long letter Rousseau wrote when d'Alembert, in his article on Geneva for the Encyclopédie, complained of the city's ban on dramatic performances, and when Voltaire, living nearby, was agitating for a playhouse there. Much of it I can dismiss as the grumbling of a man who had himself written plays, but there is one argument in it I cannot shake, and I have set it out below in a form sharper than Rousseau's own. My difficulty is that it appears to prove far too much.

(1) What moves us in a play are sentiments aroused in us as spectators: pity, admiration, indignation, directed at characters and situations that are not ours and that make no claim on us.
(2) Those sentiments are pleasant, and their pleasure is complete in the moment of feeling them.
(3) A pleasure that is complete in the moment of feeling leaves behind no motive to act once the moment has passed.
(4) A theatre prospers only by pleasing its audience, and what pleases an audience is what confirms it in the dispositions it already has.
(5) So the theatre gives us the pleasures of virtue without its practice, and confirms us in our existing character instead of correcting it.
(6) Whatever confers the pleasures of virtue without its practice, and confirms rather than corrects the character of those who use it, corrupts them.
(7) Therefore the theatre corrupts those who attend it.

Premises (1) to (4) seem to me true as stated, and that is precisely the trouble, since they seem equally true of the novel, of the concert, of painting, and of moral philosophy, and indeed of the plays Rousseau had himself written. Premise (6), applied without restriction, condemns all of them; and Rousseau's own remedy, the civic festival at which the citizens assemble and dance together in the open air, is itself an occasion of collective feeling that ends when the evening ends. If the argument is sound, everything that moves us is corrupting, which cannot be right. But I cannot see which step to surrender without surrendering the conclusion, and I would rather keep the conclusion, because I do think there is something about the stage in particular that distinguishes it from a novel read alone by the fire: the actor who is paid to counterfeit feeling he does not have, the audience seated in the dark and passive, the whole enterprise dependent on pleasing the largest paying crowd. I also gather that Rousseau confined his verdict to a small city such as Geneva and never asked for the Paris theatres to be shut, and I cannot tell whether that restriction rescues him or is merely an evasion.

So I should like you to tell me which step I must give up, and what giving it up costs. If the argument can be saved by narrowing it rather than abandoning it, say exactly what the distinguishing feature of the theatre is, why it is not shared by the novel or by the festival, and whether it survives the obvious counterexamples. If it cannot be saved, tell me what I am obliged to accept instead, and be explicit about which of my convictions I am paying with. Please write your answer in American English.
