I have been trying to write down where I actually stand on value and on reasons, and I would like a consistency check before I go further. Here is the position, stated as plainly as I can.

1. Value is fittingness. For something to be valuable is for it to be fitting — appropriate, correct — to value it. I mean fitting as a normative relation in its own right, not as a disguised way of talking about what one ought to do or what would be useful.

2. Reasons are fundamental. Fittingness is not the basic normative notion; reasons are. An attitude is fitting just when the considerations counting in favour of it are not outweighed by those counting against.

3. Two sorts of reasons for attitudes. Some reasons bear on the object of the attitude: the demon's cruelty counts against admiring him. Others bear only on the state of having the attitude: the demon will torture me unless I admire him. I call these object-directed and attitude-directed reasons respectively. Only object-directed reasons bear on whether the attitude is fitting.

4. Attitude-directed reasons are reasons all the same. If I could switch my admiration on at will, I would have a reason to switch it on; and someone who knew of the threat and could not muster the admiration would be criticisable for that failure, not merely imprudent.

5. Evidentialism about belief. Only evidence is a reason to believe. That a belief would make me happy, or that I will be punished unless I hold it, is never a reason to hold it, which is why the wager argument for religious belief fails as an argument for belief.

6. Value is objective. Whether something is valuable does not depend on anyone's attitudes towards it, nor on what anyone would approve of.

7. Ought implies can. If I genuinely cannot do something, it is not the case that I ought to do it.

Two further verdicts I would want to keep, though they may simply follow from the above: the demon's victim who sincerely admires him has not thereby discovered anything about the demon's value; and when a friend's manuscript is bad, the fact that she will be hurt if I do not admire it strikes me as giving me an attitude-directed reason, even though what supplies it is her feelings rather than the manuscript.

I am not asking which of these views is true, and I do not want a defence of any of them. I want an audit. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which merely appear to, and how severe is each real conflict? For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost. Then say which single change would clear up the most, and whether any of the seven is really ambiguous and needs to be split into two claims before it can be assessed at all. Finally, tell me which of the seven could survive exactly as written.
