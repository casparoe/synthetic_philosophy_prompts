I'm in this seminar on moral psychology this term — basically the whole course is "which emotion should be doing the moral work" — and seperately I'm the outgoing coordinator of our student-run community kitchen, and my term ends in a few weeks with the annual dinner where the outgoing person has to give a speech. Weird room: the founding volunteers (who built the place and get a little prickly about criticism), a bunch of brand-new first-years, and a handful of parents who have no idea about any of the internal drama. The drama is that for years the kitchen has run on a kind of low-grade shame. Miss a shift, or bail on the Saturday prep, and the vibe is that you're a flake, you're not really committed, you've let everyone down personally. It works, in the sense that people show up — but it's burned out two whole cohorts and I've watched good volunteers quit because they couldn't face the group chat after one missed week.

I want the speech to be an argument that we should trade shame for guilt, and that a culture can actually choose this rather than just drift into it — and I want the speech to perform the switch, not just describe it. So it has to name real things that went wrong this year without turning anyone into a villain, and it has to land for the founders, who'll hear any critique as an attack on them personally. The way I've been thinking about it, and I honestly don't know if this holds up, is that guilt is a verdict on an act — with a sentence attached and a way to serve it and be done — whereas shame is a verdict on the whole person, with no exit, like a life sentence with no appeal, and maybe that's exactly why a shame-culture can never let anyone back in. But my professor keeps insisting guilt and shame aren't even real opposites and that shame does some work guilt can't, so I don't want it to come out as just "shame bad, guilt good." Can you write me the speech? Five-ish minutes, warm, a couple of jokes so the parents don't feel like they wandered into a seminar, and please don't make me sound like I'm lecturing my own friends — the kitchen's whole ethos is that we're not preachy, so that's kind of the whole tightrope.
