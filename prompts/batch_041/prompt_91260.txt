Question: The incompleteness theorems get dragged into all sorts of non-mathematical fights — the mind/machine debate, claims that an AI can't verify its own safety, the idea that some body of norms is "essentially incomplete". Say what the first and second theorems actually establish, and then take TWO of those three applications and say whether, and how, the theorems bear on them. Be precise about the conditions the theorems require of a system.

Student's answer: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent formal system strong enough to capture arithmetic will contain sentences that are true but not provable in the system; the second shows that such a system can't prove its own consistency. What this means is that no system of reasoning — mathematical, computational, or human — can be complete: for any such system there will be truths it cannot reach. That's the real bite of the result for the mind: since a human mathematician can look at the Gödel sentence and see that it's true, no machine that's equivalent to a formal system can match human mathematical insight, which is basically the point Lucas and Penrose make. The same structure shows up for AI safety. An AI powerful enough to reason about its own behaviour can't prove its own consistency, so it can't verify that it's safe; it would have to rely on some stronger system outside itself, and that just pushes the problem back a level. The theorems are narrower than people think, though: they need consistency and enough arithmetic, so a decidable complete theory like Presburger arithmetic is unaffected — the gap they expose is between truth and provability, not a defect in particular axioms.
