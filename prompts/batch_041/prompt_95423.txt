I am a platform engineer, ten years into distributed systems and on-call work, with no formal training in philosophy. The vocabulary I actually reason in is the one I use at three in the morning: capacity planning and queueing, admission control and load shedding, circuit breakers and bulkheads, SLOs and error budgets, overcommit ratios and bin-packing, Kubernetes scheduling with its Guaranteed, Burstable, and BestEffort QoS classes, kubelet eviction ordering under node pressure, cgroup limits and the OOM killer, autoscaling with cold-start lag, spot-instance reclamation, tail latency, thundering herds, priority inversion, blast radius, and the incident-command split between the person who owns the service and the person who owns the response.

I want the lifeboat problem — the case that a boat already at capacity owes nothing to the people in the water, since taking more aboard sinks everyone; and the main objections to it, that the boat's capacity is not a fact of nature but someone's provisioning decision, that passengers and swimmers were separated by an unjust prior distribution, that the argument treats persons as interchangeable units of load, and that the boat's occupants are not thereby the ones authorized to decide who else boards — restated as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary. Restate each position as a concrete engineering claim rather than a mood: name the policy the operator is actually running, say which workloads are Guaranteed and which are BestEffort and in what order the kubelet evicts them, state what the SLO promises, say who holds and who spends the error budget, say whether capacity is reserved or elastic, and whether the queue drops requests or grows without bound.

Where a philosophical claim maps cleanly onto one of these, say so and move on. Where it maps only under distortion, name the distortion precisely — for instance, whether the distinction between failing to admit and actively killing survives when every configuration is an action the system takes and there is no privileged default state, or whether a rights claim amounts to anything more than a priority class that the operator remains free to edit. Where a claim has no counterpart at all, say that in as many words rather than gesturing at an analogue.

Then the part I actually want: the residue report. Tell me what the translation could not carry — whether the non-substitutability of persons, the demand that some things not be traded off against throughput at all, the question of who is authorized to set the boat's capacity in the first place, the possibility that the scarcity is manufactured rather than given, the apparent difference between a rescue refused and a death caused, and any obligation to one's own passengers that strangers in the water do not share (in my vocabulary every request is interchangeable, so I cannot see where partiality would even be represented) — and give me a verdict on each item: is my vocabulary constitutively unable to express it, and if so what would have to be added to it, or was the original claim resting on an ambiguity or a picture that dissolves once it is stated operationally? Finish with a judgment on my own suspicion, that lifeboat ethics is resource allocation under hard constraints with a great deal of moralizing layered on top: tell me whether that suspicion survives the translation, and if it does not, exactly which step of the translation kills it.
