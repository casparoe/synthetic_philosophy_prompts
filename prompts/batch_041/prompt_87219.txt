I keep seeing people talk about models "knowing" things they don't say, and I want to understand what that's actually supposed to mean, because I suspect the word is doing more work than it can bear.

Heres the setup as I understand it. You train a model on a big pile of human text. The only thing it's ever rewarded for is predicting what a human would have written or said. So if the humans in the data are wrong about something — say they all think a drug is safe because of a bad study, or they all misread a gauge — the model will cheerfully predict the wrong thing, because that's the thing to predict. Meanwhile, if you go poking around inside it, you can find internal states that seem to track the actual truth better than its outputs do. People then say the model knows the truth and we just can't elicit it.

Two things I can't get to fit. First: what makes that internal state knowledge rather than my own artifact? I chose the probe, I chose the question, and I could probably find some direction in activation space that correlates with almost anything if I looked hard enough. If the only thing the model was ever optimized to do was imitate humans, why is the truer internal signal not just a coincidence I dug up? Second: everyone insists this is a different problem from the model lying, that it would still be a problem even with a perfectly honest, cooperative model with no incentive to decieve. But if it's cooperative and the truth is somewhere inside it, why wouldn't it just say the true thing when asked? I feel like I'm missing a step.

So: what is "latent knowledge" supposed to be, exactly? Is the hard part really in the model, or is it in us not being able to verify anything from the outside? And what would count as evidence that a model knows something it isn't saying, as opposed to us reading our own hopes into a probe? I'd rather have the concept itself explained than the standard analogy, if that makes sense.
