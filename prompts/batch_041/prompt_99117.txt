I keep bumping into the same move in arguements about settling space: someone says colonizing Mars is just colonialism again, or that space is the new frontier / the Wild West, and then whichever side theyre on acts like that settles it -- the critics say so obviously we shouldnt go, the enthusiasts say yeah and the frontier spirit is exactly what we need. I want to know how much of that analogy actually holds before I put it in a handout for my reading group.

Start with the correspondence, as a table: in the colonialism / frontier picture, what plays the role of the settlers, the indigenous inhabitants, the land itself, the metropole back home, the extraction economy, and the frontier myth. Then the places the mapping breaks down -- and be specific about which breaks matter for the thing people are using it to argue (that we shouldnt go, or that going is a noble continuation of something) and which are just noise.

One thing I suspect it smuggles in: colonialism comes in flavors. Settler colonialism is about replacing the people who are already there; extractive colonialism is about pulling resources back to the metropole and not much caring who lives there. If Mars (or the belt, or wherever) has nobody to displace, does the settler version even apply, or is it all extractive, or neither? I think the analogys whole force might live in that distinction and almost nobody using it notices. And does it change anything if there turns out to be native microbial life -- does that make the settler version kick in after all, or is a microbe not the kind of thing that can be colonized?

Then tell me whether the conclusion each side wants survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better analogy for the actual situation. I've heard Antarctic research stations and I've heard a company town, but I dont know if either does more work than the colonial one. Keep it tight, like a page.
