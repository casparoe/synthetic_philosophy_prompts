My dad past away last year and I've been writing up his life for my kids — kind of a short book, nothing fancy — and its turned into a fight with my sister that I did not see coming.

She wants the real version: the years he drank, the job he got fired from, the way he could be nasty when money was tight. She says a cleaned up version is useless, because nobody can live up to it and the kids will know its fake anyway. I want to leave that stuff out. My reasons are that he did get better, and that's the whole point of the story, and that I want my kids to have somebody to look up to. If all they get is a man with the same flaws they've got, what are they supposed to do with that?

Only now I'm not sure what the story is even for. My oldest is 14 and she read what I've got so far and said it doesn't sound like a real person, it sounds like a poster. My nephew said something that stuck with me: reading about a guy who quit drinking after twenty years doesn't tell you it's a good idea, it only tells you it's possible, and those are two different things. And my brother says a life with the bad parts cut out teaches nothing, because you can't copy a whole person, you can only copy a thing they did, and if you cut the bad stuff there's nothing specific left to copy.

So: is there a right answer about what a written life is for? Does it matter whether the portrait is accurate, or only whether it does something to the reader? Is my sister right that an idealised life teaches nothing, or is she wrong because a story isn't an argument and doesn't have to be true in that way? And is my nephew right that 'he did it so it's possible' is a completely different lesson from 'this is worth doing' — and if so, which one is a kid supposed to take from a life? Also, if my dad's story only works because I'm smoothing it, is that lying to my kids, or is that just how stories work? I'd rather have something I can act on than a lecture on ethics.
