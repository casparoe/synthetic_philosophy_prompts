I am a legal philosopher preparing a paper for a criminal-law reform seminar next month, and what I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a summary or a defence of my argument. My thesis is a revisionist legal moralism. I accept that the criminal law may properly concern itself with the enforcement of morality, and I reject the view that the harm principle exhausts the legitimate grounds of criminalisation. But I argue that the licence to criminalise is governed by two constraints that the classical debate never properly separated. The first is a condemnation threshold: criminalisation is permissible only where the conduct is condemned by an overlapping consensus among the society's principal moral communities, not merely by a numerical majority or by the loudest one. The second is a principle of moral-risk asymmetry: where the moral status of the conduct is genuinely uncertain, the threshold rises in proportion to the severity of the proposed sanction and the irreversibility of the coercion it imposes, on the ground that a wrongful criminalisation coerces someone who may be innocent, whereas a wrongful refusal to criminalise merely leaves a possible wrong unpunished. Applied together, these constraints lead me to conclusions that please neither camp: I defend the criminalisation of one practice that liberals treat as private and harmless, and I oppose the criminalisation of another that moral conservatives treat as plainly within the law's remit.

The audience is an academic seminar of roughly fifteen people, and I want the objections this room will actually raise, not the objections I would face from a general audience or from activists on either side. Present will be three Hartian liberals who regard the harm principle as more or less settled and who will suspect that any legal moralism is the old social-disintegration thesis in a new suit; one committed retributivist who will think my moral-risk asymmetry is a machine for permanent under-criminalisation; a feminist legal scholar who will press that an overlapping-consensus test simply entrenches the morality that dominant groups already share, and that my threshold makes the criminal law's protection hostage to whatever the powerful are willing to condemn; an empirical criminologist who will want to know how the condemnation threshold is supposed to be measured, whether any of it is falsifiable, and what would count as evidence that the threshold has or has not been met; and a legal historian who knows the original Hart–Devlin exchange in detail and will ask precisely how my position differs from Devlin's appeal to shared moral feeling and from Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality, and whether my two constraints do any work that those distinctions could not already do.

Please give me a preparation sheet with the following structure. First, the objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, with the most dangerous first; for each, phrase the objection the way that particular interlocutor would phrase it, in their vocabulary and with their priorities. Second, for each objection, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, written to be spoken rather than read. Third, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, so that I know which follow-up to fear. Fourth, tell me which objections my two constraints genuinely answer and which expose a real gap in the position that I should concede rather than attempt to paper over, and what I should concede up front before the discussion begins. Fifth, identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and say why the rest of the discussion depends on it. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two further points. The discussant has read my draft and will open with a prepared comment, so where relevant tell me how the room's objections are likely to be refracted through that opening comment. And I do not want reading lists, citations, or references to particular books and articles; keep everything at the level of argument and the concepts themselves. Write the whole thing in British English.
