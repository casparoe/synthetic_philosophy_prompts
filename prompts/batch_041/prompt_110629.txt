I took early retirement from field ecology a few years back and, with all the sudden free time, I've been working my way through philosophy of mind — slowly, mostly on my own, though lately I've joined a reading group that's chewing through the consciousness literature, which is where this comes from. Before I present to them I want to lay my cards on the table and have someone tell me straight whether they hang together, because I have a nagging feeling that two or three of the things I beleive can't all be true at once, and I honestly can't tell which ones are the culprits. Here's my hand. (1) The mind is a biological phenomenon, full stop — it's the brain doing its thing, no spooky extra ingredient, nothing that couldn't in principle have come out of selection pressures on a nervous system. (2) I'm sold on that ladder of creatures that keeps showing up in everything I read: the Darwinian ones that are born with the right reflexes, the Skinnerian ones that learn by reward and punishment, the Popperian ones that run the experiment inside their own head and let the hypothesis die instead of themselves, and the Gregorian ones whose thinking gets done partly with outside equipment — language, paper, notation, other people's arguments. (3) And I think we're Gregorian all the way up; for me that means my mind genuinely leaks out of my skull and onto the desk and into the culture I happen to be swimming in. (4) Consciousness isn't a light switch, it's a dimmer — it comes in degrees. (5) After thirty years of watching corvids cache and octopuses fiddle with jars I'm dead certain they have real inner lives; they suffer, they scheme, they get bored. And (6) all that said, I still want to say there's something qualitatively different about the human mind, and I think the difference is that Gregorian layer — that reflective, self-aware, I-am-here kind of consciousness only switches on once you've got language and culture to think with.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which of those six are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they are, and how badly? Rank the real tensions by how much damage they do, and for each one tell me the cheapest fix and what it costs me. Then, if you had to name the single revision that would clean up the most, tell me which commitment is doing the most work and ought to go — and be specific about which ones I'd have to give up to keep it, rather than just listing everything as equally shaky.
