A popular book on the philosophy of the social sciences that I have been reading has a chapter on Popper that runs roughly as follows: "Popper's critique of historicism is a proof that there are no laws of history and that the social sciences can never predict anything, because human beings have free will and because historical situations are too complex and unique to fall under general laws. Popper calls the search for laws of historical development 'historicism' and argues that it inevitably issues in unconditional prophecy about the future. He also observes that announcing a prediction about society alters the society in question—the Oedipus effect—so that social prediction is self-undermining and the very idea of a theoretical social science is a mistake."

I found this convincing when I read it, but a friend insists that Popper's real argument has to do with the unpredictability of the growth of knowledge, which the summary never mentions. Could you audit the passage for me? I would like to know which claims a careful reader of Popper would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are simply wrong; whether the Oedipus effect actually supports the conclusion the author draws from it; and how much of the chapter's verdict survives once the summary is corrected.
