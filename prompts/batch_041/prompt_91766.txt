I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, working up a seminar paper on the real-patterns criterion — the proposal, usually attributed to Dennett, that a pattern counts as real when a description framed in terms of it compresses a system's behaviour, letting you say what the system will do more cheaply than you could by listing what it has done, and that the pattern must additionally be projectible, i.e. it must keep earning its keep under counterfactual perturbation rather than merely fitting the observations already in hand. The thesis I want to defend is that the realist weight is carried almost entirely by the second half of that pair, and that compression on its own is doing something more modest than the rhetoric surrounding the criterion suggests. A colleague in my reading group thinks the two halves can never come apart in the cases that matter, on the grounds that any description compressive enough to be worth stating is already projectible enough to be worth believing. I suspect she is wrong, but every case I build seems to beg the question, so I would like you to build the case for me.

What I need is a single scenario — one paragraph, an ordinary and realistic setting, not science fiction — featuring one system, S, arranged so that the intentional-stance reading of S is much shorter than the physical-stance reading and fits everything S does in the scenario, while a longer description at the physical or design stance nevertheless predicts S correctly under one specified perturbation of the scenario that the intentional reading gets wrong. Please stay away from the stock illustrations of the stances (thermostats, chess programs, termite mounds, ant colonies, flocks of starlings), since those arrive pre-loaded with the verdicts I am trying to test. Three further constraints. First, the behaviour that falsifies the intentional reading has to be exhibited, or at least clearly foreshadowed, inside the scenario itself rather than stipulated in a footnote; I want the case to show its work. Second, nothing may turn on whether S is conscious, and nothing may turn on S's internal states being representational in any robust sense — if the case needs either, it fails. Third, the intentional reading has to be a genuinely short description of S's actual behaviour in the scenario, not a description of what S would do if S were an ideal agent; I have made that mistake before and it collapses the whole exercise.

If you can build this, then: say what the real-patterns criterion delivers for your case, and whether it returns a single verdict or splits; identify the one feature of the scenario that makes the two halves come apart, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and set out the strongest reply available to someone who wants to defend the criterion here, together with your own judgement of whether that reply succeeds. I would also like you to state plainly what the case is for — what philosophical payoff it secures if it works — because I would rather discover now that it supports only something weaker than what I am claiming. And please be willing to push back. If the two halves of the criterion cannot be pulled apart for intentional-stance descriptions, or if the case as specified cannot be built without stipulating its own verdict, say so directly and explain why, instead of producing something that quietly assumes the conclusion; a well-argued refusal is more useful to me than a compliant example, and if my gloss of the criterion is off, correct it before going further. Finally, mark clearly which parts of your answer are generated by the scenario and which are stipulation on my part, so that I can see how much of the conclusion I have built in myself.
