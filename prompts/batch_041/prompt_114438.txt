I'm a novelist — two books down, the third one first-person and built around a narrator you slowly stop believing in — and I've somehow agreed to run next month's session of my writing group's philosophy slot. Eight of us, all novelists or poets, nothing past a first-year survey course, one hour, back room of a pub, whiteboard that doesn't erase. I don't need the cogito explained to me, I can read. What I need is a plan for explaining it: what has to be in place before anything else can land, in what order, and why each step depends on the one before it. My problem is that everyone in that room already thinks they know what it proves — that I exist, which nobody was worried about — so the thing has to open somewhere that makes them feel the question instead of nodding at the answer. And one of them will definately say that all it really shows is that thinking is happening somewhere, not that there's a thinker doing it, and I think that's the interesting part rather than a heckle, so it can't be something we hurry past.

The example is where I keep stalling. What I want to build it on is the experience of being three hours into a draft and realising the sentences were arriving and nobody was there writing them — no little man in the chair — and then noticing that this is more or less the state my narrator is in, which is how I ended up in this mess. Tell me whether that carries the idea or whether it quietly hands them the answer, what the example has to have and what it must not have, and why I shouldn't just reach for the wax or the dream, which is what I'd do left to my own devices. Then the sequence: I assume they have to feel the doubt as a procedure before the indubitable bit means anything, and that the sharp edge is the gap between doubting a thought and doubting that the thought has an owner — and I'd like them to end up seeing that whatever this proves is only happening in the present moment and doesn't yet give you a self that lasts till Tuesday, though tell me if that's really a different session. Misconceptions I can predict: that it proves they have a soul, and that it's a syllogism with a sneaky missing premise. Where in the sequence do I head each of those off, what else will they get wrong that I haven't thought of, and what's the one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether the idea landed rather than whether they enjoyed themselves? I'll be leaving out the God business and the real distinction and saying so out loud — check whether that leaves a hole where a load-bearing thing used to be.

If my framing is confused — if the thing I want them to feel isn't actually what the cogito is about, or if eight novelists in a pub in an hour simply can't get there — say so in your first line rather than writing me a plan that papers over it, and don't send me a reading list, I won't read it. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate what I asked.
