For my metaphysics seminar I would like you to write a short story — roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words — that stages the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism through a concrete institutional conflict rather than through the usual illustrations (the ship, the statue and the clay, the person who changes her beliefs). Please set those familiar examples aside entirely; the point is to make the debate vivid in a setting the reader has not seen before.

Here is the premise I would like you to develop, though you may alter details if the philosophy requires it. A near-future jurisdiction has, for reasons of administrative convenience, codified a perdurantist metaphysics into its law of torts: a legal person is identified with a four-dimensional space-time worm, and liability attaches to the worm as a whole. A private firm then markets temporal severance, a procedure that, for a fee, severs the legal and financial continuity between a client's present temporal stage and specified earlier stages. A woman who, as a much younger stage, committed a serious crime now petitions for severance, arguing that the stage that acted no longer stands in any relevant relation to the stage that now petitions. The victims' family argues that on the very metaphysics the court has adopted, the worm is the agent, and that severance is either incoherent or an unjust evasion. An endurantist judge writes a dissent.

Use the story to bring out at least these threads: (i) the problem of temporary intrinsics — how one and the same thing can be, at one time, F and, at another, not-F — and whether the legal fiction quietly presupposes one of the standard solutions (temporal parts, tensed properties, or properties relativized to times); (ii) whether the identity of a person over time is a metaphysical question or a normative one, and whether the law is entitled to legislate a metaphysics for its own purposes; (iii) what, if anything, is lost if persistence is treated as a matter of temporal parts — the worry that perdurantism turns a life into a series of strangers rather than a single career.

You need not let the story reach a verdict. I would prefer that it dramatize the costs on each side rather than announce a winner.

After the story, add a short author's note of no more than 300 words doing two things: state how confident you are in the reading of the debate you have used here — in particular, whether you think an endurantist can consistently speak of temporal parts at all, or whether that already concedes too much to the perdurantist — and single out the one point in the story where you are least sure you have represented the positions fairly. If you have taken a liberty with the metaphysics for narrative effect, say so there.
