I host a long-form interview podcast on the history of ethics, and next week's guest is a philosopher whose recent book defends Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying. Her thesis is that no lie is ever permitted, not even to save an innocent life, and that the wrong of a lie consists not in the harm done to the person deceived but in the corruption of the speaker's own will — the condition Augustine describes when he says that the liar holds one thing in mind and utters another, so that the liar's heart is double. She knows both On Lying and the later Against Lying closely and will not be flustered by the stock objection about the murderer at the door. Please draft eight interview questions that move past restatement, each accompanied by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following, and a single question may cover more than one: (1) whether her definition of lying tracks the speaker's inner state rather than the truth or falsity of what is uttered, and what that implies for a true statement uttered in order to mislead, or for a false statement the speaker sincerely believes; (2) the passage in which Augustine sets jokes aside as never having been accounted lies, on the ground that the joker's tone and manner make plain that he means no deceit — does a concession that licenses false speech deceiving no one threaten the absoluteness of the prohibition?; (3) the scriptural episodes in which deception appears to be rewarded, such as the Hebrew midwives' deception of Pharaoh and Rahab's concealment of the spies — does she deny that these were lies, recast them as concealment, or concede that they were lies while insisting they are not to be imitated?; (4) the occasion of Against Lying, in which Augustine rejects the proposal that Catholics pretend to be Priscillianists in order to infiltrate the sect — how far does her prohibition reach into deception undertaken for a good cause, such as undercover investigation, the concealment of refugees, or resistance to persecution?; and (5) the boundary between lying and merely misleading or concealing — may the rigorist utter what is true in order to produce a false belief, or is she confined to silence? Order the questions so that her most likely retreat — that the hard cases trade on confusing the wrongness of lying with the badness of its consequences — becomes progressively harder to make, and close with a question about whether any consideration could ever make a lie permissible, and if so what it would have to be.
