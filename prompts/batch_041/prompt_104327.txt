ok, so I've gone down a heritability rabbit hole and I think I've caught the concept doing something weird, but I want a sanity check before I start saying it out loud.

The way I always understood it, heritability is the number that tells you how much of a trait is genetic. Heritability of .7 for something means 70% genes, 30% environment. Fine.

But then I keep reading people say that heritability estimates come out higher in populations where everyones environment is more alike — same schools, same diet, same money — and lower in populations that are more unequal. Which honestly makes sense to me if the number is just the share of the variation that's genetic: flatten out the environmental differences and whatever spread is left over has to be genetic, so the number goes up. I also saw someone say the heritability of IQ rises as people get older, which at first sounded backwards but I figure it's the same idea.

Heres the part that's bugging me though. If that's right, then heritability isn't really a fact about genes at all, it's a fact about how equal a society is. And that seems to have a pretty perverse consequence. Say you wanted to make some trait less genetically determined — if you're worried about the genetic component of intelligence, or height, or whatever — then the move would be to make the environment more unequal, not more equal. More noise, more chaos, and the trait comes out mostly environmental, which sounds like it'd be easier to fix with policy. Equalize everything and you've basically turned it into a genetic trait.

So: is the way to de-geneticize a trait to introduce more environmental inequality? And a follow up, because I think it's the same issue: if a trait is 100% heritable in some population, does that mean it's totally fixed there — nothing environmental could ever move it, since there's no environmental variance left for an intervention to work on?

Not a geneticist, I just want to get this straight before I argue about it with someone at dinner. American English please.
