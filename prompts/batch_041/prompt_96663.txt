I teach a graduate seminar on the Vienna Circle, and I am marking the take-home examination. One of the questions, and the candidate's answer to it, are below. I would like a grade with itemized comments rather than a general impression.

Question 3 (25 marks)

In seminar we distinguished two questions that can be asked of any notation:

(a) Expressive adequacy — can the notation say everything the science in question needs to say?
(b) Epistemic authority — can the notation, by itself, settle what is to be accepted?

We also distinguished three readings of the phrase "picture language":

(i) a verbal language with pictures substituted for some of its words, the grammar left intact;
(ii) a notation whose syntax is genuinely pictorial, so that the structure of the representation mirrors the structure of what is represented;
(iii) a practical instrument of mass communication that is language-like in its uses but not a language in its own right.

Reading (ii) is the one that carries a suggestion of (b): if the picture mirrors the structure of the fact, then the picture might be thought to answer to the world directly, without the detour through sentences.

Discuss whether Neurath's Isotype work commits him to reading (ii), and whether it gives pictures the epistemic authority that his account of protocol statements withholds from sentences. Your answer should say what role protocol statements play in Neurath's physicalism, what he took a picture language to be for, and how the distinction between (a) and (b) bears on the case.

Candidate's answer:

Neurath's epistemology is an attack on the very idea that science has a foundation. Protocol statements are not, on his account, incorrigible reports of private experience; they are ordinary physicalistic sentences, sentences about observers and their doings, and they stand in no privileged relation to the world. Since no class of sentences is immune to revision, the business of science is not to build upward from a secure base but to repair a going concern — the ship rebuilt plank by plank at sea, the image that Neurath took over from Quine. The force of the argument lies in the impossibility of a starting point rather than in any skepticism about particular results, and it would be a mistake to read Neurath as a relativist: revisions are constrained, but constrained by the rest of the system rather than by a given.

Isotype looks at first like a reversal of all this. At the Museum of Society and Economy in Vienna, Neurath and his collaborators turned statistical material into charts for a mass audience, with Gerd Arntz drawing the symbols. The first rule of the method was that a larger quantity is shown by more symbols of the same size rather than by one larger symbol, so that the reader counts rather than estimates; the aim was to make social facts visible to people who would never open a statistical yearbook. The slogan he had painted on the wall of his office — "words divide, pictures unite" — is not the slogan of a merely decorative enterprise.

I want to argue that the Isotype chart is a picture language in sense (ii), and that this dissolves the apparent tension. A chart is not a sentence in disguise. It works because the arrangement of the symbols reproduces the arrangement of the facts, so that a reader who can see the picture can read the relation off it without translating it into a proposition. In this respect the picture language realizes an old idea, common to the Tractatus and to Neurath's own physicalism, that a representation is meaningful because it shares its form with what it represents. If sentences can never be brought face to face with the world, pictures can; and that is why a chart can be handed to someone with no training and trusted, while a protocol statement has to be weighed against its neighbors.

It might be objected that the charts involve a great deal of selection and transformation: the designer decides what to include, at what scale, and under what heading, so that the finished chart is the product of those decisions. But this is a practical matter, a question of good drafting, and it does not touch the status of the finished chart, which is a neutral transcription of the facts it presents. The picture language therefore answers question (b) as well as question (a): it says what needs saying, and it settles what is to be accepted. Neurath's anti-foundationalism was a thesis about sentences, and it leaves the pictorial register free.
