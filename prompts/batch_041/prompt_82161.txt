I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group I run — half stats grad students, half philosophy grad students, which is why I keep getting talked past on this — and instead of the usual list of names I want the handout to be a decision tree of the positions on the reference class problem. The thing I mean by it is the general one: you have statistics about a class (smokers, radium atoms, balanced coins run through a particular flipping machine) and you want to say something about a single member, but the individual belongs to indefinitely many classes that hand you different answers, so which one is the right one? It's struck me that people saying "the reference class problem" mean at least three different problems, and I want the tree to force that out early: (a) the semantic one, which class a single-case probability statement even refers to; (b) the epistemic one, given my total evidence which class I should conditionalize on; (c) the pragmatic one, which class an insurer or a doctor or a court should actually use when they have to act. So the first branch should be which of those you think the real problem is, and everything else hangs off that.

Please make every node a question somebody could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and build the tree out of the logical space first — the questions and which combinations of answers they allow — and only then hang names on the leaves. I want no more than eight leaves, and these need to come out somewhere: strict frequentism (single cases have no probability at all), propensity views, subjective Bayesianism where the worry dissolves because there's no unique correct class, objective Bayesianism with something like a maximal-specificity or max-entropy rule for choosing the class, the causal-relevance line where the class is fixed by what's causally relevant to the outcome rather than by what happens to be statistically associated, the view that the class is fixed by the decision you're facing, and the deflationary view that there's no fact of the matter but the problem is still real for anyone who wants direct inference to count as a valid form of inference. If two of those definately collapse into each other, merge them and say why rather than faking a seperate distinction.

Cases I want used somewhere on the tree, because they're the ones that actually pull the positions apart: a coin you've verified is balanced but that's run through a machine you've seen come up heads 60% of the time on other balanced coins; a radium atom in a sample; a patient whose mortality-table class and genetic class disagree; Sleeping Beauty, where the fight is exactly over which class the awakening belongs to; the Doomsday argument; and an actuary pricing one policy, where the class gets chosen by regulation and practice rather than by truth. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I just want the instrument. Give it to me in this order: "The opening questions" (about a paragraph, three or four questions max, each with the answers that split the tree); "The tree" (a nested outline, branch label at every node, as deep as it needs to go, keep it under a page); "The leaves" (one line on the position's central claim, one line on its most awkward commitment); "Empty branches" (the answer combinations nobody occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored); and "What the tree can't separate" (pairs of positions that come out the same on every case I listed, and what extra case would be needed to pull them apart).
