In 1998 the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management lost roughly $4.6 billion in a matter of months, after the Russian default, and on 23 September of that year a consortium of fourteen banks recapitalized it with about $3.65 billion in a deal convened under the supervision of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. I want to use that episode as a test case for the common prior assumption. The facts I care about are these: the fund was founded in 1994 by John Meriwether and counted Myron Scholes and Robert Merton among its principals, the two of them sharing the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics; it ran a large, highly leveraged book of convergence trades, betting that spreads between closely related securities would narrow; and among its positions it was short equity volatility, which is to say it was in the business of selling insurance. Both sides of its trades were sophisticated, both worked from the same public data, and the models they used were, to a first approximation, the same models.

Set out precisely what the common prior assumption asserts — as a modeling device in Harsanyi's treatment of games with incomplete information, as the premise of Aumann's result that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, and as the engine of the no-trade results, Milgrom and Stokey's version in particular — and say what each of these requires over and above a common prior, including what common knowledge of rationality is doing in them. Then map the episode onto the framework: who the players are, what each side's information and prior were, whether the divergence between the fund and its counterparties was a divergence in information, in priors, in objectives, or in something else, which of its trades the no-trade theorem should have forbidden, and why the other side existed at all. The theorem has escape hatches — heterogeneous priors, risk-sharing and gains from trade, liquidity and inventory motives, non-common knowledge of the structure of the game, participants who are not expected-value maximizers over the same payoffs. Tell me which of them are load-bearing here and which are merely available.

Then run the analysis the other way, since that is the part I care about most. Does the episode falsify the assumption, or does it show the assumption was never meant descriptively — and how would one tell the difference? Two things in particular. First, the crowded trade. The fund's positions were common knowledge on the street, and the framework seems to predict that shared information and shared priors yield agreement and, absent gains from trade, no speculative trade at all. What killed the fund was not disagreement but agreement: everyone was on the same side, and the shared models that produced the agreement are also what made the positions correlated. Is the informal sense in which the street had a common prior — one shared model, one shared worldview — anything more than a pun on the technical sense, or is the pun tracking something real? Second, the level at which the assumption does its work. A common prior is a prior over a fixed state space, and it presupposes that the players share the space: the same relevant states, the same model class. If the failure here is better located at the level of the state space than at the level of the prior, say so, and say what that does to the assumption's role in the theory. I would also like a verdict on the market-selection argument often used to defend the assumption — that traders with deviant priors lose money and are selected out, so surviving priors must be roughly common — given that this fund was staffed by people who had built much of the theory of financial markets and was selected out anyway.

No citations or reading lists; I want the argument. And where the framework runs out, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
