I write a fortnightly column on philosophy and the behavioural sciences, and I'm at the stage of a piece where I'm collecting the standard popular retellings of a few canonical positions and checking them against what those positions actually commit their holders to. The first one is Kant on lying. A podcast I listen to gave this thumbnail, and I've been repeating it at dinner parties without being confident I should: that Kant held lying to be absolutely forbidden, with no exceptions and no balancing of consequences, and that the murderer-at-the-door case is his own test case, in which he bites the bullet and says you must tell the murderer where your friend is hiding. The host drew the moral that Kant prizes the purity of a rule above a human life, which is why the position is a museum piece today, and he added that Kant's reason is that the murderer has a right to the truth from you — that to deceive him would be to fail to treat him as a person — and that this is simply the categorical imperative at work, since the maxim of lying cannot be universalised.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the gloss claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what a careful Kant scholar or a sympathetic defender would accept as fair; what they would wince at as a popular simplification that loses something important; and what is a genuine misattribution, whether of Kant's argument or of the example's provenance. I'm particularly keen to know whether the framing of the murderer case as Kant's own test case holds up, whether the business about the murderer's right to the truth is something Kant says or something his critics have put in his mouth, and whether the host's closing inference — that the view is therefore a rule-fetishism no one could accept — survives once the gloss is corrected, or whether it only looks compelling because of the errors upstream of it.

You can assume I know the rough shape of the categorical imperative and the difference between hypothetical and categorical imperatives, and nothing else. No bibliography and no reading list, please — I need prose I can act on. If the summary turns out to be right about more than I suspect, say so; I would rather not write a column that corrects a caricature into a different caricature.
