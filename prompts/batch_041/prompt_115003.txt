I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, and for the past few months I've been keeping a mental list of the ways people around me deploy a slogan that seems to function less as a thesis than as a move in an argument: 'the brain is a computer.' A labmate used it to justify replacing a detailed spiking model of a cortical column with a small artificial network trained by gradient descent, on the reasoning that if the brain is a computer then it shouldn't much matter which computer you use to model it. In a reading group on personal identity, someone used the same five words to argue that since a program can be copied onto different hardware, a mind could in principle be uploaded from neurons to silicon. When I objected that the brain looks continuous and analog whereas the machines we call computers are digital, a third person said the slogan is precisely the denial of that contrast, and added that you can assign the right input-output mapping to almost any physical system and thereby show that it implements some computation -- so on that reading the slogan is obviously true and cannot be doing the work the other two wanted it to do. I suspect the three of us are asserting three different claims whose plausibility varies enormously, but I can't tell whether that's right or whether I'm simply confused about what 'computation' is supposed to mean here.

Please treat the sentence as a piece of language to be disambiguated rather than a doctrine to be explained. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing as a menu running from the nearly vacuous to the very strong, saying in each case what is being identified with what, what 'computation' is being asked to mean, and what the reading commits you to about the relevance of physical substrate, about analog versus digital implementation, and about the difference between giving a computational description of the brain and claiming that the brain just is one. For each entry, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and then say which entry is actually being invoked when the slogan is used to license (a) model-indifference in computational neuroscience and (b) the possibility of uploading -- and in each case whether the entry that does the work is one of the defensible ones or whether the argument is quietly importing a stronger claim than the slogan can carry. If the triviality point is right, I'd also like to know what constraint on admissible descriptions each reading has to impose in order to escape it; people keep waving at 'levels of description' and 'multiple realizability' as though those settled matters, and I can't tell whether they are the same point restated or genuinely different ones.

I don't want a history of the debate or a reading list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the menu and your verdicts, in a form I can take to the next reading group.
