I'm a doctoral student in constitutional law and I do the drafting for a small informal body that advises our head of state's office on the reserve powers. Five of us: a retired judge, a constitutional historian, two former private secretaries, and me. The country is a parliamentary monarchy on the Westminster model — ceremonial head of state, elected house of 300, a PM who has to be able to command the confidence of that house, statutory four-year terms, no codified constitution. Our opinions aren't binding, but the office has never gone against one. The panel meets Thursday on something live and I'm the junior one, so alot rides on whether I've read the record right. I'd like you to act as an expert on Westminster conventions and the reserve powers and work through this with me: no reading list and no citations please, I can't get online right now and half of what I'd want to cite lives in scanned parliamentary records anyway. I want the reasoning from our own record. Please brainstorm the candidate rules first and only then commit to one.

Here's the record. (1) PM lost a confidence vote by 9 and refused to resign, asking for a dissolution 18 months into the term; an opposition leader had a signed agreement with a minor party worth 168 of 300 seats. We advised: refuse the dissolution, and once the PM resigned, commission the alternative. (2) PM lost a confidence vote; the two largest opposition parties had publicly ruled out any arrangement with each other, so no alternative majority existed in any form; he asked for a dissolution 20 months into the term. We advised: grant it. (3) Same shape as (2), but the statutory election date was five weeks away. We advised: refuse; the PM stays on as a caretaker with a limited agenda until the scheduled date. (4) A government that still commanded the house comfortably asked for a six-week prorogation — lawful, well inside its ordinary powers, formally to "reset the legislative programme," but plainly timed to kill a backbench bill that would have embarrassed two ministers. We advised: refuse the long prorogation, offer only the ordinary recess. One member dissented then and still does, on the ground that the Crown has no business second-guessing a confident government acting lawfully. (5) A bill the head of state personally loathed passed both houses on a free vote; we advised assent without hesitation. (6) After an inconclusive election the largest party, 132 of 300, couldn't assemble a majority; a bloc of 122 plus two minor parties totalling 158 could, and the largest party's leader asked for a dissolution only three weeks after polling day. We advised: refuse; let the house meet and let the alternative be tested on the floor. The incumbent resigned, the alternative was commissioned, and it survived its confidence vote by 6.

Now the live one. Election four months ago. The incumbent party got the most votes nationwide but only 128 seats, down twenty. The other bloc is 122 + 20 + 10, so 152 against a majority line of 151 — but the 20-seat partner has said publicly it won't vote for the budget, and the 10-seat partner says it will only turn up for confidence motions. The house has not yet met. The incumbent PM refuses to resign, argues that the only alternative is a coalition that will fall over at the first budget, and asks for a dissolution. The alternative leader says she can command the house and should be commissioned. So: reconstruct the rules that actually fit our six opinions — I suspect there's more than one, and I'd like the ones that fit everything, not just the ones that fit comfortably — tell me what each predicts here, then give me your best guess with your reasons, name which precedent is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy breaks down, and be honest about what principle we'd be adopting if we refuse versus if we grant. Then tell me the case that would best tell the surviving rules apart, because I'd rather test them than keep relitigating this one. And don't forget to say what the dissenting member would make of all of it.
