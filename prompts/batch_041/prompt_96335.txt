I am a sixth-form student taking a philosophy of mind elective, and I have been trying to make sense of an argument a classmate produced when we were discussing multiple realizability. We had been asking whether pain could be realised in silicon as well as in carbon-based brains, and I had thought that multiple realizability was one of functionalism's strengths; now I am not so sure. He began from the thought that a mental state is not a particular physical state but a functional state -- a node in a causal network of inputs, outputs, and other states -- and then argued as follows. (1) A mental state is constituted by its functional role. (2) If a physical system possesses the functional organisation characteristic of a mind, then it has a mind. (3) Any physical system can be assigned a functional organisation characteristic of a mind, provided that we are allowed to choose a sufficiently fine-grained mapping between its physical states and the states of that organisation. (4) So a thermostat, a pile of sand, or an entire nation whose citizens are each assigned the role of a neuron has a mind. (5) That is absurd. (6) Therefore one of (1)-(3) must be false.

He insists that (3) is simply a mathematical fact about the availability of arbitrary mappings -- he says that even a rock can be described as implementing a look-up table that maps every possible input to a mental state -- and that the only way to resist the argument is to deny (2), thereby giving up the functionalist claim that functional organisation suffices for mentality. I suspect that the argument proves too much, and that the real difficulty lies in the move from a mere description of a system to the claim that the system realises the functional organisation in question. But I cannot put my finger on it. Which step should I reject? If I reject (3), what does functionalism have to say about the difference between a system that genuinely implements a functional role and a system that can merely be mapped onto one by an ingenious interpreter? And if I reject (2), what becomes of multiple realizability? I would much rather keep (1) and (2) and find a flaw in (3) than accept (5), but I want to know what that costs.
