I have been reconstructing an argument from a reading group and cannot decide whether it is a proof or a trick. Here is the machinery it assumes. Call a belief safe when it is true in every nearby possible world in which you hold it on the same evidence; and grant that you know something on the basis of your evidence only if your belief is safe, with a margin to spare, since knowledge cannot sit on the very edge of error. The argument runs:

(1) To know P on the basis of your evidence, your belief that P must be safe.
(2) If a possible world is consistent with your total evidence, it counts as nearby for the purposes of (1).
(3) There is a world consistent with your total evidence in which you are a handless brain in a vat and so have no hands.
(4) That world is nearby. [from 2, 3]
(5) In it your belief that you have two hands is false, though your evidence is unchanged.
(6) That belief is not safe. [from 4, 5]
(7) You do not know you have two hands. [from 1, 6]
(8) Nothing turned on hands; the pattern repeats for any belief about the external world.
(9) You know nothing about the external world.

I cannot accept (9), and I would sooner keep (1) than give it up, because the safety idea appears to be doing real work elsewhere. So which step should go, why did it look innocent, and what do I pay for cutting it? In particular, if (2) is the culprit, does dropping it leave the safety principle any teeth — can we still run the margin-of-error reasoning about warmth, pain, and other conditions that come in degrees, or do we lose that too?
