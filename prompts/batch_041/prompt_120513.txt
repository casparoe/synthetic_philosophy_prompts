Ok so I'm making a handout for a metaphysics class on material constitution and I need a fresh case, not the statue and the clay, not the ship of Theseus, and please dont just rename them and hand them back to me. I want two things, call them A and B, made of exactly the same stuff, in exactly the same place, with exactly the same parts at every moment they both exist, so you can't pull them apart by composition or by pointing at them. But their life stories have to cross: one of them starts existing first, the other one finishes last, and neither one's lifetime sits inside the other's. So not the usual setup where the lump is there before and after the statue — each of them has to outlast the other at a different end, and they overlap in the middle.

Some constraints, because I've seen people cheat on this. Ordinary situation, a garden or a workshop or a museum, no teleporters, no gods, no atom-by-atom duplicates, no sci-fi, and the whole case in one paragraph. And the stuff-side, the one playing the role the clay plays, cant be a lump of dead matter — it has to be something whose identity over time isnt just a matter of which bits it's made of, because I want the lazy 'the matter is the more basic thing' intution to be unavailable and have to be argued for. The diference in their lifetimes has to fall out of what kind of thing each one is, not be stipulated by you. And if any part of the case only works because we quietly assume something about biology, or about what counts as killing something, flag it instead of smuggling it in.

Then do the check. What does each of these say about your case, and why:

- the coincidence / constitution view — two distinct objects, one constitutes the other, distinct because their persistence conditions differ
- the one-object view where A and B are just two sortals we apply to a single thing, with the persistence conditions of whichever sortal is dominant
- the eliminativist line that keeps some composite objects but bins artefacts (you know the one)
- the four-dimensionalist

Then the honest bit, which is probaly why I'm asking. Which feature of the case is doing the real work? If I take that feature out and put dead matter back in, does it collapse into the standard statue/clay case with the lifetimes nested instead of crossing, and does the modal argument for distinctness then run in its usual direction? And how does the constitution theorist attack the case — my guess is they go after the biology, but tell me if there's a better move, and what that move costs them elsewhere. If two of the four views above actually agree here, say so rather than inventing a difference.
