Right, so I'm a novelist and I'm about three drafts into a book where the main character keeps a diary, and the diary slowly goes wrong — she starts writing entries like "It's raining but I don't believe it's raining", and she can't work out why they read as nonsense even though, as far as she can tell, both halves could be true at once. I know this is Moore's paradox from a bit of pop philosophy, but I want to interview someone properly about it and I dont want to ask the obvious stuff she could get off a Wikipedia skim.

So take the role of a philosopher of language who's spent years on this stuff, and help me prep. Think carefully before you start writing, maybe brainstorm the angles you'd cover first, then turn them into the questions.

The guest I've got lined up defends a fairly hard line: she reckons Moore-paradoxical utterances are genuinely contradictory in what they express — not merely pragmatically odd, not just a conversational misfire, but a real clash between what the speaker commits herself to by the first half and what she commits herself to by the second. I want questions that press her on that.

Give me eight to ten questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — so really two lines per question. I want at least one on each of these:

- the difference between the "omissive" version ("p but I don't believe p") and the "commissive" one ("p but I believe not-p"), and why she thinks one is the harder case;
- what happens when you embed the thing: under "Mary thinks that...", under a conditional, in the past tense ("it was raining but I didn't believe it" sounds perfectly fine) — does the contradiction survive, and if it doesn't, doesn't that undercut the "it's a real contradiction" line?
- the self-deception case: is there a coherent description of someone who believes p and also believes she doesn't believe p, and does her view forbid it?
- whether the absurdity is specific to belief or to any first-person state — suppose the diary read "it's raining but I don't expect it to rain", is that the same kind of bad, or a different one?
- and the awkward neighbour, the preface/lottery sort of case, where someone can rationally believe each of a pile of things while also believing one of them is false — is that person in a Moore-paradoxical state, and if not, what's the difference?

Don't waste any of them on "so what drew you to this?" filler, and don't let her just restate the view. I want the ones that make her sweat a bit.

Finally, end with one line in exactly this form: \answer{...} — and inside the braces, just a short phrase naming which of your questions you reckon she'll have the hardest time with.
