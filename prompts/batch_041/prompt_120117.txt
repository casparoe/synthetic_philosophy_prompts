I am a doctoral student in history, midway through a project on poor relief in a mid-sized industrial city, working mostly from parish ledgers, charity registers, and the occasional petition, and I keep colliding with an argument I do not know how to answer. The records I use were produced by institutions that had reasons to write some things down and not others; the people I care about most, the mobile and the destitute, surface only as an entry in a list, or a note in a margin, or not at all. Whenever I say this in a seminar, my supervisor makes the same move. He says the archive is like the set of bomber aircraft that came home from their missions in the Second World War: the planes that returned were riddled with holes in some places and nearly untouched in others, and the statistician advising the air force pointed out that this pattern told you nothing about where the aircraft needed armour, because the planes that were struck in the places where the returning planes showed no damage were precisely the ones that never made it back. The archive, on this view, is just the set of planes that came home: the holes we can see in it are a biased sample of the holes that were made, and the remedy is better source criticism, not a theory of power. The whole literature on silences in the archives, he thinks, dresses up a statistical truism as politics.

What I want from you is an audit of the comparison rather than a verdict on the politics. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what in my case plays the role of the returning planes, the planes that never returned, the bullet holes, the armourer deciding where to put the plating, and the enemy doing the shooting — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in whether an archive has anything corresponding to a knowable population of aircraft that flew; whether the holes and the missing planes have the same cause in my case or different ones; whether an absence in the record can itself be evidence of a decision rather than a mere gap; and whether the analogy quietly assumes a single deciding agent with a single objective, where an archive instead involves an apparatus of clerks, committees, retention schedules, and custodians with their own interests. Then tell me whether my supervisor's conclusion survives the disanalogies — that silences call for better source criticism and nothing more — and, if it does not, what comparison would carry the weight he wants better than the bombers do.
