Alright, so I've been lurking around rat-adjacent AI safety stuff for like a decade now (Sequences, the agent foundations corner of the internet, all of it), and I've talked myself into a position I want properly stress-tested rather than just nodded along with.

Here's the position. Any agent that's strong enough to do real logic *about itself* — specifically, one that wants to trust its own future reasoning enough to self-modify, or to build a successor and hand it the reins — runs into a wall. The wall is basically Löb's theorem in a trenchcoat. If you want to prove "whatever I eventually decide to do is fine," you need something like "if I decide to do X, then X is fine," and Löb says that flavor of self-referential trust collapses: you either can't get the proof you want, or you get it in a way that's trivial and blows up your consistency (which is just Gödel's second theorem again in different clothes). So the upshot I've come to believe is: you can't have a powerful agent that *provably* trusts itself or its successors. Full stop. It's not an engineering gap to be closed later, it's a logical barrier. I'll call it fundamental.

What I want from you is the defeater map. What would actually count against this? I don't just mean hand-wavy "maybe there's a clever trick" — I mean which formal results, which explicit constructions, or which (if any) real-world developments should move me. Sort them by how decisive they'd be, from fatal to merely awkward.

Some specific things I keep going back and forth on and want your take:

A friend insists you dodge the whole thing by making the agent's self-trust merely probabilistic — like 0.999 rather than certain — or by capping it at bounded proof search / limited logical strength so Löb never bites. Does that actually escape the obstacle, or does it just relocate the bump somewhere less convenient (or smuggle in the consistency assumption out the back)? I genuinely can't tell if this is a real out or a cope.

Second, what would the world even have to look like for the barrier to be escapable in the strong sense? If the answer is "some restricted logic / weaker self-reference scheme we haven't found yet," what would such a thing have to do that current ones can't, and is that a live research direction or a logical impossibility in disguise?

Third — and this is the part I care most about — I worry the position has quietly become unfalsifiable. It's a theorem-flavored claim, not an empirical one, so "what would count against it" isn't the usual kind of question. Is the honest answer that nothing could count against it, and that this is fine because it's math? Or has it been armored up so that every proposed escape gets waved off as "that's not *real* self-trust," which would make it a problem? I want the defenders' own concessions here, not just what critics shout from the sidelines. What would someone who holds this view themselves admit counts against them?

And if you think there's a specific construction out there — or plausibly out there — that genuinely does let a self-modifying agent trust its successor without inconsistency, tell me how decisive that would be, and whether it'd sink the "fundamental" part or just the "no engineering fix ever" part.

Don't flatter the position. If it turns out to be well-armored, say so and say whether thats actually bad. I'd rather find out its a fortress than get reassured.
