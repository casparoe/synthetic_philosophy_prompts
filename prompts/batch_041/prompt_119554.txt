For the past forty years I worked in experimental physics; since retiring I have been reading my way into formal epistemology, and this term I have agreed to contribute a single question to the final examination of an upper-level undergraduate course in Bayesian epistemology. The students are comfortable with calculus and probability but have read almost no philosophy, so the question must be self-contained and closed-book: no citations, no reading list, no dependence on the students having met this puzzle before, and no names of publications. This question has 45 minutes allotted to it and carries 25 marks.

I have drafted the setup below. Please keep the numbers and the stipulations exactly as they stand; you may polish the prose, but nothing in the mathematical content may change, since the marking scheme will depend on it.

Setup. An agent is dropped by parachute into a territory that is either Red or Blue, and she is located either at Headquarters or at Second Company, giving four possibilities: Red-Headquarters, Red-Second Company, Blue-Headquarters, Blue-Second Company. Before receiving any information she assigns probability 1/4 to each. She then receives a radio broadcast that reaches her wherever she is (the broadcast is not targeted at any region or unit), and she takes its content to be certainly true: conditional on her being in Red territory, the probability that she is at Headquarters is 3/4. She has no other information.

The question has four parts:
(a) Explain why ordinary conditionalization cannot handle this report, and why Jeffrey conditionalization does not straightforwardly apply either. For each rule, state what it requires and which requirement fails here. (5 marks)
(b) Adopting the rule that the posterior should minimize relative information (Kullback-Leibler divergence) with respect to her prior, subject to the constraint carried by the report, compute her posterior. Give the four probabilities to three decimals, show the algebra, and state what happens to her probability that she is in Red. (10 marks)
(c) The result in (b) is widely felt to be objectionable, on the ground that the broadcast reaches her whether she is in Red or in Blue, so hearing it should not alter the probability that she is in Red. Give the strongest argument for that objection, the strongest reply to it, and a verdict on whether the reply succeeds. (6 marks)
(d) Suppose instead that the broadcast is transmitted only to agents in Red territory, and that the agent knows this. What are her probabilities now, and what does the contrast with (b) reveal about what the constraint-based treatment in (b) presupposes? (4 marks)

Please supply three things.

First, the examination question itself, polished, with the parts and marks as given above and nothing added that would let a student answer by reciting a summary.

Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words. It should answer each part directly, show the intermediate steps of the computation in (b) rather than asserting a result, and answer (c) and (d) with an explicit verdict rather than a survey of options.

Third, a marking scheme worth 25 marks in total, built from five or six must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose marks on it. The scheme should name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one, and it should give the numerical values in (b) to three decimals while awarding full credit for a correct method with a slipped arithmetic step. It should also identify the most natural wrong answer to (b) — keeping the probability of Red at 1/2 and rescaling only within Red — and say how much credit that answer deserves and why. I care more that the derivation in (b) is actually correct than that it matches any number I may have jotted down, so if any part of the mathematics turns out not to have a determinate answer, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

Finally, I would like the model answer to be explicit about the difference between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material. Do not present a contested position as the consensus; where you are offering a defensible line rather than the received view, mark it as such, and where a verdict depends on a choice of updating rule rather than on the mathematics, flag that dependence.
