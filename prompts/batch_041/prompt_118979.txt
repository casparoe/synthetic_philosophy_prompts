ok this has been bugging me and i dont really have the words for it. people recommend sad movies by how hard they made them cry. "i sobbed for like an hour, you have to watch it" — the crying is the pitch, not the warning. but then the same person, about the same movie, will tell a friend whos going through a breakup, no wait, dont watch that right now, itll wreck you. so the same crying is the selling point one minute and the thing you shield someone from the next. and then theres the other wierd one: people have a sad movie theyve seen ten times, they know every beat thats coming, and they put it on when they feel like crap, like a comfort blanket. a comfort blanket that makes them cry.

what im trying to figure out is what all of that assumes about sad art. where does it think the good part actually is? does it assume the sadness itself is somehow the nice part, which sounds like a contradiction? or that were not really sad when we watch? or that the good part is something riding on top of the sadness — the release, the feeling of being moved, the god that was beautiful feeling once its over, feeling close to the characters? a friend of mine says she watches them to get it all out, like a purge, and another one says she doesnt even feel sad during them, she feels full, which seem like two pretty different stories about whats going on.

so: if you had to write down the theory of sad art that this whole way of talking and behaving is running on, what is it? which parts does it actually need, and which parts would survive if you swapped the theory out for a rival? and where is it just undecided — where would two totally different theories explain everything people say and do here? and last thing, if the real story turned out to be that were not genuinely sad during a movie, were just playing along, or the opposite, that the sadness is real and were paying that cost for something else, what would actually look different about how people recommend these things and rewatch them?
