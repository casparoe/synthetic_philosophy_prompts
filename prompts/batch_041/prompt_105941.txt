My old flatmate has an argument he swears shows we shouldn't bother giving asymbolia patients painkillers, and I can't tell wether it's clever or just wrong. He lays it out like this:

(1) What makes pain bad for you is that it hurts -- the unpleasantness, the way it makes you want it to stop.
(2) In pain asymbolia, people feel pain perfectly well (they can say where it is, whether it's sharp or dull) but report that it doesn't hurt and doesn't bother them.
(3) So their pain isn't bad for them.
(4) There's no reason to relieve a state that isn't bad for the person who's in it.
(5) So there's no reason to give asymbolics painkillers or to treat their pain as something to be gotten rid of.

(5) seems obviously wrong -- these patients still don't flinch or protect the injured limb the way you'd expect, and we do treat them. But (4) looks like a truism and (1) is the whole point of the phenomenon. I'd rather not give up (1), because if the badness of pain isn't its hurting then I honestly don't know what it is. So which step do I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me?
