I'm applying for a one-year fellowship (no teaching, the usual deal) and I need a two-page research proposal out of something I've been chewing on since a shouting match in the seminar room last term. The domain is that old muddle about when a moral attitude is a preference and when it's a bias. It shows up everywhere once you start looking: the prospective juror who swears she can be impartial and says she just holds traditional views about marriage; the admissions-committee member who insists he isn't prejudiced, he simply prefers applicants "like the ones we've always taken" (there I go quoting him, sorry); the hospital ethics board trying to decide whether a surgeon's refusal to take part in a procedure is conscience or animus; my brother-in-law, who has views about who should be doing what in a household and genuinely experiences them as tastes. Institutions have to sort these cases and they sort them badly, because every test on offer is a test of the attitude itself — is it sincere, is it identity-defining, is it responsive to evidence, does it serve an unjust hierarchy — and the nasty cases seem to satisfy every test on the "preference" side while still being things we're pretty sure are biases.

My half-formed thought is that the taxonomy is missing a slot. Take the shape of a decision model: you've got credences (what the person thinks) and utilities (what the person wants), and "bias" gets used for both, sloppily — sometimes it means a false belief about a group, sometimes a warped desire regarding one. But I suspect a lot of what we call bias is neither: it's a distortion of what makes it into deliberation at all — which features of a situation are salient, what gets noticed, what never even arrives on the list of things to be weighed. There's a strand in ethics, the perception tradition, where the basic moral act is looking properly at something rather than choosing correctly about it, and I think that strand can be turned into a working third category that does real institutional work, because the remedies come apart: you accommodate a preference, you correct a false belief, but you neither accommodate nor correct an attentional structure — you train it, through habituation and exposure. That would license a different toolkit (structured deliberation, exemplars, repeated exposure) that nobody currently recommends, because nobody has the category.
