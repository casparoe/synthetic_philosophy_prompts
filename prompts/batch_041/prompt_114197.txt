Hey, so I'm a junior in high school and I run the philosophy club at my school. This semster we did a unit on hedonism (the idea that pleasure is the only thing thats good in itself, and that everything else is good only because it brings pleasure or stops pain). I'm the one who basically teaches it, so I have to write the final, which is fun but also kind of a lot.

The problem is everyone in the club has read the same intro stuff, so if I just ask "what is hedonism" or "compare Bentham and Mill" they'll all write the same recycled paragraphs and I won't learn anything. What I actually want is a question that makes them deal with the fact that pleasure looks like a total mess. Like — what does the pleasure of scratching a mosquito bite have in common with the pleasure of hearing that your least favorite teammate got benched? Or the pleasure of finally getting a math proof? Or the gross little pleasure of pressing on a bruise? If a hedonist says all of these are "pleasure," what is the thing they're all supposed to share? And if there's nothing they share, does hedonism even survive as a theory?

So here's some stuff I made up myself that I want the question to force them to use, so they can't just regurgitate:
- a case where it's only pleasant looking back — like a really hard run that felt awful the whole time but you're glad you did it and you'd do it again
- a case where you get exactly what you wanted and it feels like nothing — you wanted the last slice of pizza for like an hour, you eat it, and it's just... meh
- a case where the physical feeling is basically the same but it flips from pleasure to pain depending on your mood or the situation — getting tickled when you're happy vs being tickled when you're already annoyed

Please write me:
1. the essay question itself (just one, they get about 50 minutes)
2. a model answer, like 600 words, that actually picks a side and defends it instead of just listing views
3. a marking scheme out of 20, that names the specific moves an A answer has to make, the specific mistakes that should cost marks, and a one-line note on how people usually get each one wrong

Keep the model answer at a level a smart 16-year-old could follow — the club isn't all future philosophy majors.

Two more things. First, at the end of the model answer, can you say how confident you are in what you're claiming and single out the point you're least sure about? I don't want to stand up there and teach something that's actually wrong. Second, please don't tell me to go read a bunch of books or give me citations and page numbers, because I can't look anything up and neither can the kids — it needs to be self-contained.
