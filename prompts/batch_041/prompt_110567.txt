The claim I want priced: at least one large language model currently in use has genuine propositional attitudes — internal states that are correctly described as beliefs about particular facts, rather than states we can only usefully treat as if they were beliefs.

I want the claim precise enough to have a truth value, so let me stipulate the criterion I will be using, and ask you to price the claim under it and then, separately, under whatever criterion you think is better, so that I can see the gap between the two. The criterion: an attributed belief is genuine only if it is a node in a causal structure rather than a summary of behaviour — that is, only if intervening on it, somewhat independently of the behaviour it explains, produces the downstream effects that the belief-attribution predicts, in the way that changing a thermostat's set point rather than its output changes what it later does. Note the wrinkle that makes this a live question for models rather than an idle one: we can now routinely perturb internal activations along directions that shift behaviour in predictable ways. The question is whether such perturbations are interventions on the attributed belief or merely on a vehicle that happens to carry it, and how one would tell the difference.

Decompose at least these terms, with a number and one line of justification for each:

- the probability that current models pass the intervention criterion, conditional on realism being correct;
- the probability that passing it is sufficient, i.e. that no further condition (causal history, biological function, phenomenal presence, or something else) is required for belief;
- the probability that we could in practice tell whether a given intervention targeted a belief rather than a vehicle, given current interpretability methods.
