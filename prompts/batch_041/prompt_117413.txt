I write a monthly column on ideas and the social sciences, and I am at the early stage of a long piece on the three-way contrast that has lately been drawn between honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures — the suggestion being that societies, or segments of them, differ in how they answer the question of what a person's worth consists in and what one does when it is attacked: with self-help and the defense of reputation; with an appeal to rights and the law while treating the insult as beneath notice; or with a claim to recognition of injury that third parties are asked to affirm. Before I build a piece on that contrast, and before I let the piece take a position on whether any of the three is morally better than the others, I want the specification rather than the verdict. Tell me what any account of this kind — one that ranks, or even merely orders, such cultures on a moral scale — would have to deliver in order to be adequate.

Give me the requirements list, with the consideration behind each: the cases it must get right (a duel; a slur that a dignity culture tells you to ignore; a public accusation that only an apology and an acknowledgment can settle), the distinctions it cannot collapse (a culture's professed code versus the conduct that code actually produces; the descriptive classification of a moral order versus the justification of it; a culture's own standards versus the standards from which we judge it; the standing of the individual versus that of the group), and the facts about how such orders function that it cannot contradict (that each of them has been defended as a way of securing order without endless violence; that all three coexist within a single society, so the unit of analysis is itself contested). Say which of the requirements are genuine and which are the prejudices of one camp — the demand for a neutral vantage point is a candidate for the latter, and so is the demand that the account be able to call one culture an improvement on another. If some pair of them cannot be satisfied together, say so plainly, because that is the result I most want from you; the requirement that the taxonomy serve as a moral ranking and the requirement that it not smuggle the ranking into the description is the pair I suspect. Then, briefly, name the single requirement that does the most to eliminate otherwise attractive answers, and say how the three most familiar positions fare against your list: the one that reads the sequence as progress, the one that reads it as decline, and the one that refuses the comparison altogether. I don't need a reading list or citations — I need the criteria and the cases that motivate them.
