My oldest friend and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about lottocracy, and the argument went badly, because we were each defending a different institution while using the same word for it. I've since decided that the only way to get anywhere is to hold the recruitment mechanism fixed and vary only what the allotted body is actually empowered to do, and then ask which of the resulting designs is worth having. So for everything below, assume identical selection and support: members drawn by stratified lottery from the electoral register, mandatory service for those drawn, a generous per-diem, childcare, and a professional staff; deliberation in small facilitated groups before any plenary vote; and the same nominal size in every case. Vary only the institutional role. The five designs we kept sliding between are these. (1) A permanent allotted chamber with staggered two-year terms and a suspensive veto over ordinary legislation — it can force a bill back to the elected house for reconsideration, but cannot kill it, and it has no say over the budget or foreign affairs. (2) An ad hoc allotted assembly that exists only when a petition carrying the signatures of three per cent of the electorate forces one into being; it decides that single question, its decision is binding on the legislature, and the question's wording is fixed by the petitioners. (3) A standing allotted body with agenda power but no decision power: it chooses which questions go to the elected legislature or to a referendum, and never resolves anything itself. (4) Allotted panels that issue recommendations which the legislature is obliged to answer in writing, with reasons, within a fixed period — but with no binding force at all. (5) A second chamber in which half the seats are allotted and half elected, the two halves sitting and voting together as a single body.

What I want is a comparative judgment rather than a general verdict. Please place all five in tiers — A, B, C — according to how much you would expect each, if it were actually running in a large liberal democracy over something like twenty years, to improve the epistemic quality of that country's collective decisions relative to its own status quo. Judge them by expected effect, not by how elegant the design is and not by how likely it is to be adopted, and give each a score between 0 and 1 alongside its tier. For each design, one or two sentences on the mechanism that drives your placement, and then name the single failure mode most likely to hollow it out. After that, tell me which of the five you think would degrade fastest and why — my friend's position, which I am not yet willing to concede, is that they all rot into either irrelevance or capture, and that the only real difference between them is the speed.

One smaller thing, which may not be small: my friend wants the pool restricted to people who actually voted in the last general election, on the grounds that it screens for a minimum of civic engagement; I think that quietly reintroduces the very filter sortition was supposed to remove. Tell me whether that restriction would move any of your tier placements, and if so, which ones and in which direction.
