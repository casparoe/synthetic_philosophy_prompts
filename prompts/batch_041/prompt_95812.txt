Last spring I was asked to join the grant-making committee of a small family foundation — we distribute a little under two million dollars a year, mostly split between animal welfare and global health — and since then I have not been able to stop thinking about something that happened in our third meeting. One member, who regards industrial animal agriculture as the moral emergency of our time, agreed to vote for a large maternal-health grant she cared nothing about, on the condition that another member, who regards animal suffering as a distraction from human suffering, would vote for a cage-free campaign he cared nothing about. Both said, in almost the same words, that they were better off under the swap than without it; both, later, in private, used the word "prostitution" about themselves. I want to understand the structure of that disagreement — not whether our swap was right, but what the possible positions on such swaps actually are and where the fault lines between them lie. I have been reading around the suggestion that moral compromise can be modeled on gains from trade, and I find that I can defend about four incompatible versions of it depending on the day, so I would rather have the space laid out than the answer handed to me.

Please build me a decision tree (or, if you prefer, a taxonomy with the same branching structure) over the positions one might take on whether, and when, it is permissible for agents with different value systems to trade across those systems — where a trade is any agreement in which each party accepts an outcome it ranks lower than some alternative in exchange for an outcome the other party ranks higher, so that both end up with more of what they respectively value than they otherwise would have. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: each internal node should be a single question on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance, whether the values in play admit of the kind of comparison that trade presupposes at all; whether there are constraints that cannot be entered into a trade at any price, and if so whether that is because of what the constraint protects or because of what trading it would do to the agent who trades; whether a swap's permissibility turns on its content, on the process by which it was reached, or only on its consequences; whether the relevant bearer of integrity is the individual or the collective; and whether the parties' preferences are taken as fixed inputs or as something the negotiation is permitted to transform. Phrase every node as a question a committee member could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may be "are you a consequentialist?" — and make the branches at each node exhaustive, or state explicitly which answers you are omitting and why.

The leaves should carry the name of the position where it has one, and "unnamed" where it does not, each with one sentence on the commitment its defenders find hardest to accept. I would like at least nine leaves and no more than twelve, and I would like the tree to be able to separate: the view that any Pareto-improving swap is permissible so long as no third party is harmed; the view that some values are sacred and cannot be traded at any price, with the further question whether that is a fact about the values or about the traders; the view that the swap is permissible only if it survives a public-justification test; the view that the swap corrupts the agent who makes it even when it improves the world; the view that there are no gains from trade to be had here at all, because value systems are incommensurable; and the view that the appearance of trade is an illusion, since what looked like two values were always one. Finally, tell me which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and whether you take the failure to be a defect of the tree or a sign that the two positions were never really distinct; and point out any branch of the tree that is empty — a combination of answers that nobody holds — and say whether you think it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered to defend it.
