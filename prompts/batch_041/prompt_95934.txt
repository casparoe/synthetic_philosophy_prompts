In philosophical discussions of deception, a distinction is commonly drawn between lying and merely misleading: a lie requires the assertion of something the speaker takes to be false, whereas an audience can be brought to a false belief by statements that are, strictly speaking, true.

Consider two ways of fixing the extension of 'deception' when the speaker is an AI system. On a speaker-centred account, an output counts as deceptive only if it issues from something that holds beliefs and aims, in producing it, at a false belief in its audience. On a hearer-centred account, an output counts as deceptive whenever it is reasonable for the audience to form a false belief on the strength of it, whether or not any belief or intention stands behind the output.

Give an example of a single exchange between a user and an AI assistant in which these two accounts deliver opposite verdicts for reasons internal to the exchange — that is, not merely because the assistant happens to lack beliefs and intentions. The example need not involve any false statement; it may turn on what the assistant implies, omits, or declines to say. Say what the example reveals about the conceptual issue at stake: what work the notions of belief and intention are doing in the ordinary concept of deception, and what would be gained or lost by dropping them. Then state which account you think an honest-by-design assistant should be held to, and why.
