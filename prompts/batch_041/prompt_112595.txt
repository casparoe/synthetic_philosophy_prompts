Consider the long-running standoff over epiphenomenalism — the view that experiences are caused by brain events but cause nothing in turn, not even our reports and judgments about them. Critics say the position is self-undermining: if my report "this looks red to me" is produced entirely by neural goings-on, with the redness contributing nothing, then the report cannot be about the redness, and the grounds for believing in experience have evaporated. Defenders reply that causation is simply the wrong relation to insist on — a judgment can be about an experience because the experience partly constitutes the judgment rather than because it helped cause it — and then both sides restate themselves. I want a diagnosis rather than another round. My hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about causation or about the neuroscience, but about what a judgment's being about something requires: one side demands causal warrant (the experience must have had a hand in producing the judgment), the other accepts constitutive warrant (the experience must figure in what makes the judgment the judgment it is), and neither has said why its demand is the one that matters. Is that the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work — a hidden empirical claim about how reports are actually produced, a clash of unarguable intuitions about when a belief is justified, or two sides answering different questions (what causes the judgment? what makes it have the content it does?) under a single heading? Given your answer, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or an argument that none is available because one side's demand is not a legitimate demand to make. Engage the structure of this exchange; do not survey theories of consciousness.
