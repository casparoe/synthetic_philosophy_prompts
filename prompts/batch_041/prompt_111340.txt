I hold a Rousseauian view about a kind of entertainment that has become common: serial and interactive dramas that invite the audience to sit in moral judgment on characters, and sometimes to vote on what those characters should do, while the audience itself risks nothing. My claim is that this form of spectatorship cannot improve anyone's character and probably degrades it — it turns moral seriousness into a spectator sport, trains people to savor their own compassion, and, because the business model rewards engagement, selects for the shows that flatter the audience's existing moral self-image most skillfully. Rousseau's alternative, as I read him, was not better plays but gatherings in which people are participants rather than an audience. Tell me what would count against this view. I want the observations, arguments, and cases that should move me, ranked by how decisive they would actually be, including any evidence that audiences carry the moral exercise back into their lives; I also want to know what defenders of the view should concede as genuine counterevidence rather than explain away, and whether the position has been drawn so broadly that nothing could count against it. I would be most persuaded by a case in which sustained engagement visibly changed someone's conduct outside the fiction in a way they would not otherwise have chosen. Please respond in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, tables, or bold text.
