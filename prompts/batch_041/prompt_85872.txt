Ok so I'm in this tiny reading group on decision theory — really it's me, my flatmate, and two people from the stats department who turn everything into measure theory — and we've burned three weeks on the two-envelope problem, which is embarassing because it's supposed to be the warm-up. You know the setup: two envelopes, one holds twice what the other does, you pick one at random, open it, find X dollars, and the argument runs that the other envelope holds 2X or X/2 with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25X, so you switch — and then the same reasoning tells you to switch back, forever. One of us says it all dissolves once you notice there's no uniform prior over the positive reals; another says that's a cop-out, because the puzzle can be stated with a fixed but unknown pair of amounts and a symmetry principle and never mentions a distribution; a third just says "symmetric, no reason to prefer either one, done," which everyone hates, because it tells you the conclusion is false without telling you where the reasoning breaks.

What I want from you isn't a resolution, it's the spec for one. Write the desiderata: what any adequate treatment of the two-envelope problem has to deliver. Which cases it must get right — the open-and-see version; the never-open version; the version where the distribution is known and bounded, so that opening sometimes reveals you're holding the larger amount; the version where the amounts come from something with infinite expectation; and the money-pump version, where after switching you're offered a switch back for a penny. My own twist, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave at: three envelopes holding x, 2x and 4x in random order, you open one, and you may trade it for one of the other two picked at random — I make it out that you want that trade in two of the three cases and yet ex ante there's no gain, and I can't tell whether that's the same puzzle in a new coat or a genuinely different one. Also: which distinctions the treatment can't collapse (locating the error versus merely proving the conclusion false; the conditional expected value versus the unconditional one; the ratio between the envelopes versus their values), and which principles it can't be allowed to trample (conditionalization; the fact that a random variable isn't strictly better than itself; whatever you think conglomerability deserves). Then say which of the requirements are genuine and which are just the prejudices of one camp — I suspect "the resolution must not need a prior" is doing alot of that work for at least one of my friends — and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied, because I'm fairly sure there's at least one and I'd rather hear it from you than from the stats people.
