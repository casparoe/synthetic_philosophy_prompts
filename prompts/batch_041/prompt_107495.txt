I'm a graduate student sitting in on a reading group about the ethics of machine learning, and the discussion keeps circling back to the cluster of impossibility results that are usually summarized as showing that you cannot have every fairness property you might want at once. The setup, as I understand it, is this: there is a binary protected attribute (call the two groups A and B), a binary outcome the system is trying to predict, and either a continuous score or a thresholded decision. One desideratum is calibration within groups, so that a score of 0.7 really does mean a seventy-percent chance of the outcome for a member of either group. Two others are parity of error rates across groups, meaning the same true-positive rate and the same false-positive rate regardless of group. The theorems, roughly, say that these cannot all hold simultaneously unless the two groups have equal base rates or the predictor is close to perfect. I do not want the proof. I want the conceptual anatomy of the result, because I suspect the interesting content is being misdescribed whenever people compress it into a slogan about fairness being impossible.

Here is the suspicion I am trying to state precisely. The conflict, I think, is not really about fairness as such but about a more general tension between two things one might demand of a single statistic: that it carry all the outcome-relevant information, so that once you condition on it, group membership becomes irrelevant; and that its behavior be invariant across groups whose underlying distributions differ. If that is the right diagnosis, then the impossibility should be an instance of something more general, and it should reappear in other guises — for instance, when a constraint that holds for a coarse partition of the population (by race, say) can be fully satisfied while being violated badly on finer intersections of that partition with other attributes, so that meeting the coarse criterion conceals the finer violations rather than preventing them. Please tell me whether this diagnosis is correct and how to state it carefully; whether the impossibility is a mathematical fact about the definitions themselves or instead an empirical fact about the world's unequal base rates, i.e. whether equalizing those base rates would dissolve the conflict or merely relocate it; and whether it is best understood as a collision between an individual-level ideal and a group-level one, and if so why those two come apart precisely here. Finally, tell me where my framing is misleading me, since I expect it is in at least one place.
