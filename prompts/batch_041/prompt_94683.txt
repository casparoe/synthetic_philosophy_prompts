I'm writing a prospectus for a one-year MA thesis and I've got a half-baked idea I want you to turn into something with a spine. The old evidence problem: a theory you already believe turns out to entail some fact you've known forever (Mercury's perihelion is the stock example), and it really seems like that fact should confirm the theory a lot, but you can't conditionalize on it, because your credence in it was already 1. The usual fixes are either to ask what credence you would have had in the evidence if you hadn't believed the theory, or to model you as only now working out a logical consequence of something you already knew.

My suspicion is that theres two different questions here that the debate keeps running together. First, evidence you genuinely updated on long ago, before the theory was even on the table, so its information is already baked into your credences and there's nothing left to squeeze out — call that used-up evidence. Second, evidence that has always been sitting in your evidence set but that you never conditionalized on, because you never saw it as bearing on the theory: a lab printout in a drawer you never opened, or the fact that your kitchen floor is tiled, which you've known for years but never once considered in connection with a theory of interior design. For that second kind, plain conditionalization looks like it should still be available — you just update on the thing you'd overlooked. If that's right, then how Bayesianism handles old evidence is two questions wearing one coat, and the interesting one is which of the two the standard fixes actually solve, and whether the one they don't solve is really a problem about confirmation at all rather than about memory or attention.
