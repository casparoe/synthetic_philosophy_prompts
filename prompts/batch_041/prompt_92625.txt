I run a small business with one other owner — nine employees, and I'm the one who has the one-on-ones. Over the last year I've had to deal with maybe four or five people who let the team down, and I keep noticing I flip between two completely different modes: staying annoyed and holding them to it, or quietly writing them off as someone who *can* be held to it and just building the schedule so their weak spots don't matter. Then last month my brother stopped answering me for three weeks and came back weirdly, smoothly *nice*, and I honestly minded that more than the fight. Somebody told me this is basically Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" and I read a summary, so I know it's about reactive attitudes, the participant vs objective stance, and something about excuses being different from the case where someone's just out of the game. But I can't read the whole essay, I've got maybe fifteen minutes before each of these conversations.

So: turn it into a checklist I can actually run on a person. Questions in order, what counts as an answer to each, where it stops at "keep holding them to it," where it stops at "stop expecting, start managing," and how I'm supposed to tell an excuse from the thing where the person isn't a participant in that way at all — because those seem like they'd feel identical from where I sit. Also be honest about the parts where I'm not choosing anything, the attitude is just there or it isn't, and about what it costs me to stay in the first mode.

Run it on three: my best guy who missed a deadline once because his kid was in hospital; the guy who's been here six years and has never once been moved by being told off, so it's started to feel like scolding a vending machine; and me, on the receiving end of my brother. Tell me what the procedure can't do — I don't want a checklist pretending it can tell me whether to keep paying someone, and anything that ends in firing has to go past my co-owner anyway.
