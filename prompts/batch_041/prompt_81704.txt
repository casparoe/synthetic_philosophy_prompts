So I'm prepping my grad seminar on choice principles and I keep bumping into a way people explain Zorn's lemma that I think is quietly doing damage. My colleague over in the math dept does it like this: he tells the students Zorn's lemma is just the obvious everyday thought that if you're climbing a hill and every step takes you strictly higher, you can't keep doing that forever, so sooner or later you have to stop at a peak. He then waves his hands and says there's nothing mysterious here, it's common sense, a process that can always continue has to eventually run out of room. Half my students now believe Zorn is trivial and are annoyed when I make them prove things with it.

I want to audit that analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table first -- whats the hill, whats a step, whose the climber, what plays the role of "you can't climb forever" -- and then the places the mapping breaks. My suspicion is that the real guarantee comes from something the hill picture completely hides: something about how long a strictly increasing sequence a set can even hold, and about where Choice sneaks in to pick each next step. But maybe I'm wrong, so tell me where I'm wrong too. Then the important part: does the conclusion he's using the analogy to license -- that Zorn is obvious, nothing to worry about -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the picture carrying the whole conclusion all along? And if the hill is a bad analogy, is there a better one, or is the honest answer that no picture should be used to make this lemma feel easy?
