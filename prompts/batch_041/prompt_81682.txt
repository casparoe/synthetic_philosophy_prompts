You are a historian and philosopher of physics with two overlapping specialisms: late nineteenth-century statistical mechanics, and contemporary cosmology of the very early and very late universe. You write for scientifically trained readers who have only recently taken up philosophy, so you are in the habit of explaining a philosophical move without presupposing a philosophical education. Please work carefully rather than quickly, and resist the pull of the standard popular telling of this topic: what I am after is historical reconstruction with a philosophical spine.

I am a retired physicist — my own field was non-equilibrium statistical mechanics — and I have spent the last few years reading philosophy of science. My question takes the following form: what would Ludwig Boltzmann have made of a problem that carries his name but was formulated long after his death?

The background, as I understand it (correct me if I have it wrong, and say so plainly rather than smoothing it over):

- In the mid-1890s Boltzmann was defending the statistical reading of the second law against Zermelo's recurrence objection. Among the options he canvassed was that the universe spends almost all of its time in thermal equilibrium and that we inhabit an enormous and exceedingly rare fluctuation away from it, the whole observed order of things being one such excursion. He also offered the alternative that the universe simply began in a low-entropy state.
- His entropy–probability relation implies that the probability of a fluctuation falls off exponentially with the entropy deficit it represents, so that a small fluctuation is vastly more probable than a large one.
- In the modern argument, that consequence is turned against the fluctuation hypothesis: if we are a fluctuation, we ought to be the smallest fluctuation consistent with our observations, and should therefore expect to be a lone, briefly coherent, self-aware configuration with false memories rather than an evolved organism in a large and consistent world. Certain cosmological settings are said to make this overwhelmingly likely, whereupon the argument is used as a reductio of those settings. It is also entangled with the measure problem, with self-locating probability, and with disputes about which reference class the reasoning should be applied to.

What I want is a reconstruction of Boltzmann's likely response, not a survey of the current debate. Please proceed as follows.

1. Open with a brief brainstorm: in a few bullets, set out the recognisably distinct positions a defender of his framework might adopt when confronted with this argument, and say which of them the historical Boltzmann would most plausibly have found congenial, and why. Then give the main answer.

2. Reconstruct his resources. What could he have said using only his own statistical mechanics — thermodynamic probability, coarse-graining, the recurrence theorem, the ensemble picture, his realism about atoms — and without general relativity, an expanding universe, quantum fluctuations, de Sitter space or a cosmological constant?

3. Then sort the premises of the modern argument into three groups: those already available to him in the 1890s; those requiring physics he never saw; and those requiring philosophical machinery he never had — for instance, whatever is being claimed when we say that a randomly selected observer is probably a fluctuation. Be explicit about which premise falls into which group, and about the fact that the third group is where the argument earns its conclusion.

4. This is the part I care most about. Is the modern argument latent in his own reasoning? Would he have seen the exponential-suppression point as fatal to the fluctuation hypothesis he himself proposed, given that his version of it required a fluctuation of staggering size? Or would he have insisted that a fluctuation of that size is the only kind that could contain an observer capable of doing physics — and if so, what does that commitment cost him? Press on this: what does an observer have to be, on his own account, for his probability calculus to apply to them at all?

5. Where would he dig in? Consider whether he would abandon the fluctuation hypothesis, accept the conclusion as an acceptable price, or reject the reasoning as an abuse of probability; and what he would say to the thought that a fluctuation cannot trust its own reasoning. Note where his philosophical temperament — his opposition to treating the second law as absolute, his impatience with purely formal objections, his empiricism — bears on the answer, and keep that separate from what actually follows from his physics.

6. Finally, the residue report. Which parts of the modern discussion would he have found unintelligible, or would have dismissed as pseudo-problems: the measure problem, self-locating probability, the notion of a typical observer in a single universe, the appeal to consciousness? For each, say whether his incomprehension marks a genuine gap in his framework or a genuine obscurity in ours.

Throughout, mark your claims with your confidence: distinguish what he actually wrote, what follows from his framework though he never said it, and what is your extrapolation. I have no need of citations or a reading list, and I would far rather have a plain admission of uncertainty than a quotation; do not put words in his mouth between quotation marks, and paraphrase if you want to convey his manner. End with a short paragraph headed Where I am guessing.
