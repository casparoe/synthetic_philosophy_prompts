I retired a few years ago from a cell biology lab and have been trying to teach myself philosophy, mostly by arguing with friends who know more than I do. Last week two of them got into it at my kitchen table about whether testimony is its own way of knowing or just a kind of inference, and I couldn't referee it. I know the Nyāya school has this list -- perception, inference, comparison/analogy, and testimony (śabda) -- and that they treat testimony as a separate pramāṇa. But my friends were arguing about a modern case, not the old texts.

Maya said: If I read in a climate report that last year was the hottest on record, I know it by testimony. I didn't feel the whole planet, and I couldn't infer it from raw data myself. The report's sentence gives me knowledge directly, provided the source is reliable and sincere. That's why testimony is its own pramāṇa.

Dev said: That's just inference with extra steps. You know the reporters are reliable, they said P, so you infer P. You don't need a separate source. And if you say testimony is basic, you can't explain how you sort good testimony from bad without already using inference about who's trustworthy.

Maya said: No, inference needs a universal connection you can know independently -- the smoke-fire kind. I can't independently check every thermometer and satellite. And testimony can give me knowledge of things I couldn't have inferred, like the meaning of a new word when I was a kid. If you reduce it to inference, you make all learning circular.

Dev said: But you do check the method, not every measurement. And the word case is causal, not a separate epistemic source. A child hears a word and forms a disposition; that doesn't show the child had a pramāṇa called testimony. Anyway, if testimony is separate, what makes it knowledge rather than just a true belief you got from someone? You'll have to say the speaker knows and is sincere -- but then I'm inferring their mental states.

Maya said: The speaker's knowledge and sincerity are conditions, not the means of knowing. The means is the sentence itself, when it comes from a trustworthy source. You're confusing the causal story with the justification.

Dev said: And you're confusing a social shortcut with a basic source. If I use a calculator, I don't say calculation-by-calculator is a fifth pramāṇa. The calculator is an instrument. A reliable speaker is an instrument too.

Maya said: A calculator doesn't assert. A speaker does. Assertion is normative; it invites trust and can be wrong in a way a miscalculation isn't. That's the difference.

Dev said: Now you're just adding a social norm and calling it a pramāṇa.

Can you go through this exchange move by move -- who's right about each specific claim, where one is talking past the other, and what both are missing? I'm especially interested in whether teh calculator analogy works, and whether saying testimony is irreducible commits Maya to something weird about children or about trusting experts. Don't give me a bibliography or citations; I just want the arguement adjudicated.
