I need a teaching plan, not an explanation. In a single ninety-minute session with a reading group of non-specialists — mostly lawyers and policy analysts who have never read metaphysics — I have to bring them to the point where the question of whether a failure to act can cause an outcome feels like a real problem rather than a verbal quibble. Give me the plan: the order in which the components must be introduced and, for each step, what it depends on and why it cannot come earlier; the single case that should carry the whole session and what that case must have and must lack for the work to come out right; the misconceptions the group will predictably arrive with or generate along the way, each matched to the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off; one closing question that would show me whether they have understood the problem rather than merely acquired the vocabulary; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level, flagged as such, so that no one leaves thinking they have the whole picture.

My intended arc, which you should treat as a hypothesis rather than a constraint: begin with the ordinary difference-making or counterfactual notion of causation, so that a match striking and a fire starting sit on the table as the baseline; then introduce the puzzle that omissions are not events at all, yet we say without hesitation that a gardener caused a plant's death by not watering it; then press the point that counterfactual dependence cannot by itself do the work, since a neighbour who likewise fails to water the wilting plant is not thereby its cause; and conclude that what singles out the gardener's inaction is some norm or expectation that makes that particular inaction salient rather than the countless other things no one did. I expect to carry the session on the gardener/neighbour contrast, but I am prepared to replace it, and I want you to tell me whether that pair really carries the idea or whether some other case would do it better — in particular, whether the pair smuggles in a moral judgment where I want a purely causal one.

The misconceptions I anticipate, which you should confirm or correct and then place in the sequence: that an omission is a peculiar kind of ghostly event; that talk of omission causation is really just talk of responsibility and blame; that the but-for test settles every case once it is stated carefully; and that once omissions can be causes, almost everything becomes a cause of everything. For each, say where in the sequence it will surface and how the sequence should absorb it rather than merely contradict it.

Keep the plan honest about the state of the discussion: separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested account as though it were the consensus. Lead with your bottom line — the recommended sequence in a single sentence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
