Ok so I run a small reading group on wild animal welfare — mostly EA-adjacent folks, plus two ecology grad students who keep rolling their eyes at the rest of us — and next session is on the claim that destroying habitat might actually *reduce* wild animal suffering. Right now every discussion collapses into either "conservation obviously good" or "nature is hell, burn it down," and I want something that forces the actual disagreements apart.

So: give me a decision tree / taxonomy. Root question is "does habitat destruction reduce the total amount of wild animal suffering?" Every branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to, so nothing like "are you a negative utilitarian?" on the tree — the whole point is to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by the label they'd pick. Leaves are positions, each with a name (or "unnamed" if nobody really holds it) plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment it has to swallow.

Distinctions I definitely want on the tree, though add whatever else the logical space needs:

- Whose welfare is even in the ledger — vertebrates only, or everything sentient including insects and soil nematodes? If the small stuff counts, the numbers per hectare swamp everything else and the whole question changes shape.
- Number of animals vs. how each life goes. Razing a hectare kills the animals on it, but it also means fewer future animals born onto it; a smaller population could have better or worse lives per head, and the two margins can point in opposite directions.
- Whether dying is bad for the animal that dies — and whether that depends on whether its life was worth living in the first place.
- Population ethics: is averting a birth a harm, a benefit, or neither?
- Short run vs. long run. Destruction is usually a horrible spike first — starvation, crowding, survivors pushed into marginal "sink" habitat where they barely hang on — before the population settles lower.
- Whether biodiversity or naturalness has value that isn't animal welfare. If it does, the welfare ledger doesn't settle the policy question at all, and that needs to be visible on the tree.
- Whether a true answer here is even action-guiding. Suppose destruction does reduce suffering — does that give anyone a reason to bulldoze? "The claim is true but nobody should act on it" is a position and I want it as a leaf.

Concrete anchors to build the cases out of: a hectare of rainforest vs. a hectare of oil palm or cattle pasture; clear-cutting forest into grassland; draining a wetland; and letting old farmland rewild. For each, the tree should make clear which branch questions you'd have to answer first before you could say anything about the welfare total.

Then, two more things. (1) List the answer-combinations nobody occupies, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's coherent but repugnant, or because nobody's bothered to work it out yet. (2) Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, rather than papering over it.

Last thing, and this matters to me: be honest about which branch questions are empirical questions an ecologist could in principle answer — and how settled they actually are — versus value questions where no amount of data will resolve the disagreement. I don't want a contested empirical claim presented as the consensus, and I don't want a values dispute dressed up as a fact. Where people who actually know this stuff are split, say so and say what they're split over. And if you're unsure whether one of your leaves is a real position or something you invented for the sake of the tree, just tell me. Aim for 8-12 leaves.
