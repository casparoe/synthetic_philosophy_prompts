My friend Priya and I spent most of last night arguing about Arrival (she'd somehow never seen it, so we watched it and then argued untill 1am). Her arguement, which I think is half right and half completely unhinged: she says the film is the best account of what translation actually is that she's ever come across, and specifically better than Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — because in Hofstadter the translator is always the one holding the constraints, choosing between rhyme and meter and syllable count, grinding out version after version of that little Clément Marot poem addressed to a sick young woman, each version obeying some self-imposed rule, so the translator stays in charge of the form and the poem just has to survive it. Whereas in Arrival the constraint holds the translator. The heptapod writing is non-linear — as Chiang wrote it in the story, they don't build a sentence one symbol at a time, they draw the whole thing at once, which means whoever writes it has to know how it ends before anything is on the page — and Louise ends up remade by that form rather than in command of it. And the film does the same thing to us, since the opening scene only becomes legible as the ending. Priya thinks that's a better theory of translation than the book is; I said she's confusing being changed by a language with translating one, and that Hofstadter's whole point is that a translator who carries across only the content has already lost the poem.

So I'd like you to actually do the reading and settle it for me. Read Arrival through the Le Ton beau de Marot lens — translation under formal constraint, and what it even means to preserve a form. Please don't give me the Sapir-Whorf essay, I've read ten of those; I want the film taken seriously as a work about translation, and I want the scenes. The standoff over the alien answer that gets rendered as offer weapon, which the Chinese team reads as use weapon, partly because they've been talking to the heptapods over mahjong — that feels like a genuinely Hofstadterian point to me, the frame you translate within determining what the symbol says, and he does say somewhere that translating between languages is only one case of translating between frames of reference. The twelve ships each holding a twelfth of the message. The fact that Louise's breakthrough is not a better dictionary but a different relation to time, which in the story comes through a principle in physics about light taking the fastest path as though it knew where it would end up. And I want you to be honest about where the film resists the reading and where my picture of Hofstadter is too neat — I read the book years ago and I'm definately mangling it. Where does the movie really dramatize the idea that form is part of the meaning, and where does it cheat, i.e. treat the alien form as a key that unlocks a superpower rather than as a constraint the translator has to satisfy?

One more thing I keep chewing on, and you can tell me if it's a red herring: the poem Hofstadter spends the book translating is addressed to a sick girl, and he was doing it while his wife was dying — one of the versions in the book is hers — while Louise spends the whole film knowing her daughter will die young. Does that rhyme mean anything for the reading, or am I just pattern-matching? I'm turning this into a paper for a translation seminar where half the class thinks form is decoration, so the reading has to be fair in both directions, and I'd like it to end with something the film shows about form and constraint that the arguments alone can't get at.
