In the novel I am finishing, one of my characters is a cognitive scientist of a particular temperament — the sort who holds that the sense of self is a pattern rather than a thing, and who has spent a decade defending a position he associates with Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and the later book I Am a Strange Loop. I have come to the material at second hand, and I would rather not put words in his mouth that a genuine reader of Hofstadter would find embarrassing. At a dinner last month a well-read acquaintance, meaning to be helpful, offered me the following account of the view, and I have been turning it over ever since.

His summary ran roughly so: Hofstadter's central claim is that the sense of self — the "I" — is a strange loop. By that he means a system that loops back upon itself: a feedback loop, of the kind one sees in a thermostat or in two mirrors facing one another, except run through a hierarchy of levels, so that the system ends up representing itself. Once a brain's web of symbols grows rich enough to model itself, the self-model simply is the "I" we experience from the inside; the self is not a thing but a pattern, and because it is a pattern it need not be bound to any one brain — which is why Hofstadter can speak of a person's "soul" persisting in the people who knew them. He draws the whole line back to Gödel: self-reference is what the incompleteness theorems are about, and he thinks something analogous is at work in the mind. And since the self is only a pattern the brain constructs, it is in the end an illusion, a story the brain tells itself; that is why he says you are not really the same person from one moment to the next, and why he thinks our dread of death rests on a confusion. The obvious objection, and I think it is fatal, is that "strange loop" is merely a new name for self-reference — it re-describes the mystery in the vocabulary of computation without explaining anything.

What I want is an audit I can act on. Please go through the summary clause by clause and tell me which parts my character could accept without qualification, which are the sort of simplification a competent popularizer makes and a specialist would wince at, and which are simply not what Hofstadter holds. Where the account does land on something true, I want to see how the accurate version differs from what my acquaintance said — in particular, I suspect the notion of a strange loop has been flattened into something far more ordinary than what Hofstadter describes, and I would like that difference stated precisely. Finally, tell me whether his closing objection follows from anything true in his summary, or whether it is a separate argument that would have to be made on its own footing — and, if it is, what a defender of Hofstadter would say in reply.
