I am a student in an upper-level seminar on decision theory and its applications, and each of us has been asked to contribute one candidate essay question for the take-home final, along with a model answer and a marking scheme the teaching team can adopt. My topic is the phenomenon sometimes called the unilateralist's curse, but I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting the standard example, so I have tried to build a variant with a bit more structure and I would like you to write it up properly.

The setup I want the question to use is this. A cheap prophylactic can be administered to an at-risk population by any of a large number of independent labs, each of which has the authority to begin deployment on its own. Every lab's own estimate is that, taking its deployment as the only one, the expected value is small but positive: a modest chance of a large benefit, a modest chance of a harm of comparable size, and otherwise no effect. Two features of the intervention matter. First, the benefit saturates: most of the total value is captured by whichever lab acts first, and each additional deployment adds very little. Second, the harm accumulates: each additional deployment adds risk roughly in proportion to the number already acting, with a dose threshold above which the aggregate harm becomes large. A further complication is that the labs are not independent in their judgments; they draw on the same body of evidence and share the same optimism about the intervention, so their errors are positively correlated.

I would like the exam question to require the following moves, and I would like the model answer to show what each of them looks like when done well. First, the student should reconstruct the general condition under which each lab's individually rational decision to act, each acting on a positive solo estimate, produces a collectively negative outcome. I want them to state the condition as a relation between the marginal benefit schedule and the marginal harm schedule, and to explain why this is a coordination failure rather than bad luck or individual miscalculation. Second, the student should assess the norm of acting only if one would endorse everyone in one's epistemic position acting likewise. Does it give a determinate recommendation here, given that the estimates are both correlated and heterogeneous, and does it actually cure the failure or merely relocate it? Third, the student should assess an alternative governance fix: a unilateral veto, under which any single lab can block all deployments. Does this remove the curse or substitute a different failure mode, and what does the answer depend on? Fourth, and briefly, the student should identify the mirror-image case, in which the actions are complements so that independent action undershoots rather than overshoots, and explain why the veto fix is exactly the wrong remedy there. This last part is meant to test whether they can see the curse as one instance of a broader structure rather than as a standalone puzzle.

Please write three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased so that a strong student can see the several distinct moves it requires without being told outright what the answer is. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that actually makes those moves. Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built around the specific moves a top answer must contain, with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how a weaker answer typically goes wrong at each point, and a clear statement of what separates an A from a B. The exam is closed-book and the students cannot look anything up, so neither the question nor the model answer may require them to name a paper, an author, a date, or a citation; the reasoning has to be carried by the scenario and by arguments the students can construct from it.
