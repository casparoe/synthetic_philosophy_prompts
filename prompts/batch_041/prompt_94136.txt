I'm a journalist who covers economics and ideas, and I've been asked to give the after-dinner speech at the anual dinner of the economics society at my old college in Cambridge. It's the eightieth anniversary of Keynes's death, so the theme is Keynes and the good life. The room is a mix: half are economists who can quote the General Theory in their sleep, the other half are partners, parents, and a few students who've never read a word of him. I want to say something that lands for both — not a lecture, not a hagiography, but a real speech that uses what Keynes actually thought about the good life to unsettle the room a bit. Specifically, I want to bring in the stuff from his 1930 essay where he predicts that within a century the economic problem will be solved and we'll be working fifteen hours a week, and his worry that we've been trained to strive for so long that we won't know how to enjoy the leisure. And I want to bring in his later memoir where he turns on his own early Moorean beliefs — the ones that said only states of mind matter — and admits he'd left out the irrational, the practical, the whole messy business of action. The speech should use that tension to ask whether we've made a disaster of the good life by overvaluing work, and it should do it in a way that doesn't lose the non-economists. Can you write it? Five minutes max, British English please. And don't make it too neat — leave a bit of edge. Also, no quotes from Keynes, I don't want to get any of them wrong. Paraphrase is fine.
