I hold a broadly internalist view of reasons for action: an agent has a reason to do something only if that consideration is anchored in something she already cares about, or could be brought to care about through sound deliberation from her existing motivational set — so there are no reasons that float entirely free of any possible motivation. I want the strongest case against it. Begin with the cases that are supposed to be its undoing — the agent whose desires have been systematically deformed (by oppression, addiction, or the like) and whom we still want to say has a reason to resist, and the thoroughly unmoved amoralist — and for each tell me whether a resourceful internalist can absorb it and at what cost. Then take the structural worry seriously: once "sound deliberation" is allowed to generate new desires rather than merely rank existing ones, does the view still deny anything, or has it quietly become the externalism it set out to reject? Consider, as a test, an agent whose motivational set is exhaustive and transparent to her and who has already deliberated as well as she can, yet who has no inclination to act as we think she should; I want to know what the internalist must say here and whether that answer is defensible. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, mark which ones internalists themselves would concede rather than only what critics allege, say what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and give me an honest verdict on whether it retains any real exposure or has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if the latter, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind.
