In the formal epistemology reading group I attend, one member has a standing formulation that he reaches for whenever the conversation turns to Dutch book arguments: "a credence just is the odds at which you would bet on the proposition." It has now been used to close three separate disputes, and I would like a menu of readings rather than a verdict on the slogan itself.

The three uses. First, he says that someone who reports a credence of one-half in a coin's landing heads but declines to take either side of a bet at even odds has not really reported a credence at all: what she believes is whatever her betting dispositions reveal. Second, he says the Dutch book theorem is nearly trivial once the slogan is granted, since an agent whose betting odds violate the probability calculus is by definition inconsistent, and a sure-loss book is just the cash value of that inconsistency. Third, he says my worry — that a Dutch book shows an agent would lose money, not that she is irrational — is confused, because a credence is nothing over and above the bets it disposes you to accept, so those two things cannot come apart.

The case that makes me doubt him: a colleague with a very sick child says she is ninety-nine percent certain the child will recover, and yet will not accept a bet on the outcome at any odds, because the wager itself is unbearable to her. He takes this to show that she does not really hold the credence she reports. I suspect it shows something about the slogan instead.

What I want is the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, with a verdict for each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false — and a note on what would have to be true of credence for it to hold. I am especially interested in where the readings differ over: whether the claim concerns the meaning of the word, the measurement of credence, or the dispositions a rational agent must have; whether it ranges over every bet or only bets of certain sizes, stakes, or kinds; whether it presupposes that the agent knows her own credences and that her utility for money is linear, or at least that the currency of the bets is the currency of her preferences; and whether accepting each bet in a set one at a time amounts to accepting the set. Then tell me which of the readings would actually support each of his three uses and which would not, what the third argument would need in place of the slogan if the readings that support it are the false ones, and which reading he most likely has in mind when he repeats the line.
