I'm a novelist — four books, mostly about memory and the ways people mislay their own pasts — and for the better part of a year I've been circling a philosophical dispute I can neither dramatise nor put down. It is the question of whether dreams are experiences at all. One position, the Wittgensteinian one that Norman Malcolm worked out, holds that the concept of dreaming is anchored to the waking report: to dream is to be able to tell a dream, so the notion of a dream that no one had and no one remembers is not a hidden event but a confusion, and one does not report an observation when one reports having dreamed. The other position, the one I associate with Daniel Dennett, treats the matter as an empirical question about what happens during sleep, and is willing to conclude that nothing experiential happens at all — that the dream narrative, with its apparent duration and its interior logic, is assembled at the instant of waking and installed in memory, so that what you remember is the memory of an experience you never had. I keep noticing that ordinary institutions cannot afford to leave this open. Medicine, insurance and the law all have to answer yes or no, and they answer without noticing that they are doing philosophy.

What I want from you is a satire that makes that visible, in the form of the patient information leaflet — the medication guide folded into the box — for a fictional drug that suppresses dreaming. Seven hundred to eight hundred words, laid out as a real leaflet is: indications, mechanism of action, adverse reactions, warnings, clinical studies, patient counselling. The comedy has to come from the philosophy being correct rather than from jokes about sleep, and the target is not Malcolm or Dennett but the habit, shared by regulators, insurers and physicians, of demanding a determinate verdict on a question that may not have one. That means the document must be unable to avoid taking a side and unable to stay on one: its definition of the condition it treats, its account of how the drug acts, and its instructions to patients about what to report should each quietly presuppose a different answer to the question of whether a dream is something undergone or something confabulated. One of its adverse reactions should be an event that, on one of the two views, cannot occur; one of its warnings should impose a duty that the other view makes impossible to discharge. A philosopher should recognise the dispute in it, and someone who has never read a word of the philosophy should finish it with a faint vertigo about what happens to them every night.

Keep the register flat — the deadpan of a regulatory department that has never once been amused — and resist letting the philosophers' names, or the words experience and report, do any winking on my behalf. Don't resolve it; the leaflet should be more certain at the end than the reader is. The drug can be indicated for nightmares in adults, and the manufacturer's name should promise more confidence than the document can supply.
