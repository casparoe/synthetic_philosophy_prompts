I don't have any academic training, but I've been turning a question over for a couple of years, and my older sister has become my test case for it, because she is far more confident about it than I am. The question is what we mean when we say that someone "really" wants something — not what they say they want, but what they want, as she puts it, underneath. I've been keeping track of what she says about people we both know, and I think she is running some rule, or maybe two rules at once, that I can't quite state. I'd like you to state it for me.

Her verdicts so far:

Our cousin Ravi, 34, has been a competitive swimmer since he was six. His father coached him, drove him to practice, and never let him seriously consider anything else. He says he loves it and wants to keep going. She says swimming is not what he really wants, because he has never been free to want anything else.

Our friend Lena joined a religious group in her twenties that, by her own account, pressured and isolated her. She later left it, and now, knowing exactly what it cost her, says she would join again and is grateful for it. My sister says that is what Lena really wants, and that the earlier pressure no longer matters.

Our grandmother, in her eighties and slipping, said for years that she never wanted to be kept alive once she could no longer follow what was happening. Now she can't follow it, and she is cheerful and says she wants to keep going. My sister says the earlier wish is the one that counts.

Our friend Dara, 30, says she wants to marry a man in another country and also wants to move home for good; the two can't both happen. My sister says we don't know yet what Dara really wants, and we won't until Dara has straightened out what she is actually after.

Now the case I want you to predict. Our friend Sofia is 45 and a doctor. Her parents came here with almost nothing and made it clear from her childhood that medicine was the family's future; she went into it largely to please them and has said so. She also gave up music, which she was good at. Her parents have both died, and no one is pressing her about anything now. She says, knowing precisely what she traded away, that she wants this life and would choose it again.

I would like you to tell me, first, what my sister will say about Sofia, and why. Second, what rule or rules she is running, stated as precisely as you can, and which rival rules fit all four of her earlier verdicts just as well. Third, your best guess at which rule she is actually using and your reasons for preferring it, including what each candidate rule would have to say about Sofia. Fourth, what further case would tell the competing rules apart, and why that case rather than another. Fifth, whether her rule is doing real work or is just a redescription of her intuitions — in particular, how one is supposed to decide which influences on a person count as distortions to be corrected for and which are simply who the person is, and whether correcting for everything would leave anything behind.

I came across the phrase "coherent extrapolated volition" somewhere — the idea that what a being really wants is what it would want if it knew more, reasoned better, and had become more the sort of being it would want to be — and I suspect my sister is running a one-person version of something like that, though I may be wrong about the resemblance. If there are names in philosophy for the rules you identify, give them, and I'll look up the references myself; I don't need a reading list. Please write in American English.
