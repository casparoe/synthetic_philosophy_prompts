I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on value theory and rational choice — someone who has thought carefully about the structure of comparative judgments and about what it takes for a set of judgments to be usable in deliberation — and to help me with a practical problem that has been nagging at me. I am not looking for a reading list or references; I want the substance of the reasoning, spelled out. Before you commit to an answer, please spend a few sentences brainstorming the different diagnoses one could give of the situation below, say what each diagnosis would predict, and only then tell me which you find most defensible and why.

The situation: I have been shopping for a mattress, and the showroom displays fifteen firmness settings, 1 through 15, from softest to firmest. The increments are small. When I lie on setting 1 and then setting 2, I cannot reliably tell them apart, and I certainly would not say that 2 is worse for me than 1; the same goes for every neighbouring pair up the line — 2 and 3, 3 and 4, and so on to 14 and 15. But when I compare 1 with 15 directly, the difference is unmistakable, and I say without hesitation that 1 would be better for me than 15. My friend, an engineer who likes clean arguments, says my judgments cannot all survive. His reasoning: I claim that no single step up the scale is a step down for me. But if no step is a step down, then walking all the way from 1 to 15 cannot be a step down either — that is simply what "no step is a step down" means — yet I also insist that 15 is worse than 1. So, he says, something has to give, and it is probably my claims about the neighbouring pairs: either I really am indifferent between them, in which case I should be happy to take the cheapest, or I do prefer one to the other, in which case I should be able to say which and by how much. He then added a sharper point. The showroom, he noted, lets you change the firmness after purchase for a small fee. If I am genuinely indifferent between neighbouring settings but do prefer 1 to 15, then a patient salesman could walk me down the line one setting at a time, collecting a fee at each step and leaving me, by my own lights, no worse off at every stage — until I have paid a good deal of money to end up on the setting I like least. Any set of judgments that exposes me to that, he says, is not a ranking at all, and I should repair it before I buy anything.

So what I want from you is this. Is my friend right that at least one of my judgments must be false, and if so, which one? If the fault lies with my talk of adjacent settings being "just as good," what should I say instead — that they are incomparable, that the differences are too small to matter, or something else — and does any of those repairs actually work? What should I do in the showroom: is there a defensible procedure for choosing a setting, or is the honest answer that the problem is ill-posed and I should decide by some other means entirely? And step back with me: does this episode show that judgments like "better than" and "no worse than" cannot be relied upon to fit together into a single ranking, or does it show only that I have been sloppy about very small differences? Would your answer change if the increments were finer, or the scale longer, or if money and comfort were pulling in opposite directions? Please be concrete about the mattress, but also tell me what general lesson you would draw about how comparative judgments behave.
