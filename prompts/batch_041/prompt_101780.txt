I am a science journalist who has spent the last decade reporting on foundations of physics and on the ethics of emerging technologies, and I serve as the public member on the ethics advisory committee of the Northlight Institute for Fundamental Physics. On Tuesday the committee will vote on whether to authorize a three-year experimental program that the institute has been calling the Decoherence Valence Project. I need to circulate a written opinion before that vote, and I would like you to draft it.

The proposal uses large-molecule matter-wave interferometry and optomechanical cooling to look for empirical signatures of the intrinsic phenomenal properties posited by certain panpsychist ontologies. The principal investigator works within a family of views according to which phenomenal properties are the intrinsic natures of physical properties, and she has advanced a conjecture that the institute’s internal working paper calls valence-density. The conjecture says that any physical system with a certain kind of integrated, irreversible information flow has a momentary experiential aspect, and that the hedonic sign of that aspect, whether positive, negative, or neutral, is a function of the system’s entropy production and boundary conditions. The working paper argues that the experiment’s rapid decoherence and recombination events could create and destroy enormous numbers of micro-subjects, and that if even a tiny fraction of those micro-experiences have negative valence, the program would be a moral catastrophe on a scale that dwarfs ordinary animal experimentation.

The committee is split. One member, a philosopher of mind, says the working paper conflates panpsychism with pan-sentience. On her view, phenomenal properties are not automatically hedonic, and moral status requires a subject with a temporally extended point of view rather than a momentary event. A second member, a physicist, says the conjecture is not well-defined enough to generate an expected value at all, so the committee should treat the proposal as a case of moral risk and apply a precautionary principle. The principal investigator responds that if the conjecture were true, every existing quantum experiment would already be producing such events, so singling out her program is arbitrary; and that if the conjecture is false, rejecting the program imposes a real scientific cost for a metaphysical phantom.

I need a formal written opinion that I can circulate to the committee. It should recommend a concrete course of action, either approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it should specify a time frame and conditions that the institute could actually implement. It should engage the strongest opposing view seriously rather than caricaturing it. It should say whether the distinction between panpsychism, panexperientialism, and pan-sentience ought to affect the decision, and whether the fact that the conjecture is empirically unconfirmed changes the ethical calculus. It should also address the principal investigator’s arbitrariness objection directly.

You are a philosopher of physics and a practical ethicist with expertise in both the metaphysics of consciousness and research ethics. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a brief argument map that lays out the strongest version of each side, including the best case for treating micro-experiences as morally considerable and the best case for treating the entire worry as a category error. Then draft the memo itself, addressed to the committee. Put the bottom line up front, give the reasoning in clear prose, state the strongest objection to your recommendation, and respond to it. Consider whether moral status can attach to instantaneous experiences at all, whether the possibility of micro-suffering is better modeled as a small probability of a huge harm or a large probability of a small harm, and whether a moratorium on one laboratory is coherent if the underlying physics is ubiquitous. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not rely on obscure empirical findings; reason from the structure of the problem. Keep the memo under 1,500 words and make it something the committee could vote on.
