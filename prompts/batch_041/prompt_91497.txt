ok so I've been trying to nail down what I actually think about equality and keep going in circles, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. tell me what principle they add up to, if there is one, and where it breaks.

1. Sam and Tanya have identical incomes, identical housing, identical hours. Sam is blind and needs pricey assistive gear plus a part-time reader just to do the same job Tanya does and read the same books. I think Sam is worse off than Tanya, and that a fair arrangement hands Sam more money. So equal income doesn't look like equality to me here.

2. Ulf has spent years teaching himself to like grand cru Burgundy, and now cheap wine is undrinkable to him; Vera drinks tap water and is thrilled with it. Same incomes. I don't think justice owes Ulf a subsidy to lift his enjoyment up to Vera's. I honestly can't tell if that's because he chose his tastes or because enjoyment isn't the thing we're equalizing in the first place -- maybe you can tell me which of those is doing the work.

3. Wen was raised expecting to serve her family and says she's completly content, wouldn't change a thing. Xochitl has the exact same material life but hates it and wants out. I think Wen is worse off than Xochitl, or at the very least I refuse to count Wen's contentment as making her better off. You can't take stated satisfaction at face value when it's been shaped by having no other option.

4. Now the one I can't fit. Yuki and Zane have the same income, same opportunities, same everything on paper, but Zane belongs to a group everyone treats as beneath them -- people talk over him, serve him last, assume he's stupid. I think Zane is worse off, and not because of any of the usual stuff. His resources are fine, his options are fine, he isn't even especially unhappy. Something about the relation itself is what's wrong.

5. Sanity check, so don't read me as saying satisfaction never counts: Pia is poor through pure bad luck and miserable about it, and I think we owe her a lot.

My tentative principle is: what should be equalized is each person's real freedom to do and be the things that matter; resources matter only insofar as they secure that; bare satisfaction doesn't count on its own. That feels like it gets 1, 2, 3 and 5. But 4 slips straight through its fingers, and when I try to patch 4 in I worry I've just bolted on a seperate, unrelated principle and called it one thing.

So: give me the principle or the small set of candidates, stated precisely, and say which case is doing the work for each clause. Tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- two people with equal capabilities and equal resources, one of whom spends her evenings caring for a parent while the other doesn't, and neither gets paid for it. And be honest about which of my five verdicts your principle can't keep, and what the cheaper fix is: patch it, or drop the verdict.
