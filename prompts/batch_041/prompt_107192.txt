I've come to hold that microhistory is not merely a supplement to the history of states and elites but epistemically prior to it: macro-level claims about state formation, class, and markets are abstractions whose causal content is only as good as the local processes they compress, and since those processes are legible only at the scale of a single village, trial, or household, it is at the micro scale that historical claims are actually tested, with the macro-narrative inheriting its warrant from the micro. Tell me what would count against this, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and say which of these a committed microhistorian would themselves concede rather than leave to critics. Four things concern me in particular: that the genre's most celebrated works are selected precisely for being exceptional, so a single case cannot license a general causal claim and the alleged testing is illusory; that the poor enter the archive mainly through the records of the institutions that disciplined them, so history from below is really history from below as mediated by the powerful; that the priority thesis is either trivial — of course macro claims rest on some particulars — or strong enough to be false; and whether an asymmetry rescues it, since macro claims can be embarrassed by micro evidence while micro claims seem immune to embarrassment by macro evidence. Finally, tell me honestly whether the view is insulated from refutation, and whether that insulation is a defect.
