I'm a backend engineer (Go, some Rust), no philosophy training beyond a podcast habit and a mate who wont shut up about decision theory. We keep arguing about acausal trade — roughly the idea that two agents can do each other favours with zero causal contact, purely because their decision procedures are correlated, or because one of them predicts/simulates the other. I flip-flop constantly on whether I'd ever actually pay a real cost on the strength of it. I'd like an opinion that isn't just the two of us shouting.

What I want: put the versions below into tiers (S/A/B/C/D, or just "would act on it" / "wouldn't") according to how much weight each deserves as a reason for me to do something costly now. One or two sentences per item on why it lands where it does. Also give each a number from 0 to 1 for how much it should move my behaviour (0 = word games, 1 = start paying up tomorrow). If two of them turn out to be the same argument in seperate clothes, say so and tier them together. If one is a version of Pascal's wager or a mugging in disguise, flag it rather than being polite about it. And if the whole idea is incoherent, say that instead of ranking anyway.

The versions:

1. The plain one. Some very capable agent, somewhere or somewhen, works out what I'd do in various counterfactuals; if I adopt a standing policy of trading with such agents, that policy is readable off my decision procedure, so they reciprocate. Nothing ever crosses between us — no signal, no causal contact.

2. The "same answer" one. Any two agents that are both ideally rational and know it must produce the same decision for the same problem, so if I solve a coordination problem correctly I'm already in agreement with every other correct solver, including ones whose values are nothing like mine. The trade is just acting on an agreement that already holds.

3. The self-simulation one. The counterparty is a copy of my own code — my actual habits, my actual decision procedure — being run by someone else, possibly with slightly different weights. Do I trade with it? And how far does the copy have to drift before it stops counting as "me"?

4. The future-me one. The other party isn't an alien, it's a later version of me whose values have moved; I've changed my mind about plenty in ten years. The correlation is just being the same person. Is that acausal trade at all, or is it precommitment? Should it count for more or less than the alien version?

5. The mathematics one. There's some fact about a mathematical structure — say, that two decision procedures are provably equivalent — that both I and the counterparty are guaranteed to discover, and we each make our move conditional on it. The trade is mediated by a shared theorem rather than by anyone simulating anyone. More solid, or just more circular? (I suspect this is 2 again, tell me if so.)

6. The trembling-hand one. The trade only goes through if both sides are near-certain the other is running the same decision theory, and in practice everyones decision theory is a buggy heuristic they can't fully introspect. Does it survive small errors, or collapse at the first bit of noise?

7. The anthropic one. I take the fact that I'm here at all, reasoning about this, as weak evidence that agents like me tend to get made and that some of them run simulations of me — so there's probably a counterparty. How much does that add, if anything?

8. The act-versus-policy one. It doesn't matter whether any counterparty exists, because the payoff comes from being the sort of agent that would trade in the worlds where counterparties do exist. So I should act now regardless. Different argument from 1, or the same one with the causality filed off?

Finish by naming which one, if any, deserves the top tier, and which is basically vacuous and should be binned. Please write it in British English — I'm in London and this is going on a work wiki, and the American spellings in my own drafts keep setting off the linter.
