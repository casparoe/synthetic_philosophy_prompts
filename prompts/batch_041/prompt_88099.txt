Last winter I finally won an argument that had been running between us for two years, and I have felt worse ever since; the other person conceded, but the concession had the shape of exhaustion rather than agreement, and I have come to suspect that what I collected was precisely the kind of recognition that cannot satisfy anyone -- the acknowledgment of a party who has already been forced into conceding. I would like a poem that takes that suspicion seriously and makes it do formal work rather than merely saying it. Write it for two labeled voices, sharing a single refrain line that each speaks at least twice; the words of the refrain must not change, but its meaning must shift depending on who is speaking and at what point, so that by the end the reader can hear that the two voices are not exchanging recognition but something closer to mimicry. I want the poem to carry three things that matter to me: that the victor cannot afford the loser's death, because the dead cannot recognize; that the victor becomes dependent on the very party he has subordinated; and that the subordinated party, through the labor he is made to perform, is the one who quietly becomes self-conscious, leaving the so-called master with the hollow half of the exchange. Keep it to roughly twenty to thirty lines. Someone who knows the dialectic should be able to locate the fear-of-death moment and the point at which the roles invert, and someone who does not should still find a poem.
