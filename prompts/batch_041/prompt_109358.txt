I run a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I open the unit on transformative experience with a piece of satire — partly to get the laughter out of the way before we turn to the objections, partly because the comedy does more to exhibit what is strange about the problem than my lecture does. This year I'd like you to write the piece. Work as a philosopher of action who knows the literature on transformative experience from the inside and who also writes comedy for people who read philosophy for a living: someone with a good ear for institutional deadpan and no impulse to explain the joke. Begin by thinking through, for yourself, which features of the problem are genuinely funny rather than merely absurd, and how a reader who knows the literature would be able to tell that the author does too; then write. What I want is an in-universe document: the welcome-and-informed-consent packet, roughly 800 to 1,000 words, formatted with headings and lettered clauses, issued by a fictional and entirely straight-faced clinic that sells elective transformations to adults — a month as someone else, a conversion, a vocation, an irreversible commitment of some kind. The clinic is not sinister and its staff are not fools; it is a well-run business that has adopted a particular philosophical position as company policy and follows it wherever it leads, and that is where the comedy lives. Give the packet a title boring enough to be real.

Here is the material you should be working with, so that the humour comes from the structure of the problem rather than from invented quirks. An experience is epistemically transformative when you cannot know what it is like to have it until you have had it, and personally transformative when it changes what you care about — your core preferences, your sense of who you are — rather than merely what you believe or how you feel about things you already value. A choice that is both is supposedly impossible to make in the ordinary rational way: the outcomes can only be assessed from inside a perspective you do not yet occupy, and the preferences that would do the assessing are precisely what is at issue, so the deliberation has no stable standpoint from which to run. One influential response is that such choices can still be rational if they meet a kind of revelation condition — roughly, if what you are choosing is an experience you expect to disclose to you what it is like and what you would, from the far side, value, so that the point of choosing is to find out. A rival response holds that these choices simply lie outside the domain of deliberation and have to be made by commitment or by a leap rather than by weighing. A third observes that many transformations can only come to someone who is not trying to have them: the deliberate pursuit of a state that requires spontaneity or unwilled change defeats itself, so the sane policy is often to arrange one's circumstances and then let the change happen to one rather than to one-by-choice. Give your clinic the first of these positions as its official policy, let it follow that policy with complete consistency into its consent procedure, its pricing, its complaints process, and its testimonials, and see what a document written by people who sincerely hold it looks like.

A few constraints. The target of the satire is the practice and the position, not any person or real organisation: invent your own clinic, do not name, cite, or quote any philosopher or real business, and include nothing resembling a reading list. Keep the document deadpan throughout — no winks, no asides to the reader, no character who is in on the joke — while keeping it legible: a reader should be able to tell what is being sent up without ever being told. The philosophy has to survive translation into the idiom of a customer-service document, so the clinic must never use the terms of art; it should say, in its own language, what the terms of art mean. A specialist in the area should find the piece fair rather than a strawman, and someone who has never read a word of the literature should still laugh and follow roughly what is at stake. You are free to invent whatever sections the packet needs, but a document of this kind will probably want at least: what is on offer; what cannot be disclosed in advance, and why; what consent can and cannot mean under that restriction; a policy on signatures, given that the person who signs first and the person who signs last are not the same person; a note on the difference between deciding to change and drifting into it; testimonials; and a refunds and complaints clause. Do not pad it with fake legal boilerplate — every clause should be doing philosophical work — and if you catch yourself reaching for an obvious gag, cut it. Length: 800 to 1,000 words.
