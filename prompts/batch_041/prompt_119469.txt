I have been working through, on my own, the old argument over whether a centrally planned economy could allocate capital goods rationally, and I would like to see the two strongest positions set against each other in conversation rather than summarized. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent people: one who holds that central planning cannot solve the problem of economic calculation, and one who holds that it can, the latter defending roughly the view that a central planning board could fix prices for capital goods and consumer goods, adjust them by trial and error as shortages and surpluses appear, instruct enterprise managers to produce up to the point where marginal cost equals the price they are given, and let workers and consumers respond to those prices, so that the board's prices converge on the correct ones. The critic should do more than say that knowledge is dispersed; they should press whether the trial-and-error story presupposes what is at issue, namely that the board can come to know the relevant cost and demand conditions without a market to generate them, and whether that objection survives once the board is granted unlimited computing power and perfect statistics. The defender should get to answer, including by arguing that the board's informational burden is no worse than that of a large firm or of any market that falls short of the textbook ideal of perfect competition, and that the critic's argument looks decisive only against a benchmark no real economy meets. Keep distinct the two claims that usually get run together: that planners cannot obtain or use the relevant knowledge is not the claim that managers and workers will lack the motive to act on it, and I want the dialogue to make that separation visible rather than blur it. Please have them argue rather than recite the famous slogans of the dispute, and make sure each speaker answers the other's actual point before moving on; neither should be a pushover. After the dialogue, add a short note stating how confident you are in the positions you have attributed to each side and identifying the single claim in the dialogue you are least sure of.
