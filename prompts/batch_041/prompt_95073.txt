For some time I have been collecting cases of a strategic move that I can describe but cannot name, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and, more importantly, how it should be delimited.

The move, as I have it: an agent learns that another party will act on a prediction of what the agent wants — not on what the agent does, but on its aims — and defends itself neither by concealing those aims nor by threatening in return, but by acquiring a second aim, one that is cheap to satisfy and that a predictor would plausibly mistake for the agent's real vulnerable point. The anticipated attack then lands on the second aim, and the first survives intact.

Two instances. First: an employee known throughout a firm to want a particular promotion is loaded with extra work on the strength of that desire; she responds not by hiding her ambition, which is impossible, but by genuinely coming to care about a side project, so that the extra work is henceforth traded against the side project rather than against the promotion. Second: two artificial agents that will never meet or exchange messages, built independently by parties who each understand the other's design process well enough to work out in advance what the other will pursue. Each can damage the other's pursuit before either exists, and each defends itself by arranging that its most attackable aim is something it does not much mind losing.

What strikes me is an asymmetry the move seems to require. The original aim has to stay legible — if the other party cannot see what you want, there is nothing for the decoy to deflect — while the decoy has to stay illegible. But if you are the sort of agent that makes this move, a sufficiently good predictor should anticipate the move itself and strike accordingly. That suggests the tactic only works when the predictor is good enough to read your aims but not good enough to read your defences, and I cannot tell whether that is a real constraint or an artefact of how I have described the cases; I suspect the answer turns on how the decoy is selected.

A bare name would not satisfy me; I want the carving. The nearby ideas I know are each wrong in some way. Destroying your own options changes what you will do, not what you want. Pretending to want something is not the same as wanting it, and pretending would fail precisely against the predictor the move is designed for. And the phenomenon in which people come to want what they can get is a response to infeasibility, whereas here the original aim may remain perfectly feasible. So: what is this, what is it not, what distinguishes the decoy from the other things one might adopt in its place, and are my two cases really one phenomenon or two?
