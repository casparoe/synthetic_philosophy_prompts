I am a postgraduate student in the philosophy of science, and for a departmental reading group on the problem of induction I have been asked to contribute a piece of short fiction rather than yet another summary of the secondary literature. I should like you, taking the role of a writer who is also thoroughly at home in philosophy of science, to compose a short story that dramatises Goodman's new riddle of induction — the puzzle about grue and bleen — rather than merely explaining it. Set it in a society (a future colony, a remote field station, an isolated island community — your choice) whose colour vocabulary takes 'grue' and 'bleen' as its basic, primitive terms, so that 'green' and 'blue' are the strange, gerrymandered predicates for its inhabitants. I want the story to turn on an inductive dispute: a character makes a confident prediction about the colour of something not yet examined, a second character challenges the rule on which that prediction rests, and the disagreement cannot be settled by any observation, because both parties agree about everything observed so far. Please let the resolution — if there is one — hinge on the sort of consideration Goodman himself offers, rather than on a hidden fact about emeralds or on a clever experiment, and give real texture to the setting and the characters, since I want it to work as fiction and not as a dialogue with stage directions. Before you begin, think carefully about which single scene will carry the philosophical weight, and brainstorm two or three candidate settings and premises before committing to one; then write the story. Please do not have a character deliver a lecture on the history of the problem — the ideas should be carried by the situation.

A few further things I should like. Make definite choices — a definite setting, definite characters, a definite ending — and give your reasons for them rather than hedging or laying out alternatives; I would rather have a story that takes a view than one that surveys the options. Keep it to roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. At the end, add a short author's note, outside the story, explaining where the piece takes liberties with the philosophical material or leans on a contested reading of it — for instance, if it quietly presupposes a particular answer to whether the grue puzzle has a solution, or handles the supposed symmetry between the two vocabularies in a way that some philosophers would reject. Write in British English throughout.
