I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental biologist, most of them on the sensory physiology of invertebrates, and it was only in retirement — with the grant deadlines finally behind me — that I began to read philosophy in earnest. I have been surprised to find that the puzzle I keep circling back to is one I first brushed against at the bench, long before I had any vocabulary for it. It is the puzzle of how a process can be competent: how it can reliably produce results that look intended, or reasoned out, or understood, while nothing anywhere in the process understands anything at all. Once you see it, it is hard not to see it everywhere — in an immune response, in a breeding program, in the way a termite colony builds, in the way a market clears. And then, if you are honest, you begin to suspect that the seeing is itself an instance of the thing seen.

I would like you to write a short story that makes a reader feel this puzzle from the inside, rather than hear it explained.

Some constraints, which I hope will sharpen the result rather than cramp it:

1. Do not explain the idea; stage it. The story should not mention Darwin, natural selection, Turing, or computation, and should avoid the words "algorithm," "evolution," and "information" in their technical senses. If the idea is present, it should be present as events, choices, and failures of explanation.

2. Avoid the obvious setting. No artificial intelligence lab, no robot, no conscious machine. Put the competence somewhere unglamorous and embodied — a kitchen, a clinic, a tannery, a hatchery, a customs house, a garden — and stay inside it.

3. Give the story a maker: someone whose craft works. The crucial turn is that her competence must visibly outrun her comprehension. She can reliably get the result, but the account she gives of why it works is a story she tells afterward, and that story is not what is doing the work. Let her discover this, or half-discover it, at some cost to herself.

4. Include one moment — a single gesture or sentence will do — in which the inversion turns back on the person doing the noticing: the very act of explaining the uncomprehending competence is itself shown to be a competence its explainer does not comprehend. Do not have a character announce this; let it land as an image or a silence.

5. End without settling whether the thing at the center of the story "really" understood anything. The uncertainty should read as the correct place to stop, not as a hedge.

Length: under 1,500 words. One continuous scene, no more than three characters.
