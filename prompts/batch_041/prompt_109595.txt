Here's the view I've talked myself into. It's a family of ideas that circulates under names like "heuristic arguments" and "the presumption of independence," and here's my reconstruction of it.

Core claim: correlation requires a mechanism, mechanisms are programs, and short programs are scarce. So once you've conditioned on everything expressible in, say, b bits, you should treat what's left as independent — not as a subjective prior you're allowed to have, but as a structural fact about the world. The formal picture I've been using: a heuristic argument about an instance x is a short program H that samples pairs (x, y), where y is whatever you care about — a witness, a value, a property — and H is short enough that it can't be manufacturing the correlation by brute force. You then read off a conditional: given that the pair came out of a heuristic of complexity at most b, treat x and y as independent conditional on H's own description. All the correlation has to be paid for by the length of H; whatever's left over is noise. So "this instance is generic, there's nothing special about it" becomes a claim with content — it's the claim that no short program would have to exist for the instance to be special. And the whole thing is supposed to be approximately self-consistent: heuristics that both apply to the same question should agree up to the complexity slack, and there shouldn't be a short way to Dutch-book someone who reasons this way.

The philosophical part I actually care about: I think this is the right formalization of what a bounded agent knows. Better than just having a prior over computable hypotheses, because it lets you reason about instances you can't describe, and because it turns "there's no reason for these two things to be related" into something with content instead of a shrug. And I think the real content of the theory is the self-consistency, not any particular heuristic.
