I'm a third-year undergraduate, and this term I'm taking a seminar on value theory while also working as a peer tutor for the introductory course Reasoning and Value. Next week I have to run the fifty-minute section on whether 'better than' is transitive, and the professor wants it built around what she calls a spectrum argument: a sequence of cases where each adjacent pair seems to be related by 'better than,' yet the two ends reverse. The students are first-years who have had one lecture on relations and transitivity (they can define transitivity, symmetry, and reflexivity) and one on pairwise preference, but they have not seen any of the standard examples from the literature; the professor name-dropped a few, so I want to avoid them and have the section feel like we are discovering the problem ourselves. I have been developing a case I call the chocolate spectrum, and I want it to carry the whole section. Imagine 101 chocolate bars, numbered 0 to 100, where bar n contains n percent cacao. A panel of expert tasters, working pairwise and blind, judges bar n+1 better than bar n for every n from 0 to 99: each step adds a barely perceptible increment of intensity and complexity that the panel, tasting only those two bars side by side, prefers. But when the panel tastes bar 0 and bar 100 side by side, it overwhelmingly judges bar 0 better, because bar 100 is bitter and unpalatable. If 'better than' is transitive, the 99 adjacent judgments imply that bar 100 is better than bar 0, contradicting the direct judgment. So either transitivity fails, or one of the judgments is not really a judgment of betterness, or the pairwise context changes what is being judged. I am worried the students will hear this as a mere preference reversal or a sensory illusion rather than a genuine challenge to the transitivity of betterness, and I am not sure whether to keep the example as is or modify it so the endpoints involve something less obviously sensory.

Please plan the explanation for me; do not give me the explanation itself. I need the order in which the ideas must come and why—what depends on what—including where the load-bearing step is. I have a rough sequence in mind: relation properties, the intuitive case for transitivity of betterness, the spectrum case, the distinction between local pairwise judgments and global all-things-considered judgments, the possible responses (deny an adjacent judgment, deny the endpoint judgment, deny transitivity, or deny that the local judgments are about betterness at all), and the consequences for practical reasoning. Tell me if that sequence is wrong, what has to be in place before the chocolate case can do any philosophical work, and what I should put after it. I have also been toying with introducing a distinction between respect-relative betterness (better in intensity, better in palatability) and all-in betterness, and I want to know whether that distinction should come before or after the case, and whether it helps the students see the problem or just gives them an escape hatch. I also want to know what the carrying example must have and lack: is the chocolate case suitable, or should I replace it with something like a sequence of medical treatments or a series of policy options where the endpoints are not merely matters of taste? If I keep it, what should I change to prevent the 'just a preference cycle' objection from swallowing the lesson? And what should I deliberately leave out at this level—I am thinking the full formal proof, the literature on incommensurability, the distinction between intransitivity and incompleteness, and any attempt to resolve the paradox—and how should I tell the students that I am leaving those out?
