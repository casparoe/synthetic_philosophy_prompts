So I'm in this reading group thing that's basically me, two friends, and one guy who actually did a philosophy degree, and we've been going at the killing vs letting die thing for like three months now. Two of us have stopped even listening to each other and I want to write up the doc we should've written at the start. I'm a backend engineer so excuse the vocabulary, I'm sure I'm mangling some term of art here.

The two sides are:

World-side view: whether you killed someone or let them die is a fact about the causal shape of what actually happened. Did the harm come out of something you introduced, or was it a process already running that you declined to interrupt? On this view the line is discovered rather than decreed, and it's morally deep, because being the one who put the harm into the world is genuinely different from being the one who didn't take it out.

Rulebook view: there's no such fact. What counts as doing vs allowing depends on which action is the default in some practice, role or rule. The same physical sequence gets redescribed as doing or allowing just by moving the default, and what the distinction really tracks is what people were entitled to expect of you. Real and morally serious, but made, not found.

What I want is the joint statement these two could both sign after an honest sit-down. The claims they both accept (about the cases, about what's even in dispute, about what the distinction would have to be to do any moral work). Then the precise disagreements, each written the way its own side would actually want it phrased, not as a strawman. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. I suspect one crux is whether there's a privileged baseline at all, like "the natural course of events", or whether every baseline is chosen by whoever's telling the story. Another might be whether the line is about causation at all or just about duties. But don't take my guesses, tell me if I'm wrong and where the real cruxes are. For each crux give a concrete case that both sides agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts about that case before anyone gets to weasel.

Cases you can use, all made up, no need to cite anything: an autopilot that brakes unless the driver holds a button down, versus one that coasts unless the driver presses a button — same crash either way, but is the crash something the car did or something it let happen? The bug we knew about and shipped anyway versus the feature we decided not to build. The doctor who just doesn't order the test. And the old one about one healthy guy and five dying patients, where I think both sides agree it's wrong but maybe not for the same reason.

Format it as a numbered list of agreed claims, then a table for the disagreements, then the cruxes with the predictions. And don't let either side buy peace with "it's a spectrum" or "it depends on context" unless they actually cash that out into something that seperates the cases.
