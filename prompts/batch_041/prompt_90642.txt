You sever the corpus callosum and suddenly one hand is doing things the other has no idea about, and the speaking hemisphere will confabulate a reason for behavior it didn't actually initiate. Most of what I read says this shows two streams of consciousness sharing one skull. I'm sitting at about 0.45 on that and I honestly can't tell if I'm just being stubborn.

What I want from you: your actual credence that a patient with a full callosotomy harbors two numerically distinct streams of phenomenal consciousness -- not just two access streams, not just two response systems, but two subjects of experience -- plus the decomposition that gets you there. Something like: the probability that phenomenal unity is a real, well-defined constraint on a system rather than a folk notion we should stop reifying; the probability that consciousness can be multiply instantiated within a single brainstem and body; the probability that the dual-behavior data reflect two subjects rather than one subject with gated access; and whatever other terms you think belong. Give a number and one line of justification for each, then show me how they combine into your final figure. Then tell me which term I should be most suspicious of, and what single result or argument would move it the most. I can't look anything up, so just reason it out from what you've got.

And tell me what would even count as resolving this, since we can't exactly ask the patient.

One more wrinkle I keep flip-flopping on: suppose the two hemispheres still share the same brainstem and body, and suppose we could rig up some pathetically low-bandwidth channel between them -- call it two bits a second. Does that snap it back to being one mind, or is it still two? Wherever that lands, say how much it moves your number and why.
