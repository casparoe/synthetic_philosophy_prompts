I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, long enough to have absorbed the local folklore about consciousness: that the hard problem is hard, that zombies are at least conceivable, and that some materialists end up treating experience as a glow that rides along on top of the machinery without ever pushing back on it. What I have never seen worked out carefully in that corner of the internet is the tangle this last picture creates for the materialist's own claims about experience, and I would like you to explain it to me.

Set the picture up this way: a physical state P realizes a phenomenal state Q, and P also drives a downstream physical process that issues in the report "I am having a Q-ish experience." Q itself causes nothing. The report expresses a judgment — that the subject is undergoing Q. My question is how that judgment can so much as be about Q, let alone count as knowledge of it, when Q is stipulated to do no causal work anywhere in the story.

Work through it in order. First, say why this looks like a genuine puzzle rather than a dressed-up version of the problem of other minds, and be precise about what exactly is supposed to be impossible. Second, give the standard escape — the judgment and the phenomenal state share a common cause, so the judgment can be reliable without the phenomenal state causing it — and explain why that move seems to defuse the worry. Third, tell me what the escape costs. In particular, if the content of the judgment is fixed entirely by the physical side, then the same judgment would have been produced whether Q was present, absent, or replaced by an inverted counterpart; say what that does to the claim that the judgment is about Q rather than merely correlated with it. Fourth, and this is the part I care most about, say whether the residual problem is really a problem about causation or a problem about intentionality — about what makes one internal state represent this rather than that — and whether leaning on a causal-informational or teleological theory of content helps the epiphenomenalist or quietly converts the position into something else.

Please skip the standard tour of the hard problem, the knowledge argument, and the conceivability of zombies; I know that material. I want the specific knot that appears when someone denies phenomenal causation while still insisting that our talk about experience is about experience. Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you end up giving, and single out the step you are least sure of. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, the way you would write it in a long email.
