Write a dialogue between two historians of a single invented episode — a polity that comes apart over the course of one decisive week. Invent the setting, the actors, and the proximate event; keep the exchange anchored to that one case rather than ranging across real historiography, and let the two of them disagree about that case rather than about history in general. The first holds that the outcome was inevitable: the structural facts were already sufficient, and the dramatic week merely registered what had long been settled. The second holds that causal explanation in history just is counterfactual explanation — to say that the fiscal crisis caused the collapse is to say that without the crisis the collapse would not have happened — and concludes that the first is quietly relying on the very method they claim to distrust.

Both should be competent, and neither should be permitted to win by caricature. In particular:

- The inevitabilist should not fall back on fatalism, nor on the thought that hindsight makes everything look necessary. Their strongest position is that the outcome was overdetermined and resilient: several independent routes led to the same end, so deleting any one of them would have changed little. They should be made to say what distinguishes genuine overdetermination from a pattern noticed only after the fact.
- The counterfactualist should not merely repeat that causes are counterfactuals. They should be able to say how one of their counterfactuals is to be evaluated — by imagining a minimal revision of what actually happened — and should then be pressed on the word minimal: minimal with respect to what, and selected by whom?
- The inevitabilist should land at least one real blow. A promising one: that evaluating the counterfactualist's revisions requires some sense of how probable the revision is, or of a range of revisions, and that the historical record supplies no such probabilities — so the counterfactualist's verdicts are as ungrounded as the inevitabilist's.
- The counterfactualist should have a genuine answer rather than a retreat. They might argue that causal talk is contrastive, so that the operative question is always why this outcome rather than a specified alternative, and that once the alternative is fixed the counterfactual has a determinate answer; or they might argue that inevitability at a coarse grain of description is compatible with contingency at a fine grain, and that the two of them may be disagreeing about the grain rather than about the past.
- If either of them concludes that the dispute is partly verbal, that conclusion must be earned by the argument and must cost the one who reaches it something, and the other should contest it rather than accept it politely.

They should answer each other's actual formulations, concede where a point tells, and shift ground when they must. Do not end in a handshake or a truce. Do not name real historians or reuse the standard textbook what-ifs.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1100 words.
