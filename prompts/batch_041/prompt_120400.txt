My museum's been sitting on a small scandal for about a year now. Our most famous painting — the one on all the postcards, the one a local family gave us back in the sixties — turned out last spring not to be by the artist everyone said it was. Probably a very good nineteenth-century forgery: made to deceive, and it did, for a hundred years. The board spent months fighting over whether to take it off the wall. We compromised. It's staying up, but relabelled, in a new show I've put together about copies, versions and fakes, which also includes some period copies that were always honest about being copies.

I have to give the opening remarks. In the room: the donors grandchildren, who are upset; the curator who spotted the forgery and would rather I not soften anything; two trustees who still think we should just sell the thing; local press; and our education staff, who'll have to explain all of this to school groups. Please write the remarks — short, six or seven minutes, no hedging cliches about the mystery of art.

One thing I want the speech to actually do work with. I've been telling the staff there are at least three different things people might mean when they say the real painting is the valuable one. The surface on the wall, which our fake has every bit as much of as any original does. The causal thread back to a particular pair of hands — this object was touched by him, that one wasn't. And the achievement, which is invisible on the wall: the original is the record of someone doing something hard, first, with no model in front of them. A fake has an achievement too, but it's the achievement of getting away with it, which is a different thing, and arguably a less interesting one to stand in front of. I don't want a philosophy lecture, but the speech should be honest about which of those three we're actually asking people to care about, and it should let the donor family and the trustees each hear something true rather than something flattering.
