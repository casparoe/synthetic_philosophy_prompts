I am a senior in high school, and this term our philosophy club has been working through the question of whether people deserve what they earn. We had a long argument after one meeting about a friend whose parents both work minimum-wage jobs and who insists that anyone who works hard enough will end up fine, and I have been trying to work out my own position ever since. I am supposed to present it at our next meeting and then write it up for an essay competition, so before I commit to anything I want to know whether the views I actually hold can survive being put side by side. Stated one at a time they each sound obviously right to me, which is exactly what worries me.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) Distributive fairness requires that people be rewarded in proportion to their merit, where I take merit to be mostly a matter of how much effort they put in and how well they end up performing; two people who differ only in effort should not end up with radically different rewards. (2) No one deserves the raw talents, temperament, or family circumstances that make them capable of that effort in the first place, since none of that was chosen; so whatever desert attaches to effort sits on a foundation that is itself undeserved. (3) Even so, I do not think we should try to distribute income according to some official assessment of individual desert; wages should mostly be left to markets, because any centralized attempt to decide who deserves what would be unreliable, easy to game, and probably worse than the alternative. (4) What really matters for justice is equality of opportunity: if everyone has a genuinely fair shot at competing for the good positions, then the resulting inequalities are legitimate even when they are large. (5) Concretely, I think it is right that a surgeon earns far more than a cashier, and also right that a brilliant but lazy person generally earns more than a diligent but unproductive one, because the former contributes more. (6) At the same time, I think a great deal of top pay in law, finance, and entertainment is set by bargaining power and winner-take-all dynamics rather than by how much good the work actually does, and that much of it is therefore unjustified. (7) And I think no one is a lesser person for doing low-paid work; our habit of treating earnings as a measure of human worth is corrosive.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict and dissolve once the right distinctions are drawn, and which are in genuine tension. Please order the real tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say specifically what would have to give and what that concession would cost. If a single revision would clean up the most, tell me which one and why. I genuinely do not know which of these I would abandon if I were forced to, so do not assume any of them is the one I am secretly willing to drop.
