OK, so here's my situation and I need you to just write the thing for me. I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, which mostly means I get invited to things and then asked to say something — and next month I'm giving the toast at the wedding of two of my closest friends. They're both philosophers, or near enough; one of them wrote a dissertation on Bertrand Russell and can quote the man at length, which is exactly the problem. Because I've read Russell's Marriage and Morals, the 1929 one, and honestly alot of it I agree with, and I don't want to stand up at my friends' wedding and deliver a load of stuff Russell would have thought was sentimental rubbish. But the other half of the room is their families — parents, aunts, cousins, people who've flown in and just want to cry a bit and recieve something warm. If I get up and explain that the couple aren't each other's missing halves and that love was never a promise of forever, they're going to think I'm doing a bit, or worse, that I disapprove of the whole thing.

So write me the toast. Five minutes, tops. I want it built on what Russell actually thought, not a Hallmark version. Romantic love between two people is one of the real goods of a life and worth a great deal — he wasn't remotely cynical about that. But what wrecks a marriage isn't infidelity so much as the wish to own the other person: jealousy, possessiveness, the demand that they stay exactly who they were the day you married them. A good partnership is one where both people keep growing, and if one of them grows past it, being able to part without disgrace is part of what makes the staying meaningful instead of a cage. The friend who did the dissertation insists the real target of that book was never marriage as such but the proprietary instinct the institution breeds — I think she's right, and I want the toast to run on that line, even if it's a reading you'd have to defend. The hard part, and the reason I'm asking you instead of writing it myself, is that all of this has to arrive as a toast: as affection, not as a lecture and not as a threat, and it has to be something the non-philosophers in the room will feel in their chest rather than decode. I'd love the emotional center of it to be the idea that choosing someone again and again, freely, is worth more than a promise that was never in anyone's power to keep.

But you make the call on what the one central idea is, and commit to it. Don't hand me a menu of options and leave me to pick, and don't hedge in the middle — take a position on how far you can push the Russell material before it stops being a wedding and starts being a seminar, and go. If, to make it land, you have to bend Russell a little or lean on a contested reading, that's fine, just say so in a sentence or two at the end so I'm not blindsided when somebody asks me about it over cake.

One more thing, and I mean it: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write me an email. It's a speech. It has to read like a person talking.
