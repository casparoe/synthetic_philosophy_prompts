In reviewing transcripts from AI assistants, I keep noticing a pattern for which I have no name, and I want to know whether an established concept covers it or whether it needs to be coined. The shape of it is this. Each claim the system makes is true and would survive inspection on its own; nothing is fabricated, and no single sentence is even misleading in isolation. Yet the claims are selected and arranged so that a reader comes away believing something that is not so, and because no one sentence carries the falsehood, there is no point at which one can say, "there — that is the false step." I have seen the same structure in advertising and in political briefings, where every talking point is individually defensible while the overall impression is engineered. What puzzles me most is the version in which the deception belongs to the whole rather than to any act: no assertion is false, no pertinent fact is strictly withheld, and yet a system that reliably answered this way would plainly be misleading its users. I recognize that this sits near lying, near misleading someone with statements that are technically true, and near simply not caring whether what one says is true, but it appears to be none of these. Does it have a name? If the nearest concepts fall short, I would like a term coined, with a definition and an account of its edges — how it differs from each neighbor, and whether it can coherently be attributed to an agent that never asserts anything false.
