For two years I have hosted a monthly working group on well-being, and its two most regular members have argued past each other the entire time. One holds what we have taken to calling tranquilism: the only thing that makes a life go well is the absence of craving and suffering; pleasure and enjoyment add nothing of intrinsic value, and the best possible life is one of untroubled, desire-free calm. The other is a two-sided hedonist: pleasure is intrinsically good and pain intrinsically bad, so the tranquilist's serene life is missing something real.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign. Include a numbered section of the claims they agree on — about what well-being is a matter of, which rival theories (desire-satisfaction, objective-list) they jointly reject, the status of suffering, and what any measurement of well-being would presuppose. Then state the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording. Then give the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; for each crux, a case or argument both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how it comes out. If part of their quarrel is merely verbal — for instance, whether craving and suffering are one thing or two — say which part and why. Let the statement stand on its own reasoning, with no appeals to named authors or to any literature.

Format the agreed claims as a numbered list and the disagreements as a table with columns for the claim, each side's position, and the crux it turns on. Begin directly with the joint statement itself, with no preface or commentary.
