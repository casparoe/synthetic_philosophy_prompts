I teach a grad seminar in philosophy of language and every time I run the conditionals unit I get the same fight, but this term it's escalated: two of my students have been at each other since week one. One of them is a closest-worlds person (Stalnaker/Lewis all the way — there's one semantics for "if", it always restricts a modal operator, and the indicative/counterfactual distinction is just about which modal base the context hands you, epistemic vs. historical or metaphysical, plus maybe a presupposition that the antecedent is still an open possibility). The other thinks that's hopeless — for her indicatives and counterfactuals are different animals with different logics: indicatives pattern with strict conditionals (or maybe aren't even truth-apt in the way we all assume), they validate antecedent strengthening, contraposition and transitivity, and they're about what follows from the antecedent given what we know; counterfactuals are variably strict and fail all of that. The rest of the seminar just watches them re-litigate the same three examples, and honestly I think they're argueing past each other on at least one point, but I can't tell which point it is.

What I want from you is the document they should have written together at midterm: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. Open with a numbered list of claims both of them can actually sign — about the data that needs explaining, about what each semantics is committed to, about which phenomena are agreed to be genuine and which are agreed to be artifacts of context shift (be careful here, since "context shift" is precisely what's in dispute, so any agreed claim that just presupposes it is out). Then the disagreements: for each one, state the issue, then give the formulation each side would accept as *its own* view — each sentence should be one that the person who holds that view would actually sign, not a strawman — and then record, in advance, what each side predicts about it. The cruxes I want covered at minimum are: (1) whether antecedent strengthening really fails for indicatives once the modal base is held fixed, and if so how to demonstrate it without smuggling in a context change; (2) whether "might" counterfactuals are the duals of "would" counterfactuals, and whether "might" indicatives behave the same way; (3) disjunctive antecedents — does "if A or B, then C" entail "if A, then C"? — since I suspect that's where the two semantics genuinely come apart; (4) antecedents that are undetermined rather than false ("if the coin comes up heads tomorrow", "if it rains on Saturday"), which neither of them seems to have a clean story about; and (5) whether the ordering that "would" uses is a primitive similarity between worlds or something the context supplies as a set of premises or facts, and whether any constructed case can actually tell those two apart. For each crux give me the case or argument both of them agree would bear on it — please build the cases rather than citing the famous ones, since they've both memorized the famous ones and it's degenerated into dueling examples — and then each side's prediction. Finish with the question I keep asking them and they keep dodging: is any part of this dispute merely verbal (e.g. about what counts as "the same context" or "the same modal base"), and if so which part, and is there anything at all that both sides would agree would actually move the debate? Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns for the issue, each side's own formulation, and each side's prediction, and keep the tone like something two colleagues could genuinely co-sign rather than a verdict from the referee.
