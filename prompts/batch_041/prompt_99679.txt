I write about philosophy and the human sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on a piece about the free will debate — specifically about the cluster of thought experiments known as Frankfurt cases and about the principle that a person is morally responsible for what she does only if she could have done otherwise. Before I commit to a line, I want to check the account I have been relying on. It comes from a trade book on free will by a philosopher who is well regarded for explaining this material to non-specialists, and the book's central section, as I would paraphrase it, goes roughly like this.

The principle of alternative possibilities says that you are morally responsible for an action only if you could have done otherwise. Frankfurt cases are designed to show that this principle is false. In the standard case, a neuroscientist — call him Black — wants Jones to perform a certain action and has secretly installed a device in Jones's brain that will, if Jones shows any sign of wavering, stimulate him into performing the action anyway. Jones knows nothing of any of this and performs the action on his own, for reasons of his own. So Jones could not have done otherwise: had he been about to refrain, Black would have made him do it. Yet no one, the book says, seriously doubts that Jones is responsible. It follows that alternative possibilities are not required for moral responsibility, and therefore that free will is compatible with determinism. The deeper lesson, on the book's telling, is that what matters for responsibility is not whether you could have chosen otherwise but whether the action flowed from your own desires and character rather than from an external force; an action that comes from the agent in that way is free and responsible even if every choice in it was determined. The book concedes that critics have raised technical objections to the cases, but says these have been answered and that the cases are now the received view in the field.

I am inclined to believe the book. The author works in the area, and the argument as laid out seems clean to me. But I have been burned before by trade books that smooth over disputes that are still live among specialists, and this time I would rather not print a mistake in a magazine whose readers include people who know the literature. A philosopher I occasionally call for help did mention, in passing, that critics press something they call a flicker of freedom, and I could not get him to explain it in a sentence I could use; if that is the shape of the objection, I would like to know what it comes to. So I would like an audit of the summary, claim by claim: for each distinct assertion, whether it is sound as stated, a harmless simplification, misleading, or simply wrong; where a defender of the Frankfurt approach would object to my paraphrase; and where a critic would object to the approach itself. I am especially interested in which error is load-bearing — which one, once corrected, would change the shape of the book's conclusion — and in whether the conclusion that survives correction is still the one the book wants. Where the summary is right, please say so plainly; I do not want a reflexively skeptical reading.

Give me the audit as a JSON object with the following fields.

- claims: an array with one entry per distinct assertion in my paraphrase, each an object with:
  - claim: a one-sentence restatement of the assertion being audited, in my words;
  - verdict: exactly one of "sound", "simplification", "misleading", "wrong";
  - explanation: two to four sentences saying why it earns that verdict, including the distinction or counterexample that decides the matter;
  - pushback: what a defender of the summary would say in reply, or null if there is nothing to say.
- load_bearing_error: an object with claim (the assertion in question) and why_it_matters (a short paragraph on how the book's conclusion depends on it), or null if no single error is load-bearing.

- bottom_line: one paragraph, no more than 150 words, on how fair the book's treatment is and what I should say in the piece.

Keep the entries plain enough that I can use them in a magazine, but do not drop a distinction that matters just to keep them simple.
