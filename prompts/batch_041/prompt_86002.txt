I just spent three hours arguing with a friend who works in numerical analysis, and I need to get the disagreement straight. The recurring move is this. A classical proof establishes that some object exists—a solution to an equation, a fixed point, a choice function on a family of sets. A constructivist objects that the proof gives no way to produce the object. The defender replies that the proof is constructive in principle: if you tracked the dependencies, the object would be computable from the data, and the absence of an explicit algorithm is a fact about us, not about the proof. Then, years later, someone actually extracts an algorithm from the proof. At that point the original proof is described as having contained the algorithm all along, and the earlier defense is treated as vindicated. A related version: once a constructive proof of the same theorem is found, the classical proof is retroactively credited with the constructive result, as though the theorem’s constructive status transferred back to the proof that did not establish it constructively. What interests me is the temporal and status-changing character of the move: the same proof is first defended as constructively acceptable without an algorithm, and later praised as constructively valuable because of the algorithm, and the later discovery is read back into the original proof as if it had always been there. I know the neighborhood includes proof mining, realizability, and the general distinction between classical and constructive existence, but none of those seems to be the exact phenomenon. Is there an established name for this specific retrospective reassignment of constructive status? If not, coin a term and define it. I care less about the label than about the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. How does it differ from ordinary proof mining, from the claim that a proof is constructive in principle, and from the claim that every classical proof has a constructive interpretation? Is it a genuine form of mathematical progress, a rationalization, a category mistake, or something mixed? What would count as a boundary case—for example, a classical proof later shown to have no computable content, or one whose extracted algorithm is nonuniform?
