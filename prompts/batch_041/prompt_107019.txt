I'm prepping a grad seminar on consciousness and I keep getting into the same fight with a colleague who thinks 'qualia' is just a word for a confusion. Her line is that any attempt to specify qualia either collapses into functional/representational facts or into something we could never talk about, so the whole notion is incoherent. I want you to argue for a position on this: argue that qualia are not an incoherent posit, but that the only defensible version is a deflationary one—qualia as the intrinsic, non-representational determinables that fix the phenomenal character of an experience, where 'intrinsic' doesn't mean unobservable in principle but rather that their identity conditions aren't exhausted by their causal/functional role. To make it concrete, imagine a case: two subjects are functionally identical, report the same things, and have the same discriminatory capacities, but we discover a hidden parameter that swaps their color experiences—so subject A's experience of red is subject B's experience of green. Both say things like 'I can't tell whether your red looks like my red, but I can tell that my red doesn't look like my green.' Argue that this case supports the deflationary reading rather than undermining it, and that the incoherence charge equivocates between metaphysical and epistemic senses of 'intrinsic.'

Don't just summarize the usual suspects; I want an original arguement that takes the incoherence charge seriously and either defeats it or concedes it. Somewhere in there, say what work the deflationary reading is supposed to do that a purely functionalist reading can't. Also, I'm not asking for a literature review—just reason from the concepts and the case.
