ok so I'm a philosophy grad student and my office-mate Dana has very firm views about dispositions — fragile, soluble, poisonous, brave, generous, all of it. She thinks they're real properties that things have, not just shorthand for how things would go if you poked them. I have to argue against her next week and I'd rather know what she's actually commited to before I open my mouth, so here's her track record on cases we've been through. (She has names for these kinds of cases — "finks," "masks," "mimics" — I think she got them from somewhere, but the cases are the part that matters.)

1. An ordinary drinking glass, genuinely fragile. Someone has rigged it so that at the exact instant it gets struck, it becomes temporarily unbreakable — just long enough to survive the strike — then reverts. So it will never break, no matter how you hit it. Dana: still fragile. The rig isn't changing the glass, it's just stopping the breaking.

2. Same glass, now wrapped in thick foam padding, so a strike never breaks it. Dana: still fragile.

3. A stage magician's trick cup. Looks like glass, is actually tough plastic, not fragile at all. Bolted to the outside is a little spring-loaded device that shatters the cup every time the cup is struck. So it shatters every single time you hit it. Dana: not fragile. The shattering isn't coming from the cup.

4. Our friend Marcus, who is generous — like, it's just a settled fact about him, everyone who knows him says so. He's now two years into a posting at an Antarctic station where there's nobody else and nothing to give anyone. Dana: still generous. Nothing about him changed, only his situation.

Next week's case, the one I actually care about: a bacterium that's lethal to an unprotected human being. But basically every living human has been immune to it for generations — universal, permanent childhood immunisation, and it's been that way as long as anyone can remember and will stay that way. So it has never made anyone sick and it never will. Does she say it's still lethal? Or does she say "lethal" was always about how it goes with unprotected people, and in the conditions it's actually always in, it does nothing?

You're a philosopher who works on dispositions and causal powers, so think carefully, and brainstorm the candidate rules before you commit to anything. No need to cite anything, I'm not after a reading list.

What I want:

- her rule, stated as a rule I could apply to a new case
- at least two other rules that fit all four of her verdicts just as well, and what motivates each of them
- what each rule predicts about the bacterium, and which one you think she's actually running
- your best guess at her answer for the bacterium, with reasons, and how confident you are
- whether the bacterium case actually distinguishes the rules, and if it doesn't, what case would
- anywhere her four verdicts are just inconsistent with each other, or where she'd have to give one up

And say where you think there's no fact of the matter here — I'm suspicious that "does it really have the disposition" is sometimes just a fight about what we're using the word for.
