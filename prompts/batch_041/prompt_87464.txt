I teach a graduate seminar on killing and saving, and one of my colleagues — call her Mira — keeps handing down verdicts in discussion that I can't get to fit any single view, and it's driving me a little crazy. She's careful, she isn't confused, and she isn't just picking whatever feels warm. So I've started keeping a list. They're all variations on the old "do the numbers count" setup, where a rescuer can reach one group or another and not both. One man on the left, five on the right, boat reaches only one group, all strangers, all six would drown otherwise, no extra risk to the crew either way: either choice is permissible, and she won't say the five have a stronger claim — press her on fairness and she says the fair thing is one straw per person, so the five win with probability 5/6, but that, she says, is about equal respect rather than about the five having a stronger claim, and anyway she dosen't see how you'd add one person's claim to another's. Same boat, but the five would each lose a leg and the one would drown: no hesitation, you must save the one. Same boat again, the one would loose a hand and sixty would drown: no hesitation the other way, you must save the sixty. Five would drown unless you take the only dose of antidote from a sixth man who needs it himself and isn't otherwise in any danger: you may not take it, and the five drown. And the one on the left is the man who caused the whole accident: still permissible to save him, because his carelessness doesn't shrink his claim.

Next week's case is why I'm writing this out. A harbour ferry has gone down; a rescue launch can reach the one man in the water near the harbour mouth, or the sixty passengers on a half-submerged deck that's going under in about twenty minutes. It can reach either in time but not both, everyone in both groups is a stranger to the crew, nobody is at fault, and the trip is no more dangerous or costly either way. I want to know what Mira will say, and more to the point what rule she's actually running, because I can see at least two that fit the list so far and they come apart on the ferry.

Don't hand me a menu of options. Pick the rule you think she's running, commit to what she says about the ferry, give your reasons and a rough confidence, and name the case that would separate the readings you're choosing between. Write it in four bits, in this order: "Rules her record fits" (a short list, one line each, plus a line on what her record seems to rule out — under 150 words total); "What each predicts for the ferry" (a short paragraph); "My call" (a paragraph or two, with the prediction, the rule, the reasons, and the confidence — I'd rather you commit and be wrong than hedge); and "The case that would settle it" (three or four sentences).
