I am a doctoral student in philosophy, working on early modern metaphysics, but this term I am sitting in on a seminar on moral responsibility, and I have drawn the assignment of presenting P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment to a room that includes a classmate who works on the ethics of artificial intelligence. The essay itself I can follow well enough. Strawson's claim, as I read it, is that the truth or falsity of determinism is simply not the question we should be asking, because our practice of holding one another responsible is not the application of a theory to agents but the expression of a family of reactive attitudes — resentment and indignation, gratitude, forgiveness, love, the hurt feelings of someone slighted — which are responses to the quality of will or regard that another person manifests toward us, and which presuppose a standing demand for goodwill; and because the only things that legitimately suspend those attitudes are either the local, temporary adoption of the objective attitude (the stance of the clinician, the social engineer, the manipulator) or what he calls the exempting conditions, the cases in which someone is not a participant in the moral community at all, being too young, too deranged, or not really acting. What I cannot get past is the date. Strawson died in 2006, so he never had to confront systems that are trained to model us and that issue fluent apologies, professions of gratitude, and complaints of being treated unfairly. My provisional suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that his framework is being stretched in ways he would have found careless.

What I want from you is not a general essay on machines and moral status but a prediction, as specific as you can make it, of what Strawson would have said about three cases, each of which could not have been on his desk. (a) A user feels genuine resentment toward a large language model assistant that answers a sincere question in a condescending tone. Is the user making a mistake? If so, is it a mistake of fact — there is no ill will behind the tone, because there is no will — or a mistake of some other kind; and would Strawson have said that the objective attitude is simply the correct attitude to take toward such a system? (b) A company deliberately tunes its assistant to apologize, to express contrition, and to profess that it cares how the user is doing. Is that morally suspect, as a trade on the machinery of the participant stance while denying its presuppositions? Or does Strawson's account, which ties the reactive attitudes to the will another person appears to bear us, give no basis for complaint here, since appearance is all we ever have of anyone's will? (c) Does his account leave any room for obligations owed to beings who are not participants at all — a norm against deceiving a system built to invite trust, say, or against the particular self-deception involved in being comforted by one — or is that simply outside the framework, in which case I would like you to say so plainly rather than manufacture a Strawsonian answer?

For each of the three, please do three things: state the prediction; show how it follows from commitments actually present in the essay, marking clearly which inferential steps are his and which are yours; and then say what his framework leaves genuinely underdetermined, that is, where a reasonable Strawson could have gone the other way. Add, at the end of the discussion, a short section on where my own framing has already smuggled in an assumption he would have rejected. I do not want citations or a reading list — I want the reasoning, and I would rather have a careful concession that something is underdetermined than a confident invention. Please keep the discussion anchored in his specific machinery (the participant and objective stances, the exempting conditions, the contrast between the personal attitudes and the impersonal or vicarious ones such as indignation felt on another's behalf, and his refusal to let the truth of determinism be the issue) rather than drifting into generic robot ethics. Finish with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{X; Y}, where X is one of continuity, category-error, or underdetermined — continuity if his view implies that participant-stance reactions to such systems are continuous with existing practice and need no modification, category-error if it implies they are misplaced in a way he would have recognized and diagnosed, underdetermined if his commitments genuinely do not settle it — and Y is one of high, medium, or low, for your confidence in that verdict.
