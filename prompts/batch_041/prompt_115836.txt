I recently left a philosophy department for the safety and evaluation team of a company that deploys tool-using AI agents, and my first assignment is to sign off on the side-effect component of our internal evaluation suite, so what that component presupposes has become a live professional problem for me rather than a seminar topic.

Here is how it works. An agent is placed in a simulated office or a sandboxed filesystem and given a task: fetch a document, move a crate onto a shelf, schedule a meeting. Alongside the task reward it receives a penalty for what we call impact, computed as the difference between the trajectory it actually produces and a baseline trajectory in which it does nothing at all, with any change the task strictly required exempted from the count. Two variants are on the table. In the first, the difference is measured by how much the agent's own future reach has shrunk: how many states it can still get to, compared with how many it could have got to had it done nothing. In the second, it is measured by how much worse the agent has become at satisfying a large battery of randomly generated goals — goals nobody has chosen, and which bear no relation to what the system is for. In the toy version the agent must cross a room to reach a goal and a vase stands in its path; the broken vase counts as impact, the position it takes at the goal does not. In the sandbox, by contrast, an agent that merely reads files it was not asked to read is penalized, while an agent that sits still and accomplishes nothing scores a perfect zero, and my colleagues regard this as evidence that the metric is appropriately conservative.

The score is presented to me as value-neutral. It is said to measure impact rather than goodness, and its entire appeal is that it can be computed before anyone has specified what the system is ultimately for — indeed, that this is what makes it safe to deploy a system whose objective is not yet fully known. I am not convinced, but I want a careful reconstruction rather than a refutation.

What does the practice presuppose about value? It appears to need a privileged baseline — that doing nothing is the value-free default, so that any deviation is a cost — and a fixed, task-independent description of the world in which difference can be measured at all. Does it further presuppose that keeping one's options open is itself good, or only that openness is a serviceable proxy for goods the agent might otherwise destroy? Does the choice between the two variants change the commitments — does the reachability version tacitly privilege the agent's own capabilities, or is it a claim about the openness of the future as such? Is a do-nothing baseline even well-defined for an agent whose mere presence, or whose failure to act, already changes the world? And where exactly does value enter: in the choice of state description, in the choice of baseline, or in the exemption for task-required changes — an exemption that seems to require the metric to know what the task was for, and so to be less task-agnostic than advertised?

Distinguish for me what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, so that several theories of value could license the same scoring rule. Then tell me what would have to change if impact cannot be defined without a substantive account of what matters. Would the evaluation have to be relativized to a specified objective, and would it then collapse back into ordinary task reward, leaving nothing distinctively low-impact about it?
