I have been reconstructing an argument that a colleague has been pressing on me for weeks, and I cannot decide whether it is a reductio of a concept I rely on or a reductio of my colleague. The concept is the free-floating rationale. As I understand it: a trait or a piece of behaviour can have a rationale — there can be an answer to the question what it is for, and why it takes this form rather than another — without any creature's having that answer in mind. The dam is for holding back water and the dance for directing foragers; neither the beaver nor the bee need know this, and the rationale floats free of any representation of it. The argument is this.

(1) A trait or behaviour T has a free-floating rationale when there is a fact of the form "T is as it is because it solves problem P," and that fact holds whether or not anything represents P.

(2) Natural selection is a problem-solving process in precisely that sense: a heritable variant spreads because it solved a problem of survival or reproduction, and the problem can be specified without any organism's representing it.

(3) Wherever "T is as it is because it solves P" is true, T is for P; T has a function, and T can be done correctly or incorrectly with respect to P.

(4) If a state S of a system can be assessed as correct or incorrect with respect to a condition C, and S is produced by a mechanism whose operation is explained by its sensitivity to C, then the system represents C.

(5) Therefore any organism with selected traits represents the conditions those traits are for. [from (2), (3), (4)]

(6) But nothing in (4) mentions selection, or organisms, or life. The same reasoning applies to any system with a state that can be assessed as correct or incorrect and is produced by a mechanism explained by its sensitivity to the relevant condition: the dune resting at its angle of repose, the river's meander, the slime mould's path through a maze, the antibody repertoire that matches a novel antigen.

(7) So representation is everywhere, and "represents" marks nothing; in particular, nothing is gained by calling a rationale free-floating, since wherever there is a rationale there is also a representing.

(8) So the distinction between a rationale's being free-floating and its being represented cannot be drawn, and the concept of a free-floating rationale collapses.

I am fairly sure I would rather keep (1) than accept (7), and I would rather accept (7) than accept my colleague's own conclusion, which is that the only stable stopping place is to call selection itself a kind of reasoner. But I do not trust my grip on where the trouble is: each premise looks to me like something I already believe, and (4) in particular looks like the teleological account of representation I would have defended on any other day.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked acceptable, and what rejecting it costs — what else goes with it, whether the position that results is one that is recognisably occupied or a novel one, and whether the argument is valid and merely expensive or contains a step that does not follow. I would like the alternatives priced against each other, cheapest first; I want to know what the surviving concept of a free-floating rationale looks like afterwards; and I want to know what selection has that the sand dune lacks, since that, I suspect, is the whole ballgame.
