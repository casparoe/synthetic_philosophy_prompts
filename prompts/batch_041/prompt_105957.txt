ok so for ancient history class I have to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about something, and I want to do mine on how we can know anything about the distant past at all. Not the normal version where one guy trusts Herodotus and the other guy doesn't — I want both of them to be smart and to be right about different things, and I want them to actually reply to each other instead of taking turns giving speeches. My teacher said the mark of a bad dialogue is when one person is obviously the strawman so please dont do that.

The thing I want them to fight about is what the rule should be for believing something about the past. Person A's rule: only believe it if you've got more than one source, and the sources have to be independent — if two writers say the same thing but one of them probably got it from the other, that's one witness, not two, so it doesn't count. Person B's rule: A's rule eats itself, because you can almost never prove two ancient sources are independent from the inside, and if you apply it seriously you end up not knowing most of what everyone agrees we know about the ancient world. So B wants something more like: believe the source by default unless you have a specific reason not to, and then test it with stuff like whether the story is internally consistent, whether it makes sense given what the writer wanted, whether he had a reason to lie or to flatter, whether the archaeology lines up.

Some stuff I really want them to get into, because this is the part I find interesting and it's what my teacher said she wanted more of:

- our evidence isn't a random sample of what happened. Stuff survives because somebody wrote it down, somebody else copied it, and the copy didn't get burned. So no source about something doesn't automatically mean it didn't happen — maybe the record just got filtered out. But then when is silence actually evidence? I think the interesting answer is when the surviving channel is exactly the channel that would have carried the news, so if the record keepers had every reason to write it down and they still dont, that's different from a random text going missing.
- flip side: since the filter is real, whatever did survive is probably slanted toward whatever was useful or flattering to whoever kept copying it. So maybe agreement between two sources means less than A thinks it does anyway.
- is A's independence rule even checkable? If two writers agree, is that two witnesses or one guy quoting a lost guy? Can you ever tell without having the lost source?
- is B's rule circular? "Does the story make sense?" — make sense to who? If you judge by your own sense of whats plausible you'll just believe the plausible stories and bin the weird ones, and thats your own priors wearing a costume. B needs a real answer to this, not a hand wave.
- the double standard: we believe tons of modern things on one witness — one letter, one person in a courtroom, one guy's diary. Why is one ancient witness worth less? Is that a real principle or just snobbery about old stuff?
- the embarrassing story argument, the idea that you should trust a story that makes the teller look bad because who would make that up. But people make stuff up that makes them look bad all the time, for other reasons. Should that rule survive or not?

They should argue over at least two specific cases, one where A's rule obviously seems right and one where it seems ridiculous — the Trojan War, whatever, they can pick. And I want it to end with them still disagreeing but having narrowed down exactly what they disagree about, not with a winner. Dialogue only, no narrator explaining what they're feeling, and no citations or "historians say" — they should just argue it out. Like 1200 words. thanks.
