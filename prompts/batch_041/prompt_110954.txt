I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, which is a fancy way of saying I go to academic workshops and understand about 60% of what gets said. Last month I ended up at a hotel bar after a philosophy-of-science session, wedged between two people I'll call Mira and Dov, who spent a solid hour going after each other about what you're supposed to do when your evidence is fuzzy. I've been chewing on it ever since, and since they wouldn't settle it, maybe you can.

Heres the setup as best I can reconstruct it. Ordinary Bayesian updating is for when you learn something flat-out. But most of what you actually learn isn't flat-out — you read a study that leaves you 70% confident the drug works, or get a test result that leaves you 60% confident you've got the thing. There's a rule for that case; they kept calling it the Jeffrey rule, and also kept saying "probability kinematics," which I gather is the umbrella term. Instead of conditionalizing on a proposition, you conditionalize on the new probability you've assigned it, holding the relevant conditionals fixed.

The fight was over a wrinkle in it. Say you get two of these soft reports in a row, about two different things that cross-cut each other — one shifts your probabilities over {D, not-D}, the other shifts your probabilities over {R, not-R}. Feed them in one order and you land on one number; feed them in the other order and you land somewhere else. They did it with actual numbers on the back of a receipt and got different answers, and I got the impression this isn't some rare fluke case.

Mira said that's the whole ballgame: a rule that hands you different credences depending on the order you happened to receive two pieces of evidence can't be right, because order of receipt obviously isn't evidentially relevant. Her fix was to stop doing the fuzzy thing altogether — conditionalize on the certain fact that you received a report saying X, and treat that as your evidence, done. She also said this shows the rule as usually stated is under-described: you can't hand it a probability and expect a unique answer back.

Dov said she was junking a rule over a bad setup. If the two reports are really one episode, treat them as a single piece of evidence and do one update over the joint thing, and then there's exactly one answer. The two-step version only contradicts itself because you fed the rule two different partitions and expected it to commute, which is like expecting two different updating rules to agree. He also said her fix isn't a rival: updating on "I received a report saying X" is itself just a case of the Jeffrey rule, so she hasn't left the building. And he said if she thinks the rule needs an extra input, then say that — that's not an argument that it gives the wrong answers.

Mira came back with: you don't always have credences over the joint thing, because sometimes that refinement is something you've never once thought about, and anyway her complaint is that the rule as stated is incomplete, which is a real defect and not a technicality. She also said treating the report as certain evidence just relabels the problem, since the entire reason you needed a rule for uncertain evidence is that you aren't certain of the report either — you might have misread it, the printer might have smudged it.

Dov said fine, then the honest statement is "here's the extra thing you need," and most of the time you do have it, and Mira is smuggling in a much stronger claim — that any rule whose inputs are under-specified is false.

They asked me to pick a winner and I said I write about this stuff, I don't do it, which went over about as well as you'd expect. So: can you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move — for each move, right, wrong, or half-right, and why — then tell me who has the better of it overall and what both of them are missing? Dont send me off to books or papers, I need the argument itself, and please write it in American English.
