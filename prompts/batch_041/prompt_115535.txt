I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and I am putting together a poem to open a seminar session on Parfit's teletransporter and the question of what matters in survival. What I want is a sestina, and I want the form to be doing substantive work rather than serving as ornament.

Here is the constraint I care about. The sestina holds six end-words fixed while permuting their order from stanza to stanza, so that the same handful of elements keeps returning in ever-new relations and no arrangement is ever quite repeated. That is, I think, a fair formal image of the claim I want the poem to carry: that what persists across the teletransporter is a chain of psychological connectedness and continuity — memory, character, intention, the sense of being the same — rather than a further fact of identity that travels with it. I would like the six end-words to be chosen so that this recurrence does the work. At least one of them should be a term for the self — a first-person pronoun, or a word like this one, or same — so that the word returns in every stanza while what it picks out shifts, and a reader who knows the argument can feel the word stay put as its referent comes apart. I would rather the first person not remain stable across the poem.

The poem should move through the case as it is usually set up. The scanner reads you and destroys the original; a replica wakes on Mars who remembers your life from the inside and is, by every psychological measure, its continuation. Then the harder version: the original is not destroyed, and there are two of them, each with an equal claim and an equal store of your memories. The identity answer — that the survivor is me — can no longer do the work it was meant to, because there are two survivors and one cannot be two. If it helps, the poem may also glance at the version where the replacement is gradual, parts swapped out one at a time until nothing original remains, since that is where the demand for a sharp line is hardest to meet; but the sestina's permutation, not a catalogue of cases, should carry the thought. The three-line envoy is where I want the branching to land: both offshoots left with their claims intact and no principled way to say which is the continuation, and I do not want it resolved into consolation or uplift.

Accuracy matters to me more than beauty, though I would like both. Someone who works on personal identity should recognize the reductionist move here — that identity is not what matters — and should not be able to read the poem as an ordinary piece about uploading, or about a soul that steps from one body into the next. It should also not cheat by smuggling in a hidden further fact that settles the branching. If the poem has to give something up, I would rather it give up euphony than the argument.
