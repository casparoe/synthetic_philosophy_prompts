Next month I'm giving a talk to a weird mixed room -- half of them are the ML theory people from the CS department, half are philosophers of science who work on confirmation -- and what I need is not help with the talk, it's help with the question period. My thesis: the no-free-lunch results give Hume no independent support at all. They're theorems about averaging over a uniform measure on a finite space of functions, and that uniform measure already bakes in an inductive commitment (no problem is privileged, every input-output pattern is as likely as any other), so instead of formalising the problem of induction, NFL just re-instantiates it, with the commitment hidden inside the word uniform. The corollary I want to push: the theorems are silent on whether induction is trustworthy. All they say is that you can't get a learning guarantee with no assumptions, which nobody ever thought you could.

So write me the prep sheet. The ML crowd will think NFL is a bit of a cliche by now and will want to know which actual theorem I'm denying, and they'll know all the "free lunch under a non-uniform measure" moves -- compressible problem classes, priors that buy you something, that whole family. The philosophers will want the normative upshot and will be suspicious that "this theorem has a prior in its hypotheses" is a point about the theorem, not about the justification of induction. Those are the two moods in the room.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, no preamble and no closing paragraph. In each one: the objection as that person would actually put it, and say whether it's an ML person or a philosopher talking, then the two or three sentences I should say back, then one line on where that reply is softest. Order them by how much damage they do me if I fumble them, and make the fifth one the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
