I've got a grad student who did a CS undergrad before she came over to philosophy, and she will not let go of an analogy. Her line is that the Liar is basically the halting problem. Turing showed you cant write a program that decides, for every program, whether it halts; the proof is a diagonalization, and the upshot is a limitation on what any one machine can decide. Tarski's theorem, she says, is the same shape: diagonalize, and you get a limitation on what any language can say about its own truth. And here's the part she actually cares about -- nobody treats the halting problem as a crisis. We don't decide that halting is a broken or incoherent notion, we don't rebuild computer science around it, we just note the limit and get on with things. So, she says, the whole industry of agonizing over the Liar -- the hierarchy-building, the theories of truth that try to earn the T-schema back, the worry that truth itself is defective -- is people refusing to accept a perfectly ordinary limitation result. She wants to conclude that we should all relax and stop treating this as deep.

I want you to audit that properly, not just survey it. Give me the correspondence table first: sentence against program, language against machine, truth against halting, the diagonal sentence against the diagonal program, the missing truth predicate against the missing halting decider, and anything else the analogy needs to run. Then the places the mapping breaks, and be specific about which breaks are cosmetic and which ones actually threaten the conclusion shes drawing. Some things I'd want nailed down along the way. The halting problem is a limit on what can be decided, not on what is the case -- every program either halts or it doesn't, we just can't always find out -- so is that gap doing any real work here, or is it a superficial difference? Does the analogy quietly assume that a sentence like the Liar still has a determinate truth value that we simply can't reach, and is that something she gets for free or something she has to earn? What happens to the analogy when you patch the theory and the paradox comes back in strengthened form -- the sentence that says it isn't true at any level of your hierarchy, or isn't grounded -- does the computer science side have anything like that kind of comeback, and if it does, does that rescue her or sink her? And watch for the possibility that the analogy isnt doing independent work at all, that she has already assumed the Liar is benign and is just dressing it up in Turing.

Then land it: if the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, say so and say why; if it doesn't, tell me what the analogy was really carrying that the breaks take away, and whether there's a better comparison available -- something that would actually earn the relaxed attitude instead of presupposing it.

American English, please, and keep it argumentative.
