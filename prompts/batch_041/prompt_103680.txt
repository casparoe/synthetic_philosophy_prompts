I'm a postdoc working on decision theory and the foundations of game theory, and next month I'm giving a paper at a joint colloquium between my philosophy department and the economics department. The paper defends a deflationary reading of the absent-minded driver problem. Briefly, the setup I'm working with is the standard one: a driver is returning home along a road with two intersections; at each he may either turn off or continue, and because his memory is imperfect he cannot tell, when he is at an intersection, whether it is the first or the second. Turning at the first leaves him somewhere he does not want to be; turning at the second gets him home; continuing past both is a crash. The usual puzzle is that the ex-ante optimal plan -- continue at the first, turn at the second -- seems unavailable to him, since he cannot condition his choice on which intersection he is at, and yet it is also the only thing he wants to do. My thesis is that the paradox is an artifact of a modeling choice: once the driver is represented as choosing a plan or policy rather than as choosing an action at an intersection, the ex-ante solution is the whole story, and the insistence that his choice be "sequentially rational" at an information set he cannot distinguish is a mistake about what rationality requires, not a genuine constraint. To make this vivid I introduce a variant of my own in which the driver's absent-mindedness is only partial: he can tell, with probability q, which intersection he is at, and I argue that as q falls to zero the puzzle does not snap back into existence discontinuously but fades away, which I take to show that the standard puzzle trades on an artificial all-or-nothing idealization of memory.

The audience will be mostly game theorists and decision theorists who know the extensive-form machinery cold and who will attack the modeling rather than the prose, plus two or three philosophers of action, and one colleague who I am fairly sure thinks the whole thing is really a puzzle about personal identity and the continuity of memory. Prepare me for the question period: give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way this audience would put it rather than the way I would, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede up front, which I should refuse to give up, and which are really the same objection wearing different clothes, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
