I am a high school student planning an independent research project: about ten hours a week for a year, ending in a long essay, on what formal results in decision theory do and do not establish about disagreement between real people. I have worked through the two-agent, finite-state proof of Aumann's agreement theorem, so I understand why a common prior together with common knowledge of the two posteriors forces those posteriors to coincide, and why any actual disagreement has to be located somewhere in the assumptions: in the priors, in the information, in the common knowledge, or in the idealization of the agents as unlimited Bayesians.

What I cannot work out is where the live questions now are, and which of them deserve a year. Each candidate seems to lead to the others, and I would rather be told which are upstream than read my way through several surveys. The candidates I have collected, in no particular order:

- Whether the common prior is a substantive claim about how people's beliefs are actually formed, a requirement that rationality imposes on beliefs, or a modeling convenience with no normative force. If two agents may differ in priors for no reason beyond having different priors, the theorem appears to lose its bite; if they may not, I want to know what is supposed to forbid it.

- What happens when the common knowledge is only approximate. I have read that the conclusion is fragile in such cases, so that agreement can fail when mutual knowledge runs only finitely many levels deep or holds only with high probability, but I cannot tell whether these are exotic counterexamples or a description of the ordinary human situation.

- Unawareness: agents who do not share a space of possibilities at all, so that one of them cannot even entertain the hypothesis the other holds. Does the theorem's machinery presuppose a common state space, and how much of the result survives once that is dropped?

- Beliefs that are not probabilities. If belief is represented by something else, such as a ranking of possibilities, a set of probability functions, or purely qualitative comparisons, is there any analogue of the theorem, or is the result an artifact of the probabilistic setting?

- Agents who cannot compute their own conclusions in advance. A real reasoner does not know what it will conclude after further thought. Can the theorem be stated for such agents at all, and is there a version of the agreement result that applies to them?

- What the theorem is supposed to do outside the formalism. It is often cited in support of a claim that rational agents with common priors should not trade on informational grounds alone, which looks false of actual markets; it is also cited in the philosophical debate about how you should respond when someone you regard as an equal disagrees with you; and I am curious whether it constrains ensembles of language models that share training data and read one another's outputs.

I want an agenda, not a survey. Please rank these, add anything I have missed, and defend the ranking. Be explicit about the criteria: which questions are upstream, in the sense that settling them changes what the others even mean; which are tractable for someone with my background (no fieldwork and no graduate training, comfortable with proofs, calculus, and elementary probability, but not measure theory or graduate economics); and which would, if settled, change something outside the formalism, including whether the results should alter how an ordinary person or a scientist responds to disagreement. Where the criteria pull apart, tell me which you would sacrifice and why. Also tell me which of my six is not genuinely open, or is really two questions wearing one label, and what evidence or argument over the next year would move an item up or down the list. If only two or three of these deserve the year, say so and drop the rest. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the ordering and the reasons for it.
