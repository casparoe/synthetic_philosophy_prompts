Write a poem in blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter — in three numbered parts, and let the architecture of it carry the argument.

The speaker is the head of a company, answering a subordinate who has come to him with a proposal for a new programme that will substantially raise profits. He knows, and says, that the programme will also do something to the river that runs past one of the works. In part one, what it does to the river is damage; in part two, it is a benefit. In both parts the money is the point, the effect on the river is foreseen, priced, and no part of his reason, and he says, in identical words, that he does not care about the river either way. Parts one and two are to be as near to word-for-word identical as you can make them: sixteen lines each, in four quatrains, with the same syntax, the same line breaks and the same metre throughout, and with the differences confined to the lines about the river. A reader laying the two parts side by side should be able to count the altered words on one hand, and none of the changes should be an abstract label such as harm or help; the alteration must be carried by what the river does and what it looks like. Of the four quatrains, the one in which he states his indifference must be identical in both parts, and must be phrased so as not to name what happens to the river; taken out of context, it should be impossible to tell which part it came from.

Part three then reprints that quatrain, unaltered and last, with three or four new lines before it, and stops there. In those new lines the speaker should notice what has just happened to the reader: the same words about the river, and the same indifference, will have been heard as a confession in part one and as a boast in part two. He may run through two or three rival explanations without choosing between them — that we say he meant it because we want him blamed, the word doing moral work rather than psychological description; that we count an effect as meant when it breaches a norm we hold, so that the asymmetry belongs to the concept and not to the man; that there is no asymmetry at all, only a difference in where our attention falls. He should also wonder, and leave unanswered, whether any of it would have gone differently had he been no good at his job, so that the river's fate was never in his hands either way. He must not settle on an explanation, and the poem must not end on a settlement; it should end on the reprinted quatrain and the reader's discomfort.

Two prohibitions. The poem must never use the words intentional, intentionally, intention, deliberately or on purpose; the whole point is that the word whose application is in dispute never appears, so that the reader performs the attribution without being told to. And it must stay out of the seminar room: no philosophers' or psychologists' names, no jargon, no experiments, questionnaires, vignettes, participants or percentages, no allusion to any literature, and no naming of the phenomenon itself. Keep the scene plain and physical — a boardroom, a lift, a memo, a spreadsheet, a car park. A reader who knows the work this is built on should be able to say which part is which condition and why the closing quatrain is the sting; a reader who does not should still get a poem that stands up, and leaves them a little cold.
