I'm a 4th-year PhD student in moral psychology, and my dissertation is on guilt vs. shame — specifically whether a culture can deliberately tilt the balance from one to the other (my supervisor thinks yes, I'm not so sure). Someone on my committee told me Spinoza is the most interesting historical foil for the whole "which is the better moral emotion" question, so I've been reading Part IV of the Ethics, and I've hit a wall in the preface. Here's the bit I'm stuck on (the old translation I have from Project Gutenberg):

As for the terms good and bad, they indicate no positive quality in things regarded in themselves, but are merely modes of thinking, or notions which we form from the comparison of things one with another. Thus one and the same thing can be at the same time good, bad, and indifferent. For instance, music is good for him that is melancholy, bad for him that mourns; for him that is deaf, it is neither good nor bad. Nevertheless, though this be so, the terms should still be retained.

The thing I can't square: if good and bad are just comparative notions we form, then it looks like the question "is guilt a better emotion than shame" has no real answer — the emotions are just more or less pleasant/powerful affects for particular people, and calling one "better" is like calling music bad for the mourner. But then he goes on for the rest of Part IV ranking the affects anyway, and as far as I can tell he ends up saying repentance and humility aren't virtues, and shame is a passive affect that depends on what other people think of us. So either the ranking is doing real work and the preface is a weaker claim than it sounds, or the ranking is just therapeutic advice about which affects increase our power and there's no moral verdict anywhere. I keep going back and fourth on which.

What I want from you: help me understand what "modes of thinking" is actually doing in that passage, and then commit to a reading — does Spinoza's position here make the guilt-vs-shame comparison collapse, or is there a coherent Spinozist answer to which is the better emotion (and if there is, which one does he effectively come down on)? I don't want a menu of three readings with pros and cons; I want you to pick the one you think is right and tell me what it implies for my chapter. Also, if this turns on something in Part III about the definition of the affects, feel free to point me there — but please don't just tell me to go read the secondary literature, I'm asking you to stake a position. And flag any place where you're genuinely unsure, I'd rather have a marked uncertainty than false confidence.
