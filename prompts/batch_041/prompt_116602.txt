I've been trying to write a short essay on the Letter Spirit question — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — and I'd like some proper comments on the draft below. The idea occured to me while I was messing about with a font editor. Here's the draft, roughly 500 words:

The question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' is usually answered by looking for a shared shape: a bowl and a stem, a loop and a tail, some skeleton that all 'a's have in common. I think that's a dead end. There is no common stroke, no common topology, and no common proportion across the 'a's that actually get read as 'a's. What there is, is a role. An 'a' is whatever occupies a certain slot in a system of graphic distinctions that a community uses to encode language. The slot is defined relationally: it has to be distinguishable from the other letters, producible by the available hand or tool, and recognisable across the transformations the writing system tolerates — slant, weight, size, contrast, stroke order, and a fair amount of distortion. So the essence is a set of constraints, not a picture.

Style, on this view, is not decoration added on top of the essence. It is a second-order pattern over the tolerances. If the essence fixes a region of shape-space, style is a systematic way of moving inside that region: a deformation operator, or a bias in how the constraints get resolved. Two typefaces can both contain all the letters and still differ in style because their deformation operators differ. That explains why style can be recognisable from a single letter, and why a letter can be unmistakably an 'a' and unmistakably in a given style at the same time.

I also want to say something about counterfactual robustness. An 'a' is an 'a' if it would still be read as an 'a' under the kinds of variation the system routinely throws at it. That makes letter identity a bit like a fixed point or an attractor in a dynamical system of reading and writing. The boundaries are vague, but vague boundaries are not no boundaries. A blob that no one would read as an 'a' fails; a weird 'a' that a community accepts passes. So the essence is both normative and empirical: it is what the practice treats as the same, and what the practice treats as the same is constrained by perceptual and motor facts.

The upshot is that the question has no answer in terms of a shared visible feature. It has an answer in terms of a shared role in a system of differences plus a tolerance space. Style is the systematic residue of how a particular hand or font resolves that role. This also suggests that letter identity is not prior to style; the two are co-constituted. You cannot fully specify the essence without saying which variations are stylistic and which are errors, and that line is drawn by the practice.
