I'm a features writer on the science-and-ideas beat at a mid-sized magazine, and for most of this year I've been reporting on consent — hospital consent forms, plea bargains, a long piece on paid surrogacy, one of those apps that's supposed to record sexual consent, and a thing about terms of service that ate my summer. The annoying side effect is that I've come out the other end with an actual position, and a philosopher I interviewed for the last piece told me, politely, that I can't hold all of it at once. I've been chewing on that for two weeks and I genuinely cant find the crack, so I'd like you to audit the whole package. Here's roughly how I'd put it out loud:

(1) For consent to count, the person has to be informed — if you sit on a fact that would have changed their mind, their yes doesn't count, and that's true even when you never technically lied.
(2) It also has to be voluntary, and I don't mean just no gun to the head. If somebody's back is against the wall because of how the world has treated them and someone else comes along and takes advantage, I don't think that's real consent either. People keep telling me to draw a seperate line between threats and offers — an offer leaves you better off than you were, a threat leaves you worse — and I've never bought it, because the alternative you're choosing against is part of the choice.
(3) Capacity is a threshold and not a dial, and the threshold should sit low: adults get the presumption unless something is obviously wrong, and I'd rather let people make a bad call than let a doctor or a judge make it for them.
And then the calls I keep coming back to:
(4) A doctor doesn't mention a one-in-ten-thousand risk of death from a procedure → the consent is invalid. That was hers to weigh.
(5) A defendant facing twenty years if he goes to trial takes a two-year plea deal → valid. He chose it, and honestly I'd have taken it too.
(6) Someone sleeps with a partner partly because the partner will sulk for a week otherwise → valid. That's just what being in a relationship is.
(7) A used-car dealer doesn't mention a known engine fault → the sale stands. Everyone knows used cars are a gamble.
(8) Selling a kidney for cash should stay illegal even when the seller seems perfectly happy about it.

My own guess is that the problem is between (3) and how I actually use age cutoffs — I think a sixteen-year-old can consent to her own medical treatment but not to sex, and a friend says that's incoherent on my own terms — but she also muttered something about (2) and (5) right as my train came, so I don't know. What I want is an audit: which of these actually pull against each other and how hard, which ones only look like they conflict, and if something has to go, what's the cheapest thing to give up and what would giving it up cost me?

Please do it in this order, and don't worry about citations, I just want the argument: 'What you think I'm committed to' (100 words max, just so I know you've got the target right); 'The real tensions' (ranked worst to least bad, each one naming which items collide, how bad it is, and exactly what would have to give — call it 500-600 words); 'Tensions that dissolve' (the pairs that look bad but aren't, and why — 200-300 words); and 'The cheapest fix' (the single revision that buys the most consistency, plus the price tag — about 200 words). Casual is fine, I'm not grading you.
