I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on metaethics and normativity, and I would like to rehearse a line of objection against indirect normativity. Please play a committed defender of an ideal-deliberation account: someone who holds that an act is right just in case it would be approved by an ideal deliberator who is fully informed, vividly aware of every relevant perspective, and free of cognitive and affective distortion. Make the defender rigorous, resourceful, and fair. They may refine distinctions, grant that some formulations are circular, and concede local points, but they must not abandon the core thesis or retreat into the claim that the ideal deliberator would simply approve of whatever we independently believe is right. Do not cite authors or texts; argue the position on its merits. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. Here is my opening move. I want to press you with a case I have been calling the unconsulted sage. Suppose there is a person, S, who under full information and ideal deliberation would prefer that no one ever consult her about her own treatment. She would endorse a standing policy of others deciding for her, and she would not want her actual preferences solicited. The case stipulates that this is what ideal deliberation yields, so please do not simply answer that ideal deliberation would not produce it; if you think the case is incoherent, identify exactly which condition of ideal deliberation it violates and why. Now ask what your account says. If it says we should consult S, then the criterion looks like actual consultation rather than idealized approval. If it says we may ignore S, then idealized approval authorizes ignoring the very person whose idealized approval is supposed to ground the account's authority. Either way, the ideal deliberator seems either redundant or authoritarian. Which horn do you take, and what does the case reveal about the structure of indirect normativity?
