I am leading a ninety-minute session for a mixed reading group of graduate students in philosophy and economics, none of whom have worked on the Pasadena paradox. They are comfortable with expected utility, the St. Petersburg game, and basic real analysis; several have just been convinced that an infinite expected value is possible and now assume that an undefined expected value is simply a more exotic infinity. The topic is the Pasadena paradox: a coin-toss game whose payoffs alternate in sign and grow so that the positive and negative parts of the expectation each diverge, leaving the expected value undefined even though the probabilities are perfectly well defined, and whose payoff series is conditionally convergent, so rearranging the terms can make it sum to any real number or diverge. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation. What has to come first for the distinction between infinite and undefined expectation to feel like a real distinction rather than a technicality? What single carrying example should anchor the session, and what must it include and exclude so that the conditional convergence is visible without the arithmetic swamping the conceptual point? I am tempted to use a physical payout machine with a dial that lets us reorder the payments, but I want to know whether that helps or distracts. Which misconceptions should I expect—for example, that undefined expectation means the game has no value, that any series can be rearranged arbitrarily, that a well-defined probability distribution guarantees a well-defined expectation, or that decision theory can simply stipulate a value—and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? What check for understanding at the end would show that they can distinguish a game with an infinite expectation from one with no expectation at all? And what should I deliberately leave out at this level, and say that I am leaving out?

The group will have read nothing in advance, and I do not want the plan to depend on any particular paper, author, or notation beyond the coin-toss setup and the idea of conditional convergence. Please give me the dependency structure: which idea must be in place before which, why the order cannot be scrambled, and where the Pasadena game itself should enter. I also want you to flag the point where the session is most likely to fail—where the group will mistake the rearrangement argument for a parlor trick or where the distinction between undefined and infinite will collapse back into a slogan. If it is useful, connect the ending to the question of whether a rational agent can have a well-defined preference over a game with undefined expectation, and what such a preference would be tracking.

Finally, be explicit about your confidence in this plan and what would change your mind. Which argument or consideration about the paradox, or which observation from the room, would lead you to revise the sequence or the carrying example? Where is your reasoning most likely to fail? I would rather have a plan that marks its own weak joints than one that presents the session as settled.
