Question: In "Quining Qualia," Dennett uses the case of the coffee tasters Chase and Sanborn to argue that the traditional notion of qualia is an incoherent posit. Explain the case and explain how it is supposed to support that conclusion. In your answer, state the four features commonly ascribed to qualia and say what Dennett thinks is wrong with positing something that has those features.

Student's answer: Dennett's target is the traditional notion of qualia — the intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly apprehensible properties of experience. He lists four features: ineffability (qualia cannot be communicated, only undergone), intrinsicality (qualia are non-relational and non-physical — they do not depend on anything outside themselves), privacy (no one can compare their qualia with anyone else's), and direct apprehensibility (to have a quale is to know it completely). Chase and Sanborn are two coffee tasters whose behavior is identical — they order the same coffees, describe them in the same terms, and would pay the same prices — but who give different introspective reports about a coffee they both used to enjoy. One says the coffee itself has changed; the other says his palate has changed. Because qualia are private, there is no way to check which of them is right, and because they are ineffable, no description could settle it either. Dennett concludes that since qualia cannot be verified by any intersubjective means, they are meaningless by verificationist standards, and the concept should be abandoned. On his view there are really no experiences at all, only dispositions to behave and reports to make. The case is meant to show that we should explain taste reports by their causes — the coffee, the palate — rather than by positing inner private objects.
