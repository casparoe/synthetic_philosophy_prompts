In a graduate seminar I am taking on evolutionary psychology, the same defensive move keeps surfacing, and I have never seen it audited properly. When someone presses the familiar charge — that adaptationist hypotheses about the mind are unfalsifiable storytelling, since for any trait one can invent a selection story and for its opposite another — the reply is that this is simply what a historical science looks like. Evolutionary psychology, we are told, is like geology or paleontology or forensic reconstruction: the past has left traces, we cannot rerun it, and yet we confidently infer ancient events from present-day evidence, and a detective does not need to have witnessed the crime in order to solve it. On this view the objection proves too much, and the conclusion it is meant to license — that evolutionary psychology is a legitimate empirical science whose hypotheses can genuinely be confirmed and disconfirmed — survives untouched. I want that analogy taken apart rather than waved through.

Lay out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that helps: what is supposed to play the role of the fossil or the trace, what plays the role of the dating method or the independent physical law that constrains reconstruction, what plays the role of the uniformitarian assumption that the past resembled the present, and what plays the role of the crime scene and the culprit. Note that the geological analogy and the detective analogy are not the same analogy and pull in different directions — one supplies impersonal lawful regularities, the other an agent with intentions — and say which of them is actually carrying the argument. Then give me the places where the mapping breaks: the status and richness of the record, the availability of independent lines of evidence that overdetermine a geological inference, the fact that present-day behaviour and self-report are a far more contaminated trace than strata or bones, the underdetermination of reverse-engineering between adaptation, byproduct, drift, and noise, and whatever else you think matters.

Finally, separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the intended use from the ones that are merely colourful, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once the ones that matter are on the table: is the analogy doing real work, or is it carrying the conclusion the whole time? If there is a better analogy for what evolutionary psychologists actually do when they are doing it well, say what it is and why it fits better; if the honest answer is that no analogy can rescue the inference, say that instead, and say what the inference would have to look like to be rescued.
