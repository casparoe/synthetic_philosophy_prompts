I've been reading up on this philosopher David Lewis who wrote a book about conventions, and I think I get the basic idea, but the more I poke at it the less sure I am, so I want to test it on something real. As I understand him, a convention is just a regularity everyone follows, where everyone expects everyone else to follow it, and everyone would rather follow it as long as the others do, and all of that is common knowledge. His example is which side of the road we drive on: nothing in the world makes left better than right, it only has to be the same for all of us, and once we're all on the same side nobody has a reason to move, so it holds itself up.

What's bugging me is Sweden in 1967. They drove on the left like Britain does, and then one Sunday morning in September the whole country switched to the right. The odd part is they didn't want to. There was a referendum in the fifties and something like eighty per cent said keep it as it is, and then parliament went ahead anyway a few years later, with a massive campaign, new lines painted on the roads, all the signs swapped, the works. More than nine in ten Swedish cars already had the steering wheel on the left anyway, so left-hand driving meant you had to poke your nose into oncoming traffic to overtake. Apparently even the buses were wrong, because their doors were on the wrong side, and the trams in central Stockholm just got scrapped rather than converted, and thousands of buses got new doors cut into them, and there was some business about headlamps being cheap round ones then and about to get expensive, which is partly why they did it when they did. It worked. Everyone switched, and it stuck.

So here's my problem. If a convention is held up by everyone expecting everyone else to do it, it should be nearly impossible to shift, because the expectations are propping up the old one. But Sweden shifted in a single morning, and not because people changed their minds, since the referendum says they didn't. Either the thing was never a convention in Lewis's sense, or a convention can be flipped by one authority telling everybody at once, which seems to make the expectation part do far less work than Lewis wants it to. And if expectations alone were holding left-hand driving in place, why couldn't expectations alone have moved it, without the law? Also it wasn't really arbitrary in the way the driving example is supposed to be, because the neighbours were all on the right and the cars were built the wrong way round for the left, so there was a real argument that one side was better.

What I want is a proper going-over of this one case. Was left-hand driving in Sweden a convention by Lewis's conditions, taken one at a time, and where exactly does it fail to fit? What does the switch actually show about how a convention gets unmade, was the law doing the coordinating or just making the new expectation common knowledge or both, and what does the case show about the theory itself, whether it comes out confirmed or shows a gap? The theory seems to have almost nothing to say about how a convention travels from one equilibrium to another, and nothing at all about conventions getting welded to physical stuff like bus doors and tram rails, which is a cost that has nothing to do with anybody's expectations.

I'd also like to know where I'm muddling convention with a rule we think we ought to follow, because driving on the left felt like a law as much as a habit, and if it's only a habit it's strange that a policeman could fine you for it. And test it for me: if they repealed the law tomorrow, would everyone just keep driving on the right? If yes, then it has turned into a real convention and the law was scaffolding. If no, then it was a rule all along and Lewis is describing something thinner than what we usually mean. And say what the case can't settle, because maybe Sweden is just a lucky case where a strong state could pull off a one-day switch, and the theory might do better or worse with conventions that settle with nobody in charge, the way the side you stand on an escalator or the way we queue seems to have sorted itself out.

Write it out as plain prose please, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just a letter, that's how I read best.
