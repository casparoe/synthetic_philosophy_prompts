Hey, so I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory and my dissertation is on Kant's international thought — basically the move out of the state of nature among states and what he thinks the intermediate steps can legitimately look like — and I've been interning two days a week at a legislative office that handles foreign affairs to pay rent. My supervisor there just handed me a problem I can't dodge. Every week the committee gets a stack of proposals from members and outside groups — sell arms to X, sanction Y, recognize Z's government, fund a training program for a foreign army, sign a basing agreement, quietly pass intelligence to a party we're not officially backing — and right now the junior staffers screen them on vibes plus whatever the news cycle says that morning. She wants a one-page procedure that a 23-year-old with a poli-sci BA and zero philosophy can run in fifteen minutes on a single proposal, and she wants it grounded in Kant's perpetual peace rather than in my personal opinions, which are apparently not a rubric. She's the one who signs off, so the procedure has to make its own limits legible to a non-specialist.

So can you actually turn Kant's peace writing into a decision procedure? I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where the answer is just no and we stop, and the point where it stops being a factual question and has to go up to her as a judgement call. A few things I'd like kept, because they're exactly the bits I'd flatten if I did this myself: the preliminary articles are meant to be binding right away even though the goal they serve is a long way off, so they can't be treated as aspirations; the difference between a league of free states and a world state; and his publicity test from the appendix, which I think does real work as a screening device — a proposal that only works if we don't admit what we're doing should fail at that step, and I want that step to bite rather than be a formality everyone nods through. There's also that strange secret article at the end about letting the philosophers have their say on whether peace is possible, which I read as him saying a state should keep a licensed nuisance around on purpose. I'd like the procedure to have somewhere for that, even if it sounds absurd in a legislative office. And the thing I'm least sure about, so please don't dodge it: does it change anything that the body running this procedure is a legislature rather than a sovereign? Kant was writing for republics and princes, and I keep worrying the whole thing presupposes a unity of will that a committee simply doesn't have.

Then run it on three proposals so I can see whether it's real. (1) A request to sell a batch of surplus armored vehicles to a government that's an ally, is fighting an insurgency, and is not especially democratic. (2) A proposal to fund a two-year civil society program in a country we have no treaty with, mostly money to local NGOs. (3) Something actually coming up here next month: a quiet arrangement to share satellite imagery with a party to a conflict while we publicly call for a ceasefire. I'd like the third to come out badly for the office, since everyone wants to say yes to it and I'll need to be able to defend the no. Flag which steps are judgement calls dressed up as rules, what the procedure can't settle, where a clever staffer could game it, and — because she'll ask — what the whole thing does with a proposal that's obviously good policy but fails one step, and how it seperates that from a proposal that just needs more information before anyone can say anything.
