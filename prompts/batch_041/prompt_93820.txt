I have no philosophical training. I read on my own, mostly in the evenings, and for the better part of a year I have been stuck on one piece of Kierkegaard's writing — his long review of somebody else's novel, in which he more or less abandons the novel and turns on his own time instead. What he says there, in my own rough paraphrase, is that the press had called into existence something that had never existed before: the public. Not a crowd, not a people, not a gathering in a square, but an abstraction — a nobody, something that is nowhere and does nothing, and yet is everywhere and is appealed to as though it decided everything. And because it has no body and no name, it can never be held to anything; whatever it does, no one did it. He thought this thing was doing something worse than corrupting taste. He thought it was leveling everyone: grinding down the distinctions that passionate commitment makes between one person and another, replacing decision with reflection and talkativeness and envy, until nobody can act anymore, only discuss. The one thing he believed could stand against it was the single individual — one person, willing to be only one, willing to answer for himself.

I find the diagnosis so exact about the present that I keep forgetting it was not written about us, which is why I want a story and not an argument. But there is a detail that keeps nagging at me. He wrote much of his own work under invented names, and yet he attacked exactly this sort of nameless chatter. As I understand it, he insisted that the invented names were characters rather than hiding places, and that he himself was answerable for the whole authorship in the end. I cannot quite see the difference he is drawing, and I suspect I will only see it if I watch it happen to somebody.

So: write me a short story, somewhere between 1,500 and 2,000 words. The setting I have in mind, which you may make more specific but should not make more obvious, is a small town where the last local newspaper has just folded and an anonymous community forum has taken its place — a forum whose convention is that every post carries the same shared signature, so that no one posts as themselves and no one is ever exactly the one speaking. Set it in the present, in a place that feels lived in, with ordinary work and weather in it; I do not want an allegory with nameless figures.

What I need from the story:

- There should be no villain. The damage the town suffers should be real and should have no author. By the end I should be unable to say who did it, and so should everyone in the town, and that impossibility should be the most frightening thing in the story rather than a loose end you forgot to tie.
- Whatever levels the town should begin as something reasonable, or even good — something a fair-minded person would have welcomed. I do not want the new thing to be sinister from the first paragraph.
- At least one character should be the kind of person who, in another age, would have acted, and who now deliberates and discusses and waits. I want to feel the exact moment in which the action does not happen.
- One character should attempt the stand of the single individual: speaking in their own name, taking responsibility, willing to be only one. It should cost them something, it should not obviously work, and the story should not resolve into a lesson or a consolation.
- Somewhere in the story, something true and necessary should be said by someone who will not put their name to it. I want to feel both why that anonymity was the only way it could have been said and why, in the end, it changed nothing. This is the knot I care about most — the one about his invented names.
- Nobody should explain any of this. No character should use words like leveling, or reflection, or the public in his sense; nobody should mention Kierkegaard; nobody should deliver a speech about the age. The ideas should be doing the work of the plot, not commenting on it.
- Do not make the forum a mob and do not make it wicked. As I read him, the horror of the thing is that it is diffuse — no one is in it, and everyone is.
- Please do not end with the town repaired or the individual vindicated. I would rather finish with the problem still open and the reader slightly implicated in it.
