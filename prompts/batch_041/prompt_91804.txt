Bit of a phil-of-mind question. You know how in the clinic they'll take a patient who looks completely unresponsive — no eye contact, not following commands — and stick them in an fMRI and ask them to imagine playing tennis, and if the right area lights up they'll say ok, so there's someone conscious in there after all? But the same clinicians will happily tell you a dog or a sleeping baby is conscious, no scanner needed, even though you obviously can't ask a dog to imagine tennis. So what does that practice actually presuppose about consciousness? Does making report/command-following the gold standard mean it's quietly built on some higher-order or global-workspace picture, where a mental state only counts as conscious if it's the kind of thing that can be reported or globally broadcast? Or is the report just defeasible evidence, and the practice is really neutral between theories? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, seperate what's genuinely presupposed from what's just a convienient test, and tell me whether the practice is even consistent with how the same people treat animals and infants. And what breaks if you take seriously that consciousness might come in degrees?
