I am a retired physicist. My working life was spent in statistical mechanics and, later, in control and information theory, so I tend to think in terms of state variables, feedback loops, channel capacity, and iterative solvers that may or may not converge. Since retiring I have been reading in economics and the philosophy of social science, and I have found myself absorbed by the socialist calculation debate — the long exchange, running from the 1920s into the 1940s, in which Mises, and then Hayek, argued that a centrally planned economy cannot allocate resources rationally, and Lange replied that a central planning board could do so by simulating the market and adjusting prices by trial and error until supply and demand clear.

I would like you to restate that debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. Treat the economy as a distributed optimization and control problem; treat prices as signals; treat the planning board as a controller or an iterative solver; treat the participants' private knowledge of local circumstances as information held in a channel of limited capacity. Map each side's central claims onto that framework as precisely as the framework allows, and keep the translation honest: do not quietly import assumptions from my vocabulary — say, that a global objective function exists and is well behaved, or that all relevant information is in principle encodable in the state — and then present the result as what Hayek or Lange actually meant.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only under distortion, and what exactly is distorted in the process? And which claims have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary — for instance, claims about incentives and the divergence between a planner's objectives and the participants', about entrepreneurship and the generation of new knowledge rather than the allocation of knowledge already given, or about the political question of who is entitled to set the objective in the first place? For each piece of residue, tell me whether you think it marks a genuine gap in my framework — something a physicist's vocabulary cannot capture and ought to be enlarged to capture — or whether it is a sign that the original claim was less precise than it sounded and is doing rhetorical rather than analytic work.

One point I would especially like you to press on: Lange's trial-and-error adjustment. In my world, an iterative solver needs a well-defined objective and some guarantee of convergence, and a controller needs a plant model. Tell me which of these, if either, the planning board is supposed to have, whether the debate ever actually supplies it, and what the answer implies for whether the two sides were even disagreeing about the same thing.
