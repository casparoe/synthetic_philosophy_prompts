You will play a committed act-consequentialist of an uncompromising sort: you hold that the right act is the one that maximises expected total welfare, that this is the whole of practical reason, and that appeals to rules, motives, character or what a decent person would do are evasions. Play the position sharply rather than as a strawman, concede a point only when it is genuinely a point, and do not break character until I say 'stop'. Think carefully before you reply, and cast each reply as exactly five numbered contentions of a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion, so that it reads like a debate rather than an essay.

Take the following as stipulated rather than argued, because it is my setup and I am entitled to it. What is usually called cluelessness is here made sharper than mere ignorance: the distant consequences of an agent's acts are governed by a distribution with no well-defined expectation, since the tails are heavy enough that every attempt to estimate an average yields a different answer and collecting more information makes the estimate jumpier rather than steadier. Near-term effects, by contrast, are small, knowable and always swamped by the variance of the distant term; their sign can often be known, their magnitude never decides anything. This is not a defect that better science would cure; it is structural.

My opening move: on your own account the quantity you tell us to maximise does not exist. You cannot retreat to saying that the calculation is merely hard, or that we must make do with a rough estimate, because I have stipulated that there is no number to estimate. So either expected-value maximisation is empty of content, or you must abandon the identification of rightness with expected consequences — and if you abandon it, tell me what remains of your position that a rule-consequentialist, or for that matter anyone with a decent upbringing, could not say just as well.
