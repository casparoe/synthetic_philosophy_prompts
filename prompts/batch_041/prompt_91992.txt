I write a weekly column about the brain for a general-readership science mag -- 900 words, no footnotes, my editor will cut anything that smells like jargon -- and I keep getting the same complaint from a friend who actually finished a PhD in philosophy of mind: that half my sentences smuggle in a tiny person sitting in a dark room watching a screen. She's usually right. I write stuff like "your brain makes the decision about half a second before you do," or "attention is a spotlight that selects what enters conscious awareness," or "the visual system hands the image off to the part of the brain that actually sees it," and she says every one of those quietly assumes there's one place, one moment, and one witness where it all comes together. I've read enough Dennett to know what she means -- the multiple drafts thing, the fight against the Cartesian theater, fame in the brain instead of a stage -- but I can't reread it every week and I need something I can actually run on a draft at 4pm on a Thursday.

So: turn the multiple drafts / anti-theater picture into an editing procedure I can follow. I want the tests to run on a sentence, in order, with what counts as failing each one, plus a rewrite that passes. I especially want you to pull the thing apart into seperate axes, because I suspect my worst sentences fail different ones: one is about there being a single place where it happens, one is about a single time (this is where I always get into trouble with timing studies), and one is about there being a single someone who witnesses it. Some of my sentences probably fail two of the three and some only one, and I want to know which failure is the serious one and which I can shrug off.

Don't make it a purge. Some theater-flavored talk is innocent and I'm not gutting every vivid sentence I write -- I'd rather have a test that tells me when "the brain does X" is fine and when it's a homunculus in a lab coat. So include the cases where the test is wrong, where the metaphor is doing honest work, and where the only problem is that I've made the reader themselves the little person in the room. And be straight with me about the cases the procedure can't decide at all, where it's just my call.

Then run it on those three sentences I gave you, plus this one, which I think is actually fine and I want you to confirm or break: "the retina sends signals to the visual cortex, which starts pulling out edges and motion." And I want at least one case where the procedure tells me to kill a line I'm attached to -- if every test comes back passing I'll assume I wrote it wrong.

Last wrinkle: a lot of my worst offenders are quotes from the researchers themselves, and I can't rewrite them. So tell me how to handle those -- which failures to fix silently in my own prose, which to leave alone because they're the scientist's commitment and not mine, and which to flag for the reader as contested rather than as settled fact.

And tell me what this procedure can't do. I don't want to finish a clean draft and think I've solved consciousness, or that I've settled whether there's a hard problem, or that I've shown the multiple drafts model is true -- I want to know exactly where the editing stops and the actual philosophy starts, so I don't overclaim in the last paragraph the way I always do.
