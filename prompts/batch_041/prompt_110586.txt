I write about science and ideas for a living, which mostly means my friends now ask me to referee arguments I'm not qualified to referee. Here's the current one.

My sister is applying for a job at a small research outfit. After the interviews, they asked her to do a "trial project" — about a week of real work, unpaid, and they say they won't use anything she produces. Her boyfriend thinks this is smart. His line: anybody can claim to be detail-oriented and hardworking on a resume, and anyone can fake enthusiasm in an interview, but only someone who will actually burn a week of her life for nothing has proven she wants it. He says if they paid everyone for the trial, or cut it down to an hour, the whole point would evaporate. My sister says the opposite: she'd have done that week for literally any job she'd applied to, so all it proves is that she's desperate and has savings. Also, one of the other finalists is a kid with family money who took a month off to travel; another has two kids and a day job. If the week is the test, she says, the test measures the wrong thing.

Then she went and did the project anyway, and got the job, so now she's mad at herself and also thinks the process was rigged in her favor.

I think they're both half right and I can't articulate why. His point seems to be that the waste is what makes the claim believable — that if the trial were easy, or fun, or paid, it wouldn't tell them anything. But then her point kicks in: what it tells them depends on who can afford the waste, and it might be telling them she's desperate rather than that she's good.

Where I get stuck is the counterfactuals. If the company paid for the trial, would they learn less? If they made the project genuinely interesting, would they learn less? If it took a month instead of a week, would they learn more, or just end up fishing in a different and worse pond? And is there any version of this that gets them the information without the pointless week, or is the pointless week itself the information? I'd also like to know whether it matters that they don't use the output, and what changes if they do.

And how do I think about the ethics here seperately from the mechanics? If the filter genuinely works, does that make it fair? Would your answer change if the place were a nonprofit my sister admired rather than some random lab, or if the trial were the only way a broke candidate could get in the door?

I'm definately out of my depth. Could you walk me through the general shape of this — why a useless cost can be informative at all, when that breaks down, and what a smarter version of this process would look like? A verdict on my sister's case wouldn't hurt either.
