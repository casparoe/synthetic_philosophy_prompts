I keep getting into fights about statues and street names and I keep getting shut down by the same sentence: "you can't judge the past by the standards of the present." Last time it was a neighborhood meeting about a statue of a local guy who was a genuine philanthropist (funded the library, the hospital) and also, it turns out, an enthusiastic imperialist who wrote some pretty bloodthirsty stuff. Someone said the line, everyone nodded like it settled it, and we moved on to whether to keep the plaque. I don't think it settles anything but I can't say why, and I want to be ready for next time.

Can you lay out all the different precise things that one sentence could actually be claiming? I want the whole menu, from the boringly true to the surprisingly radical, including the readings people don't realize they're signing up for. For each one I want to know: is it plausible, and does it actually get the person to "so we should leave the statue up"? My suspicion is that a bunch of the readings are true but don't bear on the question at all, and the couple that would do the real work are ones nobody would say out loud.

Some things I'd like you to sort out along the way, since I think they're hiding in there: is it about judging the person vs. judging the act vs. judging what we choose to honor now? Is "can't" meant as "logically can't," "would be unfair," or just "shouldn't"? Whose standards, and standards that existed when — the guy's own era, or some notion of what a reasonable person then could have known? And does the slogan mean the past had different moral standards, or that there were no standards, or just that we shouldn't be anachronistic when interpreting texts?

Also my colleague tacked on that we don't blame 18th-century doctors for bloodletting, which I'm pretty sure is doing something sneaky. If you can fold that in, great.

Give it to me as a list I can bring to the next meeting, not an essay.
