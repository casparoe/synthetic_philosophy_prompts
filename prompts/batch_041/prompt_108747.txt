Okay so in three weeks I'm presenting at our department's work-in-progress seminar and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not encouragement. I've been in enough of these to know the paper is fine and the question period is where it dies.

The paper, quick version: Sidgwick's lifelong involvement with psychical research (he was one of the founders of the Society for Psychical Research in the early 1880s and its first president, sat through God knows how many seances, worked alongside Myers and Gurney, and was still at it when he died in 1900) is usually treated as the embarrassing hobby of a great Victorian utilitarian, or at best a touching eccentricity. I want to argue it's actually the empirical wing of his real project. The Methods of Ethics ends by conceding he can't reconcile rational egoism with universal benevolence, the dualism of practical reason, and that patching it up would need certain postulates, something like a moral order to the universe and personal survival. He says practical reason can't get you there. My claim is that the SPR work is him going at those postulates from the other end, empirically, by trying to find out whether anything like a soul or an afterlife actually turns up in the data. So the ghost-hunting isn't a senile appendage to the ethics, it's continuous with it, and once you see that he's a much more coherent figure than the standard story allows. Second claim, and this is the one I actually care about: his stance (keep investigating, don't commit either way, hope) is a live option for us now, not a period piece.

Here's the room, roughly a dozen people. Three analytic ethicists who teach the Methods and know the Parfit-and-after literature better than I do, and who are basically allergic to anything that smells supernatural. One historian of nineteenth-century British philosophy who knows the actual institutional history of the SPR and will have archival detail I don't. A psychologist from across campus who runs replication studies and has very strong priors that parapsychology is a case study in how not to do science. Two grad students who are into James and the will-to-believe stuff and will probably be on my side in a way that doesn't help me. And the chair, who thinks the whole topic is unserious and will make that known.

What I want: the eight-ish objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they do rather than by who speaks first (or give me both orders if they diverge). Phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it, in their vocabulary, because the ethicist's version of your postulates are doing work the evidence can't do is not the same complaint as the psychologist's version of the same thing, and the historian's will be about what the SPR actually was as an institution. For each one: what I should concede up front, a reply I can actually deliver in two minutes with no slides, and an honest flag on where that reply is soft or where I'm bluffing. Don't sand off the unfair ones, just mark them as unfair and tell me how to handle them in the room without being defensive. Then the one question I'm praying nobody asks, and why it's the one that hurts.
