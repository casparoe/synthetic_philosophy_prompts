I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of war, and a classmate has handed me an argument that I cannot fault step by step, yet whose conclusion I am certain is false. I would like help deciding which premise to give up and what giving it up would cost.

(1) A person is liable to be killed in defence of others only if he is responsible for the unjust threat that is to be averted.
(2) Being responsible here must mean being culpable: the person must know, or be in a position to know, that the cause he serves is unjust, and choose to contribute to it nonetheless.
(3) Many combatants on the unjust side of a war are not culpable in that sense — conscripts, the deceived, the sincerely and reasonably mistaken.
(4) So many such combatants are not liable to be killed.
(5) To kill a person who is not liable to be killed is to wrong him gravely; it is murder unless some further justification is available.
(6) In the ordinary conduct of war no further justification is available, since the combatant killed is not attacking anyone at the moment he is killed and his death averts no imminent threat.
(7) So much of the killing that the just side does in a just war is murder, and no war involving much killing can be fought justly.
(8) That is too much. If any wars are just, the killing in them is not for that reason murder.
(9) So one of (1)–(6) is false.

The steps I would most like to keep are (1) and (3): I cannot see giving up the thought that liability rests on responsibility, or the thought that the conscript is not to blame for the war he was sent to fight. Tell me which step should go, why it looked unobjectionable, and what I pay for dropping it — and if the cost is high, whether some reading of the remaining steps lets me keep more than I expect. This matters for the course, since it bears on whether unjust combatants are criminals or merely unlucky, and on whether the moral equality of combatants is a claim about excuses or about liability.
