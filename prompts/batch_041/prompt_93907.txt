You are a philosopher who specializes in early modern moral psychology and an experienced designer of classroom simulations. You know Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator closely, and you are skilled at building activities in which participants run into a problem with their own hands before anyone names it. Before you write the activity, brainstorm in a short section you label "Design notes": work out, moment by moment, what would have to happen for a room of people to feel the specific tension described below. Reason it through carefully, then give the runnable activity as your deliverable.

The setting: one 90-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral psychology, sixteen students, none of whom has read Smith. The only materials are paper, pens, a whiteboard, and movable chairs. The session must not open with a lecture, and no one should hear the words "sympathy," "impartial spectator," or "Smith" until the debrief.

The idea the activity has to make them feel. Smith holds that we never feel another person's emotion directly; what we call sympathy is a fellow-feeling we manufacture by imagining ourselves into their situation. Because we also cannot see our own conduct from the outside, he holds that we judge ourselves by imagining how an informed but disinterested onlooker would view us, and that this imagined figure becomes the inner standard we actually consult. Two further claims are the ones you must build the activity around, because they are the uncomfortable ones. First, Smith thinks we are constitutionally poor at seeing ourselves as others see us; self-love distorts the very judgment we rely on, and the remedy is made of the same stuff as the disease. Second, he thinks the inner judge can be captured: our moral sentiments bend to custom and fashion, and we are prone to admire the rich and the great while neglecting the poor and the obscure, so that what feels like an impartial verdict may be the room, the moment, or our own interest in disguise. The students should end up having used a standard of judgment, trusted it, and then seen where it came from. The target is not the cozy reading in which conscience is a reliable inner voice; it is the reading in which the voice is something we assembled, and could have assembled otherwise.

What the activity must include:

- Roles, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, materials, and a stake the group genuinely cares about without money or prizes. The format is yours to choose, but participants must be doing something, not listening.
- A stage in which each person must privately commit to a judgment before any public discussion, so that any later shift can be compared against a fixed point.
- A stage in which the group must construct its own standard of judgment from scratch, rather than being handed one.
- One feature of the setup that the facilitator can secretly manipulate (who is present, who benefits, what has just been made salient), such that a second round of judgments moves. The students should reach a point where they cannot tell whether they are following an impartial spectator or merely a consensus, and should feel that they cannot tell.
- No lecture beforehand. The vocabulary and the doctrines are named only in the debrief.

The debrief must map particular moments of what happened onto particular pieces of Smith's machinery: the two-step act of imagination by which the standard is built; the self-deceit that makes it unreliable; the bending of sentiment to custom and fashion and to admiration of the rich and great; and the gap between wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy. Give two or three debrief questions that do this mapping without simply lecturing.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run — at least three specific failure modes, with what the facilitator should do about each.

Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply: for instance, the fact that a real impartial spectator is the sediment of a lifetime and operates largely below awareness, and the fact that Smith takes it to carry genuine normative authority rather than being merely a social artifact. Say plainly which part of the idea the debrief has to assert rather than demonstrate.

Do not assign any reading, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to scholarship; everything the activity needs should be present in the room.
