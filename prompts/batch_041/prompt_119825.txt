Bit of a weird one, sorry in advance. I'm a backend/infra engineer, no real philosophy background beyond one ethics elective, and I've gotten into an argument with a coworker about risk that I can't resolve on my own.

Here's whats bugging me. In some kinds of engineering the practice is basically: certain outcomes are off the table no matter how unlikely they are. Airliners are certified on the idea that a failure which would kill everyone on board has to be so improbable it will never happen across the whole lifetime of the fleet -- the probability threshold they write down is a formality, a way of spelling the word never. Nuclear plants get built to a rule that no single component failing can knock out the safety system; that's not a claim about probabilty at all, it's a constraint on structure. Medical devices have the same flavor: you add redundancy, you don't just compute probability times harm and shrug when the product comes out small.

But in other places -- and honestly in my own risk spreadsheet at work, which has a 1-to-5 impact column that everyone just eyeballs -- we do exactly that. We stick a number on the chance of some disaster, multiply by the harm, get something that looks small, and then decide whether to fund the mitigation based on whether that product clears a bar. And there's a whole ecosystem doing this at much bigger scale, asteroid deflection and pandemic prep and AI stuff, where the probabilities are essentially made up and the harms are enormous, and the case for spending real money is just that even a tiny probability times a gigantic harm is still a big number.

So what does each practice actually commit you to? Does the safety-engineering approach presuppose that a very small probability can't be weighed against a very large harm -- i.e. that the value scale has a ceiling, or that catastrophic outcomes get lexicographic priority, or just that probabilities below some resolution are indistinguishable from zero and shouldn't be treated as real credences at all? Or is it not anti-expected-value, just expected value with a hard constraint bolted on? And which parts does it strictly need, versus which are precautionary styling that a different theory could license just as well? I'd like to know what it quietly rules out.

The reason I care: a friend keeps running the Pascal's wager / Pascal's mugging move on me -- some stranger says a wizard will torture an unfathomable number of people unless I hand over my wallet, the odds are astronomically small, the harm is astronomically large, so by expected value I pay up. My instinct is that the safety-engineering crowd already solved this, and that the answer is something like: the scale has to be bounded, or the probabilities have to be real estimates rather than placeholders. But I can't tell whether that instinct is a principled position or just me refusing to be a sucker. And I have a nagging suspicion the safety practice is itself mugging-able from the other side -- if any small chance of catastrophe outranks everything, what stops every paranoid scenario from outranking everything else?

If I'm conflating two or three seperate things here (risk aversion, decision theory, what regulators are actually doing), just say so. Don't smooth it over to be polite -- if the question as posed is confused, or if the honest answer is that these practices are compatible with several different theories and there's no single commitment to dig out, I'd much rather hear that straight than get a tidy answer that flatters the framing I walked in with.
