I'm in a metaphysics reading group that has spent the term on de re modality, and we've finally reached the fork between Lewis's counterpart theory and the Kripkean alternative of transworld identity. To make the dispute less bloodless, I proposed that we screen Everything Everywhere All at Once and read it through that lens, and now I've been asked to open the discussion. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the actual debate seriously, rather than using one as decoration for the other.

The film's cosmology, as I understand it, is that every choice spawns a universe, so there is an Evelyn who became a martial-arts master and movie star, an Evelyn who works a teppanyaki grill, and countless others besides; verse-jumping lets one Evelyn reach into another and borrow her skills, memories, and body; and Jobu Tupaki is the version of Joy whose mind splintered after she was pushed to jump beyond her endurance. Read that against the two theories. On the Lewisian picture, no individual exists in more than one world; what we casually call the kung fu Evelyn is not Evelyn at all but a counterpart, a distinct person who resembles her, and modal claims about Evelyn are grounded in facts about these analogues. On the Kripkean picture, it is Evelyn herself who might have been the kung fu master, and we can ask what would have happened to her without any intermediary. I want you to say which picture the film's mechanics actually implement, and whether its emotional logic agrees. My suspicion -- which I'd like you to test rather than assume -- is that the film is split: the mechanism looks Lewisian, since each Evelyn is a separate person with her own history and there is only borrowing, never literal identity, while the whole weight of the ending -- the demand that Evelyn save her daughter, that she own her own regrets, that she choose this ordinary life -- seems to require that these counterparts are one and the same person. Consider the scenes where it matters: Evelyn acquiring the skills of a self she never was; Jobu's claim to have seen every version of herself and concluded that nothing matters; the Everything Bagel as the nihilist payoff of a multiverse in which every possibility is realized; and the resolution in which Evelyn chooses kindness and her unremarkable life. Does the film dramatize Kripke's complaint about counterparts -- that they are the wrong object of our concern precisely because they are not us -- or does it refute that complaint by showing that we can care about, and be answerable for, selves that are only analogues? Where does the reading break down and force the honest critic to concede that the film is muddled, or that it is doing something neither theory anticipates? And finally, does the film add anything to the debate that the arguments alone do not -- for instance, the thought that being one person across possibilities is not a metaphysical given but an achievement of attention and love?
