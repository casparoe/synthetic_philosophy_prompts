You are a professional philosopher working in social epistemology, with a specialty in the ethics of testimony and credibility, and you are also an experienced designer of experiential workshops for people with no philosophical training. Think carefully about the design problem before committing to a design. I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Once a year I run a ninety-minute session at my engineering team's offsite. Roughly twelve engineers attend, none of them philosophers. I have a whiteboard, index cards, a timer, and no projector. I want the session to turn on a cluster of ideas I have read about and find genuinely unsettling, but I will not lecture, and I do not want the group to hear the standard vocabulary until the very end. Ideally they spend the bulk of the time arguing about a concrete decision and only afterwards recognize what they have been doing.

The ideas I want them to collide with, stated as plainly as I can:

First, a speaker's report can be discounted not because of anything in the report but because of who she is taken to be, and the discount can be invisible to the person applying it.

Second, a group can fail to hear a true and clearly stated report because the shared vocabulary contains no slot for the experience being reported. This can happen with no ill will and no false belief anywhere in the room; the failure is in the resources, not in anyone's character.

Third, the most common form of this leaves no trace at all: a speaker who anticipates that she will not be taken up says nothing, or says only the flattened version she expects to be heard, so the loss never appears in the record and the transcript looks clean.

Fourth, and this is the subtlety I care most about: not every discounting is a wrong. A hearer who discounts a source on a genuine record of unreliability has done nothing unjust, and a hearer who repairs her judgment by adopting a blanket rule — believe the junior engineer twice as much — has not done the thing that matters, which is attending to this particular speaker and this particular claim.

Design the activity. It should meet these conditions:

- It is a runnable exercise for twelve people in ninety minutes, with roles, rules, materials, setup, and a timeline marked in minutes.
- Participants make a real decision with consequences inside the fiction (I have a hunch something built around an incident review would work, but use your judgment), and they can see the decision's outcome.
- Each of the four ideas above arises from the mechanics — casting, information asymmetries, the credibility rule, the stakes — not from anything I say. Tell me exactly which rule produces which idea.
- Nothing names the concepts in the first two thirds of the session.
- Address the tension between making the discounting realistic enough to sting and not actually subjecting a colleague to identity-based disbelief. Tell me how to cast and rotate roles, and what to do if someone gets upset.
- Include a debrief: a sequence of questions, the specific moments to point back to, the moment to introduce the standard terms, and how to keep the discussion from turning into an accusation of particular individuals.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure.
- Be frank about which of the four ideas the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
- Flag the places where you have made a judgment call rather than derived something.

Assume I cannot look anything up: no reading lists, no citations, no names of papers or authors. Be concrete enough that I could run the session from your answer alone.
