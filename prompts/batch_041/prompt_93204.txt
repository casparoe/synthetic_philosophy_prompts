Me and a freind have been arguing about joint action for weeks and i want to actually get somewhere with it. Can you write me a dialogue between two philosophers, one who thinks collective intentionality is basic — that when two people do something together there's a genuinely shared state of mind, an intention belonging to the two of them and not to either one, and that this can't be analysed away into each person's private intentions plus beliefs about the other guy — and one who thinks that's hand-waving, and that all our we-talk bottoms out in individual intentions with the right contents, plus mutual expectations, plus each one adjusting as the other goes. Make them equally sharp: neither is a fool, neither is obviously the right answer, and each should answer what the other actually said instead of sliding past it into their own speech.

There are a few cases i keep throwing at my friend, please have them fight over these:

1) Two strangers see a toddler fall off a wall and both lunge and catch her at once, no words, no plan, and afterwards both say we caught her. Joint action, or just two lucky actions that happened to fit together?

2) A cellist in an orchestra thinks the piece is sentimental junk and is only there because it pays, but she plays her part perfectly and follows the conductor like everyone else. Did she share in the performance, or was she just making notes alongside the others?

3) Two guys carry a heavy crate up a hill, both adjusting to each other's steps. A thinks they're delivering wine for a wedding; B is smuggling something and assumes A is his accomplice. Both want the crate at the top. One thing done together, or two things that happen to coincide — and can two people be doing the same joint action under descriptions that share almost nothing?

Also, i have a test i half-believe and want them to attack: you're acting together with someone just in case you'd notice and adjust if they dropped out mid-stream. Call it the flinch test. On that test the cellist is in, since she'd notice if the conductor stopped, and the builders of a cathedral that took three generations are out, since nobody's around to notice when the earlier ones die, yet we happily say they built it. So either the test is wrong or that last bit of talk is. And i keep hearing that all of this is just team reasoning — people switching from what should i do to what should we do — is that a genuine third option or a relabelling of one of the two views above?

End with them having located exactly where they disagree: some case where they now predict different things, or a claim one of them has to give up. No friendly both-are-right shrug, and some real back and forth in the middle rather than two monologues with a handshake at the end.
