I'm a novelist — second book, a port town on the North Sea sometime in the middle of the seventeenth century, bad harvests, a harbour thats slowly silting up — and I've been having a long running argument with my friend Nell, who teaches early modern history, about whether the natural world gets to be a character in a story or whether it's just the weather. Nell rules on things like a judge, so I've started keeping a list. The plague: an actor, she says, it has a biography of its own and you can't reduce it to the people it kills. The big eruption, and the cold summer it caused on the other side of the world: actor. The gales that broke the Armada: actor. The blight that took the potatoes: actor. The rats that come ashore off the ships: actor, though she admits that one is easy. Then the other column. The coastline, with the one decent natural harbour that is the only reason the town exists at all: not an actor, a stage — nobody has to answer a coastline, you just build on it. The Roman road running out of town: not an actor, an instrument, somebody's decision. The harbour wall the town keeps rebuilding: same thing, a decision, not a party. And when I said the harbour making the town rich was itself a kind of acting, she said that isn't the question; the question is whether the thing has anything of its own to answer with.

Now my problem. The novel stretches across a run of decades when the weather simply gets worse — colder, wetter, harvest after harvest coming in short, and nobody alive knows it's a trend, they think God is angry or the farmers are lazy. I want that cold to be a presence in the book, nearly a character, and when I told Nell that she made a face. She also gets twitchy about anything that smells like climate explaining history — she says that's how you end up with books where the weather makes empires fall. So I need you to be the philosopher of history here and not a cheerleader for my draft. Will she call the long cooling an actor or a condition? What rule is she actually running, given everything above? What other rules would fit her rulings just as well, and where does each of them land on the cold? Then commit to a best guess with your reasons, and tell me the case I should put to her to settle it — some situation where the surviving rules come apart and her answer would tell me which one she's on. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate rules that could each survive every verdict on the list, including the ones that look like they're only about living things and the harbour that plainly does make a difference to history, and test each one against the whole list before you pick.

If it turns out her rulings don't pin down a single rule, say so plainly and tell me how far apart the survivors are, and whether her allergy to climate-and-empires stuff is doing any real work in the rule she's running or is just a mood. And one last thing, since you're being a philosopher anyway: if she comes back with "the cold is a condition, not an actor," is that a genuine disagreement with me or a fight about a word — because I need to know whether to rewrite the book or just stop asking her.
