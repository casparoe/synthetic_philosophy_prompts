Bit of an odd one. I'm a trial statistician — I design and analyse randomised clinical trials, mostly oncology, so my working life is randomisation, sampling frames, stratification, differential non-response, and lately the estimand framework (the ICH addendum where you have to write down in advance what you even mean by "the effect of the drug", including what happens to a patient who stops taking it — treatment policy vs hypothetical strategy, etc.).

Anyway, last month my name came out of a hat: I got invited to one of those citizens' assemblies, and then actually got drawn in the second-stage stratified lottery, so I'm now spending Saturdays deliberating about the local transport budget. And the whole thing feels *exactly* like something I do at work, and I cant tell whether that's genuine insight or whether my vocabulary is just eating the concept.

So here's what I want. Restate the case for sortition / lottocracy — the argument that we should fill legislatures, or at least policy bodies, by lottery instead of election — in my vocabulary. Sampling frames, probability vs convenience sampling, selection bias, stratification, differential non-response, attrition, ITT vs per-protocol, external validity, and the estimand machinery. Go claim by claim: here's the claim as its advocates put it, here's the best translation into my language, here's what got bent on the way over. Please don't just hand me "elections are a self-selected sample, sortition is an unbiased one, QED" — I basically already believe that, which is exactly why I'm suspicious of it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. What in the sortition debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Legitimacy, authority, consent, accountability — I assume those are the answers, but I want to know precisely where the translation breaks, and whether the break means my framework is missing something real or the original claim was vaguer than it looked. One test case I'd like you to run all the way through: the Ostbelgien setup in Belgium, where a standing citizens' council drawn by lot sets the agenda for the assembly that then deliberates. Is that a sampling-design decision, or is it a different kind of animal entirely? And relatedly: what exactly is the estimand of an election or a citizens' assembly — today's opinion, or the opinion the population would have reached after a week of being handed experts and time to talk? Those look like different estimands to me and I don't think anyone in the debate is being explicit about which one they mean.

Be upfront about what's settled among people who actually work on this (e.g. that elections select for a particular kind of person) versus what's genuinely contested (e.g. whether statistical representativeness buys any authority at all, or whether deliberation is a feature or a bias), and don't sell me a contested view as the consensus. Also try not to smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call it translation — "representative" doesn't get to mean "correct" for free.
