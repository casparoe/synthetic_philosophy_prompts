I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and once or twice a year I'm asked to run a session at a weekend ideas festival — the kind of event where a few hundred adults with graduate degrees in assorted fields pay to sit in rooms and argue with each other. This year I have seventy-five minutes and a room that seats about twenty-four, and I want to spend it on the socialist calculation debate: the exchange between Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange on the other over whether a centrally planned economy can allocate resources rationally without markets. Nearly everyone in the room will arrive with the slogan version, that the planners can never know what people want, and I want them to leave having felt the actual problem, which is narrower and more interesting than the slogan. So what I need from you is a runnable activity rather than a lecture — rules, materials, a timeline, a payoff scheme that uses tokens or points rather than money, and a debrief — and I need it to be photocopiable by a graduate student with no budget, no projector, and no laptops in the room. The thing I most want to avoid is a rigged game in which the planners visibly fail. My instinct is that the activity should be played twice. In the first round the planning board should genuinely succeed at the static problem — fixed wants, fixed technology, all of it written on cards, enough time to add everything up — so that the room concludes the arithmetic objection has been answered. Then something has to change for the second round: the wants and the techniques should be things nobody can fully state in advance and that are only revealed in the acting, so that any failure is not a failure of computation but of something else, and I want the participants, not me, to be the ones who diagnose what has gone wrong. Round two should then be played by the same people with the same cards, but as a market, so the comparison is clean and the room can see the difference in what they knew and when they knew it rather than only in the outcome.

Three further requirements, and they are the ones I care about most. First, the debrief has to keep apart three things that popular retellings run together: whether a board can aggregate information that already exists somewhere in the system, whether the people on the ground have any reason to reveal what they know and then act on the plan, and whether anyone in the system has a reason to discover something nobody knew yet. I care more about the third than the first two, and I would like the rules arranged so that the room trips over the distinction rather than being told it. Second, the activity must leave Lange a real reply. If somebody says that trial-and-error price-setting by the board would have got them there eventually, the game should have given them enough material to make that case with, so the argument continues after I stop talking — and since a good part of the room will have read something online about how modern computation settles the old debate, I want the design to put pressure on that claim too: if the second round fails, it should fail for a reason more computing power would not fix, or else the debrief should say plainly that computing power would fix it and spell out what that concession costs the Austrian side. Third, I need a named stopping point, so the ending is not decided by whoever is loudest and the session does not run over.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my own guess is that a few participants will decide the hidden-information cards are a cheat once they see the second round, and that the board will burn its first ten minutes reinventing a currency without noticing — and how I should handle each. Then the two things I actually want from you. Be explicit about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief, including if the honest answer is that a seventy-five-minute game can stage the information problem but not the Austrian claim about discovery. And tell me what would change your mind about the design: which observation from an actual run, or which argument about the debate, would show that the activity is rigged — that it makes the market look better, or worse, than the underlying argument licenses — and where in your own plan the reasoning is likeliest to be wrong. I would rather have an activity that cuts against my own prior than one that flatters it.
