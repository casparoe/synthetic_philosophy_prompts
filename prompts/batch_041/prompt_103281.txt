A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about dirty hands, and I've decided the only way to settle it is to make a room full of people go through it rather than keep trading intuitions. I teach a final-year seminar in political ethics — about eighteen students, one ninety-minute session — and what I want is an activity that forces them to feel the difference between two things they'll otherwise run together: someone who does something genuinely wrong because the alternatives are worse, and someone who merely permits a wrong to happen. My friend's line was that once the numbers justify the call there's nothing left over, you made the right decision, you sleep fine. I think he's wrong, and I think the way to show him is to build a session in which the decision has to be owned by a single person rather than spread across a committee, so that they run into the residue that nobody else's forgiveness and no good outcome can cancel — the point being that the guilt and the *feeling* of guilt can come apart, and it's the first, the change in what they've actually done, that survives when the second fades. So: give me the roles, the constraint that forces the choice, what's at stake, and how to run the debrief so that they locate the stain and then see where a many-hands version of the same case would have dissolved it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my bet is they'll turn it into a cost-benefit exercise and decide the hard part is just aggregation — and be honest about what a ninety-minute room cannot reproduce, since the part I care about may be exactly the part that takes years. If the premise is confused, if there's no such residue to stage or no activity that could produce it, say so plainly rather than inventing one to keep me happy. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, in the way one would write a long email, and in British English.
