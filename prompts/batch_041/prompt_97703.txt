I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year on a book about a small town that keeps a practice no one can defend. On a fixed day each autumn the town closes its one road to outside traffic and spends the day repainting the fences of the houses whose owners have died in the past year — a chore that made sense when the town was remote and paint kept the wood from splitting through the winter. The road is paved now and the wood is treated, so the chore accomplishes nothing. And here is what interests me: nearly everyone in the town would be relieved if the practice stopped, nearly everyone believes that nearly everyone else would be relieved, and still no one will be the first to skip it. Those who do skip are not punished in any organized way; they are simply, quietly disliked, and they know it, and the dislike seems to answer to no harm the practice prevents, because it prevents none.

I have been reading around the philosophy of convention to understand what I am describing, and I have got as far as an account I associate with David Lewis: a convention is a regularity in the behavior of a group in a recurrent situation, where everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and everyone prefers to conform on the condition that the others do, because the situation is a coordination problem and the regularity is a coordination equilibrium; and all of this is common knowledge, and the regularity is arbitrary, in that some other regularity would have solved the same problem just as well. That account fits how the practice began. It does not seem to fit what the practice has become. The coordinating problem is gone; the preference to conform now runs through no gain in coordination that I can identify; and something that looks like an obligation has grown on top of what was once a mere regularity.

So I would like you to write me an essay on the following controversy, and to reach and defend a verdict rather than leaving the matter open. The controversy is whether a case like this one shows that an account of convention in Lewis's style is incomplete and must be supplemented by something else — a separate story about how sanctions, or internalized norms, or the aversion to being conspicuous, generate a felt ought that the convention itself cannot supply — or whether the whole phenomenon, the survival of the regularity after its problem has dissolved and the normative sting that now attaches to it, can be recovered inside a broadly Lewisian framework: for instance by treating the practice of censuring deviants as a further convention resting on a second-order coordination problem, and by reading the preference to conform widely enough to cover the wish not to be the one who is out of step.

There are places where my own thinking stalls, and I want the essay to get them right. Keep apart the regularity that merely sustains itself from the regularity that enforces itself: in my town the deviants are resented but not punished, so any account that explains the ought entirely through sanctions owes me an account of what resentment without sanction is doing. Keep apart also the case in which each person privately wants the practice to continue from the case in which each privately wants it to end but will not be the first to end it; mine is the second, and I suspect the two demand different explanations. And take seriously the thought that the original problem has not vanished but has been displaced — that the town now faces a coordination problem about who, if anyone, may stop, rather than about whether to paint. If that move saves the Lewisian account, say so and show how; if it only relocates the mystery, say that instead.

The answer should close with a single machine-readable line of the form "Verdict: <short phrase>", naming your position in no more than fifteen words.
