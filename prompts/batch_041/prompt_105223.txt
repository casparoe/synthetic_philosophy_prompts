I run a small mutual-aid thing in my neighborhood — about forty of us chip in, we end up with maybe $600 a month, and me and two other people decide who gets it. Usually $50–$200 to one person for one specific thing: a car repair so somebody can keep their job, a deposit, meds, sometimes just groceries. Nobody's getting rich and it's rarely life or death, but people get really upset about the calls, and honestly I don't have a good answer for why we gave it to one person and not another.

The three of us keep having the same fight without knowing we're having it. My one friend says give it to whoever it helps the most, period — if $200 pulls one person out of a hole and barely registers for someone else, that's where it goes. My other friend says that's not fair, we should just be giving everyone the same, or at least treat people alike unless there's a real reason not to. And I keep feeling like they're both missing something — that what actually matters is whether the money leaves the person more able to do the things a person should be able to do: get around, be healthy, not be humiliated, take part in stuff. Two people can get the same $200 and one ends up way more able to live their life than the other.

I want a procedure we can actually run every month — something I could hand to a new volunteer. Not an essay. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, when the process says no or "not this month," and — this is the part I care about — where it stops being about facts and turns into a judgement call one of us just has to make and own. And dont tell me to just make it equal, because I've noticed "equal" can mean making everyone worse off in the name of nobody being ahead.

It has to handle the cases that actually come up, which are the annoying ones: the guy who's bad with money and will probably waste it; the woman who's been poor so long she's stopped asking for anything bigger, so on paper she never seems to "need" much; someone who'd get more out of it than anyone, but partly because they have expensive taste in general and always have; and the case where giving one person a lot helps them enormously while spreading it thin does almost nothing for anyone — except picking a favorite feels wrong.

Then run it on three real ones: (1) the car repair for someone who'll lose their job without it, (2) a deposit so someone can leave a bad living situation, and (3) someone asking for money for a hobby-ish thing that would genuinely make their life better but nobody would die without. If the procedure gives an answer I won't like on one of these, say so and don't soften it. And tell me what the procedure just cant decide — the parts where I'm kidding myself if I think there's a right answer.
