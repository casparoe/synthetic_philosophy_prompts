I am a high-school junior and one of two student leaders of our school's philosophy club, and I have been given the session for next month. We meet in an ordinary classroom for about fifty-five minutes, we usually get eighteen to twenty students (mostly juniors and seniors, a few with no philosophy background at all), and the room has movable desks, a whiteboard, and nothing else worth relying on. The question I have been asked to run is whether taxation is theft. I do not want to lecture, and I do not want the session to end the way our last attempt did, with one half of the room insisting that taking someone's earnings under threat of force is obviously a taking and the other half replying that you would have no earnings without the state, and neither half actually engaging the other. What I want is a game in which the group runs into the harder structure of the question before anyone names a position.

My rough idea, which I would like you to build out, repair, or replace: the group plays a small settlement. Each participant is given a plot, drawn at random, and the plots differ in natural endowment, so that one sits on a spring, one on a seam of something valuable, and most are ordinary. People work their plots and trade, and at fixed intervals a tax authority takes a share of what each participant holds. My hypothesis is that the room will split not over whether the authority may take a share, but over what it is taking. Everyone will have a strong intuition that taxing what someone grew or made by hand is a taking, and a much weaker intuition that taxing the value that came from having drawn the good plot is a taking at all, since nobody produced the spring. I want the rules arranged so that participants feel that asymmetry in their own choices before it is named — ideally so that the person who drew the spring ends up arguing both sides at different moments in the same game. I am also worried that the random draw will simply read as obviously fair, which would let everyone off the hook; tell me how to keep that from happening.

Please give me the rules and materials (no real money, no computers), a timeline for the full fifty-five minutes including setup and debrief, and the roles — including whether the tax authority should be a participant or the facilitator, with a recommendation and a reason. Then a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto the distinct positions at issue: that all taxation is a taking; that taxation of earnings is a taking while taxation of the value of unearned natural endowment is not; and that ownership is a legal arrangement all the way down, so that calling the state a thief begs the question — along with the related thought that there may be no such thing as income before the rules that define what counts as income. I would like the names of the philosophers and the standard labels for these positions kept out of the room until the debrief.

Two further things I need. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this with a real group of teenagers: where the game will fail to produce the intuition I am after, what someone will say to break it, and how I should handle that in the moment. Second, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — which part of the argument has to be supplied by me in the debrief because no fifty-five-minute game can make the group feel it. I would rather know the limits in advance than be surprised by them.
