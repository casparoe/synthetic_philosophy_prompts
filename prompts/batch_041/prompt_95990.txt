You are a philosopher of testimony who writes for non-specialists. I need a speech. I'm a physician who also teaches epistemology, and in two weeks I'm giving the address at the white-coat ceremony welcoming our hospital's incoming interns; the room will be about half clinicians who have lived through a thousand end-of-shift handoffs and half family members who have never heard the word "epistemology."

I want the address built on the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimony: the reductionist (in the tradition of Hume) holds that a hearer is justified in accepting what she is told only if she has independent, non-testimonial evidence of the speaker's reliability in that kind of case, while the anti-reductionist (in the tradition of Reid) holds that we are entitled to accept testimony by default, absent specific grounds for doubt. This distinction has to do structural work rather than decorate the speech — it should govern what I can and can't say to these two audiences, and it should determine where the address comes down. In particular, don't let it rest on the textbook examples (a stranger telling you the time, someone shouting that the house is on fire). The clinical case that matters here is the handoff: it transmits not only observations but assessments ("I think she's turned the corner"), it travels along a chain from one exhausted person to the next, and the hearer typically cannot obtain the independent evidence the reductionist demands. Say something honest about what this means for an intern who must act at 3 a.m. on the strength of someone else's judgment.

Keep the jargon out of the spoken text — a sharp family member should follow every sentence — but make the argument exact enough that the clinicians, who argue about this at the nursing station anyway, hear the real thing and not a slogan. Do not cite or name any literature in the speech itself. Before drafting, briefly note the two or three places where the philosophical distinction actually bites in this clinical setting; then write the address, about six to eight minutes spoken.
