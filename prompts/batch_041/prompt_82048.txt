I have no philosophical training and I am not asking for encouragement, but I have been turning over a puzzle about how we treat success, and I would like it worked through carefully.

Where I live, and in the schools and companies I have passed through, two habits sit side by side and seem to pull in opposite directions. The first celebrates winners. Grades are curved so that only a few can come out on top; one student is named valedictorian; sales figures are posted on a whiteboard; the year ends with an employee of the month and a banquet for the top performers. The second habit does the opposite. The same institutions hand out participation trophies, insist that every contribution matters equally, decline to name a best worker, and treat open competition for praise as faintly shameful. I have also seen this second habit described as a cultural trait in some countries -- sometimes called the law of Jante, and related to what people elsewhere call the tall poppy syndrome -- where standing out, or claiming to be better than your neighbours, is treated as a failure of character. And I have seen the first habit described as the national creed of a different country, where not celebrating the successful is taken to be a betrayal of what makes that country what it is.

I would like you to work out what each habit presupposes, philosophically.

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the celebrating habit. What must it assume about what makes a life go well, about what people deserve, and about whether the goods at stake are the kind one person can only have at another's expense? It occurred to me that some of these goods are strictly rivalrous -- there can be only one valedictorian -- while others are not, since everyone in a room can be excellent at once. Does the celebrating habit secretly assume that all success is of the rivalrous kind? And does it need the strong claim that the successful earned their standing, or can it manage with something weaker, such as the idea that excellence is worth having in the world whether or not anyone deserves credit for it?

2. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the levelling habit. What must it assume about human worth, about humility or self-esteem, and about whether comparison is nourishing or corrosive? Is it a claim about what people deserve, a claim about their welfare, or a claim about the sort of society one wants to live in? And is it a claim that everyone is equally good, or only that everyone is equally to be respected -- because those seem to me very different?

3. Tell me where the two habits genuinely contradict each other and where they do not. My suspicion is that they can coexist more easily than the shouting suggests, and I want to know exactly which parts collide and which only look as though they do.

4. Separate what each habit strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where the practice is underdetermined -- where several quite different theories would license the very same behaviour, so that watching what people do cannot reveal which theory is actually at work.

5. Suppose we replaced the implicit theory behind one habit with the other's. What would concretely have to change, in a school, a workplace, or a country's public life? And what would be lost that people might not have realised they valued until it was gone?

Argue rather than survey. Assume I know nothing of the technical literature, but do not simplify the reasoning on that account. I do not need references; I will look those up myself.
