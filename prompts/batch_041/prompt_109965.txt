I'm taking a seminar on medieval philosophy of mind, and next week I have to present on Avicenna's flying man, but the more I read the less confident I am that I understand what the argument is meant to establish. The setup I've been working with is this: suppose a person is created fully formed and suspended in mid-air, with his eyes covered and his limbs splayed so that he touches nothing and receives no sensory input of any kind; Avicenna's claim is that such a person would still affirm his own existence and be aware of himself, while being aware of nothing whatever about his body. My difficulty is that I can see two quite different arguments being run on this one scenario, and I cannot tell whether they are the same argument described in two ways or two arguments that merely happen to share a picture. On the bolder reading, the experiment is supposed to show that the self is apprehended without the body, hence is not the body, and hence that the soul is an immaterial substance. On the thinner reading, it shows only that each of us enjoys a primitive, non-sensory awareness of our own existence — a self-givenness that does not run through the senses — and says nothing on its own about whether that self is material or immaterial.

What I would like you to explain, as carefully as you can, is the inferential distance between these two readings: what exactly has to be added in order to move from a premise about being aware of oneself without being aware of one's body to a conclusion about being able to exist without one's body? Is the bridge a general principle about conceivability and possibility, a premise about what kind of thing can serve as the subject of a first-person awareness, or a claim about the difference between being aware of something and being aware of it as such? I am also bothered by a worry that the bolder reading seems to prove far too much: the flying man is equally unaware of his brain, his memories, his temperament, and any number of things that might be essential to him, so why should his failure to be aware of his body carry any special weight? I would like to know whether the argument can distinguish the body from these other cases — for instance by appealing to what can be stripped away from the conception of a self without destroying it — or whether any such repair merely relocates the difficulty rather than removing it.

Finally, I have seen it suggested that the flying man cannot reach immateriality on its own and needs to be supplemented by a separate line of thought: roughly, that a body, being extended and divisible, could not be the subject of a single unified awareness that binds many disparate perceptions into one experience, so the subject of awareness must be something other than a body. Please tell me whether that supplement really is needed, whether it does the work the flying man leaves undone, and whether the two together are stronger than either alone. Throughout, I care much more about the structure of the inferences than about labels or who held what: show me where each step would have to be licensed, which premise is doing the real work, and which one I should be most suspicious of.
