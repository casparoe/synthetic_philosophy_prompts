I would like an analogy audited rather than endorsed. I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy; a colleague in my reading group, who does, argues as follows: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. He takes this to establish that thought is substrate-independent, that the same program could in principle run on neurons or on silicon, that what matters about a mind is the program rather than the machinery, and — more strongly — that the question whether the brain is a computer is thereby settled in the affirmative, since anything that runs the program is a computer.

My working life supplies the vocabulary for the comparison, so please set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the source code, the compiler, the instruction set, the runtime or virtual machine, the emulator, and the notion of the same program that survives recompilation onto a different machine; and where nothing on the brain side answers to one of these. Then tell me where the analogy breaks in ways that bear on his conclusion, keeping the disanalogies that actually threaten it separate from the ones that are merely loose.

Three things trouble me in particular. First, in my world the identity of a program depends on a description someone has chosen: with enough ingenuity about levels of abstraction, one can write an interpreter that makes almost any physical device count as running almost anything. Is there a fact of the matter about which program a brain runs, independent of a describer, and does the analogy need there to be one? Relatedly, a program's identity is fixed by a specification and honored by an interpreter; is there anything on the brain side that answers to either role? Second, we routinely emulate one machine inside another, faithfully enough to run its software and reproduce its faults, so that the emulated machine's behavior is indistinguishable from the real machine's. Does that fact help his case or undercut it? Third, a virtual machine really does let you forget the hardware, but that portability was engineered on purpose, and nothing analogous was engineered in the case of the brain.

Once those points are on the table, does his conclusion survive — that the interesting questions about a mind are questions about its program, and that the brain is a computer in some nontrivial sense? If the analogy cannot carry that weight, say what comparison would do better, and be specific about what it preserves that the software analogy loses.
