I'm in my third year of a joint biology and philosophy degree, and for a seminar on the species problem I have to produce something usable rather than another essay. I want to build a diagnostic decision tree that takes a reader from a handful of commitments they already have about living things to a named position in the species debate, sorting people by what they actually say about cases rather than by which label they happen to like. The audience is my classmates, who know the standard catalogue of concepts — biological, phylogenetic, morphological, ecological, evolutionary — and are thoroughly bored of it, so please do not organise the tree as a list of those names. Build the spine out of the questions that genuinely separate the positions, in roughly this order or one you can defend: first, whether "species" names anything real in nature at all or is a conventional partition we impose, and if real, whether it is a kind (a class with members) or an individual (a particular lineage with parts); second, what the grouping is supposed to track when the evidence pulls apart — interbreeding, exclusive genealogy, ecological role, or nothing more than diagnosable difference; third, whether a single concept is expected to cover all of life or whether bacteria, asexual organisms, hybrids, and ring species are permitted to break it and license a different treatment. I need leaves for at least the following, each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the interbreeding-based concept, the monophyly-based concept, the ecological concept, the evolutionary-lineage concept, the view that these are really one concept whose defenders differ only about the criteria for applying it, the view that species are individuals rather than kinds, the pluralist who holds that several of these concepts are legitimate, and the eliminativist who would drop the category altogether. I also want the combinations of answers that nobody occupies marked and briefly diagnosed — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored? — and any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate flagged as such rather than fudged. Wherever possible, phrase a node as a question a reader can answer about a concrete case (a ring species, a hybridising plant complex, a bacterial strain, an asexual rotifer) without already knowing which position it leads to.

One further constraint on how I want this written: keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the species problem, and in particular treat the disagreement between the single-concept and the several-concepts camps as live rather than as a problem that has been solved, and do not present any one concept as the consensus view. Begin the response with the tree itself, with no preamble and no restating of what I have asked for, and put the caveats and the notes about what the instrument cannot do at the end.
