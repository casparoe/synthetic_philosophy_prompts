Bit of a weird ask, I know. I'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy training, but for the last three years my nights-and-weekends project has been a novel about my great-great-grandmother Rose. Real person: born in Ireland around 1860, came over as a teenager, spent forty years in a laundry in a mill town, kept a diary that is 95% weather and grocery accounts and about six pages where she clearly isnt okay. My aunt photocopied the diary for the whole family, that's basically my only source, plus two newspaper clippings. Book comes out next month, my publisher has booked the launch in the local library reading room, and so I have to give a short speech — six, seven minutes max — to a room that is: a) about fifteen relatives, a couple of whom actually knew Rose's daughter and are already not thrilled that I turned their grandmother into a character; b) the local history society, who are lovely but basically think novelists are liars with better lighting; and c) my climbing buddies, who came for the free wine and will start checking their phones at minute four.

Here's the hard part, and where I need you to be careful. I made stuff up. I invented the reason her husband left — family story is he just vanished, my book says she threw him out, which is more flattering to her and possibly false. I invented a friendship with a union organiser that the book very much implies was romantic. And I invented a stillbirth that, as far as I can tell, she never told a single soul about, and my narrator states it like a fact. I dont want the speech to be a philosophy lecture, but I also really dont want it to be the standard novelist hand-wave ("fiction tells a deeper truth") because my relatives deserve better than that and honestly so does she. What I've been chewing on with a friend is this: I think there are two different ledgers you can spend from when you write about a dead person. One is the ledger of what she actually did, one is the ledger of what her life was like to live and what she meant. My instinct is you're allowed to spend freely in the second and almost never in the first, and the whole mess in my book is that two of my three inventions are withdrawals from the first ledger. My friend says that's too neat. I also have this rule I made up, I call it the kitchen test: before an invention goes in, ask whether you'd be willing to say it out loud in your aunt's kitchen with the word "invented" attached. The stillbirth fails that test, which is why I kept it in the book and keep feeling bad about it, and the speech needs to own that without turning into a confession that makes the evening about me. There's alot of feeling in that room and one uncle is definitely going to say "sure she's not here to mind" and I'd like one line ready for that which isn't me explaining posthumous harm like a jerk.

So: act like a really good speechwriter who also happens to know a lot about the ethics of writing about real people, and think this through carefully before you write anything. Start by brainstorming the tensions in that room, and tell me which one the speech can actually resolve and which ones it can only hold. Then write the speech, in my voice — plain, a bit dry, no literary cadence, I'm an engineer not a poet — and make the two-ledger thing and the kitchen test do real structural work in it rather than getting name-dropped: they should be the reason certain sentences are in and others are out, and I want to feel that in the result. It has to land for the relatives without becoming an apology, and for the historians without becoming a defence of lying. End it by naming, out loud and in plain English, the three things I made up and the one thing I refused to make up, in about ninety seconds, and then just stop. No citations, no quotes from scholars, no reading list — it's a speech, nobody in that room wants a bibliography. Also give me one backup sentence in case my aunt stands up halfway through, and tell me which line you'd cut first if it runs long.
