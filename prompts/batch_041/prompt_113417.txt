I am applying for a small internal research grant that would buy me a single semester of part-time work on one paper of roughly eight thousand words, and I need a two-page proposal to accompany the application. Here is the half-formed idea I want you to turn into something fundable.

The familiar objection that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the separateness of persons is almost always run as an objection to aggregation: the theory, it is said, permits the losses of some to be justified by the gains of others. I suspect the objection conflates two claims that can be pulled apart. The first is a claim about what there is — that persons are distinct loci of experience and agency, and that there is no super-person whose good could be the object of a sum. The second is a claim about the shape a justification must have — that it is a defect in a theory to permit one person to bear a cost for another's benefit without some independent distributive principle governing the exchange. The first claim looks like something the utilitarian needs rather than something that threatens her: adding up interests only makes sense if those interests belong to genuinely separate subjects, so separateness is what makes aggregation intelligible rather than what refutes it. If that is right, the weight of the objection falls entirely on the second claim, and the real question becomes whether that claim can be defended without covertly leaning on the first. I am also unsure whether the objection is about persons at all, rather than about time-slices: utilitarianism equally permits a person's later interests to be traded against her earlier ones, and if that is objectionable it cannot be because the persons involved are separate, since here there is only one.

What I need from the proposal:

1. The question, stated precisely enough that there is a fact of the matter about whether it can be answered, and sharpened so that it does not simply collapse into the general question of whether consequentialism is true.
2. Why the existing positions on both sides leave it open. The utilitarian replies, as I understand them, tend to be either that aggregation is not the same thing as sacrifice, or that the objection begs the question against consequentialism; I want to know whether either reply actually engages the second claim, and exactly where the exchange stalls.
3. The method, staged across a semester rather than described as a survey: what I would actually do, in what order, and why this conceptual approach rather than the obvious alternative of constructing a formal model of aggregation — say what such a model would obscure.
4. Which version of utilitarianism the objection is aimed at, and whether its force survives against views that aggregate but subject aggregation to a distributive constraint. If it turns out to indict only pure sum-ranking, say what that reveals about what the objection is really about.
5. What a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and what would show that the objection is a restatement of a distributive ideal rather than an argument.
6. An honest assessment of what would make the project too large for a semester, and what I should cut.
