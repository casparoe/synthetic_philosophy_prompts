I am a doctoral student in international relations, and I have been trying to use the outside view in a paper on the longevity of great powers. A colleague pressed me with the following argument, and I cannot decide whether it exposes a flaw in the method or only in my use of it.

(1) When forecasting whether a great power will decline, one should begin from the outside view: identify the reference class of comparable past powers and take the base rate of decline in that class as one's prior.

(2) In the historical record of great powers, decline is the overwhelming norm; sustained hegemony is the rare exception.

(3) Therefore, for any current great power, the probability of decline is high, and its particular domestic and strategic features should not shift that prior much.

(4) The same reasoning applies to every current great power.

(5) So the outside view assigns a high probability of decline to each of them, and we should expect the present order to be populated by powers in decline.

(6) But if the reference class is defined over a long enough horizon, the base rate of decline is not a discovery about any particular case; it is close to a tautology, since every power eventually declines.

(7) And if the base rate cannot be updated by case-specific evidence, the outside view ceases to be a forecasting tool and becomes a deterministic law.

I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) and reject something later. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fix is to specify a horizon or to condition the reference class, tell me how that avoids the same problem without collapsing back into the inside view.
