Occasionalism — the view, traditionally credited to al-Ghazālī, that no created thing has causal power of its own and God alone acts, with the fire's burning the cotton being only the regular occasion on which he does — has been half-convincing me lately, and I keep noticing that my verdicts on ordinary causal claims refuse to settle into any single story. Here is where I come out.

The fire burned the cotton: true, and the fire did it. The virus caused her illness: true. The surgeon's slip caused the bleeding: true. My deciding to raise my arm caused it to rise: true, but in a different register, an agent's doing rather than a push. The shuffle caused the ace to come up: false, or anyway I resist it — the shuffle only left the ace on top. The rock in the road caused the car to swerve: true, and yet that is equally just the prior arrangement of matter. The missing guardrail caused the death: true, though nothing happened. God caused the fire to burn the cotton: if anything sustains the fire from moment to moment, this has to be true, but I can't tell whether it is the same claim as the first or a different sense of 'cause' altogether.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets most of these — each clause motivated by the case that forces it — and what does it predict about a thermostat switching on the furnace, a hypnotized subject's arm rising, and the moon raising the tide? If the principle that saves the most of my verdicts forces one out, which should go: that the fire did it, or that only God does?
