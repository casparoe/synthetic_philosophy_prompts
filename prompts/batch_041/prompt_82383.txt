I chair the definitions committee of my professional body. We meet monthly, most members are practitioners rather than philosophers, our wording binds the whole organisation, and six months ago I lost a vote I should have won because I spent two hours arguing with a colleague who turned out to mean something different by 'harm' than I did. Write a procedure the committee can run in a single sitting on the disputes that reach us: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal to treat the matter as a disagreement about words, and where it must hand the question to a vote because what is at stake is values rather than facts. It should end in one of three verdicts rather than a bare yes or no — the dispute dissolves; the dispute is verbal but the choice between the rival meanings is itself the substantive issue; the dispute is substantive — and it should say how to tell the second from the third, and why agreeing on every fact does not by itself make a dispute trivial. Be honest about where the procedure cannot decide, including the fact that its central test, whether two people mean the same thing, is itself something the parties can dispute, and about the risk that calling a dispute verbal is just a way of winning it. Keep it to a page, since nobody on the committee will read more. Run it on two cases: whether a particular practice counts as restraint or as protective intervention, and whether our standard's term 'clinician' covers nurses, where both sides agree on everything nurses do. On the first, I am the one who will not like the verdict.
