A colleague of mine defends Jantelagen — the Nordic norm that one should not set oneself above the group — with an analogy that circulates widely in these arguments: society as a crab bucket. The moment one crab starts to climb out, the others drag it back down, and they would all have been better off had they let it go. From this she concludes that the tall-poppy instinct is nothing but self-defeating envy, that the successful are sabotaged by people who might have risen themselves, and that the ethos is therefore indefensible.

I am not persuaded, not least because the image can be turned round: a gardener cuts the tallest poppy so that the rest of the bed may thrive, and there the levelling looks less like spite than husbandry. Could you lay out what corresponds to what in the crab-bucket picture — the bucket, the crabs, the rim, the world beyond — and then where the mapping breaks? I am especially keen to know whether the analogy quietly assumes something a defender of Jantelagen would reject, namely that every crab wants to escape and that escape is the only good at stake; whether it presupposes that status is a zero-sum contest; and whether, once the disanalogies are on the table, the conclusion that the instinct is mere envy survives. If a better analogy is to hand, what would it be? Please answer in British English.
