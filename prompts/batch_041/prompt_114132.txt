Next week I'm giving a talk to our philosophy of science reading group and I want to be ready for the Q&A. My thesis: the underdetermination of theory by evidence has been mis-framed by almost everyone. The version worth caring about isn't the logical one (here are two theories, here's the same evidence, pick either) but a resource-bounded one -- theories are underdetermined only relative to what's cheap to compute and cheap to test, so the gap between "the evidence" and "the theory" shrinks or widens with our computational and experimental budget instead of being a fixed, logical feature of the situation. The room is mostly Bayesians who will say this just collapses into a story about priors and likelihoods and adds nothing, plus a couple of old-school scientific realists who think the whole underdetermination literature is a cottage industry of toy examples and Duhem-Quine hand-waving. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a Bayesian or a realist would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is soft. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Don't worry about being nice about the weak spots -- I'd rather find out now.
