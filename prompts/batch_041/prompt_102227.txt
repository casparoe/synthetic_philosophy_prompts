I am drafting a position paper on futarchy — governance in which policy is selected by prediction markets rather than by votes — and before I circulate it I want an adversarial audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is the set.

1. For any question with a well-defined, externally verifiable resolution, the price of a liquid prediction market is a better estimate of the answer than the considered verdict of any deliberative body. The explanation of this superiority is the incentive structure of a resolvable bet: traders who are wrong lose money to those who are right, and the resolution event supplies a truthmaker that deliberation, however well conducted, does not supply on its own.

2. A coercive political decision is legitimate only if everyone subject to it had an equal opportunity to influence it. Influence proportional to capital, or to the confidence with which one holds a belief, is not equal opportunity.

3. My preferred institutional design is as follows. The legislature stipulates in advance, by ordinary majoritarian procedures, a scalar welfare index — say a weighted composite of median disposable income, healthy life expectancy, and a periodically administered life-satisfaction survey — together with its weights and its horizon (the index is read five years after a policy's implementation). Conditional markets then trade contracts of the form "the index will be at least v in five years if policy P is implemented," with stakes returned in the event that P is not implemented. The policy with the highest conditional price is implemented; if the top two fall within a fixed margin, the status quo stands. On this design the normative question — which index — is settled politically, and the empirical question — which policy best serves that index — is settled by the market.

4. I am a value pluralist. Social states are not rankable on a single scale: liberty, welfare, and equality are not commensurable, and any scalar index is a substantive and contested normative choice rather than a neutral measuring instrument.

5. I accept the familiar Goodhart-style lesson that a measure used as a target decays as a measure: once an indicator governs the allocation of resources, agents optimize the indicator rather than the thing it was meant to track.

6. I am unmoved by the complaint that ordinary citizens are ignorant of policy detail. Legitimacy, as I understand it, rests on authorization rather than on comprehension; a decision is not made less legitimate by the fact that those who authorized it could not have explained it.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and what is the least costly way to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one state precisely which commitment has to give and what is lost in giving it. I would particularly like to know whether any single revision would resolve more than one of the tensions, and whether some of my commitments are quietly doing work that another one already undermines.
