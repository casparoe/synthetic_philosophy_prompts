Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend who chairs the grant committee of a small foundation that funds research and interventions on global problems, and the argument was about whether her decisions follow any principle at all. Here is her record over the past three years, as I reconstructed it afterwards.

She funded two things. The first was a project to develop a low-cost therapy for a rare but fatal childhood disease that almost no other funder touches, where a cure looks achievable within a decade. The second was a speculative basic-science project on a deep but presently impractical physics question, also almost entirely unfunded, on the grounds that a breakthrough would matter enormously. She turned down two things. The first was a proposal to scale up an education program in a wealthy country, where the evidence base is already large and many foundations are active. The second was a proposal on a problem she herself described as essentially intractable within our lifetimes, despite almost no one else working on it.

Next month she has to rule on a proposal to develop a cheap, scalable treatment for a disease that already draws enormous government and philanthropic funding, where the science is unusually far advanced and the projected number of lives saved is very large.

What rule is she actually running? Lay out the two or three candidate principles that fit her past decisions equally well, say what each of them predicts about this new proposal, tell me which one I should bet on and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly tell the candidates apart.
