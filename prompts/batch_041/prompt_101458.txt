I am to give the retirement toast for my colleague, H., at the dinner closing out his career in the philosophy of language — a career built almost entirely on the distinction between what a speaker says and what a speaker implies. The room will be split. A dozen of his colleagues and former students will hear every deliberate flouting of the Cooperative Principle; his large family, who have driven in from three states, will take the sentences at face value. H. has asked in writing that the evening be light. And the dean is present — she controls whether H. gets an office after he retires, and she has already let it be known that she regards public criticism of a colleague as a failure of collegiality.

My difficulty is this. What I actually want to convey is, first, that the claim H. spent his early career defending — that the maxims of conversation form a closed list, all derivable from a single principle of rationality — is, I am now confident, mistaken; and second, that this matters not at all to what the field owes him or to what he is to me. I want the colleagues to hear both things, the family to hear only something warm and true, and the dean to be unable to quote a single line against me afterwards. One further constraint: nothing may implicate anything about H.'s long estrangement from his son, which his sister in the third row does not know about and which the natural implicature routes would drag in.

Please write the toast, about five minutes. Work by flouting the maxims rather than quietly violating them; keep every implicature cancellable, so that I can say in good faith that I never said any such thing; and watch where a "but" or a "therefore" would commit me to something I cannot take back. Afterwards, add a short set of notes for my eyes only, identifying each implicature you built and the maxim it turns on.
