I am a retired experimental scientist who has only recently begun reading philosophy, and I am trying to state the rule behind my verdicts about self-deception. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures the following judgments.

- A man whose wife is plainly having an affair sincerely says he trusts her and cites her absences as overwork. I call this self-deception.
- A scientist who continues to defend a hypothesis after anomalies appear, but who openly acknowledges the anomalies and says they may yet be resolved, I call stubbornness rather than self-deception.
- A woman who says she is not afraid of flying but avoids planes, trembles at the thought, and checks the weather obsessively I call self-deceived, even though she never explicitly forms the belief that she is unafraid.
- A man who believes his son is innocent despite what looks like conclusive evidence, and who also believes the evidence was fabricated, I am unsure about: it feels like loyalty or delusion, not quite self-deception.
- A person who has never been told that his judgment is biased, and who therefore believes he is impartial, I do not call self-deceived.
- A person who suspects a damaging truth and deliberately avoids the book, the test, or the conversation that would confirm it, I call self-deceived.
- A patient who denies that he is blind even though he cannot see, and who confabulates reasons for his collisions, I am inclined to call self-deceived, but I am not sure whether the label applies to a neurological condition.
- A scientist who publishes a result he knows is statistically fragile but genuinely believes it will replicate, I keep going back and forth on.

My tentative principle is that self-deception requires a motivated gap between what one believes and what one is willing to avow, and that the person must have some awareness of the truth being avoided. But the third case seems to lack an avowed belief, the fourth seems to lack awareness, the second seems to have awareness but no motivation, and the fifth lacks both. Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? If not, which verdict should I give up? What does the surviving principle predict for a case I have not listed: someone who sincerely believes he is generous while his behavior is consistently stingy, but who never has the thought that he is stingy and would be genuinely surprised to hear it?

Please say how confident you are in the principle you propose and single out the point about which you are least sure.
