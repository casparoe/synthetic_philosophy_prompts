Here's the question I posed: Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed III:32, offers an account of why the Torah commands animal sacrifice. Explain that account, and assess whether it is consistent with his claim elsewhere that the Torah is eternal and its commandments will never be changed. Then briefly consider how a critic such as Nahmanides might respond to Maimonides' account.

And here's her answer:

Maimonides argues in Guide III:32 that the laws of sacrifice were not given because God needs sacrifices or because they are intrinsically the best form of worship. Rather, they were a concession to the historical circumstances of the Israelites. At the time the Torah was given, the surrounding nations — especially the Sabians, who were star-worshippers — practiced animal sacrifice in their temples. The Israelites had grown up with this practice and were habituated to it. Maimonides says that it is impossible for a person to abandon suddenly all the practices he is used to, so God, in His wisdom, did not command the Israelites to give up sacrifice entirely. Instead, He transferred the practice from the idols to Himself: the same acts of slaughter, incense, and prostration that had been directed to the stars were now to be directed to God alone. In this way, the Torah weaned the Israelites away from idolatry without asking them to do something psychologically impossible. Maimonides describes this as a kind of divine accommodation — God meets human beings where they are in order to bring them to the truth. This shows that the commandments have reasons, and that even the sacrificial laws are rational in their purpose, contrary to those who say they are mere decrees without reason.

Is this consistent with Maimonides' claim that the Torah is eternal? I think there is a real tension here. If the sacrifices were only a concession to a particular historical situation, then it seems they should be temporary: once the Israelites had learned to worship God properly, the sacrifices would no longer be needed. And indeed, Maimonides seems to suggest in the Guide that in the Messianic age, the sacrificial laws will be abolished, since the reason for them — the need to accommodate a primitive people — will no longer apply. But this seems to conflict with his statement in the Mishneh Torah that the Torah will never be changed and that the Messiah will restore the sacrificial order. So Maimonides may have been inconsistent, or he may have held an esoteric view that he concealed from the masses.

Nahmanides would object that Maimonides has completely misunderstood the nature of sacrifice. For Nahmanides, sacrifices are not a concession to human weakness; they are intrinsically meaningful. When a person brings a sacrifice, he is symbolically offering himself: the animal takes his place, and the act of slaughter represents the death he deserves for his sin. The sweet savour that God smells is not a physical pleasure but a sign of divine acceptance. Nahmanides also points out that sacrifices predate the giving of the Torah — Abel and Noah brought offerings — so they cannot have been merely a response to the idolatrous practices of the Canaanites. Thus Maimonides' historical explanation is both philosophically and exegetically inadequate.
