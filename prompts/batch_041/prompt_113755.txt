I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of action, and I'm writing a chapter on what it would take for a theory of agency to be genuinely naturalistic — to treat an agent as one more part of the world rather than a special observer standing outside it. I'd like you to explain the central conceptual difficulty here in enough depth that I can see where the real work lies.

Start from the textbook decision-theoretic picture: an agent has a set of actions available to it, a set of possible states of the world, and a utility function, and it selects the action with the greatest expected utility. My worry is not that this is computationally demanding but that it presupposes a boundary — between agent and environment, between representation and represented fact, between the chosen action and the world's subsequent state — that an agent which is itself a proper part of the world it models cannot draw for itself without circularity. Explain why that is so, and be concrete about where the circularity actually bites rather than gesturing at it.

Then assess the following three-way breakdown, which I've been using as scaffolding and whose adequacy I want you to judge. First, the boundary problem: where does the agent end and the environment begin, and is there any non-arbitrary answer? Second, the ontology problem: the agent's actions and observations must be defined over some carving of the world into variables, but the choice of carving itself looks like an act of the agent, so it is unclear how the agent could be handed an action set in the first place. Third, the self-reference problem: the agent's decision is one of the facts about the world, so any model the agent uses to choose an action must already contain the outcome of that choice. As a concrete instance of the third, consider an agent that models the world in complete detail except for a single slot reserved for its own future action, to be filled in by its own deliberation: explain why filling that slot accurately is in tension with the deliberation doing any causal work.

For each of the three, say precisely what the difficulty is and why the obvious replies fail. Then give me your judgment on the scaffolding itself: are the three independent, or does each presuppose the others? My own suspicion is that the ontology problem is upstream of the other two — that you cannot even state the boundary problem or the self-reference problem until you have fixed a vocabulary in which agent, world, and action are well-defined terms — but I want the argument either way, and if you think the dependence runs in the opposite direction, show me.

Finally, what would a satisfactory resolution look like? Is the embedding problem the sort of thing that gets solved, dissolved, or exposed as a demand we should stop making? Concretely: what would it take for a formalism to count as having handled embeddedness rather than having assumed it away, and what would it be for a theory of agency to show that the Cartesian picture was a mistake from the start? I don't want a survey of proposals; I want the conceptual shape of an answer.
