I host a podcast on applied ethics and political economy, and next week's guest is an economist who spent years working inside the effective altruism community before becoming one of its most pointed internal critics. She defends a view she calls the attribution funnel thesis: the movement's deepest failure is not that it measures the wrong things, but that its funding pipeline selects for interventions whose effects can be credited to a particular funder's marginal dollar. On her account this selection pressure, rather than any explicit doctrine, is what quietly steers money away from political, structural, and long-horizon work — and the standard reply that effective altruists simply follow the evidence is circular, because the evidence base was itself shaped by the funnel. She holds two narrower views as well. First, that earning to give is defensible only under conditions almost no actual earner-to-giver satisfies: roughly, that the counterfactual use of one's working time would have been low-impact, and that the donated marginal dollar would not have been given by someone else in one's absence. Second, that the remedy is not to abandon quantification but to impose a counterfactual-robustness standard on donations, requiring a donor's theory of change to survive scrutiny under at least two independent causal models, together with a fixed share of every giving portfolio reserved for interventions whose impact cannot be measured on the donor's own metrics.

I want the interview to be genuinely adversarial rather than a cordial airing of grievances. Draft nine to eleven questions that go well beyond letting her restate the thesis.

Requirements for the set:
- At least one question on the attribution funnel mechanism itself: what evidence would distinguish it from the duller explanation that donors simply gravitate toward what can be counted, and how she would tell the two apart in a real funding history.
- At least one on her two conditions for defensible earning to give, pressing on why she thinks so few earners-to-give meet them and on whether the second condition (the marginal dollar would have been given anyway) doesn't prove too much and indict ordinary charitable giving generally.
- At least one on whether the counterfactual-robustness standard is a measurability fetish in disguise — or, worse, an invitation to motivated reasoning dressed up as epistemic humility.
- At least one on the reserved-share proposal: how large the share should be, who sets it, and why it isn't merely a hedge against her own acknowledged uncertainty.
- One question that forces her to state what would change her mind about the funnel thesis.
- One question that turns the critique on itself: whether the institutional critique functions, within the very institutions it indicts, as a career strategy with its own attribution funnel.

Sequencing requirement: the deflection that this is a measurement problem rather than an institutional one should become progressively harder to use. Construct at least three questions so that this reply is available for the first, strained for the second, and effectively unavailable for the third. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and make that follow-up do real work rather than merely asking her to elaborate.
