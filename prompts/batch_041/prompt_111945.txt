I teach a final-year seminar on early modern philosophy and its afterlife in political economy, and I have spent the past fortnight reading Berkeley's economic writings with my students — the series of queries he published in Dublin in three parts between 1735 and 1737 under the title The Querist, at first anonymously. The theses I want to put to work are these: that money is not a commodity with an intrinsic value but a ticket or counter (his own words: whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter"); that gold, silver and paper alike are counters for reckoning, recording and transferring claims to goods; that the denominations might well survive the disappearance of the bullion they name; that money is useful only so far as it stirs up industry and lets men share the fruits of one another's labour; and that the true source of wealth lies in the elements and human labour rather than in coin. He sketched a plan for a national bank and then quietly dropped the relevant queries from later editions, observing that it might be time enough to take the matter in hand when the public should seem disposed to make use of such an expedient. My colleague in the economics faculty finds the whole doctrine charming and dangerous in equal measure, and we agreed that the only honest way to test it is to make somebody inhabit it.

So I should like you to write the offer document for a bank founded on these principles: a prospectus, of the sort a real institution would put out to attract subscriptions, drafted in the flat and euphemistic register of a present-day offer document, as though a modern concern had revived the doctrine in earnest. Around 800 words, with headings and numbered clauses, opening with a short letter from the chairman and including a section stating plainly what the bank's notes are and what stands behind them, and a section of principal risks written in the usual deadpan manner of such documents. The humour must come from the doctrine rather than from any winking at the reader: the promoters are to be entirely sincere, and every claim they make should follow from the theses above, taken seriously and pursued without flinching. Let the objections land in the fine print rather than in the jokes — who decides how many tickets are to be issued and on what ground; what a ticket is worth when nobody will take it in exchange; what a claim on the labour of others amounts to when those others turn idle; what is to be said to the depositor who is offered a ticket where he expected a coin; and what the theory has to tell the man who hoards his tickets instead of returning them to circulation. You may close with a brief dissenting note from an invented subscriber, provided the objection is a real one rather than a straw man.

Keep the register dry and legible to an undergraduate who has never opened the Querist, while a specialist in eighteenth-century monetary thought ought to see that the author understands exactly what is being satirised and finds the satire fair. Do not put words into Berkeley's mouth: the only quotation you may use is the fragment I have given above, and no further quotations, titles or dates need appear. British English throughout, please.
