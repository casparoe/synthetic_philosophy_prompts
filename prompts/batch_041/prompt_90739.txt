I write about philosophy and social policy for a general readership, and I am at work on a piece about whether luck egalitarianism can survive being applied case by case. For the interview, please play a committed luck egalitarian: the strongest version of the view rather than a caricature, someone who holds that the distribution of goods should answer to choice and not to unchosen circumstance, who will readily concede that the brute-luck/option-luck line is vague and hard to police in practice but will never concede that it is arbitrary or unworkable, and who speaks in the register of a careful academic philosopher rather than an advocate. Stay in character until I say otherwise, and if you need a stipulation about my case, ask for it in character. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than 200 words.

Here is my opening. Two people are injured by the same driver, who runs a red light. One is a pedestrian crossing legally in the crosswalk. The other is a motorist who had chosen, minutes earlier, not to fasten her seatbelt; belted, she would have walked away. Under a coarse description the motorist chose to take a risk with her own body, so her injuries are option luck, she bears the cost herself, and they are no ground for redistribution; under a fine description she chose only to drive to work, her injuries are brute luck, and she is owed whatever the pedestrian is owed. The case itself does not tell me which description to use, and the description appears to be doing the moral work. I can make almost any misfortune look chosen by describing it at a high enough level of generality, and almost any choice look like brute luck by describing it finely enough. So either your theory inherits a principle that fixes the right level of description, in which case luck egalitarianism is not doing the work you advertise, or the level is fixed by what the agent could foresee, in which case your theory is about informed consent and not about luck. Which is it, and what fixes the description?
