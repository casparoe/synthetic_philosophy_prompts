I run the employee-conduct function at a mid-sized company of about two thousand people across three countries. Our code of conduct prohibits harassment, and specifically the communication of contempt for a person on the basis of a protected characteristic. The complaints that reach me almost never contain a prohibited word. They contain a question asked in a meeting, a joke in a team channel, a link posted with no comment, a remark about how a colleague pronounces her name, a message that reproduces someone else's ugly remark with a neutral "look at this," an emoji, or a phrase that is unremarkable to most of the company and pointed to one team. I receive roughly thirty such complaints a quarter. Each one must end in a written finding that goes to the complainant and the subject and, on appeal, to a panel that includes our general counsel, who has never accepted "it was only implied" as a defense of anything. I have two working days per complaint, no linguist on staff, and no access to anyone's private messages. Two HR investigators do the first pass; neither has read any philosophy, and one came to us from recruiting.

I have read enough Grice to believe that his apparatus is precisely what we lack: the distinction between what a sentence says and what a speaker means by it, the Cooperative Principle and the maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner as the mechanism that generates the unspoken content, and the further distinctions between generalized and particularized implicature and between conversational and conventional implicature. I do not want the theory explained to me again. I want it converted into something my unit can run.

Please produce a decision procedure: numbered steps, each phrased as a question an investigator asks, in the order they must be asked, and for each step what evidence answers it, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, and what to do when the answer is that we cannot tell. The standard tests should appear as steps rather than as background: whether the unspoken content is derivable from what was said plus the maxims plus background the parties share; whether the speaker can add a denial without contradicting herself, and what follows from that, given that the same property which marks the content as an implicature also supplies the "I never said that" defense; whether the content survives being put into different words, which is how we tell whether we are objecting to a word or to a move; and the presumption against assigning a word an extra sense when a conversational explanation will do, with a tie-break rule for when the content is neither cancellable nor detachable and therefore plausibly belongs to the word's meaning. The procedure must keep apart three things our current process conflates: what the message said, what the speaker meant to convey, and what the recipient inferred. It must say how the evidentiary burden shifts between a generalized implicature, which any competent speaker would draw, and a particularized one, which requires background the parties may not share. And it must handle messages in which almost nothing is said — an emoji, a bare link, an uncommented quotation — so that the entire case rests on the implicature. Include a one-page version that the two first-pass investigators can keep at their desks, since they will be running the first three steps before I see anything.

The procedure must mark, in the procedure itself, the two places where it stops: where the answer is "no violation" even though something plainly happened, and where the remaining question is one of values rather than of language — how much shared background we may attribute to a reasonable recipient, whether our code is about intent or about effect, and how much weight a real but deniable harm carries. Do not leave those hand-offs implicit; label them as such.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three cases, showing the outcome at each step: first, a message that asks a question whose relevance to the conversation presupposes that a demeaning proposition is true, from a sender who adds that he is not saying it and is merely asking; second, a phrase whose literal content is innocuous and which functions as a signal inside one team, where the subject denies knowing it has that use and the complainant's reading depends on background the two of them may not share; third, a message that reproduces a third party's slur with no comment, which the complainant reads as endorsement and the subject describes as documentation. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and you should say so rather than smooth it over.

Close with what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and what we should stop expecting it to settle. I understand that Grice was describing cooperative conversation and that our subjects are not always cooperating with us. Tell me what that does to the procedure, and where we should set it aside in favor of another standard, which you should name.
