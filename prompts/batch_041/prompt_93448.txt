I'm a graduate teaching assistant in a science-and-society program, and next week I have to carry a seventy-five-minute session on the tragedy of the commons for about thirty second-year students. They are a mixed bunch: the environmental-studies students arrive convinced they already know the topic, since to them it just means that people ruin shared things, while the economics students arrive convinced it is the prisoner's dilemma with grass. I have one whiteboard, no slides, and I want the session to leave them able to say precisely what makes a commons a commons and why the usual headline version of the idea is only half right. Don't explain the topic to me; plan how I should explain it.

Tell me what has to come first and what depends on what. I am fairly sure the rivalry/excludability grid has to be on the board before anything else, but I cannot decide whether to draw it before or after they have felt the pull of the dilemma, and I would like your reasoning for the order. Then the carrying example. I had planned to build the whole session on the dirty dishes in a group house, but I suspect that is really a free-rider story about a public good rather than a commons, and I would rather find out now. If the example has to turn on a genuinely rivalrous and hard-to-exclude resource, propose the one that carries best — a shared aquifer, a fishery, the atmosphere — and say what it must have and must not have; and tell me whether the textbook cattle pasture is the wrong choice for reasons that go beyond mere pedagogy.

Then the misconceptions, in the order these students will actually form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that the tragedy is about human greed rather than about structure; that a commons is the same thing as open access; that the only exits are privatization or state regulation; and that communities reliably solve the problem themselves as soon as one invokes local governance. I want the last third of the session to complicate the story honestly — that the historical English commons were in fact regulated rather than a free-for-all, and that self-governance is a finding about particular conditions rather than a slogan — without tipping over into the conclusion that the tragedy was never real. Tell me whether it is worth planting the mirror image, the anticommons, where too many separate owners each hold a veto, as a signpost they can chase later, or whether that is one idea too many at this level. Finish by telling me what to leave out and say so out loud, and give me one closing question that would show me whether they have grasped the concept rather than merely memorized the catchphrase.
