Write a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, that puts a character inside the paradox of deontology. Stated plainly, the paradox is this: a rule forbidding you to do a certain thing also forbids you to do it in the one case where doing it would prevent that same thing from being done many times over, so that keeping the rule appears to bring about more of precisely what the rule condemns. The story should place a character in that position and refuse to let the position collapse into a riddle with a trick answer.

Requirements:

1. The forbidden act must not be killing, harming, or lying, and the story must not be a variant of the trolley problem. Choose instead a rule recognizable from ordinary life — a promise, a confidence, a debt, a signature, a vow — and make its violation cost something concrete. Set the story somewhere specific and unglamorous: a workplace, a household, a ward, a crew.

2. The protagonist should have kept this rule for years without effort, so that what is at stake is what keeping it has cost rather than whether it can be kept.

3. Two characters must hold the same rule while locating the wrong in different places: one in what is done to the person on the receiving end of the violation, the other in what the one who breaks it becomes or forfeits. Include a moment where these two groundings require different things of the same person, so that the two characters disagree while each remains faithful to the rule.

4. A third character should press the minimizing question in its most sympathetic form — why not break it once, if breaking it once prevents many breaks? — and the reader should feel the full force of the question. Any answer must emerge from the pressure of events rather than from a speech.

5. At least one character should find that question not merely answered but malformed, as though it took for granted that the point of the rule is to keep a count down. Let the reader feel why the question might not land, without any character explaining that this is what is happening.

6. No one in the story should use the vocabulary of moral philosophy: no deontological, consequentialist, agent-relative, utility, or constraint, and no character naming the paradox as a paradox. The argument has to be carried by what people do, refuse, and notice. The piece should read as fiction, not as a thought experiment narrated from outside.

7. The ending should leave the paradox live: no punishment or reward that settles whether the refusal was integrity or complicity, and no revelation that the dilemma was fake.
