I have no training in philosophy or in military affairs, and I should say at the outset that I am not asking for a survey of what people have written about conscription — I have done enough reading on my own for that, and I would rather have one argument run properly. Here is the position I have arrived at, and I would like you to defend it as strongly as it can be defended. A state that conscripts is taxing its citizens in the one currency a state cannot print, namely their bodies, and I cannot see how such a tax is redeemed except by the defence of the place where those bodies live. So I take it that conscription is legitimate only when it comes with what I have come to call a territorial lock: a standing legal commitment that those who are conscripted will never be deployed beyond the borders of their own country, or beyond the immediate neighbourhood in a declared collective defence. Where the lock is missing — where a state wants a conscript army and also wants to fight in other people's wars — the position seems to me incoherent, and the honest conclusion is that a country which intends to project force abroad must buy its soldiers rather than take them. I have a Finnish colleague who did his service and describes it not as a duty owed to the state but as a tax paid in the one currency the state cannot print, which is why it feels unlike every other tax; and a Swiss friend who insists the deterrent was never the army so much as the knowledge that an occupier would have to work through every village in the country, one kitchen at a time. Those two pictures are what persuaded me, and I would like the argument built on them rather than on abstractions about the social contract. It matters to me practically, because there is talk again here in Britain of some form of national service, and we are precisely the sort of country that does fight abroad.

The obvious trouble is that Finland has just joined NATO, which by my own logic ought to have been the beginning of the end for Finnish conscription, and it plainly has not been. Sweden is the other awkward case: it let conscription lapse, brought it back, and has now given up two centuries of non-alignment by joining the alliance. So please tell me whether the territorial lock can be made consistent with alliance membership, or whether my position really does imply that a conscript state must stay out of alliances altogether — which I concede sounds reckless. Beyond that, I want the standard objections met rather than dodged: that a conscript army is simply a worse army, so the moral question is idle; that the real scandal is not neutrality but fairness, since the burden falls on the young and disproportionately on the poor while the people who decide on wars are exempt, and since in both Switzerland and Finland the obligation falls on men alone, however willing women may be to serve; and that conscientious objection with civilian alternative service is either the escape hatch that makes conscription respectable or the admission that the state never needed these particular bodies at all. Finally, tell me where I am wrong and what the position would have to give up in order to survive — in particular, whether it can survive the thought that staying out of a war between others is itself a way of taking sides.
