I have been trying to work out, case by case and without cheating, what I actually think about material constitution, and I have reached the point where I need someone to tell me what my verdicts add up to. The argument began in a friend's ceramics studio, over the question of how many things were on the bench: a lump of clay that had been sitting there since Monday, and the bowl it was thrown into on Thursday. Here is what I find myself wanting to say. The lump and the bowl are two things, not one. The lump was on the bench before the bowl existed; if I break the unfired bowl up and soak the pieces, I get my lump back — the same clay, the same lump, so far as I can see — but not the same bowl; and if instead the bowl goes into the kiln, the bowl comes out the other side, still the same bowl, while the clay does not, since what comes out is earthenware and not clay. But I also think there is only one material object on that bench. I have never been able to believe in coincident objects: I do not think there is a chair in the next room over and above the wood and the screws, and I do not think the bowl is a second thing lying in the same place as the clay. That pair of verdicts is, I take it, the problem. It gets worse, because I also think the bowl is nothing over and above its matter — no extra ingredient, no ghostly bowl-stuff, only the clay arranged bowlwise — and I am nevertheless not prepared to say that the bowl simply is the clay.

Some further verdicts, less central but I think they belong to the pattern. If the bowl is smashed and I later glue the fragments back together, the thing I end up with is the very same bowl and not a replica of it; but at the moment of the smashing, I want to say, the bowl was destroyed. If I pinch a piece of clay off the lump, the lump survives, only smaller; the bowl, if the pinch comes from its rim, is damaged but survives as well. A lump of clay that has never been shaped but happens to look like a horse is not a statue; a lump that was shaped in order to look like a horse, and then abandoned, is a statue, though a bad one. A piece of driftwood mounted on a plinth in a gallery is a sculpture; the same piece of wood floating in the sea is not. And a plaster cast taken from the bowl is a new object, a copy, rather than the bowl itself, even if the bowl is destroyed in the taking.

What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets these verdicts right, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work, together with an honest statement of the verdicts it cannot accommodate: I would sooner give up a verdict than keep a theory held together with tape. Please apply it to at least two cases I have not mentioned, one of which should involve living matter — a hedge clipped into a shape, or a bonsai trained over decades, will do — and say what it implies for the question I keep going back and forth on, namely whether the throwing of the bowl brought a new object into being or only made a new description true of an old one. Where two of my verdicts cannot both be kept, tell me which one a competent defender of the principle would surrender, and why. There is no need for references; I can place the positions myself, and I am after the principle rather than a survey of what others have said.
