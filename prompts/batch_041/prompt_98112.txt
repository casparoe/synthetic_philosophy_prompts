I'm writing a short essay for a reading group and I keep tying myself in knots over whether akrasia and hyperbolic discounting are even the same phenomenon. The standard story is that present bias — the fact that we discount the near future much more steeply than the far future, so preferences reverse as the deadline rolls closer — is just the economists' name for what philosophers call weakness of will: you judge that the diet (or the early night, or the saving) is best, and then at the moment of temptation you do otherwise. But I've started to think that's a category error. If your discount curve is genuinely hyperbolic, then your later self doesn't act against its own all-things-considered judgement — it just has a different one, so there's no weakness anywhere, only a preference change. And once you add the sophisticated/naive distinction (the agent who knows their future self will cave and so buys a commitment device versus the one who doesn't), it looks like present bias models explain how we manage akrasia rather than what it is. Am I right that the two come apart, or is there a way of reading them together so that hyperbolic discounting really does illuminate the old puzzle of how acting against your own best judgement is even possible? Write me an essay that takes a side. Dont just survey the literature — I want a verdict.

Structure it like this: a section called "The puzzle" (~150 words) setting out why akrasia looks impossible and what the discounting story is meant to do about it; then "Where they seem to meet" (~250 words) making the best case that present bias just is weakness of will; then "Where they come apart" (~350 words) on the synchronic/diachronic gap, the role of second-order desires, and what sophistication does to the picture; and finally "My verdict" (~250 words) with a clear position, the strongest objection to it, and your reply.
