For my school's philosophy club I'm presenting the finding that people are far more willing to call a side effect of someone's action intentional when they disapprove of that side effect than when they approve of it. I want to run a fresh pair of cases rather than reuse the example everyone has already seen, so design the pair for me. Specifications: the two cases must differ in exactly one respect -- whether the side effect is good or bad -- with the action, the agent's knowledge, the agent's indifference to the side effect, and the certainty of the outcome all held fixed; the side effect must carry no moral charge in either direction, so that no critic can say one agent is simply more blameworthy, which means the value at stake has to be aesthetic or prudential; and both must be ordinary school or workplace situations, each readable aloud in under twenty seconds. Add a third case in which I expect the asymmetry to shrink or vanish, and explain why that case is the right test of the underlying mechanism. Then, for each case, give the verdict predicted by the explanation that treats the asymmetry as a blame-driven bias and by the explanation that treats it as reflecting an evaluative element in the very concept of intentional action; tell me which single feature of my design is doing the work, what happens if I remove it, and how a defender of the blame-based explanation would attack the pair. The point of the exercise is to show my classmates that the asymmetry isn't only about blame, so a pair that wins by making one agent look worse than the other would be cheating.
