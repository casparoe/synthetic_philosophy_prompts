I am a novelist, and I have spent the past year trying to build a thriller around a decision problem that I can describe but cannot name. My antagonist is a blackmailer who never makes a demand without having first made a prediction. The demand is always the same: send money, and I will give you the key to the file I have encrypted; refuse, and I will destroy the key. The peculiar part is the selection. He encrypts a person's file only if he has already predicted that the person will pay. So if you pay, you were selected, and the key is real. If you refuse, the likeliest explanation is that he never encrypted your file at all; there is no key to lose. My protagonist, advised by a game theorist, is told to refuse, because refusal is evidence that the threat was never active. But she cannot shake the thought that her refusal is safe only because the blackmailer predicted it, and that if she were the sort of person who would pay, she would be in the world where refusal is costly. The blackmailer insists he is not punishing refusal; he is simply giving the key to those who pay, and he chose his targets by looking at what they would do.

I have a lay interest in decision theory, and I recognize the neighborhood. I know the puzzle about a predictor and two boxes, and the one where a predictor rewards you in a branch that did not occur if you would have paid in the branch that did. But this structure seems different in at least two ways. The predictor's act is a threat rather than a reward, and the threat is conditional on your action in the very world in which you are deciding, not only in a branch that did not happen. I also suspect that the name, if there is one, is not the point. What I need is the carving. Is there an established term for this exact configuration? If so, what is it, and what does it include that ordinary blackmail, the two-box puzzle, and the counterfactual-reward puzzle do not? If there is no established term, can you coin one and define it precisely enough that I can tell whether a given plot is an instance of it? In particular, I want to know whether the structure changes when the blackmailer's prediction is imperfect, and whether the protagonist's decision should depend on the base rate of people who pay. I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list. I want the concept, its boundaries, and the strongest reason someone might say the protagonist should pay.
