In a graduate seminar on epistemic rationality, a colleague summarized the lottery and preface paradoxes with the remark “Consistency is a requirement on truth, not on belief.” She then used that line to argue that neither paradox shows anything troubling: the lottery is just a case of high probability without outright belief, and the preface is just a case of fallible belief about one’s own fallibility. I want a precise inventory of what that one sentence could be claiming, because I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them would license her conclusion.

Please lay out the possible readings as a menu, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say what precise thesis it commits one to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something false or incoherent, and whether it would actually support the dismissal of the lottery paradox, the preface paradox, both, or neither. Distinguish at least the following kinds of claim: that consistency is a property of propositions or truth rather than a psychological property of believers; that consistency is a norm on belief but not a requirement of rationality; that consistency is a requirement on ideal or fully rational belief but not on human belief; that consistency is a requirement on credences but not on binary or outright belief; that consistency is a requirement on the content of a belief set but not on the act or process of forming beliefs; that rational belief is not closed under conjunction; that fallibilism plus conjunctive closure is the real source of the preface paradox; and that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox require different treatments because one concerns beliefs about a partition and the other concerns beliefs about one’s own fallibility.

Then tell me which reading my colleague most likely meant, and which of the readings would genuinely dissolve the paradoxes rather than merely relabel them. I am especially interested in whether the slogan has different force in the two cases. Suppose we shrink the lottery to two tickets, or shrink the preface to two claims each held with probability 0.9, or add a preface containing only one substantive claim plus a second-order claim that the author is fallible. Does the slogan still do the same work, or does one of the paradoxes reappear under a different description? Finally, say what the slogan would have to mean in order to be a serious philosophical thesis rather than a truism about the word “consistency,” and identify the reading that a careful defender of the paradoxes should most worry about.
