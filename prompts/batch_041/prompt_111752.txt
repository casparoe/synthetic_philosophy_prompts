ok heres one I keep chewing on and I want a proper essay out of it, not a survey. Pain asymbolia. The short version as I understand it: someone still feels pain — they'll say yes, that hurts, they'll give you a number, they can tell you where it is — but it doesn't bother them. They don't suffer from it. The case reports make them sound almost indifferent to a sensation that would have me climbing the walls. So the question: what does that actually tell us about why pain is bad?

Don't do a lit review, don't cite anyone, I don't care who said what — I want the argument. Heres the shape I think it has to take. "Pain is bad" can mean at least three or four pretty different things, and I want you to pull them apart before you do anything else: (1) the sensation is intrinsically unpleasent — the hurtiness itself is the bad thing; (2) pain is bad for you because of what it does or tells you — it signals tissue damage, ruins your sleep, makes you stop using the hand; (3) pain is bad in the reason-giving sense — it gives the person a reason to stop and gives other people a reason to help; (4) pain is bad because it's imperative, it's a "get out of this now" directed at you. Figure out which of those asymbolia actually bears on, which it leaves untouched, and then defend one.

Two cases I want you to run it on. Both hypothetical, I made them up:

- A patient rates the pain 8/10, is completely unbothered by it, but if you leave the room they still pull their hand off the hotplate and nurse the burn. Are they suffering? Is anything bad happening to them at all?
- The inverse: someone who reports no pain whatsoever, but groans, guards the limb, avoids the stimulus, begs you to make it stop. Suffering or not?

Then the ordinary case, which I think is actually the hardest: the runner, the ascetic, the masochist — pain that is unpleasant, unmissably so, and endorsed. If endorsement can stop pain from being bad for you, does that show the unpleasantness was never where the badness lived?

Then land it. Commit to a view about what the badness of pain consists in and defend it against the best objection — including the objection that asymbolia is just a general deficit in caring about anything, or that these patients are saying the words without meaning them, or that we're reading too much into old case reports. If the honest answer is that asymbolia shows less than everyone wants it to, fine, say that, and say what it does show. No "it's complicated" ending, no both-sides-have-a-point, no "pain is multidimensional" hand-wave. I want a verdict and the reasons for it.

And say what follows if you're right: for how pain should be defined, for whether a patient who says it hurts but he doesn't care needs painkillers or a psychiatrist, and for how much weight self-report should get in the clinic at all.
