Right, so I edit a small quarterly called The Long View — Annales-school history, longue durée stuff, the kind of magazine where noone's allowed to mention a battle in the first thousand words. Every December the owner makes us run a Year in Review special, and its always the worst thing we publish: ten historians pretending the last twelve months were a story with a beginning and an end.

This year I want to do it as a joke. Write me the Year in Review 2025 as our most sincere contributor would write it — a Braudelian who genuinely believes l'histoire événementielle is a moral failing and has said so at two weddings. Headline plus 500-600 words.

The rules he'd actually keep, and which you should keep too, deadpan, no winking:

- no named individuals, no dated events, no elections, no wars-as-spectacle, nothing that resolves inside a century
- everything has to come out of the structures underneath and the conjoncture — the mid-length cycles, prices, harvests, demography, the stuff that turns over every few decades
- the news cycle isn't a story to him, it's a price series, so he should genuinely try to plot it across about three centuries and be mildly annoyed that the resolution is too poor to say anything
- he's been trying to get a term he invented into print for years — "the thin conjuncture," meaning the attention economy treated as a price series — and he uses it here as though everyone already knows it
- he must never admit that his framework can't say anything about what just happened, or that it quietly writes human agency out of the story; a careful reader should feel both of those, and he shouldn't notice

The target is as much his habit of mind as the magazine's event-addiction. Then finish with a 100-word letter to the editor from our resident microhistorian, saying the piece explains everything and therefore nothing, and that he's cancelling his subscription for the third year running.
