I'm a policy analyst in the stats office of a small country, and our new minister has decided that within ten years we're going to be top ten in the World Happiness Report. She wants me to write the strategy memo. Heres my problem. The headline number in that report is basically the average of answers to one question -- where would you put your life on a ladder, worst possible to best possible -- asked once a year to a sample of people. Its not how good last Tuesday actually felt. It's how people judge their life is going, against whatever they personally think the best possible life looks like. And then the report explains that number with a handful of things -- income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and how corrupt people think their country is -- and the minister is treating that list like a checklist to pull on.

Two worries I can't shake. First, one of those checklist items, how corrupt people think the country is, is itself just another survey answer about how people feel, so it's a bit odd to treat it as a lever you can go and pull. Second, the minister's plan also includes a national kindness-and-gratitude campaign, and that one feels less like making anyone's life better and more like nudging how they answer the ladder question. A colleague says it's worse than that -- he swears there's research showing people who move from poor countries to rich ones often report lower life satisfaction because their reference point shifts, so climbing the ladder nationally might partly be about managing what people compare themselves to.

Can you lay out the most important implications of all this -- of how the ranking is built and of what it's actually measuring -- for whether a country should try to climb it at all? I want the ones that genuinely bear on the decision, including the ones that undercut the whole exercise. And tell me what would change your mind, i.e. what evidence or argument would make you go fine, chasing the ranking is a reasonable thing for a government to do -- and where you think your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
