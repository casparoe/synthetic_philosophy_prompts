I'm running the next session of a reading group that started life as a LessWrong meetup and has since drifted into "we all read the same blogs" territory, and next month's topic is acausal trade — ninety minutes, whiteboard, maybe twelve people. The room is split in a way that bugs me: half of them have absorbed the phrase as a shibboleth and nod along whenever it comes up, and the other half are pretty sure its just confused mysticism about backwards causation. Don't explain acausal trade to me, I don't need the explanation, I need the lesson plan: what has to be in place before the question even parses for them and in what order (what depends on what), what the one example is that carries the whole thing, which misconceptions they'll bring or form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, one question I can put on the board at the end to see whether the idea actually landed, and what I should openly say I'm skipping at this level.

Two things I want the plan built around, because I think they're where the standard telling goes wrong. First, every intro I've seen teaches this with the copy/twin case — one-shot prisoner's dilemma, superrationality, both cooperate, done — and that only ever shows cooperation; it never shows trade. Trade needs the two sides to want different things, each doing something for the other that they'd never do for themselves, and I want the carrying example to make the exchange the point rather than the agreement. (My current sketch is a lighthouse keeper and a monk on islands with no contact, each maintaining something the other values, but it keeps collapsing into a precommitment story, which is exactly the thing I don't want them walking out with.) Second, I've been chewing on the thought that what does the real work isn't identical minds but correlated reasoning — the other party has to be able to predict your disposition from the same considerations that lead you to it, which is weaker than "is a copy of you" and a lot less spooky; if that distinction is wrong, or is load-bearing in the wrong place, tell me, because I'd rather the session be right than vindicate my framing.

The misconceptions I'm expecting, roughly in the order they'll show up: that this requires retrocausation; that it only works between literal copies; that it's just precommitment or Newcomb in a costume; and then the one that worries me most, "so I should be trading with every conceivable agent," i.e. the Pascal's-mugging version where they conclude they owe something to everybody. Also, I don't want to burn twenty minutes on the proof-based cooperation tricks or on how you'd count the branches — say what to cut and why, and give me the end-of-session question that would show they've got the load-bearing piece rather than just the vocabulary.
