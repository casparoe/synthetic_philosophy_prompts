A friend in a writing seminar recently summarized Douglas Hofstadter's A Person Paper on Purity in Language for me, and I want to know how much of her account I should trust. She summarized it as follows: Hofstadter writes in the voice of a conservative language columnist—the name is a play on a famous usage guru—who complains that feminists are ruining English by insisting on chairperson and he or she, and who insists that chairman and mankind are perfectly generic and that changing them is pointless. The essay's real point, she says, is that these words are not generic at all: they carry a male default, and the columnist's defenses only sound reasonable because we are used to that default. To expose it, Hofstadter transposes the whole debate into a racial key, imagining a society with a parallel vocabulary in which the racial terms function the way man and woman do in ours, so that the columnist's arguments become obviously absurd and offensive. She concludes that the essay is a reductio of anyone who defends gendered generics, that it proves sexist language is a form of violence, that Hofstadter himself is committed to the strong claim that changing words is both necessary and sufficient for changing attitudes, and that the piece is essentially a brief for politically correct speech codes. I have read enough to suspect that the essay is more interested in how default assumptions hide inside ordinary words than in prescribing anyone's speech, but I do not know it well enough to say where her summary goes wrong. Please audit it. Go claim by claim: what would a careful reader of Hofstadter accept as accurate, what is a common simplification that a specialist would wince at, and what is simply a misreading? In particular, I want to know whether the satirical persona is being confused with Hofstadter's own voice, whether the racial transposition is being described in a way that captures what the analogy is doing, and whether the conclusions my friend draws—about violence, about the sufficiency of changing words, and about speech codes—follow from anything the essay actually establishes. Where the summary overstates or misattributes, say what a more defensible version of the point would be.
