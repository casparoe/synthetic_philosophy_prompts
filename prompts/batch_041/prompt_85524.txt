I teach a ninety-minute session on cooperative bargaining in a mid-level seminar on rational choice and welfare economics — sixteen students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, a few with a semester of game theory behind them and a few with none. My difficulty is that the standard axiomatic material goes down too easily. Nash's conditions all sound platitudinous when taken one at a time: that the parties' names should not matter, that rescaling anyone's utility function should not change the agreement, that the agreement should be efficient, and that if a new option becomes available and is not itself chosen, its mere availability should not disturb an agreement that was already on the table. Kalai and Smorodinsky's alternative sounds equally platitudinous: drop that last condition and require instead that when one party's prospects improve at every level of the other party's payoff, the first party should not end up with less. Students assent to both lists, then cannot see why the two solutions differ or why anyone should regard the choice between them as forced. I want a session in which they bargain under two conditions, compare what they did, and are then made to legislate a single rule for all future cases — at which point the two attractive conditions collide in their own hands, before either solution is named.

Design that session for me. I picture it in three movements. In the first, the students bargain in pairs over a physical representation of a bargaining problem — a table or a spread of cards giving the feasible payoff pairs, plus an explicitly stated disagreement point they receive if no agreement is reached within a short deadline; the payoffs are points, redeemable at the end for something small and non-monetary, such as first choice of the snack at the final meeting or exemption from one short response paper, and no real money changes hands. Half the pairs receive a base feasible set; the other half receive a superset of it that shares the same disagreement point and adds options that raise one designated player's best attainable payoff without raising the other's. In the second movement the two conditions are put side by side and the class compares the splits reached in each, with the pairs who had the larger set and the pairs who had the smaller set confronting each other directly: the player whose options improved will feel entitled to more, and the other player will insist that since the added options were not chosen they should change nothing. In the third movement the whole group becomes a constitutional convention and must write down, before seeing any further cases, one rule that will be applied mechanically to any bargaining problem whatsoever; I then test their rule against a prepared battery, including a relabeling of the parties, a mirror-image problem, a case in which an added option is not selected so that the previously chosen point is still available, and a case in which one party's prospects improve at every level of the other's payoff. The rule they wrote will almost certainly fail one of these, and I want them to locate the failure themselves.

Give me the rules, the materials, the exact structure of the two bargaining conditions (including the shape the expansion must take for the monotonicity condition to bite, and a case in which the added options are irrelevant in the relevant sense, so that a rule honouring independence cannot move while a rule honouring monotonicity must), a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money. Then give me the debrief: it should map specific moments from the activity onto the independence condition and the monotonicity condition, show why Nash's solution keeps its answer fixed across the base and expanded problems while the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution moves along the ray from the disagreement point toward the ideal point, and explain that the two conditions are each independently compelling and that honouring one costs you the other. Name neither solution until the debrief. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — pairs collapsing onto a fifty-fifty norm, a dominant personality dictating the split, some pairs failing to agree and taking the disagreement payoff, the convention deadlocking or writing an ad hoc rule with exceptions — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the generality of the axioms beyond the handful of cases they see, the convexity and randomization assumptions that make the feasible set an interval, the fact that the axiomatic approach is a normative exercise rather than a prediction about how people actually bargain, and the precise sense in which the incompatibility of the two conditions is a theorem rather than an artifact of the cases I chose — all of which I will have to supply myself.
