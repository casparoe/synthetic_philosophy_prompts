I'm a novelist assembling a collection of stories that each dramatize a single puzzle in the philosophy of action, and I'd like your help with one of them. Take on the role of a writer who is also well versed in the free-will and moral-responsibility literature, and think carefully before you begin: first give me a short plan — a paragraph naming the puzzle your story will isolate and the narrative choices that will keep that puzzle in view — and only then write the story.

The situation I want the story to grow from is this. In an invented city, a junior official signs the order authorizing the demolition of a district to make way for a flood barrier. She signs on her own initiative, convinced it is right, and she does not hesitate. What she does not know is that her superior, who wanted the same outcome but feared she might refuse, had quietly arranged a contingency: had she balked, a forged directive would have surfaced compelling her signature, and she would have signed under a threat she would afterward have despised. The contingency never fires. Make the superior neither a villain nor a cipher — she shares the official's conviction and regards her arrangement as a safeguard rather than a manipulation.

I want the story to make the reader feel, at once, the pull of the thought that the official is fully responsible for what happens, and the pull of the thought that since the outcome would have occurred regardless, her choice somehow does not count — and to complicate both rather than settle them. Let the reader end unsure whether it matters that the contingency never fired, and whether it would have mattered differently had it fired.

A few constraints: no character should state the lesson or use the vocabulary of the debate; let the situation carry the argument. Avoid making the guarantee feel contrived — earn it with ordinary bureaucratic detail. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
