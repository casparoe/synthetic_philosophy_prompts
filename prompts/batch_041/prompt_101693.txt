I'm writing a novel whose central scene is an all-night argument between two characters — one a moral philosopher, the other a historian of cultures — over whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion and whether a society can deliberately choose between them. Write their dialogue, and make it a real contest rather than a tour of positions. Let them fix on the distinction I care about: that shame answers to an audience, real or imagined, while guilt answers to a norm the agent has internalized — and let them draw out the consequence, that shame's audience can be plural, which makes it adaptable and democratic, whereas guilt's law tends toward the singular, which can make it either universal or tyrannical. Have them test both views against a single concrete case — a person who has done something genuinely wrong that no one will ever discover — so that each can use the case to expose what the other's emotion cannot reach. Then let them turn to the question the argument really turns on: whether a culture can choose its dominant moral emotion at all, or only the practices by which that emotion is displayed and repaired — confession and restitution on one side, concealment and the recovery of standing on the other. Neither should be a strawman; each should concede the other's strongest point and then regroup. Keep the register of two sharp people talking late at night, not lecturing.
