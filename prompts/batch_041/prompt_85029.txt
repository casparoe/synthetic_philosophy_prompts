I have been reading around this corner of the internet for years, and I want to pin down the target before I read any more proposals for a heuristic estimator. Let me state the setup so that we are working from the same thing. A heuristic estimator is a device that takes a formal question, together with a set of premises, and returns an estimate — a real number, or a probability — meant to capture what a competent reasoner would guess on the basis of those premises alone, without a proof. What it is meant to formalize is a kind of defeasible reasoning whose central move is the presumption of independence: in the absence of any specific information about how two quantities are correlated, default to treating them as independent — estimate the expectation of a product as the product of the expectations — and revise that default only when the premises give you a reason to. The reasoning is intuitively compelling, widely used, and often accurate, and it is entirely informal; the project is to formalize it without losing what made it work.

I do not want another candidate. I want the desiderata: what any adequate formalization of this reasoning has to deliver, each item with the case or consideration behind it. Sort them into four groups. First, the cases it must get right — the particular applications of the presumption that any competent reasoner would endorse and that the estimator therefore has to reproduce. Second, the spurious arguments it must reject — the arguments that superficially resemble valid applications of the presumption but that would let the estimator be gamed or would let it prove too much, including the self-referential and diagonal cases in which the premises or the question concern the estimator's own output, so that it has to reason about itself. Third, the distinctions it must respect — proof versus estimate, the estimator's output versus a probability, a defeasible default versus an assumption, and, a distinction I suspect proposals blur, treating two quantities as independent because the premises are silent about their correlation versus treating them as independent because the premises show the situation to be symmetric in the relevant respect. Fourth, the results it must be consistent with — agreement with ordinary probability theory and with deductive logic wherever those are defined, and consistency with the behaviour of the proposals already on the table.

Then tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp, and where two of them pull against each other. My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that a single requirement does most of the excluding work — the demand that the estimator reject spurious arguments — and that it stands in tension with the demand that it reproduce every intuitively valid application of the presumption, so that the interesting question is not how to satisfy the whole list but whether the list, as usually stated, is jointly unsatisfiable. If some of the existing candidates can be scored against the list in a sentence or two each, do that at the end; but keep the weight on the criteria.
