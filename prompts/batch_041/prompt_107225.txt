Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I'm a retired geologist — thirty-odd years on mud and ice cores — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy and annoying my old colleagues about it. I've got no internet on my end worth the name, so please don't send me off to a reading list or a bibliography; I want you to reason it out from scratch.

Take the role of a philosopher who works on population ethics and on why future generations matter. Think carefully before you write — reason it through properly, and open with a short brainstorm so I can see the working.

I've been chewing on Samuel Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* (the Tanner lectures, published 2013). What grabs me is his "afterlife conjecture" — the guess that a good deal of what we value in our own lives leans on our confidence that human life will simply keep going after we're dead, what he calls the collective afterlife. I want a thought experiment built to order, so I can see exactly how much weight that idea can carry and nothing else. I'm a scientist by training, so what I want is a controlled comparison: one variable, everything else pinned down.

Build me a pair of scenarios that differ in exactly one respect — whether human life continues after the present generation. Everything else must be identical. Then, and this is the part I care about, prove to me that the case actually meets each of these constraints:

- The deaths are identical in both scenarios: same people, same moment, painless, no warning, and nobody left behind to grieve.
- Nobody's welfare differs between the two. The present generation is the same in both. For the people who would live later in the survival scenario, stipulate that their lives net out to zero welfare — as much misery as joy, and nobody who'd rather not have been born. I know that's a heavy assumption; treat it as a limiting case, and tell me flat out if you think it's incoherent.
- No appeals to the intrinsic value of humanity, of the species, of consciousness, or of nature as such.
- Realistic. No aliens, no gods, no simulations, no time travel. An ordinary sterilising event is fine.
- Nothing hinges on anyone's feelings about the news. Nobody learns in advance, nobody mourns.

After the scenarios, walk through what each of four positions says about the comparison: (1) a narrow person-affecting view, where an outcome can only be worse if it's worse for some particular person; (2) an impersonal totalist view that just adds up welfare; (3) Scheffler's conjecture; (4) a flat skeptic who says extinction adds nothing over and above the deaths themselves. Say which of them can call the extinction scenario worse, and by what mechanism each gets there.

Then finish the job: tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if I pull it out — do the two scenarios collapse into each other? Then give me the skeptic's best counterattack: how does somebody who denies the conjecture attack the case itself rather than just the intuition? And be honest if you think I've rigged it.
