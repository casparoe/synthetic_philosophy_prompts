I have no academic training in philosophy. I work in local government, I read a great deal, and for the past few years I have been arguing about the problem of dirty hands in politics with friends and strangers online, without ever managing to pin down exactly what the people I disagree with are committed to. I would like you to act as a philosopher who specialises in moral and political philosophy and who is unusually good at mapping the logical space of a dispute, rather than as an encyclopedia. Think carefully before you write: begin by brainstorming in private the questions on which people who disagree about this actually come apart, test each candidate question against the case of someone who has never heard any of the standard labels, and discard any question that merely restates a doctrine or that could be answered by ticking a box marked consequentialist. Only then produce the finished instrument.

What I want is a decision tree, laid out as a taxonomy, that takes a reader from their answers to concrete commitments and lands them on a named position, together with the pattern of answers that produces each landing place. Build the tree from the space of possible answers first, and only afterwards put views into the slots; do not begin from a list of famous thinkers and sort them, and do not let the tree become a list of labels with questions bolted on. Work at the following size and shape: five to seven splitting questions, each one a concrete case or a plain claim that a person can answer without knowing where the answer leads, arranged in an order that keeps the reader oriented; and eight to twelve leaves, each carrying a plain-English name for the position, one sentence stating what it holds, the pattern of answers that gets you there, one concrete case that makes it vivid, and the commitment its holder would find hardest to give up. Every split should state the available answers and say what turns on the choice between them.

I would like the tree to be able to reach at least the following views, which I have put in my own words because I do not know their proper names: that there is no dirty hands problem at all, because the right act is simply the one with the best outcome; that the politician must do the wrong and must be marked by it, so that guilt is part of the job rather than evidence of a mistake; that politics runs on its own code and the ordinary moral vocabulary does not apply to it; that the office authorises the wrong and the person is not really its author; that the whole notion is self-indulgent, since the politician's remorse is a luxury that centres him rather than the people he hurt; and that the conflict is genuine but admits of no general answer, only particular judgements. Anything else you find in the space is welcome.

I have also been thinking about this long enough to have my own axes, and I want the tree to accommodate them even if it ends up showing that some of them are really the same axis, or that one of them is not a genuine question. First, permission versus requirement. When a wrong really is the only route to a large good, does the politician merely have permission to do it, or is refusing itself a failure, so that the person who refuses is not a better person but a worse one? Second, the receipt. Some wrongs only work if they are never admitted, and admitting them later destroys the good they bought; I want to know whether a wrong that can never be acknowledged in public can still count as justified, and, if it can, who is entitled to do the forgiving — the people who were wronged, the public, or nobody at all. Third, borrowed wrongdoing versus spent wrongdoing. Is the wrong done in the name of the office, so that the office carries it and the person is only a placeholder, or is it done in the person's own name, so that no office can absorb it and resignation or retirement cannot pay it off? Fourth, the remainder. After the right decision has been made, is anything left over — a debt, a stain, a need for atonement — and if so, whose is it: the person's, the office's, or the country's? Fifth, the clock. Does it matter that the necessity is usually visible only in hindsight, and that the person claiming necessity is also the person who benefits from the claim being believed?

Use cases rather than abstractions wherever a case will do. Invent them if you like, or use plain ones of the kind that get argued about: the mayor who lets a plant open because refusing costs the town its jobs and a handful of residents their health; the minister who lies to a legislature to keep a negotiation alive; the official who breaks an oath and leaks a document to stop a policy that will kill people; the interrogator who does the forbidden thing to someone who turns out to know nothing. None of this should require a reading list, and please do not give me one: I cannot check citations, and a bibliography is useless to me. Do not put years, titles, or names of scholars into the tree as load-bearing parts of it. If a position has a familiar label, give the label in brackets after a plain description, but the tree has to be fully usable by someone who ignores every bracket. The same goes for technical vocabulary: if a view needs a technical term, define it in a sentence of ordinary words, because I intend to run this past a discussion group of about a dozen people, half of whom have never read any philosophy.

Three things I want at the end, and I would rather have them honestly than neatly. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say why the questions fail to distinguish them. List the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and say for each whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but repellent, or merely because nobody has bothered to defend it. And close with a short note on where you are unsure, including which of my five axes you think is not really an axis, and which question you think I should have asked instead of one of the ones above.
