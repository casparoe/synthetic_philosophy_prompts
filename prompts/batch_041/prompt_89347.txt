I run a tiny nonprofit — we do a free after-school homework club out of a church basement, four volunteers, about thirty kids — and last year our funder asked us to start reporting a number every quarter, so we picked "average number of sessions attended per kid," because obviously we want kids to keep coming back. Within two months we had kids showing up, signing the sheet, and leaving after ten minutes, and one volunteer started basically bribing them with snacks to sign in. The number went up. The thing we actually cared about went down. A friend of mine who reads alot of economics blogs told me this is just Goodhart's law, that whenever a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, and that theres nothing to be done about it, like it's a law of nature for organizations.

I'm not sure I buy that, and I'd like you to write me an essay that settles it. Here's the part I keep getting stuck on. It seems to me there are two different failures getting lumped together. One is where the number drifts away from the thing you wanted — the sign-in sheet stops telling you anything about whether the kids are actually being helped. The other is where the number is still perfectly accurate but it has quietly become the thing you're optimizing, so "getting kids to show up" has replaced "helping kids" and nobody notices the swap happened. I don't know if that distinction is real or if I'm just making it up, but it feels like it matters for whether the problem is fixable. So what I want is: is Goodhart's law an inevitable law, some kind of iron rule that any target will eventually rot, or is it a design failure that a careful enough organization can dodge, maybe by using lots of measures, or rotating them, or just refusing to tie money to them? Give me the best case for each side and then actually commit to a verdict — don't leave me with "it depends on the context," I want to know what you think is true.

One request about how you write it: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write a long email to somebody. And please don't tell me to go read the literature or point me at books — I can't look anything up right now, I just want the argument itself.
