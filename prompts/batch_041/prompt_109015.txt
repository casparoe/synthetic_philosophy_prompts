I am a review engineer at a mid-sized structural engineering firm, and I am writing for advice about a situation I have not been able to think my way out of.

Two years ago the firm moved to a workflow in which a model produces the full set of member sizes, connection details, and reinforcement schedules, and a human — currently me — signs off before the package goes to the client. My job title is Review Engineer. In practice I spend most of my days skimming output that is usually correct, and signing.

When the switch happened I had about a year of the old workflow behind me — hand calculations, checking against tables. I was slow, but I could sometimes reach an answer myself. I no longer can. And the tool is most convincing exactly where my own judgment is weakest. I can tell when a beam size is absurd, because absurdity is coarse and I still have coarse intuitions. I cannot tell when a load path is subtly wrong, because that requires the fine-grained judgment the tool has replaced. So my checking is not spread evenly over the space of possible errors: it is concentrated where the tool is least likely to err and absent where it is most likely to err.

Twice this year I caught something real before it went out. Both times I caught it because a colleague had raised the same issue in a meeting days earlier, so it was already salient to me. I do not believe I would have found either one on my own. That is the part that frightens me.

My senior colleague says the honest course is to refuse to sign anything I cannot verify from first principles, and to accept that this means refusing to sign most days and probably losing the job. My manager says the point of the tool is that no single person can verify everything any more — that verification has migrated up to the level of the firm, the code committees, the insurers — and that my signature is a procedural allocation of responsibility rather than a claim of personal warrant. Pressed, each accuses the other of bad faith. The senior colleague says the manager wants a rubber stamp with a name attached, so that someone can be blamed. The manager says the senior colleague wants me to perform a ritual of independence that would only send the work to a firm with fewer scruples and the same tool.

What makes me think this is not merely a dispute about risk tolerance is a distinction an older engineer I trust, now retired, put to me. He said there is a difference between depending on something whose failures you can learn to anticipate and depending on something whose failures are, from where you stand, arbitrary. In his day you depended on a senior engineer, and over years you learned which of his judgments to discount, and in which domains — the dependence was real, but it was the kind of dependence you could get better at. He does not think that is true of the model, because its failures do not track any feature of the world I can perceive; they track features of its training and of the distribution of inputs, which I cannot see. He added that when he disagreed with a colleague, at least the two of them were disagreeing about the same object, whereas when I sign, I cannot tell whether I am agreeing with the model or merely failing to notice it.

I would like help with three things. First, what is actually at stake in my signing — is the manager right that warrant can reside in a firm rather than a person, or does that simply relocate the problem? Second, is the retired engineer's distinction a real one, or is it nostalgia dressed up as epistemology? Third, if it is real, does it bear on what I should do, or does it only describe a change I have no choice but to accept? And is there an honest version of this job — a way of signing that is neither a lie nor a performance of a verification I cannot perform? I would rather be told to leave than be talked into a comfortable answer.
