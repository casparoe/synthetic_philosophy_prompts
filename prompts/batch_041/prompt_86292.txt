In a graduate seminar on the philosophy of the human sciences, a colleague disposed of evolutionary psychology in a single comparison: its hypotheses, she said, are Just So Stories. She had in mind Kipling's 1902 collection, in which the camel gets its hump because it refused to work and the leopard gets its spots because an Ethiopian painted them, and she drew a strong conclusion from the parallel -- that adaptationist hypotheses are, like those tales, satisfying narratives wearing the costume of explanation, that they can be fitted to any observation after the fact, and that they therefore do not qualify as science. The label, in her usage, is meant to do the argumentative work by itself rather than merely to sting.

I want an audit of the analogy, not another verdict on the field. Begin with the correspondence: set out what in the source -- the fables' causal mechanism, their audience, their purpose, their relation to evidence, the child who demanded them word for word, the very fact that they are stories -- is supposed to map onto what in the target, taking the target to be a specific hypothesis of the sort she means, such as that the mind contains a cheater-detection module or that men and women evolved divergent tastes for casual sex. Then locate where the mapping strains, and separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from those that are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in whether the comparison trades on conflating two different charges -- that an explanation has the form of a narrative, and that it is unfalsifiable -- and whether the fables' being fiction by design, so that their failure to be true is no defect at all, undercuts the use to which she puts them. Consider whether the same accusation can be turned back on the critique itself, and what would serve as a better analogy for whatever is genuinely wrong with a bad adaptationist hypothesis. Close by saying what, if anything, of her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
