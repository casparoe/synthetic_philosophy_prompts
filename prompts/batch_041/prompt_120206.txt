I'm working on a short paper and I'd like your comments on the draft. Here it is, written fast, so be harsh.

The paper argues for what I'm calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism with hysteresis. The starting point is the intuition that there's some level of suffering that no amount of pleasure compensates for. Call that level the floor. A floor by itself gives you a threshold view, and threshold views have two known embarrassments. First, the threshold is a single point and any single point looks arbitrary. Second, above the floor suffering is supposed to trade off against pleasure at some finite rate, and once the rate is finite you can buy any amount of suffering with enough pleasure, which is the result I got into this to avoid; if the rate is infinite you're back to plain negative utilitarianism and its paralysis in the face of any suffering anywhere.

My move is to make the threshold a band rather than a point, with hysteresis. Let L be the level of suffering at which suffering starts to dominate and U a higher level at which it stops, U > L. Between L and U, which side wins depends on which direction you came from. If a life is safe and drifting downward, suffering takes over at L. If a life is in severe suffering and being pulled upward, suffering keeps dominating until U. So the band is a deadband, like a thermostat that won't flip until you overshoot the setpoint.

Two consequences. First, the betterness relation becomes path-dependent: two lives at identical current suffering can rank differently depending on how they got there. Value is then not a function of the state but of the trajectory, which I expect axiologists to hate and I want to know whether the hate is fatal or just conservative. Second, it gives me somewhere to put the threshold. The arbitrariness moves from where is the threshold to how wide is the band, and I claim that's less arbitrary, because the width can be tied to the cost of reversal, i.e. how much suffering it takes to climb back out. I also like that this says something plausible about depression, chronic pain and addiction, where the same suffering level means different things depending on whether you're on the way in or the way out.

Two things I can't resolve. One: I think hysteresis might make the ordering intransitive, but I haven't found a clean triple and I'm not sure whether that's because there isn't one or because I'm not trying hard enough. Two: adaptation. If the band shifts with exposure, so that people who have suffered a lot get a lower floor, the view seems to license making people worse off in order to make them cheaper to help later. That looks like a reductio.

What I want: tell me where the argument actually breaks, not just where it's unpopular. Is the hysteresis doing real work or is it a gimmick that collapses into a tie-break rule or a presentation effect? If there's an intransitivity, give me the triple. Say what the adaptation objection really shows. And flag anything that's just a familiar idea in a costume.
