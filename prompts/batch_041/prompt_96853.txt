I'm taking a seminar on German aesthetics this term, and we've just finished the third book of Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Representation, where he assigns music a unique place in the hierarchy of the arts: whereas painting, sculpture, and poetry copy the Platonic Ideas, music bypasses the Ideas altogether and is a direct objectification of the Will itself. On his account the bass figures the lowest grades of that objectification, the inner voices the intermediate grades, and the melody — which he treats as the highest grade — traces the life of the human will as it strives, is momentarily satisfied, and strives again; music therefore does not express this or that particular joy or sorrow but the essential nature of joy and sorrow as such, and it does so without the mediation of concepts. I find this the most striking and also the most precarious claim in the whole book, and I've been trying to test it against a work that seems to take the metaphysics of music as its explicit subject: Miloš Forman's Amadeus.

The film is built around Salieri's conviction that Mozart's music is divine while Mozart himself is vulgar, childish, and obscene, and around his decision to take revenge on God by destroying the man through whom the divine voice reaches him. Read through Schopenhauer, this looks less like a crisis of theodicy than a category error: if music is the immediate objectification of a blind, impersonal will that lies outside space, time, and individuality, then the empirical character of the composer is simply irrelevant to the metaphysical content of what he writes, and there is nothing scandalous about a reprobate producing music that sounds like the voice of God. Salieri keeps treating the music as a personal address, as a message from an agent who has chosen Mozart and rejected him; Schopenhauer's theory implies that there is no one on the other end of the line. I'd like a reading of the film that takes this seriously in both directions. Where does the Schopenhauerian lens illuminate scenes — the moment Salieri hears the Adagio of the wind serenade and takes it for the voice of God, the scene in which he examines Mozart's manuscripts and finds them free of corrections, the Requiem dictation at the end, and the closing speech in which he absolves the mediocrities of the world? And where does the film resist the reading?

In particular, I want you to press on a tension I can't resolve. Schopenhauer presents aesthetic contemplation as the temporary suspension of the will, a will-less absorption in the object that affords respite from striving, yet Salieri's listening is the most willful listening imaginable — envy, ambition, and resentment are what he hears in the music. Is the film dramatizing a genuine contradiction in Schopenhauer's account, or is Salieri simply a bad Schopenhauerian? And does the film itself make a case that music is unintelligible except as address, as something that speaks to a listener who cannot help hearing a person behind it? I'm writing an essay for the seminar, so I'd like a genuine critical reading rather than a summary of the film or of Schopenhauer; feel free to say where the film gets the philosophy wrong and where the philosophy gets music wrong.
