I'm in a third-year ethics course and we've been doing Taurek on whether the numbers count. A friend in my seminar built this argument to show Taurek's view proves too much, and I can't decide if it's a trick or a real refutation. It goes:

(1) If the numbers don't count, then in a case where I can save either one stranger or five strangers, it's permissible to save the one.
(2) If it's permissible to save the one, then the one's claim on me is at least as strong as the combined claims of the five.
(3) If one person's claim can be at least as strong as five people's claims, then by repeating the same reasoning it can be at least as strong as any number of people's claims.
(4) So it's permissible to save one person instead of a million.
(5) But a moral view that says saving one instead of a million is permissible is absurd.
(6) So the numbers do count, and Taurek is wrong.

Assume all the people are strangers, there are no special obligations, and no quality-of-life differences. The part that bugs me is that (1) sounds like the whole point of Taurek, and (5) sounds like common sense, but they can't both be right. I'd rather keep (1) if I can, but I also don't want to be committed to saying a million lives are a toss-up against one. Which step do you reject? And whatever you reject, tell me what it costs: does it force me to say something about equal claims, or about permissibility versus comparative strength, that I might not want to say? Keep it under 600 words, and don't just tell me Taurek's conclusion -- I need the diagnosis.
