Take Aumann's agreement theorem and its dynamic extension: two Bayesian agents who share a prior and whose posterior credences in some event are common knowledge must have the same credence, and if instead they truthfully announce their credences to each other and update, they reach agreement in finitely many rounds in a finite state space. Give me your credence that this machinery licenses a normative inference about real disagreement — specifically, that a persistent disagreement between two well-informed and apparently rational peers, where each knows the other's view, is itself evidence that at least one of them is irrational or epistemically defective, rather than a normal and permissible state of affairs. Decompose the number: the probability that the shared-prior assumption is a defensible constraint on ideally rational agents with the same evidence; the probability that common knowledge of credences, or truthful and fully revealing announcement, is attainable in the cases that matter, or that the conclusion survives when the relevant knowledge is only approximate, coarse, or one-sided; the probability that the agents in question are the ideal Bayesians the theorem requires, with well-defined and computable credences in the relevant event; and any further term you think belongs. Give a number and one line of justification for each, then the combination, then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.
