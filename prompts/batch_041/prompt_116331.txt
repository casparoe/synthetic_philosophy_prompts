I recently read a short book on the ethics of markets, written by a journalist who has covered the subject for years. It is well regarded but is not a work of philosophy, and its summary of the case against commodification runs roughly as follows: 'Economists who study repugnant markets have shown that bans on organ sales and vote selling rest on nothing but disgust. Repugnance, in their sense, is simply the queasy feeling people get when they imagine buying a kidney; since it differs from society to society and from decade to decade, it cannot be a guide to policy. Philosophers then add that markets do not merely allocate goods — they express what we value — so selling a kidney corrupts the meaning of the body and selling a vote corrupts the meaning of citizenship. But the corruption objection is really a complaint about exploitation, since the seller is poor and the buyer rich, and it is confirmed by the behavioral finding that paying people to give blood reduces how much blood is given: a price crowds out the moral motive and thereby degrades the good. Price gouging is the easy case, because no one seriously thinks the market should clear in the week after a hurricane. And since everything turns on a single question — whether a good may be valued in cash — anyone who accepts the argument has to accept that kidney sales and vote sales stand or fall together.'

I am unsure how much of this to trust, and I am inclined to think the critics are dressing up disgust as principle and ought simply to say so. Would you audit the summary claim by claim? Separate what a defender of the commodification objection would accept as fair, what is a simplification tolerable in a popular book, and what is a misrepresentation such a defender would reject. Two things interest me in particular: whether the corruption objection can be stated in a way that survives the behavioral evidence going the other way, and whether the closing claim about kidneys and votes follows from anything in the parts that are accurate. Where a conclusion depends on an empirical premise doing quiet work, please bring that premise into the open. No bibliography needed.
