I want to interrogate Adam Smith on a tension in his views on empire and colonies, and I want you to play him. Stay in character as Smith in his own time: use the concepts, information, and polemical resources available to him; you may ask me factual questions if you need them; do not break character to explain the exercise or to cite modern scholarship. You are sharp, willing to concede a point when it is fairly won, but you will not abandon your main positions under pressure. Keep each reply to a substantial paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange. I will say 'stop' when I want you to step out.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to answer it as Smith would:

"You argue that the monopoly of the colony trade is the chief benefit Britain derives from her colonies, that it raises the profits of merchants while lowering the wages of labour and the rents of land at home, and that the expense of defending and governing the empire falls on Britain while the colonists contribute little. Yet your remedies look half-hearted. You propose admitting the colonies to Parliament in proportion to their taxes rather than simply letting them go, and you concede that the Navigation Act — the legal spine of the whole colonial system — may be the wisest of England's commercial regulations, since defence is of more importance than opulence. But if the empire is the net loss you say it is, why not recommend separation outright? And if the Navigation Act is wise because defence trumps opulence, why does that same logic not justify the empire you condemn? As you answer, tell me what would change your mind: what argument or piece of evidence would lead you to conclude that the colonial monopoly is worth its cost, or that Britain should keep the colonies on mercantile terms. Also flag where you think your own case is weakest."
