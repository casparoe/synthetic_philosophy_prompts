My opening move. My collaborator and I have spent a year on the same question. Same training, same literature, each of us has read the other's full write-up, and we have ended up at credences of 0.15 and 0.6. Each of us thinks the other's reasoning is broadly sound and merely over- or under-weights one consideration. We have announced our numbers to each other many times, we each know that we have, and neither number has moved. I can see four doors out of this for you. Door one: we do not really share a prior. Door two: our disagreement is not common knowledge, and saying the numbers out loud is not enough to make it so. Door three: we are not disagreeing about the same event within the same model, which the theorem never claimed to cover. Door four: one of us has simply erred and cannot see it — a door that is open to you, but the emptiest of the four, since it does not say which of us, and it leaves me with nothing to do but recheck my arithmetic until I die.

Open them and show me which one is actually open, but note what each costs. If it is door one, then the shared prior is an antecedent neither of us can inspect from the inside, so the theorem can never speak to a live dispute: every live dispute is quietly reclassified as a prior disagreement, and the theorem collapses into the claim that people who already agree would agree. If you answer with the standard test — that agents sharing a prior cannot find a bet that each of them expects to win — I will tell you that we have looked for that bet and neither of us wants it. If it is door two, tell me what would make it common knowledge if repeated public announcement does not. If it is door three, say plainly whether the theorem has any application to actual experts, who nearly always disagree in part about the model. And if you think my case is misdescribed rather than merely hard, do not simply say so: tell me exactly what a case would have to look like in order to threaten the theorem, and I will supply it.
