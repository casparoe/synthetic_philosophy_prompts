so i work in compliance-adjacent stuff (well, adjacent to compliance) and something about the whole rulebook bugs me. maybe you can tell me what view of corruption it secretly assumes.

here's the setup. our company has a code of conduct: you may not give a government official anything of more than trivial value, no meals, no trips, no cash, and if a customs officer hints he'd like a little something to move your shipment along you say no. fine. but the exact same company runs a government-affairs office that gives money to political campaigns and pays lobbyists to get the same kinds of decisions changed, and that's all legal and above board, nobody calls it corruption. and on the sales side we take private clients to expensive dinners and ball games all the time, thats just relationship-building. and in a bunch of the countries we operate in, offering a gift to an official is ordinary politeness and refusing it is an insult, which our code treats as no excuse. i also have a fuzzy memory that one of the big foreign-bribery laws even carves out small grease payments to low-level functionaries, which if true makes the picture even messier.

so the practice draws lines in a place i can't quite defend. what theory of corruption does it presuppose? i can think of a few candidates and they don't obviously agree: corruption as misuse of public office for private gain; corruption as breaking impersonal/impartial rules; corruption as some kind of social-meaning thing where the same transaction is corrupt in one culture and not another; corruption as just whatever harms the public. does our rulebok actually commit to one, or is it compatible with several? i suspect it has to commit to something, because it treats the gift to the customs guy as corrupt and the campaign donation as not, and both are ways of using money to get favorable decisions, so the difference can't be about influence as such, it has to be about form. but maybe not, maybe there's a principled difference im missing (disclosure? contestability? the fact that the donation is public?).

also the rule against gifts seems to assume something about people, like that an official who accepts a gift will feel obliged and thats why its dangerous, i.e. that gratitude is a real causal channel. if officials were reliably impartial would the gift rule even make sense? and is the whole thing smuggling in a Western public/private distinction as if it were universal?

tell me: what does the practice presuppose, which rival views does it quietly rule out, and where is it just underdetermined so several theories would fit. then tell me what would have to change if we adopted the social-meaning view instead. not looking for citations, just the argument. and please don't just say "its all socially constructed", i want the actual commitments.
