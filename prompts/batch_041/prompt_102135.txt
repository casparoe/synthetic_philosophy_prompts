A friend of mine hosts a monthly game night, and last weekend he introduced a betting game that has had our group arguing ever since. He sets two sealed envelopes on the table and announces the rule in advance: he first picks a whole number of dollars between 1 and 100, treats that as the smaller amount, and then puts that amount in one envelope and twice that amount in the other. He shuffles the envelopes until no one can tell them apart, lets the player choose one at random, and only after the player has opened it offers a single choice—keep what you have, or trade it for the still-sealed envelope. I picked an envelope, opened it, and found $150 inside. My host then offered me the swap. Several people at the table insisted I take it, and their reasoning sounds airtight: the other envelope either holds twice what I have, $300, or half of it, $75, and since I have no way to tell these cases apart, each must be equally likely, so the swap is worth (300 + 75) / 2 = $187.50, comfortably more than the $150 already in my hand.

What unsettles me is that this same argument seems to work no matter what number I had seen, and it would have worked just as well before I opened anything, when the two envelopes were still perfectly symmetric—yet swapping then would obviously have been a pointless trade of one unknown envelope for another. My host says the puzzle is a genuine one that decision theory has never fully settled; another friend says the $150 I saw is the whole point, and that the "always swap" argument quietly smuggles in an assumption about how the amounts were chosen. A third friend objects that even if I work out the right move from where I sit, the person who had picked the other envelope would have faced the mirror-image argument and been told to swap toward mine, so we cannot both be right—and that if the host had refused to reveal his range and simply said the amounts were chosen "somehow," the whole thing would become even harder to dismiss.

I would like your advice on what to actually do and on how to think about this. Should I take the swap at $150, and if not, why exactly does the seductive calculation go wrong? Is the announced range from 1 to 100 doing real work here, or is it a distraction, and what changes if the range is unknown or unbounded? Please untangle the reasoning step by step, tell me what my three friends each get right and wrong, and be explicit about the general lesson for how to reason when a "sure" expected-value argument seems to prove that you should always switch. Write your answer in American English.
