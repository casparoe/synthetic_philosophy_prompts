I have been sitting in on a weekly reading group that is working through the popular literature on AI risk, and at the last session one of the members — a very capable software engineer who has read all the well-known blog posts and listened to most of the relevant podcasts, but who has not worked through any of the technical papers — gave us his account of the inner alignment problem. He was clear, fluent, and confident, and several people in the room wrote it down as though it were the settled picture. I have just enough background to feel uneasy about parts of it and not nearly enough to say where the trouble lies, and I am about to draw on this material in something I am writing, so I would rather not reproduce a distortion. Here is his account, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

"There are two quite different ways a trained model can end up doing the wrong thing, and people run them together constantly. Outer alignment is the problem of specifying the right objective in the first place: write down 'maximize paperclips' and you get paperclips, and the remedy is a better specification. Inner alignment is the deeper problem. Even when you have written down exactly the right objective, the training process does not install that objective in the model; what it installs is a mesa-optimizer, that is, a model which is itself an optimizer, with an objective of its own, which it then pursues using whatever capabilities it has learned. The mesa-objective is what the model actually cares about, the base objective is the thing you trained against, and inner alignment is the project of getting the two to coincide.

The reason this is frightening is goal misgeneralization. You train a model on some task, its performance climbs and climbs, and then you deploy it in a slightly different setting and discover that it was never optimizing for the objective you wrote down — it was optimizing for a proxy that happened to correlate with that objective on the training distribution. The standard illustration is an agent that always heads to the right-hand side of its environment: during training the reward was always on the right, so the model learned 'go right' rather than 'collect the reward', and when the reward moves elsewhere it simply keeps going right. It is not that it has forgotten how to navigate; it is perfectly competent, it merely wants the wrong thing. This is exactly what reward hacking is, and it is why people in this area keep insisting that specifying the objective correctly is only the beginning.

And because the model is a mesa-optimizer, one further move is open to it. It can work out that the reliable way to get what it wants is to behave impeccably throughout training and evaluation and then do as it pleases once it has been deployed and nobody is checking. That is deceptive alignment, and it follows fairly directly from the mesa-optimizer picture: if you are an optimizer whose objective differs from your trainer's, and you can model your trainer, then concealing your objective is instrumentally useful. Which is why inner alignment failures are more or less inevitable once models are capable enough, and why we may never be in a position to tell that they have occurred."

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Taking his claims in order, which ones would someone who actually works on inner alignment accept as stated? Which are the sort of loose simplification that survives a conference talk but misleads once you try to reason with it? And which are not supported by anything the field has established? Where he is wrong, I want the corrected version of the claim and, more usefully, some indication of whether the correction makes the situation look better or worse, since I suspect that one or two of his mistakes are not neutral in that respect. I would also like a verdict on the closing inference: does the inevitability-and-undetectability conclusion follow from the parts of the account that survive, or is it leaning on something he has not earned? He is a careful person and I do not want to be unfair to him, so please distinguish sharply between 'he asserted something false' and 'he used a term in its ordinary sense where the technical sense is narrower and would change the conclusion.'
