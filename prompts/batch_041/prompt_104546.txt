Below is an argument I assembled for a seminar and now find suspicious, because I cannot accept where it ends up.

Setting: a hiring committee of five must decide whether a candidate is appointable. Appointability is not a further fact about the candidate; the committee treats a candidate as appointable just in case the candidate clears the teaching bar and clears the research bar. So its judgments on three propositions are logically linked: it cannot affirm the teaching judgment, affirm the research judgment, and deny that the candidate is appointable. The committee also keeps meeting new linked questions (whether a given sort of publication counts toward the research bar, whether a given teaching load counts as clearing the teaching bar), and it revisits its criteria every year.

(1) A procedure for reaching the committee's verdict is legitimate only if it is responsive to each member's judgments and treats members equally: no member's judgment is systematically ignored and none is given extra weight.
(2) The verdict must also be internally consistent: the committee cannot affirm the teaching and research judgments and deny appointability, since those cannot all be true.
(3) These two demands cannot be met together. On some profiles of individually consistent judgments about linked propositions, majority voting yields an inconsistent verdict; and any procedure that always yields a consistent verdict while remaining responsive must, on some profiles, let a single member's judgments prevail against the others'.
(4) No committee escapes this, since any question can be broken into a conclusion and the considerations bearing on it.
(5) Nor does it help to settle the propositions one at a time: whichever proposition is fixed first constrains the rest, so the order of decision can change the verdict, and whoever controls the order controls the outcome.
(6) So every committee is either unresponsive, or incoherent, or in the grip of whoever sets its agenda.
(7) A body in any of those conditions cannot legitimately bind anyone.
(8) Therefore no committee, and no deliberative body generally, can legitimately decide anything.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. Tell me which step to give up, explain why it looked acceptable, and price what giving it up costs; if more than one step is available for rejection, rank them.
