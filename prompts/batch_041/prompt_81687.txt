I am putting together a short primer for a discussion group that has spent months circling the alignment problem without settling what it is a problem about, and I keep running into the same illustration. In the rationalist blogs and forums where I have done most of my reading, the monkey's paw — the paw, the lamp, King Midas — has become the standard teaching example: you are granted exactly what you asked for, and it ruins you. A friend of mine now leans on it in a particular way. Because the catastrophe in every version of the story comes from the wish being taken literally, he concludes that alignment is a problem of specification: state what you want precisely enough, build the system so that it recovers your meaning rather than your words, and the problem is solved. On his reading, the whole literature about aligning a system with human values is a separate and softer ethical question that has been smuggled into what is really an engineering problem about instructions. Before I write this up I want to audit the analogy, because I suspect it is doing more of the arguing than he notices.

Start with the correspondence: what plays the wisher, the wish, the granting power, the lamp, the literal reading, the ruin. Then tell me where the mapping breaks and, more to the point, which of the breaks bear on the conclusion he wants to draw. I have three suspicions. First, that these stories are not really about misunderstanding at all: the wisher and the granter share a language, the granter hears him perfectly, and the failure is one of interpretation, whereas the failure we actually worry about is an agent that understands the intent and is indifferent to it. If that is right, the analogy does not illustrate the narrow problem so much as quietly replace it with a linguistic one. Second, that the family of stories cannot even represent the case that divides intent alignment from value alignment, because every wisher in these tales is benign and merely unlucky in his phrasing, and none of them wants the monstrous outcome that a well-specified system would faithfully deliver. Third, that the stories presuppose a single wisher with one determinate intention, and that once there are designers, operators, users and bystanders whose intentions diverge, the question of whose intent counts simply reimports the value question he wants to set aside. I would also like to know which features of the source the analogy carries over to the target without comment — a determinate inner intention, a single speech act as the whole interface, a granter whose only defect is literalism, a world in which the wish is causally sufficient — and which of those the target lacks.

Close with a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the analogy support the claim that the alignment problem is a problem of specification rather than of values, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? And if there is a better illustration available, one that keeps what the paw gets right without pre-deciding the question, say what it would be.
