You are the instructor for an upper-level course on language and mind, grading the following exam answer. Assign a grade (A–F with plus/minus, or a percentage) and then give the specific comments that justify it: which claims in the answer are correct, which are wrong or unsupported, where a step does not follow, and what a fully satisfactory answer would have added. Respond to particular sentences rather than giving a general impression, and judge the reasoning rather than the prose.

Exam question. A researcher writes: "The child's linguistic experience consists only of positive evidence — the utterances she hears — and never of explicit instruction about which sentences are ungrammatical. Yet every speaker converges on a grammar far more restrictive than the input licenses: no one, for instance, forms yes/no questions by a structure-independent rule, even though such a rule is compatible with every sentence the child encounters. Since the input does not determine the grammar, the grammar must be innate. This also shows that language is a domain-specific faculty and that general-purpose statistical learning cannot account for its acquisition." Assess this argument. Distinguish what the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument entitles the researcher to conclude from what it does not, and say what further evidence the stronger claims would require.

Student's answer. "The argument is a textbook instance of poverty-of-the-stimulus reasoning, and its core is sound. The child's evidence is limited to the utterances she hears, and those utterances are consistent with many grammars besides the one she acquires, so something must be supplied by the learner: the input alone cannot select among the competing hypotheses. The researcher is also right to fasten on the absence of negative evidence. Since nobody corrects the child's syntax, a learner that tried to eliminate overgeneral hypotheses by trial and error would have no way of knowing which of its generalizations were too broad. This is what makes the structure-dependence case so telling: a linear rule for forming yes/no questions fits everything the child hears, yet she never adopts it and never produces the questions it would license. That the erroneous forms never appear shows that the constraint on the child's hypotheses is not something she arrives at by testing and discarding.

From this the researcher's conclusion follows. If the constraint is not extracted from the input, it must be part of the child's biological endowment; and since it concerns sentence structure specifically, it belongs to a language-specific faculty rather than to some general capacity for statistical learning. That is why approaches that treat acquisition as the extraction of distributional regularities from a corpus cannot work: they have no negative evidence to work with, and no route from the attested sentences to the restrictive grammar that speakers actually have. What the argument establishes, then, is not merely that the child contributes something to acquisition, but that the contribution is an innate, domain-specific body of grammatical knowledge.

The one qualification I would add is that the argument is abductive, an inference to the best explanation of the acquisition facts, so it is only as strong as the alternatives it rules out. A fully adequate defence would need to show that no domain-general learning procedure with any plausible prior could bridge the gap. But that is a burden the researcher can reasonably be asked to discharge, not a defect in the reasoning."
