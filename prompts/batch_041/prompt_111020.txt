Two people in a Discord server that grew out of my local meetup spent most of last week's call arguing about whether "better than" is transitive, and I said I would write the exchange up and get an outside verdict. I honestly could not tell who came out ahead. The puzzle they were both working from is the kind that circulates every few years: take a thousand years of a mild headache; now consider a slightly shorter stretch of slightly more intense pain and ask which is worse. Almost everyone says the second, on the grounds that a hair more intensity outweighs a hair less duration. Apply the same step again — trim the duration, raise the intensity a notch — and keep going until you reach two days of pure agony. Each step, taken alone, looks obviously right. Taken together they say that two days of agony is worse than a thousand years of a mild headache, which is not what anyone believes. So either one of the steps is false, and it is strangely hard to say which, or the transitivity of "better than" fails. Here is the argument, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

Dana opened: transitivity is not an optional feature we could discover to be missing, because it is part of what "better than" means. If A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C, full stop; a relation that fails this is not a betterness ranking, whatever else it is. The spectrum must therefore contain a false step, and the real work is locating it.

Owen: I have heard that, and I don't see how it helps. The point of the spectrum is precisely that no step can be identified as the bad one. Each adjacent comparison is one I would make confidently and that you would make confidently; they cannot all be fine and the conclusion false unless transitivity goes. Nor is it absurd to think a "beats" relation can fail transitivity. Nontransitive dice do exactly that: die A beats die B more often than not, B beats C, C beats A, and each comparison is an honest computation. Nobody concludes that "beats" is meaningless because of it.

Dana: The dice example cuts against you. "Beats" for dice is defined by a comparison procedure — the probability of rolling higher — and the dice win along different profiles, so the relation is a summary of several dimensions rather than a single ranking. If "better than" is like that, we should say so and then ask which dimension we are ranking by; betterness in the ordinary sense is supposed to be all-things-considered. And an all-things-considered ranking that cycles is one you can be pumped: as you read the spectrum, the headache-life is better than the agony-life and the agony-life is better than the headache-life, so someone holding that ranking will pay a little to be moved from each option to the next one round the loop, arriving back where they started with less money. A ranking that makes you a money pump is not a ranking we should revise transitivity to accommodate; it is a sign that something upstream is wrong.

Owen: Money pumps are an argument about choice, not about betterness. The spectrum asks which outcome is worse, and I can hold pairwise-confident judgments while refusing to be led around a loop once I see the loop — that I can be tricked if I am careless does not show that any adjacent comparison is mistaken. And nontransitivity is not a mark of incoherence anyway: "more similar to" is nontransitive, and it is a perfectly respectable comparative. If we tolerate it there, why not here?

Dana: Because when a comparative is nontransitive we can usually say why, and the why is that it summarises several respects at once. Similarity is like that: a is closer to b in colour, b closer to c in shape, and once you fix the respect, transitivity comes back. Which suggests the spectrum is an argument against completeness rather than against transitivity. If adjacent options are on a par, or roughly as good, the chain never gets going and "better than" stays transitive; what we lose is only the assumption that for any two outcomes one is better or they are equally good. You have been attacking the wrong premise.

Owen: Then tell me what "on a par" is, other than a polite name for vagueness or for our ignorance about which is better. And if it is a genuine third status, I want to know whether it is itself transitive — because if parity is nontransitive, you have not saved transitivity, you have just moved the failure one level up.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or right, with the reason in a sentence or two; I care more about the individual moves than about a score, though if one of them is closer to right overall I would like to know. Then say what each of them is missing, including anything neither raised that bears on the case. I don't need citations or a reading list; I will track those down myself.

One request about the verdict: distinguish what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is a live disagreement, and don't hand me one camp's position dressed up as the consensus. I have been on the receiving end of that before — a crisp answer that turned out to be the idiosyncratic view of whoever wrote the explainer — and it is worse than useless for deciding what to think.
