I lead the platform team at a mid-sized software company, and I have become the person who signs off on the word 'understands' before it appears in our product documentation, our internal evaluation reports, and the model cards we hand to enterprise customers. I have no formal philosophy training, and I suspect that what looks like a routine technical judgement is really a conceptual one that I am making badly and inconsistently.

Write me a reusable procedure, addressed to someone who knows machine learning but not philosophy, that takes the debate over whether systems like these can genuinely understand language -- the arguments that fluent output can be produced by statistical pattern-matching over text with no grasp of what the words are about, together with the replies to those arguments -- and turns it into an ordered set of questions with pass and fail criteria. Make it operational rather than verbal: at each step, say what evidence I would need, what counts as sufficient, and what I should do when the evidence is unavailable because I only have black-box API access.

Build on the informal test my team already uses, because I want to know how far it can be trusted: treat a claim that the system understands X as a claim that its behaviour is sensitive to the differences that matter for X and invariant to those that do not, so that meaning-preserving rewording leaves the output stable and meaning-changing edits reliably move it. Tell me where that test is too weak, where it is too strong, and what it leaves out that a philosopher would insist on -- the difference between what comes out of the system and what is going on inside it.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: a code-completion model that reliably fills in a function from its docstring; a model that scores well on domain exams but collapses on trivially reworded versions of the same questions; and a transformer trained only on sequences of legal moves in a board game, whose internal activations can be probed to recover the board state it was never shown. I would like at least one of the three to come out with an answer I will not like.

End with the cases the procedure cannot settle, where the decision is a values call rather than a fact: how much weight to give a model's own step-by-step explanation when it may be a rationalization rather than a cause of the answer, and whether a competitor's freer use of the word 'understands' in its marketing is a reason to loosen or tighten mine. I do not need a reading list; I need something I can run in an hour and defend in a review, with the soft spots marked.
