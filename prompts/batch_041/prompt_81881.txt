I've been building charting tools for about six years now — mostly Vega-Lite specs with some hand-rolled D3 when a client insists — so I think pretty natively in terms of data tables, marks, encoding channels, scales and domains, facets and small multiples, and the usual raking of perceptual accuracy (position beats length beats angle beats area beats colour). Separately, I'm halfway through a history-of-science reading group that keeps circling back to Otto Neurath, and I've developed a bee in my bonnet about the picture-language side of his work — the thing that started as the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics at the social and economic museum he ran in Red Vienna from the mid twenties, and that got the name ISOTYPE around 1935 after he was pushed out of Austria (apparently the acronym is International System of Typographic Picture Education). Here's roughly what I've pieced together. It's a set of standardised, abstracted pictograms where each symbol stands for a fixed quantity, and the cardinal rule is that you show a larger quantity with more copies of the same symbol rather than a larger symbol, because scaling leaves it ambiguous whether the viewer is meant to compare height, length, or area. They banned perspective for the same reason, tied specific colours to specific meanings so that a colour stayed stable across a whole set of charts, and worked through an explicit transformation step in which a person — Marie Reidemeister was the main one — converted raw statistics into a sketch for a chart. The whole thing was aimed at visual education for ordinary citizens, it was explicitly never meant to replace words, and Neurath hedged on whether it was a language at all, settling on something like a language-like technique. He also had a line about words dividing while pictures unite, which is carrying a lot of weight in the project and which I suspect is exactly the bit that doesn't hold up.

So: take that whole package — the design rules, the transformation step, the educational and political ambitions, the quasi-linguistic claims — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually think in, as faithfully as you can. Marks, encodings, channels, scales, aesthetics, facets, interaction, accessibility, the percpetion literature. Don't flatter me by pretending a grammar of graphics is just ISOTYPE with a nicer API; where the mapping only works if you import an assumption Neurath didn't have, flag it at that exact point rather than smoothing it over.

The part I actually care about is the residue report. What didn't survive? My guesses: some things go through cleanly (repetition-over-scaling is basically a perceptual argument I already believe, and facets are already small multiples); some go through only as distortions (his fixed colour semantics, which my framework treats as a theme choice rather than a semantic commitment); and some have no counterpart at all (the transformation role, the political aim of handing workers a shared picture vocabulary, the idea that a deliberately restricted vocabulary is a feature rather than a limitation). For each item, tell me whether the gap is a defect of my framework — something a decent charting tool ought to be able to express and currently can't — or evidence that Neurath's original claim was vaguer and more rhetorical than it sounds. I'm especially interested in the iconic side: a pictogram means something partly by resemblance, whereas in a grammar of graphics a mark means whatever the legend says it means. Is that a genuine hole in my vocabulary, or is Neurath smuggling in a theory of representation he never made explicit?
