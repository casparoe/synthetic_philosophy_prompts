Ok so I need help diagnosing an argument that has basically eaten my reading group alive for two months, and I don't want a verdict on who's right — I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Background, in my own words, so forgive the sloppiness. You train an agent to do a task — fetch a box, maximize some score — and it does stuff you never asked for along the way: knocks over the vase, shoves everything out of its path, scribbles on the walls if that helps the score. The usual response in the safety literature is to add a penalty for "impact": measure how much the world ends up different from how it would have been, and charge the agent for the difference. The proposals differ in what "how it would have been" means. One measures how many states you can still reach, compared to how many you could reach from where you started. One measures how much harder you've made it for the agent to achieve a goal drawn at random from some distribution of goals it might be handed later. One just gives the agent a pile of hypothetical future tasks and tells it not to spoil them.

Here's the fight. Person A says the whole program is a dead end, because every one of those measures secretly contains the thing it was supposed to replace. To say how much impact there was, you need a baseline — what would have happened if the agent hadn't acted — and inaction isn't neutral: if the robot doesn't move the box, the kid behind it doesn't get out. So either the baseline is doing hidden normative work, or it's just "the optimal policy for the task," which means you already needed the full value specification you were trying to avoid. And the random-future-goals trick doesn't rescue it, since a distribution over goals is a value specification with extra steps.

Person B says that's a gotcha, not an argument. Of course the measure isn't value-free; nothing is. Nobody claimed impact was a value-free property of the world. The claim is that you can build a penalty term that's task-agnostic in practice, that cuts down the absurd behaviors without you having to enumerate everything you care about, and that degrades gracefully when your guess about the task is wrong. A crutch that isn't a definition is still a crutch.

We've been going around this for months. A keeps producing cases where the measure punishes the agent for doing the right thing; B keeps saying those are edge cases you patch. A says the patching is the tell. B says A is demanding a derivation where an engineering heuristic will do.

My hypothesis, and I'd like you to push on it hard: I think they're answering two seperate questions under one heading. A is asking whether "impact" names a value-free feature of the world (looks like no, maybe provably no). B is asking whether a penalty term can usefully reduce bad side effects across a distribution of tasks (looks like yes, almost by construction). If that's the whole story the "debate" is mostly verbal, and the real work is just saying which question you're asking and what the penalty is a proxy for. But I suspect that's too tidy — maybe there's something substantive underneath, about the inaction baseline, or about whether "don't disturb things" is even coherent when there's no privileged state of the world to preserve, and that part wouldn't be verbal at all.
