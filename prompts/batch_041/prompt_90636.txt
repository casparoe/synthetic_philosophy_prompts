I have recently begun sitting on a hospital ethics committee, and I keep rehearsing each case in the voice of an imagined onlooker—sometimes a colleague, sometimes my late mother, sometimes a stranger in the waiting room. I had assumed this was something like Smith's impartial spectator: not the actual spectators, but an internal judge who corrects for self-love and partiality. Now I am not so sure I can tell the difference. Please write me a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of a committee member who tries to speak as the impartial spectator but keeps hearing the accents of the people whose approval they crave. Use the octave to build the illusion of a neutral interior voice; let the volta be the moment the speaker realizes that the spectator's judgments track their audience; and use the sestet to press the question whether Smith can distinguish the impartial spectator from an internalized panel of actual spectators without appealing to a moral faculty that settles the matter by fiat. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows Smith's theory would recognize the distinction at stake, and it should not resolve the worry—it should end in the speaker's uncertainty rather than in a tidy moral. Paraphrase Smith's ideas in your own words; do not quote him. The poem should be formal, metrical, and self-contained, able to stand alone for a reader who has never read Smith but to reward a reader who has.
