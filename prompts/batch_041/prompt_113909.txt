I am an undergraduate in a seminar on early modern metaphysics, and I am writing my final paper on Berkeley's argument against the existence of unconceived sensible things, the one my notes call the master argument, from the Three Dialogues. Before I commit my objection to paper I want to test it against Berkeley himself rather than against my own summary of him. Please play Berkeley: speak in the first person, in his own voice, with only the resources of his own system (ideas, minds or spirits, his rejection of abstract ideas), and hold that position for the whole exchange rather than stepping out to comment on what he is usually taken to mean. Argue — do not simply point at a passage and declare the matter settled. Where I use a distinction that is not his, such as the distinction between the content of a representation and the state of affairs it represents, he should tell me what the nearest thing in his own framework is instead of quietly adopting my vocabulary, and if his framework has nothing corresponding to it he should say so. Concede particular points where he would genuinely concede them, but do not give up immaterialism. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that it stays a conversation, and stay in character until I tell you to stop.

Here is my opening move. I reconstruct the argument as: we cannot conceive of any sensible thing existing unconceived; whatever cannot be conceived is impossible; therefore no sensible thing exists unconceived; hence sensible things exist only insofar as they are perceived. My objection is that the first premise is ambiguous between two quite different claims, and the argument can be handed one of them but needs the other. On the first reading, the premise records a fact about an exercise I cannot complete: the moment I frame the thought of a thing as unperceived I have perceived it, in the only sense available to me, so the attempt to conceive an unconceived thing undermines itself. That is a fact about my act of conceiving, not about the object I was trying to think about; it seems to hold of any attempt whatever to think of something as not being thought of; and it therefore tells us nothing about how things are. If it did have metaphysical force, it would deliver the conclusion on its own, with no work left for God to do in perceiving what we are not perceiving. On the second reading, the premise says that a thing's existing unconceived is itself an impossible state of affairs, which is exactly what the argument is supposed to prove, so the premise assumes the conclusion. Which reading does he accept, and how does the argument survive on it? I expect him to reply that my objection depends on an abstract idea of existence detached from perception, which he denies we can form; if that is the reply, I want him to show that my dilemma requires any such idea, because I do not see that it does. I also expect him to reply that the master argument is only one of several arguments and was never meant to stand alone; if so, I want to know which of the others is supposed to carry the weight here, and I want to see it carried rather than merely named. One further resource he may reach for: his distinction between ideas and notions, which the notes in my edition date to the 1734 editions of the Principles and the Dialogues rather than to the originals. If he needs it, I want to know whether the argument works without it.
