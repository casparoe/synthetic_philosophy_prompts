I am a teaching assistant for an introductory graduate seminar in the philosophy of psychiatry, and I have to open the term with something better than a lecture on social construction. The recurring problem is that students arrive already half-convinced that psychiatric categories are constructed but cannot say what the conviction commits them to: some mean that the categories are arbitrary, some that they are merely instruments, some that they cause the very symptoms they name, and some that they are instruments of governance. I want a one-page diagnostic handout that sorts them by what they actually believe rather than by the label they would volunteer.

Please write me a decision tree that takes a student from the starting commitment that something is wrong with psychiatric classification to a named position. Use roughly six to eight branch nodes and nine to twelve leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a claim that the student can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node may be phrased as, say, do you think psychiatric categories are real, or are you a social constructionist. Label each edge with the answer that sends you down it. Each leaf should carry a name for the position (descriptive labels are fine; attach a theorist's name only where you are confident of the attribution) plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, and a further sentence stating something the position obliges you to say about a specified later case, so the reader can run a consistency check on themselves. Add a lookup key mapping answer paths to positions so the sheet doubles as a quiz, and a short section on combinations of answers that no one occupies, diagnosing each empty branch as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, say so rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them.

The tree has to cross two axes that my students constantly run together. The first is what sort of thing a category is: a natural kind, a cluster of co-occurring properties held together by a mechanism, a practical kind whose adequacy is measured by usefulness for a purpose rather than correspondence to a joint in nature, an interactive kind that changes as the classified respond to being classified, a social role that the label itself brings into being, a self-sustaining network of symptoms with no underlying latent entity, or nothing worth keeping at all. The second axis is what looping effects mean for the standing of a category: a defect to be engineered away, a neutral feature of any human kind, or the very mechanism by which a category improves. Make sure the following appear as leaves: natural-kind realism, a homeostatic-cluster realism, a practical-kinds instrumentalism, Hacking-style dynamic nominalism, role constructionism, the harmful-dysfunction view, a dimensional or transdiagnostic alternative in the style of the framework debates, an abolitionist position, and an identity-first or neurodiversity position that keeps the category while refusing the pathology. Two nodes I want included because I find them genuinely under-discussed: whether the loop runs through the classified person's self-understanding or only through the behaviour of clinicians, insurers, and institutions, and whether the relevant kind is the individual or the population, given that diagnostic thresholds are population-level devices. Anchor the nodes in cases a student can reason about from general knowledge — the removal of homosexuality from the diagnostic manual, the rise and fall of multiple personality diagnoses, the contested boundary of autism and the role of autistic self-advocacy, burnout and gaming disorder as candidate new categories, and the historical case of drapetomania. Please give no citations, bibliography, or reading list, and gloss in a clause any technical term you use, since I want a student who has read nothing to be able to answer honestly.
