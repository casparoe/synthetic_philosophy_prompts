I chair the ethics consultation service at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and I need to write the standing procedure my on-call ethicists will use. The consult I keep getting is the one where a patient's advance directive and the patient's own current expressed wishes point in opposite directions: a woman who refused dialysis in writing five years ago now asks for it; a man whose directive authorizes comfort care only is now, still articulate, demanding everything; a patient who wrote that she never wanted to be kept alive by machines is being kept alive by a machine and, on a good day, says thank you. We get two or three of these a month. The decisions are irreversible, the family is in the room, and the on-call ethicist is usually a rotating clinician with a weekend course in clinical ethics and forty-five minutes before the next admission.

I don't want the standard checklist. Capacity, benefits, burdens, substituted judgment, best interest — we all know it, and it goes silent at exactly the point where these cases are hard. What I want is a procedure that takes a position on the question underneath, and then makes that position runnable by someone who has never read a word of the philosophical literature. The question is what, if anything, makes a past decision authoritative over the person who is here now. The candidate answers I want you to work with: the earlier self bound the later one deliberately, and that binding is to be honored; the document is not a command but the best evidence we have of what this person values, and evidence can be outweighed; the person is the author of a life, and a directive is part of that authorship, so honoring it is respecting the author rather than the present occupant; authority belongs entirely to whoever is speaking now, and the document is at most a hint. There is also a distinction that has to survive the translation, whatever else you drop: between the ends that gave this patient's life its shape and the patient's moment-to-moment comfort. And there is a possibility the procedure must be able to name: that a present wish is not a considered judgment but a symptom, or a response to being asked, or a wish to please the person asking.

Deliver the procedure as ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what happens when the answer is missing or contested, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it stops by handing the decision to a person — with a note on which of those handoffs are genuine value disagreements and which are just places I was too lazy to write a rule. Include the bedside tests an on-call ethicist can actually run: what to ask the patient, what to ask the family, what to ask the attending, in what order.

Then run it on three cases: the dialysis patient who changed her mind, with capacity intact; a patient with moderate dementia whose directive is silent on the treatment now proposed, where the family's account of what she would have wanted contradicts the team's judgment; and one case where the procedure requires the ethicist to override a present, clearly expressed wish. Say plainly which of the three the procedure handles well and which it only appears to handle.

Finally: tell me what this procedure cannot do. Which cases it will resolve wrongly and confidently, and which questions in it are judgment calls wearing the costume of a rule. The output has to be defensible in a chart note and has to survive the ethics committee and our counsel, who will read it as though it were a policy.
