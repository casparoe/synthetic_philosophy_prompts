I'm in the back half of a graduate seminar on virtue ethics, and the session after next is on the situationist challenge; we've been asked to circulate a short statement of our own position beforehand so the rest of the room can go at it, and I would rather find the weak joint myself than have it found for me in front of an audience. Some context you should have before you read the list: I've been reading rationality blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and I suspect that has shaped what follows more than the seminar has, particularly item five. What I want from you is an audit, not a survey — I don't need a literature review, and I don't need every pair of items relitigated, since most of them are compatible and I know it. I want the two or three joints that actually creak, ordered by how much damage they do, with a specific account in each case of which item has to move and what moving it costs me.

Here is the list. (1) The primary objects of ethical assessment are traits of character rather than individual acts or rules, and I take the skill analogy seriously: virtue is acquired by long practice under correction, the way a craft is, and is exercised through practical wisdom rather than by the application of rules. (2) I accept the descriptive core of the situationist critique in roughly the form Doris gives it — the global traits of folk psychology are weak cross-situational predictors, and the experimental work on helping behaviour, where a hurry manipulation swamps everything we would ordinarily call the agent's dispositions, is the part I find most convincing. I take the replication crisis seriously enough that I rest nothing on the prison study, and I'm aware that even the obedience paradigm has been reread as participants identifying with the experimenter's project rather than submitting to authority; the point survives for me on the helping and honesty data, which have held up better. (3) That is not a defeater, I think, because the virtues are themselves local: what I acquire by practice is honesty-in-this-kind-of-setting and the like, indexed to situations, rather than a single trait running through everything a person does. (4) I do hold the unity of the virtues — practical wisdom is one thing, and one cannot have any virtue in full without the others — because without it the skill analogy loses its point and virtue ethics stops being a systematic account of how to live rather than a list of admirable qualities. (5) The most action-relevant thing I believe about character comes from the rationality material rather than from Aristotle: willpower is a bad interface, and the reliable lever on behaviour is the situation, so the way to become more honest or more generous is to redesign the environment, precommit, and choose the people around you rather than to grit your teeth. (6) Ethical theory has to be empirically responsible — a conception of character that social psychology falsifies should be revised, not quarantined behind a distinction between the normative and the descriptive — and I take the ought-implies-can constraint seriously, so the virtues have to be within reach of creatures with our psychology. (7) I think it is worth ascribing virtues to people somewhat beyond what the evidence supports, and worth believing such ascriptions about oneself, because the ascription and the social reinforcement that comes with it are part of what makes the trait real; character talk is a useful fiction that tends to make itself true.

So: which items are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look tense, and what is the cheapest repair for each genuine one, with an honest note on what the repair takes down with it? If a single revision would clear up more than one of the real tensions at once, tell me that too, even if it is the revision I would least enjoy making. And be concrete about which item you would give up, rather than proposing that the whole list can be held together by being careful about formulations — I would rather hear the cost than the reassurance.
