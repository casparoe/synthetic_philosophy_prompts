The fourth meeting of a reading group on the legal afterlife of the Second World War falls to me, and I want to spend it on the drafting of the London Charter in the summer of 1945 rather than on the trial itself. Sixteen participants, ninety minutes, mostly philosophy and law graduate students, most of whom know the Nuremberg story in outline and none of whom have thought about where the categories came from. What I want is for them to feel the retroactivity problem from the inside — to end the session having *written* an ex post facto criminal code, in a room with no neutral arbiter, and to have noticed the price they had to pay for four-power agreement — instead of hearing me explain nullum crimen sine lege afterwards. No framing lecture at the start; the last time I tried this it became a seminar on Allied hypocrisy inside ten minutes, and I'd rather the structure make that outcome unavailable.

Design it as a drafting conference rather than a mock trial: four delegations of three or four, each with a brief and a sealed file describing something its own state has done or is doing that a broadly worded rule would catch. Invent those files, but keep them plausible for 1945 and drawn from the kind of conduct the prosecuting powers themselves engaged in or tolerated. The delegations negotiate a short charter — a definition of the crimes and a clause on superior orders — and then ratify it. My guess is that unanimity will only be purchasable with vagueness, and I want them to discover that the vagueness is the mechanism rather than the sloppiness; if the design can make the finished text's ambiguities feel necessary, that is the whole session. Give me rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the four briefs and sealed files, the drafting template, and the ratification procedure.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the real thing: the count of crimes against peace and its grounding in the 1928 renunciation of war, the tu quoque defence, the connection requirement that tied crimes against humanity to the war and the dispute over whose interests that limit served, the Tokyo variant where the same objections arose before a different bench, and what actually did the legitimising work afterwards — the General Assembly's affirmation of the principles and their later codification, rather than the verdicts themselves.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I expect anachronism, someone reaching for the Rome Statute, and I expect one delegation to dominate the table — and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Finally, say how confident you are that this arrangement will produce the discovery rather than a morality play about hypocrisy, and single out the one design feature you are least sure of.
