I'm a postdoc in social and political thought, and in about ten days I have to present a working paper to our interdisciplinary reading group, whose regulars call themselves the Power/Knowledge and Its Discontents crowd. I'm using the session to pressure-test the paper's central claim before I send it out, and I'd like you to build me a question-and-answer prep sheet for the hour after the talk. My thesis, roughly stated, is that what Foucault calls disciplinary power is at bottom not a claim about visibility or the gaze at all but a claim about commensurability: discipline begins wherever an institution manufactures a standardized, comparable record of the person — a case that can be ranked, normalized, and moved along a scale — and the panopticon, which everyone fixates on, is a sideshow next to the examination and the dossier. On my account a school, a clinic, a prison, and a content-recommendation system can all be disciplinary in the same sense even with no central watcher, because what does the work is the template rather than the tower; and by the same token, surveillance that never yields comparable cases — a camera no one reviews, a form no one files — is not yet discipline. I call this the commensuration thesis, and the paper defends it against the more familiar visibility reading.

The room is the difficulty. Three of the regulars are Foucault specialists — one who works almost entirely on the prison and on Discipline and Punish, one who reads the lecture courses and cares about biopower and governmentality, and one who approaches the whole thing through power/knowledge and the human sciences — and they know the corpus far better than I do and will not relish being told that the panopticon is a red herring. Sitting with them are two analytic social epistemologists who regard the word "power" as it functions in this literature as close to unfalsifiable and who will keep pressing me on what observation could count against my thesis. There is also a historian of medicine who spends her days in nineteenth-century asylum and hospital records and will have strong views about whether the dossier really did the causal work I assign it, and a visiting STS scholar who builds models of algorithmic classification and will want to know whether the thesis earns its keep on contemporary systems rather than only on the historical ones.

Please draft the prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific audience — not the objections a generic Foucauldian or a generic positivist would raise, but these people, in these voices — ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumble them, with the likely questioner identified for each. For every objection give me a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I am bluffing; mark the one or two objections I should simply concede up front, and flag any point where two of the audience members will be pressing the same worry from different directions so that I don't answer them as if they were separate. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what my best available move is if it comes anyway.
