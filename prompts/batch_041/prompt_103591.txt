I serve on the model-release committee of a small nonprofit AI lab, and until this cycle the open-weights question was theoretical for me; now the model on the agenda is one I helped train, my vote is one of five, and the vote is Friday.

Here is what the committee actually has in front of it. We fine-tuned a base model whose weights are already public. The fine-tuning procedure is fully described in a paper we intend to publish whether or not the weights go out, and the dataset is small — a few thousand curated examples — such that a well-resourced group could plausibly reconstruct something close to it from the paper alone. What the fine-tune adds is a behavioral shift rather than new knowledge: given a patch diff for a memory-safety bug in C or C++, it will reliably attempt to write a working exploit, whereas the base model mostly refuses or flounders. The capability is real but conditional — it shows up mainly when the model is driven by a harness we also describe in the paper.

The committee has split, and I find the split itself interesting. One camp says that since the base weights are already open and the method is about to be published, withholding our fine-tuned weights protects nothing; a competent actor could reproduce them, so the only thing a hold accomplishes is to burn our credibility with the open-research community we depend on. The other camp says that argument confuses the existence of information with the accessibility of capability — that the stock of readily deployable capability is what matters, that every release lowers the floor a little, and that we should not be the ones to lower it for a marginal gain in reputation.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Friday's vote. It should recommend one concrete disposition — full release, an indefinite hold, or a staged release with named conditions and a date certain — and give the reasoning a colleague could act on. Three things I want it to do that I have not seen done well in our internal memos. First, say plainly which framework the committee should be reasoning within: whether we are estimating our marginal contribution to some global stock of capability, or whether we are asking instead what norm our decision establishes for the releases that come after this one, and defend the choice rather than sliding between the two. Second, interrogate a rule the committee has adopted without ever arguing for it — that the base model's existing public availability operates as a discount on our marginal contribution — and tell me whether that discount is coherent or whether it is doing illicit work in our deliberations. Third, engage the strongest version of whichever position I end up arguing against, so that the losing side recognizes its own case in the memo rather than a caricature of it. Keep the recommendation to something the five of us can vote on, not a menu.
