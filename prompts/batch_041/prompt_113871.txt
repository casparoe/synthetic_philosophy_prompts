In most legal systems, when the state takes your land for a highway it must pay you what the land is worth; but it takes a substantial share of your wages every year and owes you nothing in return, and in a war it can compel your labor for a fraction of what anyone else would pay. The same tax code counts your wages as income but not the rental value of the house you own and occupy, nor the value of the childcare or cooking you do for yourself, though both are worth real money; and yet governments publish tax-expenditure budgets that count deductions and credits as spending programs run through the tax code, as though the money were the state's to begin with and whatever you keep were a grant. What conception of property, and of the relation between a person and her own labor, does this pattern presuppose? Does it require the thought that one owns one's labor as one owns one's land, which would make the wage tax a partial taking standing in need of justification, or does it instead presuppose that pretax earnings are never the taxpayer's to begin with, so that what counts as a taking is fixed by whatever baseline the law adopts? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say where it is underdetermined between rival theories and whether its parts are mutually consistent, and tell me what would have to change in law, and in how we ordinarily talk about taxes, if the presupposed view were replaced.
