I have spent the better part of a decade writing about forecasting — the quasi-industry of people who assign numbers to political futures — and I have just been offered a nine-month fellowship at a university's journalism institute that requires a research proposal rather than a reporting plan. What I want to use the time for is a suspicion I have never managed to state cleanly. The "outside view," as its advocates present it, is supposed to be the corrective to the vivid, story-driven inside view: instead of reasoning about the particulars of this case, you ask what usually happens to things of this kind and take the base rate seriously. But the choice of which things count as "of this kind" appears to be made by exactly the sort of contextual judgment the outside view is meant to discipline. Ask how long a given empire or hegemon has left and the number you report depends entirely on the class you treat as relevant — all empires, all great powers, all polities that ever commanded some threshold share of world output — and each carving is defensible, each yields a different figure, and nothing in the method tells you which carving is right. I want to turn this into a proper research question, and I need the proposal drafted.

The question, as I would state it: is there a principled and non-arbitrary criterion for reference-class selection, or is the outside view at bottom a device for laundering inside-view judgments through a number — and if the latter, is that fatal, or does it merely relocate the intellectual work to a place where it can be inspected and argued about? I have been half-inventing two things I would like the proposal to work with, because they are what make the problem tractable rather than merely despairing. The first is a distinction between treating a reference class as a sample drawn from a population and treating it as an analogy set assembled by resemblance, where only the former supports the usual statistical inference and the latter is doing something closer to case-based reasoning that has been dressed up in frequentist clothing. The second is a reframing I cannot shake: reference-class choice looks like a bias-variance trade-off, since narrow classes are low-bias and high-variance and broad classes the reverse, which suggests the honest output of the outside view is not a point forecast but a sensitivity profile — the spread of base rates across every defensible carving — and that this spread, not the number, is the actual finding. There is also a selection problem I want addressed head-on: the historical record of empires and states is conditioned on having been salient enough to be named and remembered, so any base rate computed over it is a rate within a sample selected partly by the outcome, and I suspect this is where the technique is weakest and least discussed.

Please shape this into a two-page proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the standard defenses of the outside view leave it open, including why the obvious replies — that class choice is a matter of judgment refined by practice, or that any reasonable class will do — do not settle it; the method for a nine-month, non-empirical project, described in enough concrete detail that a skeptical reader can see what I would actually be doing week by week, bearing in mind that I am not going to build a database of empires; a clear statement of what a real result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a candid assessment of whether nine months is enough or whether I should narrow the scope to, say, the admissibility condition that class membership must be determinable without knowing the outcome. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I will supply the citations, and I know the literature better than I care to admit. What I need from you is the argument's skeleton: the framing, the moves, and the places where it might break.
