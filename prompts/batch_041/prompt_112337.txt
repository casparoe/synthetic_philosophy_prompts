I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy writing a chapter on how liberal states justify imposing technological risk, and a running argument with a friend who works on French grid planning has left me stuck on a contrast I can't get to the bottom of. Germany and France are both liberal constitutional orders with precautionary safety law, and yet they draw the line between acceptable and unacceptable risk in opposite places. In 1978, in the Kalkar case, Germany's Federal Constitutional Court held that the constitution cannot demand a licensing regime that excludes every possible hazard with absolute certainty: uncertainties beyond what it called the threshold of practical reason have to be borne by citizens as a socially acceptable burden, and the open-ended safety standard in the Atomic Energy Act, which ties licensing to the current state of science and technology, exists precisely so that protection of life and health can be tightened as knowledge advances. That doctrine was never repudiated, and yet Germany shut down eight of its seventeen reactors after the Fukushima accident, reversed a lifetime extension it had granted only months earlier, and finished closing the rest in 2023, replacing much of the lost output with fossil generation; nuclear supplied about 13% of German electricity in 2021. France, whose constitution has contained a precautionary principle since 2005 and which also closed its oldest plant, runs roughly fifty-six reactors supplying about two-thirds of its electricity, is extending the lifetimes of the rest, and is building new ones. The German reversal involved no new evidence about German reactors, only a catastrophe on the other side of the world; and the same constitutional court that licensed the residual risk has since insisted that the climate law must not push the burdens of mitigation onto future generations.

What I want is not a verdict on either policy but a reconstruction of the theory of risk that each presupposes. Concretely: when a state licenses a plant on a standard of minimizing risk as far as the state of the art allows and then accepts whatever residual uncertainty is left, what is it committed to — that risk is a quantity to be driven down and then borne as a shared cost, that the acceptability of a risk turns on the relation between who benefits and who bears it, or only that no regulator can ever know enough to do better? And is the difference between the two countries a difference of theory or only of parameters within a single theory? My suspicion is that both are working with a threshold conception on which some hazards are disqualifying as a kind rather than merely costly as a magnitude, but that they apply the threshold to nuclear power while using a purely budgetary, expected-value logic for coal, gas, and the risks of decarbonizing too slowly; I'd like you to tell me whether that is the right carving and what the alternative carve-ups would be. Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, say where it is underdetermined so that several incompatible theories would license the same statute, and tell me what would have to change in either country's policy if the implicit view were replaced by a straightforward expected-value budget applied across all generation technologies.
