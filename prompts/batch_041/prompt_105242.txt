I'm a graduate student putting together a session for an undergraduate seminar on Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons, and I've reached a conclusion I'm uneasy about but cannot locate the error in. My reading is this. Williams holds that an agent has a reason to act only if acting would be served by some element of his subjective motivational set — his desires, dispositions, loyalties, and projects, whatever he actually cares about — on the ground that a reason must be able to guide and explain what the agent does, so that a consideration which could never move him is not a reason for him at all but at most a piece of rhetoric, an attempt to get him to act by other means. I have been testing this with a case of my own invention rather than a textbook one, because I want the students to work it out from scratch: the Ombudsman, a hospital administrator who allocates scarce beds and who is scrupulous about the coherence of her own evaluative outlook and bitterly resents being managed by anyone, but who is entirely unmoved by the suffering of the patients she is allocating among. Nothing in her set is served by helping them, and on my reading the internalist must therefore say that she has no reason to help them, full stop — and that anyone who tells her she ought to is simply trying to install a new desire in her, which is precisely the manoeuvre I take Williams to be warning us about. On that reading, moral criticism of the Ombudsman amounts to persuasion dressed up as a truth-claim, and internalism collapses into a relativism about reasons that I doubt Williams wanted. Since I have to decide by Friday whether to present internalism to the class as a position that makes moral criticism impossible, I would rather not stand up and teach something false.

What I want from you is not a survey. Tell me plainly whether my reading is right or wrong, where exactly it goes wrong if it does, and what the internalist is actually entitled to say about the Ombudsman and about those who criticize her — including whether her own commitment to coherence and her hatred of being managed have any bearing on what she has reason to do, and whether the internalist can hold that she is mistaken about her own reasons rather than merely unmotivated. Take a definite position and give your reasons for it, and be candid about the strongest case that can still be made for my reading, since I may have to defend it in front of students who find it intuitive. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} whose contents name, in a handful of words, the mistake you take my framing to rest on.
