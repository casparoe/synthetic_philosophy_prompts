I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep catching myself and my sources reaching for 'the tragedy of the commons' to explain everything. In the last month I've seen it attached to a fishery collapse, antibiotic resistance, why nobody wants to pay for local news, why every journalist I know reads the same three newsletters, and why the group chat never picks a restaurant. I've got a feeling these are four or five seperate claims wearing one coat, and that some of them aren't really claims at all. I'm definately part of the problem here, so I want to sort it out properly before I file.

What I want is the menu. What are the distinct precise things 'the tragedy of the commons' could actually be asserting -- from the version that's just arithmetic about scarce resources and open access, up through the game-theoretic version, up to the strong normative one that says the only fixes are privatising it or coercing people? For each, tell me what has to be true for it to hold, whether it's a truism or a real empirical bet or just false, and whether it actually supports the conclusion the person reaches right after saying it -- because I've heard it used to conclude 'so privatise it', 'so regulate it' and 'so it's hopeless', and I doubt one sentence licenses all three.

A few things to keep in mind. I already know the obvious names (Hardin, Ostrom) and I don't need a reading list, citations or a history of the debate -- precisify, don't summarise. Second, I suspect half the uses I've seen aren't about something getting used up at all but about something being underprovided, and I want to know whether that's a different structure or the same one wearing a hat. Third, a source told me the whole dilemma evaporates the moment the players can talk to each other, which sounds too neat -- tell me what's right in it and what's too neat.

And someone in the office shut down a discussion by saying the commons is really a prisoner's dilemma and that anyone who says otherwise hasn't understood the payoffs. Is that true of all the readings or only some? If some of these situations are actually assurance games or coordination problems -- where everyone would prefer the cooperative outcome but nobody wants to be the sucker -- then what follows from the slogan changes, and I want that said out loud rather than glossed.

Format: a table, then two short bits after it. Columns: reading (short nickname for the precise claim); the claim (one sentence, no hedging); what must be true for it to hold; verdict (one of: truism / substantive and defensible / contested / false); does it support the conclusion people draw from it (yes / no / only with an extra premise -- and name the premise); test case (a real or invented case that pulls this reading apart from the others). After the table, one paragraph on which reading I should assume someone means when they give me no context at all, and one paragraph on what the slogan can't settle no matter which reading turns out to be the right one.
