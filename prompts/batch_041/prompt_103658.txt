My old roommate said "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" about pretty much everything — his band, his startup, a soup he made, the country. It came back up last week in a seminar where one person used it to argue that a corporation is a real agent that can be blameworthy over and above its employees, and another used it to argue that a mind can't be just neurons firing. I suspect half the time it's a truism and half the time it's a very strong metaphysical thesis, and nobody notices the switch. I want the menu of what that one sentence could actually be claiming.

Can you lay out the distinct readings, from boring to wild? Stuff I want sorted: is it just that an unordered pile of the parts doesn't include the arrangement, so of course the arranged thing "has more" (which sounds trivial)? Or that the whole has properties none of the parts have? Or that the whole is a further thing existing over and above the parts — and if so, what kind of over-and-above? Or that the whole does causal work the parts don't? Or — the one I actually care about — does it say something about when a bunch of things compose a whole at all, i.e. that some collections just don't add up to anything and others definately do?

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a real and defensible claim, or just false, and which reading the corporation person and the mind person would each need for their argument to go through. Give me a few cases where the readings come apart — a heap of sand vs a working clock, a pile of bricks vs a wall, a traffic jam vs a marching band — so I can tell which reading I'm actually tempted by. Bonus if you can say what each reading implies about whether a random scattered bunch of objects, like the set of all my left socks, is itself a whole of any kind. Assume I don't care who said the line or how old it is, I just want the options laid out as a list I can argue with.
