I built the following argument for a graduate seminar on value theory, and I have come to suspect that it proves too much. I would like a diagnosis.

Set-up. Specify a world by a population and a level of well-being for each person, on a scale where 0 is a life not worth living, 1 is a life barely worth living, and 100 is a life going extremely well. Say that world X is *no worse than* world Y when X does not rank below Y, and that X is *at least as good as* Y when X ranks at or above Y.

The argument:

(1) Consider the sequence of worlds W0, W1, …, W49. W0 contains 1,000 people, each at level 100. Each successor W(i+1) is obtained from Wi by lowering the level of every person already present by 2 and adding 500 new people at level 2.
(2) At every step the loss to the people already present is small while the gain — 500 additional lives worth living — is large, so each successor is no worse than its predecessor. Each step is either an improvement or, at worst, a change too slight to register as a worsening.
(3) *At least as good as* is transitive: if X is at least as good as Y and Y is at least as good as Z, then X is at least as good as Z. Equivalently, *better than* is transitive and *as good as* is transitive.
(4) By repeated application of (2) and (3), W49 is at least as good as W0.
(5) But W49 contains 25,500 people — the original 1,000, now at level 2, together with the 24,500 added along the way, also at level 2 — every one of whom lives a life barely above the threshold of being worth living. A world in which no one's life is more than barely worth living is plainly worse than a world in which 1,000 people flourish.
(6) So (4) and (5) contradict, and something in the chain must give.

Where does it go wrong? Of the steps I would most like to keep (3): I cannot see how *better than* could fail to be transitive, and if it did I would not know what to do with the notion of value at all. Of the steps I would most readily give up (2), if I must — but I want to know what that costs me, because the same judgment looks obviously correct at each individual step. Tell me which step or transition you reject, why it looked compelling, and what the live options each have to pay: abandoning transitivity, denying the step-by-step judgment, denying (5), or something else. In particular, say whether the argument's real weakness lies in a slide between *no worse than* and *at least as good as* — a slide that would matter only if two worlds could fail to be comparable — or whether the argument is valid as it stands and I simply have to choose which plausible claim to abandon. If you take the latter route, tell me which premise is least costly to drop and why the alternatives are worse.
