I am a novelist, about a third of the way into a book set among cosmologists, and the hinge of the plot is a discovery that turns out to be a false alarm. The episode I am working from is the 2014 one in which a collaboration operating a small telescope at the South Pole, under the name BICEP2, announced that it had detected a distinctive swirl pattern in the polarization of the cosmic microwave background — a pattern that, if it originated in the early universe, would count as evidence of gravitational waves from cosmic inflation. The announcement was made in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on March 17, 2014; the team reported a tensor-to-scalar ratio of about 0.20, a result excluding the null hypothesis at high significance (seven sigma as reported, 5.9 sigma after a correction for foreground emission). Within months confidence began to erode, and within about a year the community had concluded that the swirls were most likely produced by dust in our own galaxy. That is the shape of the episode as I have it, and I would like you to tell me at the outset if any of it is wrong or misleading, because my characters say these things out loud. What I want from you is not a history — I can get the history from journalists — but an analysis of the episode through a piece of formal epistemology I have been reading about, Jeffrey conditioning, also called probability kinematics.

As I understand it, Richard Jeffrey's point was that the evidence a scientist actually receives is rarely of the clean kind that lets you conditionalize in the textbook way. When you learn something with less than certainty — when a measurement moves your confidence in a hypothesis from, say, 0.7 to 0.9 rather than to 1 — the rule is not to condition on the hypothesis as though it were established, but to take a weighted average: your new probability for any proposition A is the sum, over the cells of some partition you have settled on, of your new probability for the cell times your old conditional probability of A given that cell. The update works by holding the old conditional probabilities fixed inside each cell — the rigidity condition — and moving only the weights on the cells. I also gather that the rule is order-sensitive in a way that ordinary conditionalization is not, that its verdicts depend on which partition you choose, and that Jeffrey defended it as part of a broader view on which nothing short of a logical truth is ever certain. If I have any of that wrong, correct me and then work with the corrected version. What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions: what the rule says the actors faced and what it says they should have done, which features of the episode it explains, and what the episode does to the rule.
