For the past term I have been in a reading group on mental causation — two philosophers of biology, a physicist, and me — and I have ended up defending a position I did not expect to hold. I used to think the causal exclusion argument was close to decisive against non-reductive physicalism: if the physical realiser of a belief already suffices for the behaviour, and nothing is overdetermined, the belief looks like a causal dangler. I have come round to thinking that the argument only bites if one assumes a production conception of causation, on which a cause must push, generate, or transfer something into its effect. On a difference-making conception instead, where a cause is whatever an intervention on it would change the effect of, holding the rest of the system fixed, mental properties are not excluded at all, because intervening on someone's belief is simply not the same operation as intervening on the neural configuration that realises it. So my position is this: mental properties are causally efficacious, the physical does not crowd them out, and the exclusion argument is not so much refuted as an artefact of a mistaken metaphysics of causation which ought to be replaced rather than answered on its own terms.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed that position actually is. What would count against it — which arguments, formal results, intuitions, or developments in neighbouring debates — and which of those should genuinely move me rather than merely irritate me? Please rank the objections, from the ones that would sink the view to the ones that would only make me shift in my seat, and be explicit about the difference. I am as interested in what someone who holds my view ought to concede as counting against them as in what critics say from the outside; I would rather hear the concessions a defender would make than a list of external complaints. And I would like you to be candid about whether the position has been insulated by its own manoeuvre: if I have defined causation so that the mental comes out efficacious, have I done any philosophical work, or have I merely relocated the problem into the choice of a theory of causation? What would distinguish a substantive defence from a verbal one?

Three pressures I would particularly like you to develop. First, the generalisation question: if the exclusion argument threatens not only mental properties but the properties of any special science, then saving the mental by switching to difference-making causation saves biology, economics, and meteorology too — tell me whether that is a cost or a benefit, and whether a defender could consistently save the mental while giving up the rest. Second, permissiveness: a conception of causation generous enough to let beliefs cause behaviour may also let in disjunctive, gerrymandered, or merely correlated properties, and I want to know whether that consequence is acceptable or whether it is the sort of commitment that tells against the view; what would move me most would be a demonstration that the difference-making account either fails to apply to mental properties as we actually individuate them, or applies so liberally that it licenses causation by any arbitrary property whatsoever. Third, the rival conclusions: a friend in the group insists the whole dispute is verbal and that the two sides are talking past one another — say what would have to be true for him to be right, since that too would count against me, and tell me which position my view collapses into if the strongest objections land, whether property dualism, eliminativism about higher-level causation, or plain physicalism with a decorative vocabulary. Please write in British English.
