I have no academic training in philosophy or in statistics, but I have been working through Keynes's A Treatise on Probability on my own, and I have gotten stuck on a distinction in it that I cannot turn into anything concrete.

Keynes treats a probability as a relation between a proposition and a body of evidence, so that "the probability of X" is incomplete until one adds "on what evidence." Alongside the probability itself he puts something he calls the weight of the argument: how much relevant evidence the judgement rests on, how substantial its basis is. Two people can agree that a proposition is exactly as likely as not and still differ in how much stands behind that agreement, and on his account new evidence can pull a probability down while still adding to its weight.

Here is the conceptual issue I would like an example of. If weight never enters into the probability, I cannot see what work it does. The obvious way to domesticate it is to say that weight is just a second-order probability — my probability that my probability is roughly right — in which case the two dimensions collapse into one and the distinction is either redundant or a slightly unusual way of talking about confidence in an estimate. The other possibility is that weight is a genuinely independent dimension of a belief, in which case it is not obvious how it should enter a decision at all: two options with the same probabilities and the same payoffs would no longer be equivalent, and I would like to see what breaks the tie.

So give me an example of this conceptual issue. I want a single worked-out case, concrete enough to carry numbers or at least a determinate structure, that displays the distinction doing real work — or, if you think it does no work, that displays it collapsing. Two constraints on the case. First, no coins, dice, or balls in urns; I want evidence of the sort that actually accumulates outside textbooks, heterogeneous in kind and partly overlapping, so that "amount of evidence" is not simply a sample size. Second, I want the case built so that the two readings come apart: a situation in which the judgement with the greater weight in Keynes's sense is the one with the lower second-order probability, so that the example cannot be quietly explained away by reading weight as confidence in the estimate. If you think no such case can be built, that is itself an answer I want, with the reason made explicit.

Then tell me which reading you think is correct, and be explicit about what would change your mind: what an opponent would have to show, or what a case would have to look like, for you to abandon it. I would also like to know where your reasoning is most likely to fail — whether the weak point lies in the case you construct, in the general argument, or in the way I have set up the two readings in the first place.
