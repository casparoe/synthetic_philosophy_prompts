I've talked myself into a view in the equality-of-what debate and I'd like you to try to knock it down, or at least map out where it's exposed. The view: the currency of distributive equality isn't welfare, resources, or capabilities as they're usually drawn, but what I've been calling a person's 'endorsable options' -- the set of lives they could actually lead and could, on reflection, wholeheartedly endorse leading. Two people count as equal when their menus of endorsable life-plans match, even if they pick differently from the menu, and even if one menu looks richer in some other currency. Part of the appeal for me is that it seems to handle expensive tastes and adaptive preferences without ruling them out of bounds by fiat, and it treats autonomy and welfare as tangled up rather than as seperate rivals. I don't want to be the guy with a pet view that nothing could dislodge.

So: what would count against it? I want the cases, intuitions, and results that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Does it quietly collapse into one of the views it was meant to improve on -- the capability approach, or opportunity for welfare? Which objections would a committed defender of a view like this concede as real costs, and which would they just wave away? Is there a case where two people have the same endorsable menu but one is plainly worse off, and does that kill it or only bruise it? And be honest at the end: has a view like this insulated itself against refutation through how it defines 'endorsable', and if so is that a bug or just the price of a view that's doing real work? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a clean case where the menu is identical but we still want to say the two people aren't equal -- if you've got one, lead with it.
