I'm a science-and-ideas reporter and I've somehow ended up as the person who runs fact-checking for our weekly long-form section. Here's the thing that keeps biting us: every sentence in a piece can be one we'd bet on — we hold each claim to roughly a 90% standard — and yet we all know that across forty checkable claims, at least one is going to be wrong, and we publish the whole thing as if it were solid. My editor says thats just journalism. I think it's a real problem and I want a procedure, not a pep talk.

So: can you take whatever the epistemology here actually says — the lottery paradox, the preface paradox, the idea that believing something is just being confident enough — and turn it into something a fact-checker can run at 11pm on deadline? I want it as a checklist, in order: how to treat a single claim at a given confidence level, when a batch of claims can be asserted together, where hedges go and how many a sentence can carry before it stops saying anything, and whether we should print some version of 'we know at least one thing in here is wrong' — and whether doing that quietly knocks the confidence out of every other sentence too. Ground it in the theory (no citations, I'll look up who said what myself), mark the steps that are real rules versus my judgement in a lab coat, and keep the whole thing runnable in ten minutes a piece.

Then run it on two cases. One: a feature where all forty claims clear the bar individually and the argument only works if they're all true. Two: a piece where one claim is load-bearing and it comes in at 80%. I want to see where the procedure makes me do something I won't like, and where it just gives up and hands the call back to me.
