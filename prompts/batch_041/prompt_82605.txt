I have just been seated on the board of zoning adjustment in a mid-sized American city, and in three weeks I cast my first vote on the application described below. Because I will also have to write the findings, I want to know what the board's own record commits it to before I decide whether to join a majority or dissent.

The board's decisions over the last eight years, as I reconstruct them from the minutes:

1. Ridgeline Commons (2017). A local builder in the middle of his third project in the city sought a variance for a four-story, 44-unit building on a corridor zoned for three stories, with 12 units reserved at below-market rents for thirty years. Abutting owners objected to height, shadows, and parking; staff recommended denial on the strength of a shadow study showing that one neighbor's vegetable garden would lose forty minutes of winter sun. Approved 5-2.

2. Ninth Street (2018). An owner-occupant sought a special exception to convert a detached garage into a two-unit accessory dwelling in a single-family district. It was his first application of any kind. The parcel met every other standard in the ordinance; neighbors objected about on-street parking; there was no affordability condition. Denied 4-3, the findings citing the established character of the block.

3. Fairweather House (2019). A nonprofit sponsored by the county sought a conditional use permit for a 60-bed transitional housing facility in a light-industrial zone, against fierce neighborhood opposition. The county's fair-housing analysis had identified a shortage of such beds. Approved 6-1 with a good-neighbor agreement on lighting and curbside management.

4. Cardoon (2020). A restaurateur of ten years' standing in the neighborhood sought an extension of patio service to one in the morning. The restaurant was fully compliant with the noise ordinance and had drawn no prior complaints; three households in the building directly above testified that they could not sleep. Denied 5-2, the findings citing the welfare of abutting residents.

5. St. Aedan's (2021). The parish sought a height variance for a 90-unit affordable senior building on its own land; neighbors objected to shadows and traffic. Approved 7-0.

6. Marina Tower (2021). An out-of-state developer making his first application in the city sought to build a six-story, 90-unit market-rate building on a parcel zoned for four stories, two blocks from a rail station. The documented harms were a ninety-minute winter shadow on a public playground and the loss of a view from two condominiums. Staff recommended approval. Denied 4-3 on scale and consistency with the neighborhood.

7. Ridgeway Arms (2022). A local firm with two prior projects sought to demolish a vacant, long-boarded sixteen-unit building to build seventy market-rate condominiums, with a $1.2 million payment into the housing trust fund. Approved 4-3, the majority finding that the payment would produce more affordable capacity in the city than the derelict building provided.

Two seats turned over in 2022, and both decisions since have been approvals.

The application before us. A regional community land trust, headquartered two counties away and with no prior project in this city, holds an option on a parcel occupied by a fourteen-unit garden apartment building. Eleven of the households are below half of median income, several have lived there more than twenty years, and the building's rear yard holds the vegetable plots they have cultivated for decades. The trust proposes 120 units, all restricted to households between 30 and 60 percent of median income, financed in part by a state grant that requires breaking ground within eighteen months. Eight stories, no on-site parking, on a bus corridor; the parcel is zoned for three stories and forty feet. The massing study shows a seventy-minute winter shadow falling across the adjacent public playground and the tenants' plots. The relocation plan guarantees every current household a right of return at its present rent, but construction will take thirty months. The tenants' association is split: six households have written to oppose, citing the disruption, the loss of the plots, and their doubt that the return right will survive a change of ownership; the rest support the project. Neighbors oppose on height, parking, and shadows. Staff recommend approval with conditions. The comprehensive plan's housing element, adopted last year, directs that at least forty percent of new affordable capacity be sited in districts whose affordable share is below the citywide average, and this district qualifies; the fair-housing analysis finds the city's affordable supply concentrated in two districts far from this one.

Two of my colleagues both invoke the right to the city. One means that the people who live somewhere have a claim to shape it and to stay in it; the other means that the people locked out of the city have a claim against those already inside.

What I need from you:

(a) the rules that fit the board's seven decisions, including any I would not have thought of and any that fit all but one or two of them;

(b) what each rule predicts for the land trust's application, stated as a vote together with the findings that would accompany it;

(c) your own best guess at how this board will vote, which rule is doing the work, and why that reading beats the others;

(d) which of the seven decisions is the closest analog, and why;

(e) for each pair of surviving rules, the further application that would tell them apart, specified concretely enough that I could place it on a future agenda;

(f) if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly and tell me what principle I would be adopting, and what I would be giving up, by voting either way. Give me a definite prediction rather than a survey of possibilities.
