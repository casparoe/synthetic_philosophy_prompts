My friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about how the major AI labs actually decide which values to build into a model, and we broke off without agreeing on what the practice even commits them to. I want you to help me reconstruct that. Set aside whether the practice is good or bad; I want to know what it presupposes.

The practice, as I understand it, runs roughly like this. A lab wants a model that is helpful and harmless, and it does not specify in advance what that means. Instead it collects judgments from human raters, who are shown pairs of model outputs and asked which is better; it fits a model of those preferences and then tunes the system to score well against that fitted model. In some versions the raters are steered by, or partly replaced by, a written list of principles that the model is asked to apply to its own outputs, so the operative values are in part authored rather than merely elicited. The stated aim is alignment with human values, but the working target is whatever this pipeline yields, and the pipeline must settle on one set of values rather than remain indifferent among the values people actually hold.

Here is what puzzles me. The labs describe the aim in the language of neutrality—serving human values, not imposing a particular morality—yet the procedure cannot be neutral, because it has to produce a single policy that the model will follow even when users disagree with one another. My friend finds this unremarkable. On his view the procedure is just a preference-aggregation mechanism, a kind of vote among raters, and it inherits whatever legitimacy voting has and no more; it presupposes nothing about values beyond a majoritarian or average-of-preferences standard. He also waves an impossibility result at me—that no aggregation rule over three or more options can be at once faithful to individual rankings, neutral among them, and immune to strategic misreporting—which he takes to show that no procedure could do better than his reading and that my worry is idle. I suspect the practice presupposes something stronger and more contentious: perhaps that there is a fact about which values a model ought to serve and that the raters' judgments are imperfect instruments for tracking it, or perhaps that legitimacy attaches to the procedure rather than to the content, in the way liberal neutrality is supposed to work. I cannot tell which of us is reconstructing it correctly, or whether the practice is underdetermined among our two readings and several others.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value, legitimacy, and aggregation that it only makes sense under. Distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Say which rival accounts it quietly rules out—for instance a substantive moral realism for which the raters are fallible instruments, a value pluralism that refuses any single ranking, a per-user relativism that would tailor the model to each user, a Rawlsian public-reason constraint, or a purely procedural majoritarianism of the kind my friend favors—and why each is or is not excluded. Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it equally. Identify any internal tension between the neutrality of the stated aim and the singularity of the trained policy, and say whether that tension is a contradiction or merely an unadvertised substantive commitment. Finally, for each rival you name, say what would have to change in the practice if we adopted it in place of the presupposed view. Treat my friend's aggregative reading and my realist reading as two live hypotheses: tell me which the practice better supports, and what evidence about the actual pipeline would settle the matter.

Give me your answer as a JSON object with the following fields: `practice_summary` (one or two sentences restating the practice as you understand it); `implicit_theory` (the commitments the practice presupposes, as a list of explicit theses); `strictly_presupposed` (the subset of those commitments without which the practice could not work as described); `merely_compatible` (views the practice fits but does not require); `rival_views_excluded` (each rival account, with why the practice rules it out); `internal_consistency` (whether the parts cohere, and any tension you find); `underdetermination` (where several theories would license the practice equally); `whose_values` (how the practice answers the question of whose values the model should serve, and whether it is neutral); `if_replaced` (for each rival, what would have to change in the practice); and `residual_uncertainty` (what one would need to know about the real pipeline to firm up the reconstruction).
