Hey, so next month I'm running a session for a room full of ML engineers and interpretability people. Sharp crowd, they can read a residual stream like a weather map, but almost none of them have taken a philosophy of mind class, and they talk about models as if there's a specific place inside the network where "the model knows it's Paris" -- layer 30, the logit lens, whatever. I want to teach them the difference between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model, and I need a plan for the lesson, not the lesson itself.

What has to land first for the theater to feel like a real commitment they can recognise in themselves rather than a strawman they can shrug off? I'm guessing they'll only feel the pull of it once they've caught themselves doing the layer-30 move, so I want that named early, but tell me if something else has to come before it. Then the dependency order: what has to be in place before the multiple drafts picture is even sayable, and what in that order is load-bearing vs just nice to have. For the carrying example I'm tempted by color phi -- the two dots, the colour change that seems to happen before the second dot has even flashed -- because it's vivid, but I suspect it's a trap: it turns the whole thing into a claim about the timing of a conscious event, and I'll spend twenty minutes refereeing an argument about whether the experience is delayed. So what should carry it instead, and what does that example need to have and need to lack (it cant just be "brains are parallel", they already know that). Misconceptions I expect, roughly in the order they'll show up: that the no-theater view is eliminativism, i.e. that Dennett is denying consciousness exists; that "multiple drafts" means there are literally several drafts each with its own little reader, or that it's just an ensemble / mixture-of-experts thing; that "no theater" means no self, so they're free to be nihilists about experience; and that the drafts story is really a claim that your brain rewrites your memories afterwards, so it lives or dies with false-memory effects. For each one, tell me where in the sequence it gets headed off and what I say there, and flag which single misconception, if it survives to the end, makes the rest of the session pointless. Then give me one question I can put to the room at the very end that tells me whether the idea actually landed -- something where the tempting answer is the wrong one and the right answer isn't just a recitation. Last thing: tell me what I should openly refuse to cover, so I don't get dragged into qualia or free will and run over.
