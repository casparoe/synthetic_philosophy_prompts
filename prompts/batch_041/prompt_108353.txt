I cover science and medicine for a mid-size daily, and I sit on our standards committee, which sounds fancier than it is. We meet Thursday morning and I have to circulate a written opinion Wednesday night, so I need this to be usable.

Here's the case. A colleague on the health desk has spent four months on a woman in her late fifties: pancreatic tumor, months to live, a pilgrimage with her church, and eighteen months later the scans are clean. Nobody disputes she was sick — the before-and-after imaging is in the file, and two oncologists with no connection to her church say the remission is real and unexplained by anything she was treated with. The diocese has opened one of its quiet inquiries, which is how these things go. The reporter wants to run it, lede: "doctors can't explain how...". The paper across town is definately running it Sunday as a miracle no matter what we do.

Two people on the committee want to kill it. Not because the reporting is wrong, but because they say "doctors can't explain" does the same work as the tabloid version with better manners. They point out we would never run the weeping-statue story out of Ohio, or the Indian holy man who supposedly hasn't eaten in decades, and if the answer is "our readers aren't Hindu," that's not a principle, it's a market. Two others say the opposite: we report facts, the scans are facts, and spiking the story because the explanation might be religious is exactly the condescension our readers already accuse us of. The reporter insists the honest word is "unexplained" and that "inexplicable" is a different word entirely, which I think is the whole fight in one syllable.

I know the Hume material the way most people do — the bit about it being more likely the witnesses are wrong than that the dead got up. A friend who teaches philosophy also says the definition is a mess on Hume's own view of laws: if a law of nature is just a summary of what always happens, then an exception isn't a violation, it's a correction. He says that's supposed to matter for how we write the story and I don't fully follow it, so use it if it's relevant.

What I want is a memo that settles two things. First the story: run, hold, or run with conditions, with the conditions spelled out — what the lede and the nut graf can and can't say, whether we describe the diocese's process, and what we do when the other paper goes first. Second, a rule we can apply again, because this is the third one of these in two years and we keep starting from scratch. I've been putting it off and now its due.

Be honest about the hard parts and skip the philosophy seminar. The thing that bugs me: a miracle is by definition a one-off, so you can't rerun it, which means the instincts that normally keep us honest — second source, wait for replication — just don't apply. Does that make us more careful or less? And if Hume's argument really does cut against all surprising testimony and not just the religious kind, say so, because that changes what the memo should say. Give the other side its best case, not a strawman.

No preface — start with the memo itself, recommendation in the first line, reasoning after.
