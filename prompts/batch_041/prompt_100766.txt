I'm a novelist — three books, no philosophy degree, which I mention because it's relevant to what I'm about to ask. For reasons I can no longer reconstruct I've agreed to run a two-hour session at a small residential writing workshop next month: a dozen people, mostly poets and fiction writers, no real philosophy behind them beyond one intro course a few of them took years ago. What I pitched, in a moment of misplaced confidence, is G. E. Moore's claim that the two things worth having in themselves are beauty (and the taking-in of it) on one side and friendship and love on the other, together with the strange machinery he builds around that claim — the idea that a whole can be more valuable than the sum of its parts, so that a gorgeous thing nobody ever experiences is worth less than the same thing plus a single person actually drinking it in; and the thought experiment where you strip the world down to one item at a time and ask whether that item, alone, is any good at all. I don't want you to explain Moore to me, I've read him badly and I don't need the lecture again. I want you to plan the session: the order the pieces have to come in so that the conclusion feels earned rather than announced, which single example should carry the whole two hours and why that one and not a painting or a symphony, and what that example has to contain and what it has to be missing for the whole thing to work.

Then the misconception map, in the order they'll actually arrive: my guess is they'll hear all of this as just "beauty is subjective," or as a fancier version of art-for-art's-sake, or they'll collapse it into "so maximize pleasure," and I need to know precisely where in the sequence to head each of those off before it sets like plaster. Tell me what to leave out and to say so out loud — I'm already tempted to drag in the naturalistic-fallacy material and I suspect that's a trap that'll eat forty minutes. And give me one question to ask at the end that would tell me whether they've got the shape of the view or have just been nodding politely for two hours. Be decisive about the order and about the example — definately don't hand me three options and wish me luck. If the example I've got in mind is the wrong one (something like: a world containing only an unperceived magnificent landscape, versus a world containing only a fairly ordinary one that somebody loves looking at), say so and pick the one you'd actually use, and defend it against the obvious objection. I'd honestly rather you commit to a plan I end up disagreeing with than hedge.
