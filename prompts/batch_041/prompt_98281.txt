I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I would like a case built to order rather than the usual round of fission and teleportation puzzles. Your task is to construct a single scenario that sets two conceptions of the self against each other under controlled conditions.

The first conception is the pattern view associated with Hofstadter: the self is a self-referential pattern, a strange loop, that admits of degrees and need not be confined to a single brain, so that a person's I can be partly reproduced and carried on in other people. The second is a single-track psychological-continuity view of the familiar Lockean or Parfitian sort, amended with an explicit no-branching or uniqueness clause: what preserves a person is an unbroken, non-branching causal chain of psychology, and identity itself is all-or-nothing rather than a matter of degree.

I want a case in which the two views agree on one verdict and part company on another. Specifically, they should agree that the original person survives as herself so long as she continues to exist, yet disagree about what to say of a second individual who, through a realistic and gradual process, has come to run a full, self-referential model of her — her voice, her habits of thought, her first-person standpoint — so that she is, in the pattern theorist's idiom, present in him as well.

Some constraints. The mechanism must be ordinary and human: no brain scanners, teleportation, cortical slices, silicon uploads, or any other science-fictional device. Think instead of what decades of unusually close mutual modelling — a marriage, a long collaboration, a parent and child — can actually do to two people's inner lives. The scenario itself should run to no more than a paragraph or two; the analysis is where the work should show. Keep the disagreement between the two views localised to a single variable, and make plain which variable that is.

Then do the following. State, for each theory, the verdict it returns on (i) the original and (ii) the second individual, and explain why each verdict follows from the theory rather than from your stipulation — a case that merely asserts the answers is worthless. Identify the one feature of the scenario that is doing the real work, and say what becomes of the disagreement once that feature is removed (for instance, if the original does not survive the process). Give the strongest line of attack open to a defender of whichever theory you have left worse off, and say how much of the case survives it. Finally, note briefly what would have counted as cheating here — what you would have had to stipulate for the case to be rigged rather than generated.

Write in British English. End with a single final line beginning Verdict: that names, in no more than fifteen words, the theory left worse off and the feature that does the work.
