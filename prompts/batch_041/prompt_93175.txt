I sit on the bylaws committee of a mid-sized member-owned agricultural co-operative, and we are drafting the standing clause that will govern how accumulated equity is divided whenever a member withdraws and cannot agree with the board on a buyout price. Because the clause is fixed now but will apply to departures years from now, neither side knows at the moment of drafting whether it will turn out to be the party with the stronger outside option or the weaker one. A consultant has modeled a representative dissolution as a two-person bargaining problem whose disagreement point is zero for both sides and whose efficient frontier is the convex hull of the points (0, 0), (0, 6), (4, 5) and (10, 0), with the first coordinate the withdrawing member's payoff and the second the co-operative's, both in thousands of dollars. On that frontier the five division rules we are considering land at the following points: the Nash product-maximizing rule at (5, 4.167); the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule, which equalizes each party's proportional attainment of its own best feasible payoff, at (5.814, 3.488); an equal-gains or egalitarian rule, which maximizes the smaller of the two payoffs, at (4.545, 4.545); an equal-sacrifice rule, which equalizes each side's shortfall from its own best feasible payoff, at (6.727, 2.727); and a straightforward utilitarian rule, which maximizes the sum, at (10, 0). We spent most of last Thursday's meeting arguing about this and got nowhere, largely because two members kept talking past each other about whether the clause should be judged by what it yields on average or by the floor it guarantees.

Please rank these five rules from most to least defensible as the ex ante default, that is, by how plausible it is that the members would have agreed to that rule before knowing whether they would end up as the high- or the low-threat party, and give your reasoning for each placement. I would like you to be explicit about how much weight a behind-the-veil argument should give to worst-case outcomes as opposed to average outcomes, since that choice seems to be doing most of the work in the disagreement, and to say where your own ranking commits you on that question. For each rule, also identify one concrete way a member could game it once the rule is known and its application is predictable, and whether that manipulability should count against the rule at the drafting stage or whether it is irrelevant to the ex ante case for it.

Please write this as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter to a colleague.
