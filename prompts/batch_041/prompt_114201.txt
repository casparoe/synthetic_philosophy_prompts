I'm prepping questions for a podcast episode — small show, one guest, about 45 minutes, no live audience — and the guest is a philosopher who's been developing a view she calls the two-books account of moral luck. I want the conversation to actually engage her position instead of rehashing the usual four kinds of luck for the hundredth time, so here's a sketch of what she holds so you can aim at it.

Her claim is that moral assessment runs on two seperate ledgers and that most of the moral luck literature is what happens when you try to merge them. Book One is about the act: did what she did conform to the norms she could reasonably have been expected to access at the time? Outcomes don't enter Book One at all, so it's luck-free by construction — no resultant luck, no circumstantial luck. Book Two is about what she owes afterward: repair, restitution, apology, making amends. Book Two is drenched in luck and she thinks that's fine, because debts aren't deserts. Her line for the episode is that luck can change what you owe but not what you are.

She also sorts the classic cases into three piles and says only the third is a genuine puzzle. The careless driver: really about risk imposition — the failure to check the brakes was already wrong, the collision adds nothing to the verdict, only to the debt. The Gauguin-style case: really about epistemic luck concerning one's own capacities, since whether he was any good isn't something he could have known. The third pile is cases where we revise our read on someone's character after the fact — the resister who turns out to have been motivated mostly by vanity — and she says that's not a paradox about responsibility but an artifact of how character attribution works, since we attribute traits partly by what they produce. Against the standard objection that moral luck is just epistemic luck, she says that move works for piles one and two but not three, because in pile three the relevant fact isn't hidden from the agent, it's hidden from us, and it's a fact about the agent rather than about the world.

Give me nine or ten questions, ordered the way you'd actually run the interview (open warm, then tighten), and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question pressing on whether Book One can really stay luck-free once you take constitutive luck seriously — if who she is fixes which norms she could access, hasn't the luck just moved house? — at least one on what work desert is doing if the debts are carrying all the moral weight, and at least one on collective agents, where a group does something wrong but no individual's Book One records it. Please also flag the question she'll most want to dodge and tell me why she'll dodge it, and end with a closing question about what would actually change her mind. No softballs about how she got into philosophy, obviously.
