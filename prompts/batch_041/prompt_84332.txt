Suppose Beccaria were handed the modern death-penalty debate. Three features of it postdate him: a retributivist revival that justifies punishment by desert rather than by deterrence; a retentionist practice in which executions are rare, arrive after decades of delay, and are defended with contested empirical work on whether they deter at all; and DNA exonerations, which turn the risk of executing the innocent into a measurable property of the institution rather than a rhetorical point. Say which parts of his case survive that world. What I most want is an answer to whether his premises can reach the innocence-based case for abolition without borrowing the deterrence claim — is the institutional-risk argument available to him, or does it sit outside his framework? If the econometrics came out in favour of a deterrent effect, what would he have to surrender: the abolitionism, or only the argument for it? He proposed a lasting punishment in place of death; would life without parole be the substitute he had in mind, or does the modern version fail his own test that severity go no further than deterrence requires? And what has he to say to the retributivists, given that he never supplied a theory of desert? Please write the answer in British English, and leave out the citations — I want the load-bearing structure, not the scholarship.
