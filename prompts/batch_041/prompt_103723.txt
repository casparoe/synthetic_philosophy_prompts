You are advising a program officer at a small foundation that has decided to commit roughly four years of funding to about eight researchers working on embedded agency — the cluster of conceptual problems that arise once an agent is understood as part of the world it models and acts in, rather than as a detached reasoner contemplating a world that neatly excludes it. The money is real, the researchers are capable, and the foundation has ruled out funding empirical machine learning; it wants theory, philosophy, and conceptual groundwork.

The program officer has adopted a deliberately blunt filter and wants you to apply it before you improve on it. A question counts as live only if a good answer would change how someone would actually go about building or evaluating an agent that must operate inside the environment it represents — as opposed to yielding another elegant impossibility result that everyone concedes and no one can act on. Use that filter, but say plainly where it misfires and what it wrongly excludes.

One candidate puzzle has been circulating among the applicants, and the officer wants to know how much weight to give it. Call it the self-model regress: for an agent to act deliberately it must represent itself as a causal part of the world, but that self-representation has to be produced by a process that is itself part of the agent, so either the representation must include the very process computing it, which threatens an infinite regress, or it must leave that process out, which leaves the agent systematically blind to something about itself. The applicants disagree sharply about whether this is a deep structural fact, a restatement of something more familiar, or a pseudo-problem that dissolves once the agent's self-model is described at the right level of abstraction.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Rank the open questions you think this program should actually be built around — the self-model regress among them if you judge it worthy, and whatever else you think belongs. For each, explain why it sits where it does: how many other open questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is for people doing conceptual work rather than experiments, how neglected it currently is, and whether its resolution would genuinely change practice in the sense of the officer's filter. Name the questions you think are overrated, or are distractions dressed up as foundations, and say why. Finally, tell the officer what evidence over the next two or three years would move any of these up or down the list, and where you would put the first year of effort if you had to commit before the rest of the agenda was settled.
