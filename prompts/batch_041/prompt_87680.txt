I'm writing the final for a senior seminar on institutions and long-run development — it's cross-listed, so I've got econ students and a few philosophy grad students who wandered in for the causal inference. 90-minute in-class exam, closed book, no phones, no internet. The essay section is going to be one question, 20 marks, and I need it to be markable by a TA who isn't me. My problem with every previous version of this question is that it collapses into students reciting the settler-mortality / reversal-of-fortune story and the geography-vs-institutions shouting match, and I genuinely can't tell from the scripts whether they understand what a design is doing or just have the literature memorised.

So I want the question built around a vignette I'll paste into the exam paper. Roughly:

In the 1890s two European powers drew a colonial boundary through the middle of the territory of a single ethnolinguistic group. Most of the boundary follows a river for about 150 km. But a stretch of roughly 40 km cuts across open savannah with no river, no ridge, no mineral deposit, and no obvious economic feature, where the line was fixed by a surveyor's straight-edge and a slightly disputed treaty map. On the western side, land was administered under communal tenure, with chiefs recognised as intermediaries and no formal registration. On the eastern side the administration introduced individual title registration in the 1910s to encourage a particular export crop, and that system survived decolonisation more or less intact; the western side's tenure was nationalised and then partially re-customised in the 1970s. A 2019 household survey finds that households on the eastern side report about 40% higher cash income, about two more years of schooling, and much higher rates of adherence to the missionary-introduced religion. The two sides are, by every ethnographic account, the same people, speaking the same language, with the same pre-colonial political organisation. There's also a paved road on the eastern side built in the 1960s, and a smuggling route that crosses the river at two well-known points.

What I want from you: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so that a student who's memorised the literature and a student who can actually reason about identification get clearly different marks; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; (3) a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, 4 marks each, total 20, with a one-line note per point on how candidates typically miss it, plus a short list of the wrong moves that should cap someone at a pass no matter how well written.

The question needs to force them to actually locate the identifying variation (and notice that the river stretch and the straight-edge stretch are not the same comparison), to say what the estimand is and whether "institutions" is even one thing here, to deal honestly with the schooling and religion variables (I keep getting answers that just say "control for them"), to say what the result does and doesn't license about the aggregate question of why some nations are rich, and to say what would have falsified it. Don't let the model answer turn into a lecture on why institutions matter — it should be about what this design can and can't carry. And nothing in the question can require them to know a specific paper or author, since they can't look anything up.
