I'm in my final year of a history degree, and our historiography module is assessed partly by a task in which we each set an exam question on an assigned topic, together with the answer we would want to see. My topic is Whig history -- the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The paper is closed-book and students won't be expected to name particular historians, so nothing should turn on attribution or citation.

The question has to be one a candidate cannot answer by defining Whig history, paraphrasing the usual warning against it, and listing three progress narratives. I want it built around two things. First, a distinction I've been using in my own notes: between present-mindedness (asking the past questions that only arise now) and directionality (treating the past as having been on its way to us, so that an episode is explained by what it led to). The candidate should have to say which of the two is the real vice, whether either can be had without the other, and -- the part I care about most -- whether the critique survives its own application, since the story in which historians gradually learn to stop being Whiggish is itself a story of progress toward the present. I've been calling that the second-order problem.

Second, a case, so that the answer cannot stay abstract: the historiography of the asylum, where nineteenth-century reformers campaigned for humane institutional care that later generations came to condemn, and which is now often placed on a road leading to community care the reformers would have rejected. The candidate should have to say what is actually wrong with reading that story as progress given that some things genuinely did improve, and then to sketch what a non-Whiggish account of a real improvement would look like. That last requirement is the crux: I don't want an answer that only forbids things.

For the marking scheme, use five must-hit points worth four marks each, with one line per point on how candidates typically miss it, and let the A/B boundary rest on whether the candidate offers a positive specification of a defensible present-minded history rather than merely a prohibition.
