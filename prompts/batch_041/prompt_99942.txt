I'm trying to figure out what a theory of rational choice under risk actually has to deliver before I commit to one, and I'd rather have the spec than another tour of the candidates.

Context, so you know what "adequate" is relative to here: I'm helping a health agency build a decision aid. Patients (and sometimes clinicians choosing on behalf of patients) are asked to pick between a treatment with a small chance of a severe outcome and a prevention programme with a larger chance of a milder one. The classic Allais pattern shows up constantly — same people, same stakes, flip the common consequence and their preferences flip — and I can't just tell the agency "those preferences are irrational" and move on, because the aid has to advise them either way, and because when we strip the framing the same person gives both answers.

So: give me the desiderata. What any adequate theory of choice under risk has to get right, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are just house rules of one camp.

I want the list to cover at least:

- the cases it has to handle — the common-consequence and common-ratio Allais pairs, the ambiguity cases where the odds are only bounded rather than known, choices where one outcome is effectively infinite or lexicographically worse (death, ruin), the St. Petersburg-style gambles, the fact that people who violate independence in a single-shot choice often reverse when the same two gambles are embedded in a longer tree, and the fact that people will pay to commit now against their own later re-evaluation
- the distinctions it can't collapse — risk vs ambiguity, preference over acts vs preference over consequences, being intransitive vs merely incomplete, small-stakes vs large-stakes risk aversion, the Allais pattern treated as a datum to be explained vs a mistake to be corrected, descriptive success vs normative warrant
- the formal results it can't contradict — stochastic dominance and mixture/eventwise dominance, the representation results that tie which axioms buy you which functional form, the money-pump and Dutch-book arguments, the tension between independence and any first-order (i.e. non-vanishing at the margin) risk aversion
- the constraints that come from my actual use case, which is where I suspect the interesting ones are: the theory has to be elicitable from a handful of questions a clinic can plausibly ask, it has to be robust to the probabilities being estimated with error rather than known exactly, it has to say something defensible when the agent is choosing for someone else rather than for themselves, and it has to keep working when the same person faces the same choice again next month and can anticipate that they may feel differently about it

Then the part I most want: flag any subset of these that can't be jointly satisfied. In particular, can you keep independence, keep the Allais pattern as a genuine datum about preference rather than an error, and keep the no-money-pump requirement, all at once? If not, say which one has to go and what the cost of dropping it is. I'd also like to know which single desideratum on the list does the most work in excluding candidates — the one that kills the most theories by itself.

You can finish with a short pass over how the main families do against the list (expected utility, the rank-dependent and cumulative-prospect style models, regret-based models, disappointment/divergence-aversion models, the ambiguity-averse families), but keep it short, I'll do the reading myself. Don't give me citations or a bibliography — I can't look anything up right now and I don't want you guessing at who said what. Just make the arguments stand on their own.

One more thing: a theory that only tells me the agent's preferences are incoherent is useless to me, because we still have to print the leaflet. So somewhere in the list I want a requirement about what the theory has to be able to recommend, not just diagnose.
