Draft the joint statement that a committed Hartian and a committed Devlinian could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, and write it as the document the two of them would have produced rather than as a summary of the historical debate between Hart and Devlin. Treat the Hartian as holding that the harm principle, properly understood, is the only legitimate ground for criminalization, and the Devlinian as holding that a society's shared morality is among the conditions of its continued existence and may therefore be enforced for that reason alone. Neither party may appeal to what the historical authors wrote or to any secondary literature; each must argue in its own voice, and the document must contain no citations.

Ground the whole exercise in one concrete statute. A jurisdiction is weighing a private member's bill that would criminalize the licensed, non-medically-indicated performance and procurement of irreversible body modifications -- for instance, tongue bifurcation, extensive scarification, and subdermal implants -- on consenting adults who seek them for aesthetic or recreational reasons. The bill's stated justification is not that any participant is coerced or deceived, nor that any identifiable third party is injured, but that the practice is degrading and that its open toleration corrodes the society's shared standards of bodily integrity and self-respect. Make this case the running example throughout.

Part I should be a numbered list of claims both parties accept. It must cover, at a minimum: the legitimate grounds for criminalization as each side actually understands them; the status each assigns to the harm principle, including whether either treats it as a necessary condition, a sufficient one, or neither, and what each means by harm -- in particular, whether the alleged corrosion of shared standards is to be counted as a harm to others or as a separate, non-harm ground; the distinction Hart drew between the morality a society happens to accept and the morality defensible on reflection, and which of the two each side invokes when it calls the practice immoral; what both concede about the case itself, including its consenting and self-regarding character and the fact that the practice is widely found degrading; what the dispute is explicitly not about; and at least one conditional bridging claim stating what would follow for each side if the alleged social-erosion effect were shown to be large and real. Reject any agreed claim that is merely vacuous or that quietly adopts one side's framing.

Part II should be a table with one row per residual disagreement and columns for: the question at issue; the Hartian's position, phrased as the Hartian would accept it; the Devlinian's position, phrased as the Devlinian would accept it; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the argument or case that both parties agree would bear on that crux; and each side's prediction about how it would come out. For the crux concerning social erosion, require the Devlinian to specify the mechanism by which tolerating the practice is supposed to weaken the shared morality -- for example, visible counterexample, a cascade, or a threshold effect -- and to name the indicator that would register it, and require both parties to state what they would count as evidence against their own position.

Close with a short section distinguishing genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones, naming any you find and explaining why, and stating plainly which disagreements cannot be reduced to any test and why not.
