For some years a colleague of mine in the action-theory group has been giving verdicts on a single recurring question — when is a failure to act on one's own judgment an instance of weakness of will, and when is it something else? — and before I put a new case to her I want to predict what she will say. Her verdicts on the cases she has been given so far are these.

She says there is weakness of will when a man who resolved the night before to decline dessert at a formal dinner, and who still holds at the table that he ought to decline, orders it and eats it. She says there is no weakness of will when a woman who had planned to spend Saturday finishing a paper learns in the morning that a close friend has been hospitalized, spends the day at the hospital, and never opens the file. She says there is no weakness of will in a man who formed no plan and reached no judgment about how much to drink, and who drinks steadily all evening while thinking his intake reasonable. She says a woman who washes her hands for an hour against her own judgment, and who describes the urge as alien to her, is not weak-willed but compulsive. She says there is no weakness of will when a man who had judged that he ought to train for a marathon revises that judgment after tearing a ligament. And she says there is no weakness of will when someone who had resolved to write five hundred words a day writes three hundred on a Tuesday, says nothing to herself about it, and is back at five hundred on Wednesday. What she has never done is state the criterion. When pressed she offers gestures rather than a test, and I suspect she is running one rule while several others fit the same verdicts.

The new case. Two people, both of whom know themselves unusually well. The first is a graduate student who resolves in September to decline a certain tempting invitation in November so as to finish a chapter. She has noticed over the years that her preferences reverse as such dates approach, and she says so. The reversal duly occurs: in November she does not merely want to go, she sincerely and reflectively judges that going is the better use of the time, and she goes. Nothing about the merits has changed; only her ranking has. She took no external precautions, reports no inner conflict, and does not experience the decision as a defeat. The wrinkle is that the considerations she cites in November in favour of going are precisely the ones she predicted in September that she would find persuasive then, and she said in September that she would be able to make the trip sound like the better choice. This is the pattern in which the same two options are ranked one way at a distance and the other way once the earlier one is near, because the rate at which the future is discounted is not constant over time — the pattern usually called hyperbolic. Whether an agent anticipates her own reversal, and whether she arranges in advance some cost or obstacle that makes the later choice harder (a commitment device), are what my colleague calls the questions of sophistication and precommitment.

The second person is otherwise the same, except that in September she anticipated the reversal and arranged a commitment device — a non-refundable deposit forfeited if she goes, and a friend who agreed to hold her keys — and then in November, with the same sincere judgment that going is better, deliberately circumvented it, buying back the keys and paying the forfeit.

Tell me which rules fit her six verdicts equally well, what each rule predicts about these two people, which rule I should expect her to be running and why, and which further case would decide between the surviving readings. Note also the possibility that the November judgment, though sincere, is not independent evidence about what is best, since it is the very state of mind she predicted in September; say whether that consideration should change which rule she runs, and whether it bears on her verdict or only on how we interpret it. I am not asking for a survey of the literature on weakness of will, and citations are of no use to me here. I want the criteria reconstructed from her verdicts, applied to the new case, and the residual ambiguity located precisely.
