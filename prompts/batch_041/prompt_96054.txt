I retired a few years back after 35 years as a fisheries/field ecologist, and since then I've been reading alot of philosophy — mostly philosophy of biology, since it's the one corner where I feel like I actually know what the objects are. I've gotten stuck on the function debate and I'd like an accuracy audit rather than a lecture. I wrote reports for years saying things like the function of a swim bladder is buoyancy control, and it never once occurred to me to ask whether that was the effect it had been selected for, so I'm genuinely not sure which side of this I'm on.

Here's the thing. I read an essay on this — I think it was a chapter in one of those trade books about biology and philosophy, and I lent it to my neighbour so I can't check the names — and this is basically what it said, as best I can reproduce it:

Both camps agree that when we call something a function we're talking about what it does for the organism, and the fight is only over whether you need the past. The etiological crowd — Wright, Millikan, Neander — say a trait's function is the effect it was selected for: the thing it did that made its bearers leave more surviving offspring back when it was spreading. The function is set by the history, not by whatever the trait happens to be doing now, and that's the payoff, because it lets you say a heart that can't pump is malfunctioning — failing at the job it was selected for — without smuggling in spooky purpose. The causal-role crowd — Cummins is the name — say forget history: a function is just the contribution a part makes to some capacity of the system it sits in, which makes it forward-looking, since what matters is what the part does now to keep the whole thing alive and reproducing. The trouble is that Cummins lets the investigator pick which system and which capacity to analyze, so on his view function statements come out subjective: the heart's function is to pump blood if you care about circulation, and to make a thumping noise if you care about the noises bodies make. A broken heart, on Cummins' view, has the function of sitting there not pumping. That's why most philosophers have given up on the causal-role account. The remaining problem for the etiologists is novelty: feathers were selected for warmth and only later got used for flight, and a brand-new trait hasn't been selected for anything yet, so on the etiological view it has no function until it's already spread — he calls this the functions-arrive-too-late problem and thinks it's fatal. His verdict is that the etiological account has won, that the only live issue left is how to handle novel traits, and that anyone who says the two accounts are just answering different questions is fence-sitting.

I'm inclined to believe him, partly because the malfunction point landed for me. But something feels off in the middle there and I can't put my finger on it, and I don't have a way to look any of this up right now.
