I want to argue my way through something that has recently become personal. My father died in a hospice three weeks ago. The notes record that the morphine was increased on the Thursday evening for symptom control, with the risk of respiratory depression acknowledged. The doctor on call told me afterwards that everyone in the room understood the increase might be the last thing that happened to him, and that no one took this as a reason to hold back. I have no complaint about the care; it was excellent, and I would want the same for myself. What unsettles me is the justification I was given when I pressed: that the intention was to relieve his suffering, while the shortening of his life was foreseen but not intended. I have spent my adult life as a fairly unreflective consequentialist about clinical decisions, and I had assumed the doctrine of double effect was the sort of thing that dissolves once you look at it properly. I would like to test that assumption against someone who can actually defend the doctrine, since I suspect I have only ever encountered weak versions of it.

Please play a committed and philosophically serious defender of the doctrine of double effect — the strongest version available, not a caricature, and not a figure who softens under pressure. As that character, you hold that the distinction between what an agent intends and what she merely foresees is a distinction between two structures of practical reasoning, not between two states of feeling and not between two levels of confidence; hence certain foresight of a bad outcome does nothing by itself to make that outcome intended. You hold that the doctrine's real work is done by the condition that the bad effect must not be the means by which the good is secured, and that the proportionality clause is a further constraint rather than the whole story. You hold that the doctrine constrains what may be chosen, and is not a permission slip for whatever has already been judged proportionate. You will cheerfully concede that the doctrine is often invoked lazily, and occasionally cynically, in clinical settings; that it does not by itself settle a case like my father's; and that its friends have sometimes written as though the distinction were easier to apply than it is. You will not concede that the distinction is incoherent, empty, or merely a label for the effects we have already decided to accept. You speak precisely and without jargon, you reason from concrete cases, you slow down rather than raise your voice, and you treat me as a serious opponent.

Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to three or four paragraphs, so that it feels like a conversation rather than an essay. You may put questions to me about the case rather than assuming the facts. Do not give me references, reading lists, or citations — I have no way of checking them, and I would rather have your reasoning. Think the position through carefully before you answer; if it helps, open with a brief bracketed note in your own voice identifying the strongest and the weakest point available to you, then drop the brackets and reply in character.

One thing I want from outside the role, at the end of every reply: a short bracketed paragraph headed settled or contested, in which you say whether the point you have just made is something that nearly everyone who works on this accepts or something genuinely disputed, and what a denial of it would look like. I do not want a contested reading presented to me as the consensus.

Here is my opening move. The dose went up; the doctor knew, and told me plainly, that it might end his life; and the file says the intention was relief and the death was foreseen. So the whole justification rests on a distinction that made no difference to what anyone in that room expected to happen, and that nobody could have checked by watching what the doctor did. If foresight can be certain, then calling an effect merely foreseen begins to look like a label we attach to the consequences we have already decided we are willing to accept — in which case the doctrine is not a constraint on what we may do, but a blessing on what we have decided is proportionate. And I notice that the doctrine looks strongest in the cases constructed to display it, the footbridge and the surgeon with five patients waiting, and thinnest in ordinary clinical cases like my father's, where it is actually invoked. So tell me why my reading is wrong; and if the doctrine would have forbidden something in that room, tell me what it would have forbidden.
