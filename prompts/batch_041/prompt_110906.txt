I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on decision theory — my thesis is on whether risk attitudes can be rational in their own right, so this is squarely in my wheelhouse and I keep going in circles on it. I've been trying to use Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility as a lens on real decisions, and the case I keep coming back to is the Challenger launch. Quick setup so we're on the same page (correct me if I've got any of it wrong, I'm going off the standard accounts): the night before, the engineers at the contractor that built the solid rocket boosters wanted to hold off, because the rubber seals in the field joints had a history of erosion and it was going to be unusually cold in Florida; their own management reversed that recommendation after NASA pushed back on them; the launch went ahead the next morning and the shuttle broke up about a minute in, killing all seven aboard. The investigation afterward pinned the failure on the seals and was pretty scathing about the schedule pressure. Now Buchak's framework, as I understand it, keeps all the expected-utility machinery but changes how probabilities weight outcomes: you rank the outcomes from worst to best, and the weight you give each one is the difference your risk function r makes to the cumulative probability of doing at least that well — r is nondecreasing, r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1, convex r is risk-averse, linear r collapses back to ordinary EU, concave r is risk-seeking. The point of the machinery is that a risk attitude becomes a free-standing rational parameter rather than something that has to be laundered through diminishing marginal utility of the good in question, and that EU (unlike REU) forces rational agents to be risk-neutral over utility gambles.

What I actually want is the case analysis, run in both directions. Set the decision up as the agents plausibly faced it — who the agent even is, what the acts were, the outcomes ordered worst to best, the probabilities — and then tell me what REU says about it. My hunch, and the reason I'm stuck, is that a convex r makes *not* launching look like the obvious call for anyone with a normal fear of losing a crew, which is awkward, because it was the engineers who wanted to hold and the people above them who went. So I want you to take each candidate repair seriously and tell me which one is carrying the weight: (i) the managers' probabilities just weren't the ones we'd assign with hindsight; (ii) the risk function was doing something a single global r can't encode — steeper on the catastrophe than any one convex curve allows; (iii) there was no single agent, so "the risk function" is close to a category error for a decision made by a committee inside a hierarchy; or (iv) REU simply can't reach the interesting part, because what went wrong was belief formation rather than risk attitude — a run of survivable flights got read as reassurance about the seals, which I think is Vaughan's normalization of deviance — and REU takes the states and the distribution as inputs and has nothing to say about how they were arrived at. Which of those does the case actually support, and where does it push back on the theory rather than just illustrating it?

The part I care most about, because it's what I'd need to write up: the Columbia accident seventeen years later looks like the same failure with different physics — foam, a hole in the wing, and again an organization that talked itself out of the worst case — and if it happens twice in the same institution, does that help the REU reading (the place just has a stubbornly non-convex risk function over its own missions) or does it show that all the explanatory action is in the belief-formation and organizational layer, with the risk function as a redescription of the choice rather than an explanation of it? I'd also like the straight expected-utility person's best shot at Challenger, and whether they can tell the same story without any of the extra machinery. Flag anything where I've got the empirical history wrong or where you'd want to check it — I'd much rather have a gap marked than a confident guess.
