I hold a version of Scanlonian contractualism that I want stress-tested. On my view, an act is wrong when it is ruled out by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and the only complaints that count toward rejection are individual complaints, each taken one at a time; numbers matter only as a tie-breaker among complaints that are alike in kind, so that no number of people each facing a slight loss of one kind can outweigh a single person's complaint of another kind. I want a map of what this position risks. Which cases would a committed contractualist have to concede count against it, and which would they legitimately dismiss as question-begging? I am especially interested in three pressure points: cases where the complaints really are alike in kind but the numbers are enormous; cases where the only complaints available are ones no single person can press, because each person's loss is too small for anyone to notice; and the fact that whether complaints count as alike in kind seems to depend on how finely the harm is described, so the same choice can come out differently depending on whether we treat both losses as losses of time or treat one as an hour and the other as a year. Tell me what would be decisive against the view rather than merely awkward, what it predicts that a moderate aggregationist or a hybrid view does not, what its defenders would themselves give up, and whether the tie-breaker has quietly converted it into aggregation — and if so, whether that is a defect or a clarification.
