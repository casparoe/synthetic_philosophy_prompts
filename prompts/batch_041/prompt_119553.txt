Hi — I'm a junior and I run the interview slot for our school's econ club (we bring in one outside guest a term and I'm the one stuck writing the questions). Next month it's a professor from the university in our town who works on the old socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek saying a centrally planned economy can't do economic calculation, Lange saying sure it can, just have a central planning board fix prices and let the managers adjust them by trial and error the way a competitive market does. I've read the Wikipedia version of all this like five times so I can say the words, but our faculty advisor says if we ask anything like "so, can socialism work?" he'll just give the same polished five-minute answer he gives at every panel, and I want to actually make him work.

The reason I picked him is he has kind of a weird line on the debate. In a talk I went to he argued the whole thing gets retold wrong: Mises's original point was narrower than the version people quote now, Lange basically answered the narrow version, and the argument that really does the damage is a later and different one about discovery rather than calculation — his way of putting it was that the planner's problem isn't that he can't solve the equations, it's that nobody knows what the equations are until somebody has already stumbled onto a way of doing things and priced it. He also kept insisting the real disagreement is whether it-didn't-happen can ever add up to it-couldn't-have, and that alot of Hayek fans quietly cheat when they slide between those. And he has this analogy he's fond of about a hospital administrator setting internal prices between departments, which I never fully understood.

Could you write me eight to ten interview questions, ordered so they build, that go at the actual content of his position instead of letting him do the standard recap? I'd like at least one on whether his discovery framing is really a separate argument from the old calculation argument or just a nicer way of saying the same thing (and if it is separate, what exactly Lange's trial-and-error story fails to answer); one on whether Lange's planning board secretly has to already know the list of goods and the production possibilities before it can start adjusting prices, i.e. whether there's a circularity in there; one on whether a board setting prices for its own managers is really like a market, given that in a market the prices are something a firm has to take as given rather than something it also gets to set; one on whether the whole fight is about information or about incentives and whether he thinks people have been talking past each other on that; and one that takes his own counterfactual line seriously and asks what happens to the discovery argument if you hand Lange a modern computer — does the argument survive better computing, or was it never about computing at all. Also his tic is that whenever he gets cornered he says the objection is assuming we're still in equilibrium, so please set things up so that dodge is harder to use. For each question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, and add one closing question about what argument or evidence would actually make him drop his reading of the debate, because I always chicken out of asking that one.
