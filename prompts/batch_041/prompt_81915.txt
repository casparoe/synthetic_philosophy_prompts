I want to audit an analogy that a colleague relies on, and I would like the audit to be thorough rather than polite.

The setting is an ongoing discussion about how to govern risky research. Many labs, firms, or individuals are each independently in a position to develop or release some capability whose expected value, judged from the inside by the actor, is positive, though a minority of actors will be wrong about that and the action will turn out to be harmful. The argument usually called the unilateralist's curse says that if enough actors are placed like this, the probability that at least one of them acts wrongly approaches certainty even when each actor is individually reliable: forty labs, each of which judges its own case correctly 97 times out of a hundred, still yields something close to a 70 percent chance that at least one of them errs. So each actor may be blameless and yet the group's situation is bad, and the usual conclusion is that there is a case for restraint, in the sense that an actor should hold back from acting unilaterally when it cannot be confident that it is better placed than the others to judge, or when it would prefer that every actor similarly placed refrain.

My colleague's move is to say that this is simply the tragedy of the commons with the sign reversed. The story is that the herder who adds a cow receives the whole benefit of that cow and pays only a fraction of the cost to the pasture; the unilateral actor receives the whole expected benefit if the judgement is right and shares the cost of the catastrophe with everyone; the pasture corresponds to the world's margin of safety; overgrazing corresponds to the accumulation of risky undertakings until one of them proves fatal. Since commons problems are, the colleague says, routinely solved by emergent norms rather than by central authority, the conclusion drawn is that the unilateralist's curse is no argument for anything heavier than bottom-up restraint and a bit of professional custom, and in particular no argument for a mandatory norm of conformity or for centralising the decision.

Please audit this. Give me the correspondence table first: what maps onto what, and at what level of description the mapping is supposed to hold. Then the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the conclusion being drawn and those that do not, since I do not want a list of incidental differences. I am especially interested in whether the source analogy quietly imports features the target lacks: whether the harm is additive in the number of actors or disjunctive; whether anyone can observe the state of the thing being depleted; whether the interaction is repeated with the same parties and the same feedback; and whether there is an owner who could internalise the cost the way a single herder owning the whole pasture would. Then tell me whether the colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a different analogy would do better, say which and why. A second colleague has suggested that the arithmetic here is really the multiple-comparisons problem, since the quantity at issue is the probability of at least one error among many; I would like your view on whether that comparison earns its keep, including where it fails, given that a retracted false positive is not a retracted catastrophe.

Finally, tell me where your own audit is most likely to be wrong and what would change your verdict. If you think the disanalogies leave the conclusion intact, say what would have to be true about the actors' feedback or about the structure of the harm for that to be so, and what kind of case would move you the other way.
