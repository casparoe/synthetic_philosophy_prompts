I'm the chair of a small lake committee in my town — a rep from the paper mill upstream, one farmer, the fishing club, the tourism board, a public-health nurse, and me — and we have to put out one report with one conclusion sentence about whether the lake counts as polluted and whether the mill is to blame. I have to sign it. Four of us are sure were right and we're going in circles.

Someone lent me a book on Jain philosophy and I've been stuck on anekantavada, the many-sidedness thing. Here's my rough version, correct me if I've mangled it: it isn't that everybody has their own truth, it's that the object itself has indefinitely many sides, so any flat assertion about it is one-sided, and the fix isn't "well, everyone's a bit right" but naming the respect in which the assertion holds. There's a formal scheme of seven qualified predications hanging off that, built out of is / is-not / can't-be-put-into-words, and a separate doctrine of standpoints (nayas), where a standpoint taken as the whole story is the error. I've been reading the little "syat" qualifier as "maybe" — I suspect that's wrong. And the standpoints in the traditional lists look to me like kinds of description (substance-wise, mode-wise, the practical view, the momentary view, a couple of verbal ones), not people. That worries me, because what I'd be doing is handing a standpoint to each of the five others, and those are interest groups, not categories of description. I also gather the Jains used the whole apparatus to argue that rival schools each grabbed one real aspect of things and absolutised it, which sits oddly with the tolerance reading everyone gives it.

So: what are the most important implications of anekantavada for what I should actually do — the wording of that conclusion sentence, how I run the meeting, and whether it changes the substance of the answer or only the phrasing? Rank them, with whatever's doing the real work at the top, and be straight with me about which of these are implications of the doctrine and which are just my committee's ordinary problem in Sanskrit clothes.

Take the role of someone who knows both the classical Jain material and how committees actually behave. Think it through before you answer — brainstorm the candidate implications first, then cut the list down. And tell me how confident you are in each one, and which single claim you're least sure of; I'd rather know where the floor is thin then get a tidy list. No reading list please, I'm not near a library.
