I am a third-year philosophy student, and this term I am taking a seminar on nineteenth-century German aesthetics; we have just finished three weeks on Schopenhauer, and the paper I want to write (roughly six thousand words, due at the end of the semester) is one my supervisor has asked me to sketch as a one-page proposal first. My half-formed idea concerns the claim that is normally treated as the summit of Schopenhauer's aesthetics. On the usual telling, painting, sculpture, and poetry copy the Ideas — the grades at which the Will objectifies itself — whereas music alone is said to copy the Will directly, dispensing with the Ideas altogether. I think I see why this is supposed to be startling, but I cannot get past the suspicion that the single word 'copy' is doing two very different jobs. In the first case it names a relation between a representation and a representable content, and the point of the artwork is that it is about something, however indirectly; in the second case, if music copies the Will, and the Will is precisely what can never become an object of representation — being the thing in itself, outside the forms of space, time, and causality that make representation possible at all — then either 'copy' has quietly changed its sense, or music has been credited with a cognitive achievement that Schopenhauer's own epistemology should rule out. A second snag seems to me to lie in the same neighbourhood: music is a temporal art, constituted by succession, tension, and resolution, while the Will as he describes it is not in time at all, and is moreover a striving that is never finally satisfied; so it is unclear what it would be for a temporal structure to copy an atemporal one, and unclear whether the resolution of a cadence is meant to correspond to anything in a Will that never rests. My professor remarked in passing that on this view the resolution of a cadence would have to be a kind of lie about the Will, and I would like to know whether that follows or is merely a clever aside.

Please turn this into the one-page proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer — I suspect the honest formulation is not whether Schopenhauer is right, but whether the two uses of 'copy' can be reconciled, and if not, which of his commitments has to give — and explain why the standard readings of the music section leave that question open instead of settling it. Then lay out the approach: what I would actually do, in order, across roughly eight working weeks; why a close conceptual reconstruction of the two copy relations is the right method rather than the obvious alternatives (a source-historical study of what he took from his predecessors, or a survey of what later composers and critics made of him); what a successful result would look like, since I suspect success here is a defensible taxonomy of the possible readings with a verdict on each rather than a defence of any one of them; and what would count as the project failing, or as its turning out to be too large for a term paper. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — I will supply the references myself — and I would be grateful if you would flag any point at which I am leaning on a distinction that Schopenhauer himself would not have recognised.
