I am designing the opening session of a graduate seminar on the Eleatic legacy, and I want to begin with a diagnostic exercise rather than the customary historical survey. I should therefore like you to sort the four canonical paradoxes of motion — the Dichotomy (or Racetrack), Achilles and the Tortoise, the Arrow, and the Stadium (the Moving Rows) — into tiers according to how much genuine philosophical difficulty each retains once the standard modern dissolution, via the mathematics of convergent series and the continuum, is simply granted. Treat "genuine difficulty" as the residual puzzlement that survives even for someone entirely at home with the calculus and with the at-at theory of motion: a paradox that evaporates under that treatment belongs in the lowest tier, whereas one that still forces a substantive choice between metaphysical packages belongs near the top.

To keep the exercise from degenerating into a recitation, I should like the ranking performed twice, against two different background ontologies, with the two orderings then compared. First, assume that space and time are continuous and infinitely divisible, as we ordinarily do. Second, assume instead that space and time are discrete — that there is a smallest interval and a smallest distance — and ask how each paradox fares once infinite subdivision is simply unavailable. I am particularly interested in whether any paradox that looks shallow on the first pass becomes deep on the second, or vice versa, and in what that tells us about which of the four are really arguments about the continuum and which are arguments about the instantaneous character of motion. For each paradox, please give a sentence or two of justification, indicate where it lands in each of the two orderings (ties are permitted), and close with a brief note on whether any two of the four collapse into a single underlying argument once this distinction is applied. Kindly write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with the secondary literature or with citations — I am after your reasoning, not a bibliography.
