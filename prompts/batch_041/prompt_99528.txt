I lead the assessment unit at a national agency that must authorise chemicals and biological products before they are sold or released, and I need a written decision procedure my case officers can run when a notification arrives. Our statute permits us to restrict a product where there are reasonable grounds for concern that it could cause serious or irreversible harm, and it states that a lack of full scientific certainty is not by itself a reason to postpone protective measures; it also requires that any restriction be proportionate and that we publish reasons a court can review. We see roughly forty notifications a year on a six-week statutory clock, with no budget for new testing and with most of the underlying studies funded by the notifier. My officers are competent scientists rather than philosophers, and my director signs the final decision.

Write the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the process terminates in a decision (including termination in refusal), and the points at which it must be handed up because the answer turns on values rather than evidence. It has to preserve whatever is genuinely defensible in the precautionary principle without collapsing into a general injunction to be careful, and it has to survive the objection that protective measures carry risks of their own, so that the principle cannot by itself select action over inaction. Where the procedure must fix a threshold — how much evidence, how much uncertainty, how much irreversibility — say so plainly and mark it as a choice rather than a finding. Note which reading of the principle each step presupposes, and flag any step that goes beyond what our statute actually requires. The case officer's version should fit on one page; the version that goes into the file can spell out the reasoning.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a flame retardant proposed for children's mattress foam, supported by a plausible mechanism, a single small rodent study and structural similarity to a compound we already restrict, with substitutes that are themselves poorly characterised; (b) a self-replicating engineered soil microbe for nitrogen fixation, whose harms, if they materialised, would be irreversible and would cross borders, and for which the evidence is entirely model-based; (c) a drinking-water treatment that removes a suspected carcinogen but produces a better-characterised disinfection by-product.

I will be candid about my priors: I want the procedure to come out against (b), because I think irreversibility should be decisive. If a procedure I could actually defend before a reviewing court does not get me there, I need to know that now.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do, and where a case officer will be tempted to use it to launder a decision that was reached on other grounds.
