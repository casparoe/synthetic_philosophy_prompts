I write fiction, and for the past year I have been circling a question that I suspect belongs less to craft than to philosophy: which displays of commitment a reader will actually believe, and why. I keep handing my characters gestures and then finding, in revision, that some of them land as genuine and others ring hollow, and I cannot yet say what separates them. What follows is a run of my intuitions, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

The cases, as I feel them:

A woman of modest means sells the tools of her trade — the only asset she has — to fund a friend's escape. I read this as a real declaration of loyalty.

A billionaire writes a check of precisely the same size, for the same purpose. I read this as almost nothing: courteous, perhaps, but not a declaration of anything.

A man weeps while lying to the woman he loves. The tears are involuntary and cost him nothing, yet I read them as evidence of something true.

A man fasts publicly for a cause. The fast is genuinely unpleasant, and anyone could undertake it equally, and I find I read it as little more than theater.

A woman spends twenty years and a fortune building a garden. I am honestly unsure whether I read this as a statement about her character or merely as a fact about her wealth and leisure.

A man wades into a flood to pull out a stranger. I read this as the strongest kind of declaration.

A man makes a great show of renouncing an inheritance and then, through a side door, ends up with it anyway. I read this as no declaration at all, even though the show was costly.

The pattern I half-see — and I offer it only so that you have something to attack — is this: a display reads as a true signal when faking it would cost the faker more than it costs the one who is not faking. On that reading, the woman's sacrifice counts because a disloyal woman would have to give up what she actually needs, while the billionaire's check costs a disloyal man and a loyal one alike. It seems to explain why the fast moves me so little, since a fraud can endure it as easily as a believer.

But I am not satisfied, and I would like you to test it rather than defend it. Does it survive the weeping man, whose tears are cheap and yet convincing? Does it survive the garden, which is ruinously expensive to fake but may not be signaling anything about character at all? Is there a single principle that keeps all my verdicts, or must I surrender one of them — and if so, which, and what does the loss cost me? State the principle or principles precisely, tell me which case compels each clause, and then say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a character who keeps a promise at real personal cost when no one is watching, and lets the fact surface only years later.

Two requests about how to answer. First, please write in American English. Second, I would rather be told that my cases are incoherent or that no tidy principle is available than be handed a smooth synthesis I cannot actually use. If the framing is confused — if, say, I am quietly conflating what a signal costs with what it proves — say so plainly and tell me where the confusion lies, instead of accommodating me.
