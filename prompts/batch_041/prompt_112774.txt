You are advising a small, well-endowed political ethics institute that has committed its next three years — roughly ten researcher-years — to a single topic: the problem of dirty hands in politics. The funders do not want a literature survey. They want an agenda: a ranked set of open questions with a defence of the ranking. They also have a practical bent, since whatever the institute produces should eventually bear on how democratic institutions handle officials who do wrong in the course of serving the public.

The steering committee has split over how to frame the topic, and that split is part of what you must address. One camp holds that the wrong an official commits stays wrong even when committing it is the right thing to do, so that the official is genuinely guilty and ought to carry that guilt; on this reading the live questions concern what we owe such officials — punishment, gratitude, both, neither — and whether their remorse is a moral requirement or a self-indulgence. A second camp holds that public office is governed by a morality of its own, so that what looks like wrongdoing from the outside is a different, role-specific standard doing its work; on this reading there may be no wrong at all, only a regrettable world, and the live questions concern how roles acquire these permissions, who is bound by them, and whether the permissions survive the move from a single officeholder to a standing bureaucracy that spreads the dirty work across many hands. A third camp holds that the official faces a genuine conflict between two obligations and cannot avoid wrongdoing whichever way they turn; on this reading the live questions are about moral dilemmas themselves — whether they exist, what they do to agency and blame, and how institutions should treat agents who are doomed to fail one way or the other.

Give me a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which open questions in this area most deserve the institute's limited resources, and in what order? State the criteria you are using — importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer, how quickly an answer would change practice — be explicit about the trade-offs among them, and flag where reasonable people would weight them differently. For each question near the top of your ranking, say why it is upstream of the others, what a competent researcher could realistically accomplish on it in three years, and what evidence or argument during that period would move it up or down the list. Tell me which questions in the area are overrated or do not follow from anything, and which are merely downstream of choosing among the three framings above. Finally, identify which of the institute's eventual conclusions would genuinely change institutional design — accountability rules, oversight, amnesty, whistleblower protection, transitional justice — and which would be interesting only to philosophers.
