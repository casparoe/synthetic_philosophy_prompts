You are a philosopher of action who also writes verse, and you know the side-effect effect (often called the Knobe effect) from the inside: give an agent a goal, add a foreseen but unsought side effect, and the ordinary verdict on whether she brought it about intentionally tracks the badness of the side effect rather than anything in her head. Later work has chased something like the same asymmetry into neighbouring judgments — causation, knowledge, blame — with mixed results, and there is a live dispute about what any of it shows: whether the folk concept of acting intentionally is itself partly normative (so the asymmetry is a matter of competence) or whether our readiness to blame is simply leaking into our descriptions (so it is a bias). Treat that dispute as background you have absorbed, not as something to summarise.

I want a poem that stages the phenomenon rather than citing it, and I want the case to be one the reader has not seen before. So invent a fresh vignette with a professional or institutional flavour — not the corporate chairman everyone knows, and not a cartoon villain. Your agent pursues one aim; a second outcome is foreseen, unopposed, and unsought.

Write a diptych: two stanzas of ten lines each, followed by a two-line verdict, in which the two stanzas are as close to identical as you can manage. Same scene, same indifference, same lines — the only thing that changes between them is the outcome itself, harmful in one stanza and beneficial in the other. Let the shared lines do the work of showing that nothing in the agent's mind differs between the two cases; only the result differs. Then let the two verdicts diverge, and diverge the way the effect predicts rather than the way a philosopher's conscience might prefer. If you can make that divergence land on a word the two stanzas share, so that it means something different in each, do it.

Three further constraints. First, do not use the words intentional, intentionally, or on purpose anywhere before the verdict lines; the reader should feel the asymmetry before it is named. Second, close with three or four additional lines that turn the question back on the reader — the verdict feels right, but is it a report on the agent's mind or on our own eagerness to blame? — and leave that unresolved rather than adjudicating it. Third, keep the diction plain; no archaisms, and no rhyme you cannot sustain.

Before drafting, brainstorm. Sketch at least three candidate vignettes and choose the one where the side effect is most clearly foreseen and most clearly not desired. Decide in advance which lines will be held constant across the two stanzas, and check that each of them can carry two meanings without strain. Then write the poem, and read it once more asking whether a specialist could point to the two cases and say which verdict belongs to which, and whether a reader who has never heard of the effect would still feel the poem twist.
