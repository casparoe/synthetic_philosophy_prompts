The members of the OECD's Development Assistance Committee report each year what they have given as "official development assistance", and the rules determining what may be counted seem to me more revealing than the totals. The category is defined as flows of official financing whose principal purpose is the development and welfare of poor countries. No country is obliged to give, and the accepted measure of seriousness is a target — 0.7 per cent of the donor's own gross national income — rather than anything owed to or received by any particular recipient. Several items count which either never leave the donor's territory or cost it very little. Some of the costs a state incurs in receiving and processing refugees and asylum-seekers on its own soil may be reported as aid. So may the writing-off of debts, with the amount forgiven entered as assistance from the creditor, including in cases where repayment had become unlikely in any event. A concessional loan counts, provided at least a quarter of its value is effectively a gift. And aid may be tied to the purchase of the donor's own goods and services — a practice the Committee has repeatedly recommended against but never prohibited.

What I want is an analysis of the conception of international obligation that this accounting presupposes, rather than a general survey of the ethics of aid. I should be grateful for answers to the following.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory: what exactly is owed, by whom, to whom, and what counts as discharging the obligation? Explain in particular what follows from the obligation's being scaled to the donor's own income, rather than to the recipients' needs, to a shortfall specifiable independently of any donor, or to a harm the donor has itself done.

Second, say what work the notion of a voluntary transfer is doing here, and whether counting domestic expenditure on refugees, and the cancellation of debts unlikely to be repaid, is a mere bookkeeping convention or a commitment to the view that the obligation is a duty of beneficence assessed from the giver's standpoint rather than a duty of justice owed to determinate people.

Third, say which accounts of what the rich owe the poor the practice rules out, and which it merely fits awkwardly. Is there a coherent theory — a Kantian imperfect duty of beneficence, a sufficiency-based duty of assistance, or some other — that licenses all of these features at once, or is the accounting internally inconsistent? Donors speak of their targets as commitments while counting expenditure they would have incurred regardless, and while attaching conditions that benefit their own exporters; is that a genuine inconsistency or only an appearance of one?

Fourth, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and tell me where it is underdetermined, so that incompatible theories of obligation would license exactly the same reporting rules.

Fifth, if the presupposed conception were replaced by a restitution-based one (the rich owe compensation for a global order that harms the poor), or by a recipient-centred or outcome-based one, state precisely which features of the accounting would have to change, which could survive unchanged, and what the corresponding measure would look like.

Please write your answer in British English.
