I run a monthly discussion group at a public library — about fifteen adults, none of them with academic training to speak of, and I get two hours with them including a break. Lately I've been reading about an argument among historians over whether epidemics, shifts in climate, and volcanic eruptions should count as causes in their own right or only as the backdrop against which people made their choices, and I've found that I can't quite say what the difference is supposed to be. I'd like my group to run into that difficulty themselves instead of hearing me summarize it. Please act as an expert in the philosophy and history of causal explanation and in designing activities that teach by making people do something rather than listen, and help me build an activity I can actually run.

Before you design anything, think this through carefully and brainstorm a little for yourself: what are we actually doing when we single out one thing as the cause of an event? What would have to be true for a non-human factor to be an actor rather than a mere condition? And why do historians grow nervous when nature is handed the leading role — what is the trap they are afraid of? Work that out first, then design the activity around it.

The activity should be built on an invented case, not a famous one. I don't want my group leaning on a story they half-remember, and I don't want them rewarded for recall. Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve: over roughly forty years, a farming and trading town in a river valley empties out. Give me a packet of evidence cards — say a run of cold, wet years; a blight in the staple crop; the river shifting course; a distant volcanic eruption that dims the summers; a council decision to raise tolls; an outside power rerouting a trade route; an outbreak of disease. Each card should be individually plausible and none of them decisive, and the cards should be written so that no team can simply check the others' claims.

Then the rounds. In the first, teams of three each receive one card and must argue that their factor was the real cause, lobbying the others; end it with a secret ballot that names a single prime cause. In the second, change the rule: each team must now argue that the outcome would have happened anyway without their factor, and the room must decide whether the town's end was inevitable or contingent. In the third, introduce one late piece of evidence — a letter showing the council was offered a way out and refused it — and let them keep or overturn their verdict. Finally, ask them who, if anyone, is to blame, and let the room slide from cause to blame while you say nothing.

What I need from you: the full case packet with the text of every card; the roles and the exact rules and timing for each round, budgeted against two hours; how the ballot should be run and how ties should be handled; and a debrief that maps each round onto a piece of the underlying problem — the difference between a necessary and a sufficient condition, why the same factor counts as a cause under one standard of explanation and a mere condition under another, what is seductive about a single environmental explanation and what it costs (how it makes outcomes look inevitable and human choices beside the point), and where causing something comes apart from being to blame. I'd also like you to predict what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and to be frank about which feature of the problem the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own words. Do not name the technical term for the trap until the debrief, and give me a facilitator's script for the first three minutes and for the debrief only.
