I write a column on science and ideas, and I've reached the point in a piece where I need a number rather than a summary of a debate. The subject is the old suspicion that the morally best life may not be the best life for the person living it — the moral-saints problem, for readers who know it, and for the rest, the thought that there may be a ceiling on how much of a person's life morality may legitimately claim.

I want your credence in one tightly stated claim. Compare two lives, identical in length and in every non-moral respect that bears on well-being — health, friendship, curiosity, aesthetic pleasure, achievement — and differing only as follows. In the saintly life, every discretionary choice the agent makes is the choice impartial morality most requires, and she reserves none of the world's resources for a non-moral project of her own. In the decent life, the agent discharges her ordinary obligations and spends the bulk of her discretionary time and resources on non-moral projects she loves. Stipulate that the saint feels no frustration, regrets nothing, and endorses her life from the inside. What is your credence that the saintly life is, on balance, worse for the person living it than the decent life?

Give me the decomposition rather than a bare number — a probability and one line of justification for each of the following: (a) that "better for the person living it" is determinate enough for the comparison to have a truth value, rather than a category mistake or an incomplete question; (b) that the crowding-out of non-moral goods is conceptual rather than merely psychological — that a saintly life cannot in principle contain them, as opposed to merely tending not to; (c) that the moral worth of one's own actions is itself a constituent of one's well-being, and how much of the gap it can close if so; (d) that the saint's own endorsement settles the matter rather than being a symptom to be explained away; (e) whether, given that the saint is stipulated to be content, the whole comparison collapses into the question of whether non-moral goods are good for a person independently of her wanting them — and if so, whether that makes the claim easier or harder to defend; and (f) any term you think belongs that I have not named. Then combine the terms, tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to, and name the single argument or piece of evidence that would move your number most.

For what it's worth, my editor puts the claim near 0.8 and I am nearer 0.35. If your decomposition licenses a verdict, say which of those two numbers it makes more defensible, and where you think the two of us are actually disagreeing.
