OK so I've been trying to write something about the Death in Damascus case and I want your comments on the draft notes below. Here's the case as I'm setting it up. You're in Damascus. Death (or a perfectly reliable predictor of your actions, whatever you want to call it) tells you it has an appointment with you. The way it works: it forecasts what you'll decide, and then it turns up wherever that forecast says you'll be, and it's never wrong. You can stay in Damascus or ride for Aleppo. It doesnt matter which you pick, because whichever one you pick, the forecast will have said so, and Death will be waiting there. You die either way. That's the whole thing.

My thesis: people call this a hard problem for decision theory, and I think that's wrong — it's not a hard problem, it's a trivial one, and the 'paradox' is a fact about a certain way of deliberating, not a fact about the choice. (The causal/evidential business is the frame I have in mind: one side says only causal influence counts when you weigh up acts, the other says you just condition on the act and see what the world looks like.) Here's the argument.

Stated as a choice — acts on one side, outcomes on the other — the case has an obvious answer: both acts end with you dead. There's no deliberation to do. The reason it feels like there is, is that a particular deliberative habit kicks in. You notice yourself leaning towards staying, you take that lean as evidence about what the forecast said, you conclude Death is probably in Damascus, so fleeing looks better; then you notice yourself leaning towards fleeing, take that as evidence, conclude Death is in Aleppo, and staying looks better; and round you go. This loop is not a discovery about the case. It's what happens when you let your own current impulse count as evidence about the world and then re-optimise on the updated belief, over and over. Any case where your act is evidence about the state will do this to you — Death in Damascus just makes it vivid, because the correlation is perfect.

Two things I want to hang off that. First, the money-pump version: offer the agent a £1 ticket that pays £11 if it survives. The loopy agent buys it, becuase at the point where it's balanced between the two cities it reckons it has a 50% chance of living, when by hypothesis its chance is zero. So the deliberative procedure isn't just non-convergent, it's incoherent — it assigns itself probabilities of survival that the case rules out. Second, the case is under-described, and the under-description is doing the work. 'Death forecasts your decision' — when? If the forecast is made before you deliberate and targets your pre-deliberative state, fine, you're doomed. But if there's any genuinely indeterministic step left after the forecast — a coin you flip at the last minute, say — then you survive half the time, and the doom was an artefact of assuming the forecast catches everything. So the paradox is bought with an unstated idealisation, and the interesting philosophical question is what to say when you drop it.

Upshot: the case is best read as a constraint on theories of deliberation — how an agent should manage its own impulses and its own evidence while it's deciding — rather than on theories of right action, which is where the causal/evidential framing puts it. And I think that's why the standard framing misses what's good about the case.
