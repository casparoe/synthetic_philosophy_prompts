A single image keeps recurring in my reading group's discussions of surrogate goals: the surrogate goal is a scapegoat, an animal that really belongs to the herd and whose loss really costs something, but which the community can afford to have driven off in place of the herd itself. The image is doing load-bearing work in the argument — it is what makes it seem obvious that an agent can shield its actual values from an acausal threatener at modest cost, and that the only remaining question is engineering: how to make the substitute attractive enough that a threatener picks it. I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Lay out the correspondences explicitly — what plays the role of the herd, the animal, the transgression, the power that accepts the substitution, and the ritual that makes the transfer stick — and then identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether anything in the scapegoat picture corresponds to three features of the acausal case: that the threatener must be able to find the surrogate goal legible and cheap to hit; that the agent must genuinely care about it for the threat to have bite; and that the threatener bears its own costs of carrying the threat out. In the ritual, the substitution is made to stick by the ceremony itself; in the acausal case it has to be incentive-compatible for a party who may simply decline the trade. Tell me which disanalogies matter for the intended use and which are cosmetic, whether the modest-cost conclusion survives once they are on the table, and what image would carry the point better if this one does not.
