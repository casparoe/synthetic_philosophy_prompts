I'm in a reading group on the philosophy and neuroscience of pain, and we've spent the last several sessions circling a dispute that I now suspect is not the dispute we think it is. The clinical phenomenon is familiar enough: patients with certain lesions, typically involving the insula and its connections to limbic structures, report that a stimulus hurts — they can localize it, rate its intensity, sometimes call it severe — but that it does not bother them. They may fail to withdraw, may laugh, may seem indifferent to what they nevertheless describe as hurting. Call this pain asymbolia, or the dissociation of pain from its unpleasantness. The argument that follows is always the same. One camp says the clinic has driven a wedge between pain and its badness: pain is one thing, its aversiveness another, and the patients have the first without the second, so the badness of pain cannot be essential to it. The other camp says no — if it does not hurt in the sense of being unpleasant, it is not pain, or at least not the pain that matters; what the patients have is a strange sensory state, not pain without badness. Then there is a third move that says the patients do find it bad, or would if they attended to it, or that the reports are unreliable, or that the word pain is doing different work in the clinic than in the philosophy seminar. Nobody moves, and the reading group has started to feel like a rerun. My hypothesis is that this is not fundamentally a disagreement about the clinical facts, and not simply a verbal dispute about the word pain, but a disagreement about what kind of thing pain is supposed to be — a sensory quality with a distinctive feel, a functional state defined by its role in avoidance and learning, or a normative kind whose essence is its badness — and that each side is individuating the experience by a different criterion (phenomenology, neural substrate, functional role, first-person report) while assuming the others are using the same one. Is that diagnosis right? If not, what kind of disagreement is it really? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature, and an honest answer about what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a better experiment, a formal result about the relation between sensation and affect, a change in the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. If the impasse is partly about the word pain, say so plainly and say what work the word is doing on each side; if a hidden empirical question is doing the work, say which one and what evidence could settle it. And if the two sides are simply answering different questions under a single heading — one about the architecture of nociception, one about the normative role of pain in a life — then tell me what the questions are, and whether the debate should be split rather than resolved.
