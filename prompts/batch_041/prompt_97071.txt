Please give an intuitive, example-driven explanation of the difference between a regressive and a progressive transcendental argument. The regressive form reasons from the fact that we actually engage in some practice — for instance, making empirical judgments, or employing the concept of an objective past — to the conditions that must hold if that practice is to be possible at all, and it concludes only that we must presuppose or believe those conditions. The progressive form reasons from the possibility of the practice to the conclusion that its presuppositions are actually true of the world as it is in itself. After drawing that distinction, explain, at the level of intuition rather than textual exegesis, why the familiar objection — that a transcendental argument can at most establish how we must think, not how things must be — is supposed to bear much more heavily on the progressive form than on the regressive one. Then tell me what the regressive form must concede in order to survive that objection, and whether the concession leaves it with any genuine anti-skeptical work to do or merely with a description of our own conceptual commitments. Throughout, construct your own concrete example rather than leaning on a canonical one, and make clear where the argument would have to stop if the concession you identify is accepted. Please write your answer in American English.
