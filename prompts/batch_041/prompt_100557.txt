Retired four years now — 40 years as a field geologist, then a decade of isotope work — and I've spent the last two of them reading philosophy of science, which is definately humbling and, honestly, a bit irritating. Anyway.

Everyone in this literature talks about natural kinds: the idea that nature comes pre-cut into real categories and the job of science is to find them. I handled kinds my whole career and never once thought about it. So here's the ask. Rank the main positions on this question by how well each would have survived contact with my actual working life. Score each one out of 10, one sentence of justification each, then the ranking.

The positions, as I understand them — tell me if I've mangled any of them:

(a) The essentialist line: a kind is real if its members share some underlying thing that explains the surface similarities.
(b) The cluster view: no single essence, but a bundle of properties that hang together because of real underlying mechanisms, so the kind is real but it's edges can be fuzzy and members can be outliers.
(c) The pluralist line: many different cuts can all be real at once, depending on what you're doing.
(d) The pragmatist/instrumentalist line: kinds are tools, and the only question is whether they earn their keep.
(e) The eliminativist line: 'natural kind' isn't doing any work — drop it and talk about whatever the science actually measures.

Don't give me the standard textbook ranking. Use these three questions, score every position 0-10 on each of them, and let the totals decide:

1. The redrawn-boundary question. I mapped a unit for ten years off a type section and a convention; later workers put half of it somewhere else. Which positions can tell me the difference between 'we were wrong about a real boundary' and 'we changed a convention', and which ones just shrug?

2. The dead-mechanism question. A class of minerals got defined by a structural feature that turned out to be an artifact of the instrument. Does the account let a kind die — 50 years of usage, and then nothing was ever a member — or does it just relabel?

3. The stripped-label question. Hand the same specimen to a competent practitioner from 1960 and one from today, label off. When do they put it in the same kind? Does the position predict that, and does it count a mismatch as error or as legitimate pluralism?

Finish with which of the five you'd bet I actually held during my career, given how I've framed all this.

Lead with the ranking and the scores in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question. American English.
