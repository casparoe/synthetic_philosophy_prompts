I sit on a small committee at an examinations board that is rewriting the biology specification for the national school-leaving qualification, and we have reached an impasse over a single word. One of my colleagues, who came to the committee from the philosophy of biology, wants every occurrence of 'code', 'instruction', 'information' and 'message' struck from the sections on genes and replaced with plain descriptions of chemistry: this sequence is copied, that sequence is bound by such-and-such a molecule, and so on. She argues roughly as follows. Nothing in the chemistry dictates the pairing between triplets and amino acids; the relationship is arbitrary in the way that the relationship between a word and the thing it names is arbitrary, and it could have been otherwise. Arbitrariness by itself, however, shows only that we may treat a sequence as a notation; it does not show that the cell treats it as one. A message, she says, has to be a message for somebody: either the cell understands it, in which case we owe an account of what understanding amounts to in a cell, or the machinery merely responds to shapes, in which case the sequence no more carries a message than a key carries instructions for the lock it turns. She adds that the machinery which reads a sequence is itself built from sequences of the same kind, so the supposed message is defined in a circle, and that when telephone engineers speak of information they mean only how much a signal narrows down the possibilities, a notion that is silent on whether anything is being said. Her real objection is to the everyday sense of the words, which she thinks does covert work in the specification and encourages pupils to believe that a plan and a planner are hiding in the chromosomes.

I think she is right that the metaphors are dangerous and wrong that they are merely metaphors, for these reasons. A human gene inserted into a bacterium yields the same protein, as though the same sentence had been read aloud by a different reader in a different country; were this bare chemistry, the change of setting should change the outcome. Arbitrariness seems to me not an obstacle to calling DNA a code but the very feature that distinguishes a code from a trace: a footprint or a tree-ring can be read only because the causal link between it and what it records is fixed by nature, whereas a code is precisely a relation that the physics does not fix and that must therefore be read by something set up to read it. There are further structural features — the redundancy of the triplet table, the way errors can be corrected, the fact that in some very compact genomes a stretch can be read in more than one frame and still yield something that works — which look to me more like the properties of a notation than of a residue. And the circularity she objects to seems to me what a self-reproducing code must look like: a code that specifies the very machinery that reads it is a code rather than a record, and we do not say that a printing press understands the book it prints.

I would like advice on how to settle this, and I would rather have an argument than a survey. Which considerations on each side actually bear on the question, and which are red herrings — for instance, does the near-universality of the genetic code count for anything, and does the fact that it is not perfectly universal count against? Are my colleague and I disagreeing about the world, or only about which description to adopt, and if the latter, what really turns on the choice? If we are disagreeing about the world, what would settle it — an observation, a thought experiment, a clarification of what each of us means by a code? And whatever the verdict, what wording would you put in the specification, so that it is accurate, does not mislead a fifteen-year-old into fatalism, and does not smuggle in a philosophical position that we could not defend to the teachers who will have to teach it?

Please write your answer in British English.
