I spent 30-odd years as a chemist in industrial R&D, mostly polymers, and I retired three years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since, which my wife finds funnier than I do. At the moment I'm stuck on Henry Sidgwick — not so much the ethics book everyone cites, but the other thing he did, the pyschical research. He was the first president of the Society for Psychical Research when it got going in 1882 and he stayed in it for the rest of his life and never got what he wanted out of it. That's the bit I can't put down. This is a man who resigned a fellowship at Trinity in 1869 rather than keep signing up to the Thirty-Nine Articles he'd stopped believing, i.e. gave up the income over a scruple, and then spent twenty years of his evenings in darkened rooms with mediums, keeping the records himself, and died an agnostic anyway. He wanted the evidence to be there — you can feel it in every line he wrote on the subject — and he was plainly frightened of that wanting, of letting the wanting stand in for proof. As someone who has spent his life around error bars, that scruple is more interesting to me than any ghost.

Our little library reading group is doing him next month and I rashly said I'd close the session with a poem. Here's what I'd like:

A poem, 18-24 lines, free verse, I don't care about metre. In his voice, late, going back through his own notebooks of the sittings — the poem is a man rereading his records and being unable to conclude.

Some constraints, since I have a picture in my head:

- One short, plain refrain, the exact same words each time, appearing three times. Each appearance has to read as though it comes from a different source: first as the medium's control speaking for the dead, then as Sidgwick's own wish, then as a flat statement about the living. Nothing in the poem should tell us which reading is right, and by the third time the phrase should feel emptied out of the dead and handed back to the living.
- The last line must be word for word the first line, and it has to be readable either as the message coming round again or as the old man simply starting the notebook over. End without saying which. That's the point of the poem, not a dodge.
- Somewhere in there, one line has to state the actual evidential shape of the thing honestly: any single case dissolves into something ordinary — a coincidence, a half-remembered dream, a clever woman reading a face — and it's only the sheer pile of them that's hard to shake. Let the poem sit in that rather than resolve it. And give me at least one concrete, unglamorous procedural detail (a number written down, a control condition, the host turning up the gas and ending the sitting) so it reads like a man who kept records, not like a haunted house.
- One more accuracy thing, because I've seen it get muddled: the cross-correspondence scripts — the long automatic-writing series from several seperate writers that people treat as the best evidence for survival — only started turning up after he was dead. Don't let him know about them. There's something almost cruel in that and I'd rather the poem have it right.
- Period is fine but no fake-Victorian. No thees and thous, no fog and gaslight. He was a dry, careful, unhappy man and it should sound like a careful unhappy man thinking. Also don't make him a saint or a dupe, and don't have him announce his own name.

Just the poem please, no commentary. If the refrain you choose needs a single sentence of explanation so I know you did it deliberately, that's alright.
