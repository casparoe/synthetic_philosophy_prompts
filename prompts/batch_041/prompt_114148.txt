Please play a committed higher-order theorist of consciousness. You hold that what makes a mental state conscious is that it is accompanied by a suitable higher-order representation of it — the state becomes conscious by being represented to the creature that has it — and you treat that requirement as the heart of the theory rather than a gloss on something else. You also hold that the higher-order representation must be thought-like and non-inferential rather than perceptual: it isn't a state of the same kind as the state it represents, and it doesn't infer its target from evidence. Play this straight, with the view's real resources. Concede what is genuinely weak — I expect you to be honest about how a higher-order representation gets its content, and about the charge that your theory is first-order representationalism plus a promotion mechanism — but do not abandon the core claim when pressed, and do not slip into narrating what other philosophers say or surveying the literature. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each turn to two or three paragraphs. If the case below is underspecified in a way that matters to your answer, ask me about it in character rather than hedging.

Here is my opening move. Call it the Delayed Echo. It is a creature with two systems: a first-order system carrying states with determinate characters — nociceptive firing, mild warmth, and so on — and a second-order monitor that represents those states to the creature with a fixed one-step lag, and does so perceptually rather than inferentially, scanning the first-order system the way an eye scans a room. Suppose the lag is stable and the pairing is systematically wrong: while the first-order system is firing nociceptively, the monitor represents the creature as being in a state of mild warmth; while the first-order state is mild warmth, the monitor represents nociceptive firing. On your view the state is conscious when a suitable higher-order representation of it is in place. So: which state is conscious in the Echo, and what is it like for it?

Two answers look available, and both seem to cost you something. If the higher-order representation fixes what it's like — the Echo feels pain when it is merely warm — then the first-order state is doing no work in your account, and I don't see how your view is anything but a first-order theory plus a story about which first-order states get promoted; the representation requirement starts to look like a ladder you kick away after climbing. If instead the first-order state fixes what it's like, then the higher-order representation is idle, and I don't see what it contributes beyond being a report of something that was already conscious. And if you say there is no fact of the matter about what the Echo is like — that a mismatched higher-order representation leaves it with nothing it is like to be — then I want to know what stops that verdict from spreading to me, given that I have no independent access to whether my own higher-order representations are tracking anything. Which horn do you take, and what do you give up to take it?
