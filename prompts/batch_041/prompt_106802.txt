And the student wrote:

"The demandingness objection says act-consequentialism asks too much of us. Since the theory tells us to maximize the good, we have to keep giving away our time and money until giving any more would make things go worse overall — in effect until we're no worse off than the people we're helping. Singer's drowning-child example is the standard illustration: if it costs me little to save a child, I'm obligated to, and the same logic applies to people starving abroad, so I'm on the hook to give away most of my income.

The objection is usually credited to Bernard Williams. His point is that a morality this demanding forces us to set aside our own projects and commitments, so it alienates us from our own lives and violates our integrity — that's why the demand strikes us as repugnant. Some consequentialists try to soften this by adding an agent-centered prerogative (Scheffler), which lets us give extra weight to our own projects.

Rule-consequentialism is supposed to get around the whole thing. Instead of asking what each act would produce, it asks which set of rules would produce the best consequences if generally followed, and then we follow those rules. Because the costs to the agent of following a rule get factored in when we pick the rules, the selected rules won't be as crushing as act-consequentialism's demands, so rule-consequentialism escapes the demandingness objection while keeping the consequentialist core.

On (d): a sound demandingness objection shows act-consequentialism conflicts with ordinary moral intuitions, but that only refutes the theory if you already trust those intuitions, so it's a bit of a standoff."
