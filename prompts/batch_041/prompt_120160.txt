Two months ago I took part in a demonstration at a university open day: I was shown two faces, asked which I found more attractive, and then, without my noticing, handed back the face I had not chosen and asked to explain my preference. I explained it fluently, at length, and in entirely the wrong direction. I have since been asked to design a year-long module on self-knowledge for the final-year students at the school where I teach, and I would rather build it on what is actually known than on the popular claim that introspection is largely fiction. I have a year, one colleague, and no illusions about how much reading we will get through.

Which open questions about the reliability of introspection should we try to settle first, and in what order? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, whether it can be advanced by argument or is waiting on empirical work, how crowded the area already is, and what would move it up or down the list over the next two years. I am particularly interested in the distinctions I suspect the popular treatments collapse — between reporting what one experienced and reporting why one acted; between a report being inconsistent over time and being inaccurate; and the awkward fact that for an experience, unlike a photograph, there is no independent record to check the report against. Finally, tell me which of the familiar implications people draw from this literature are overrated or simply do not follow — that self-reports are worthless as evidence, that we are strangers to ourselves, that trained introspectors escape the problem — and which, if any, the module should teach as settled.
