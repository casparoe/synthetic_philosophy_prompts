Two nights ago a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about a city ballot we had both just voted in — three separate questions about funding a transit extension, each one voted up or down on its own, plus a fourth question asking us to approve the financing package as a whole. He came away convinced the whole thing was rigged by whoever wrote the questions. I came away realizing that I hold a fairly settled position on how collective decisions ought to be made and had never once written it down. Before I go back and defend it, I'd like it audited, because I suspect I'm carrying at least one contradiction and I genuinely can't tell which piece is the problem.

Here is the position as plainly as I can put it. (1) Legitimacy is procedural: what makes a collective decision authoritative is that it came out of the right kind of process, not that it got the answer right. (2) Voters should decide the individual questions at issue, one at a time, rather than accepting or rejecting whole packages — people's views on particular matters are more real than any bundle they can be sorted into. (3) The set of things a body ends up committed to has to be internally consistent; a council that resolves both that the library will stay open and that it will close has decided nothing, because a self-contradictory verdict is not a verdict. (4) Wherever there's a conflict, the larger number should win; I don't believe there's any principled way to weight one person's judgment above another's. (5) The real power in all of this sits with whoever controls the agenda — which questions get asked, in what order, and how they're worded — so a legitimate procedure must be neutral about the agenda and must not let the agenda-setter's preferences show up in the result. (6) There is a fact of the matter about which policy is correct, and a decision can be entirely legitimate and substantively mistaken at the same time. (7) Ordinary people's judgments on different issues aren't constrained by any single underlying ideology, so there's no reason to expect their votes to compose into anything coherent.

My friend's summary is that this is a heap of contradictions and that I should keep two of them and throw the rest away. I doubt that, but I don't trust my own ear: I may be papering over a real conflict precisely because each piece sounds so reasonable on its own. Take the set pairwise. Tell me which pairs genuinely cannot both be held, which merely look as though they can't, and for each real conflict which member has to go and what I lose by dropping it. Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and finish by naming the single commitment whose revision would repair the most. Be explicit about your confidence as you go: say how sure you are of each verdict, and single out the one judgment in your answer you're least confident about.
