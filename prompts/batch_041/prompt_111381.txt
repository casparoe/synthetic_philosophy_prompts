I am working on the ethics of everyday local conventions — the norms that govern tipping, queueing, and where one may cross the road — and I want the specification before I commit to a view. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of the normative force of such conventions must satisfy: what the theory must deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must preserve, and which phenomena it must explain, with the consideration that makes each item a requirement rather than an optional nicety. Fix the standard of adequacy relative to a purpose — I mean to defend the claim that a visitor who knowingly flouts a local convention is open to criticism — and tell me if no candidate theory can meet the list.

I want the following cases handled, all of them invented so that we are not trading familiar examples. (1) In one city the tip is understood to be the server's wage and the menu price the employer's fee, so a visitor who pays the listed price and tips nothing has, by local lights, left the server unpaid rather than merely been stingy; the theory must explain why one and the same act of paying is a breach here and not elsewhere. (2) A visitor crosses an empty road at night against a local custom of never crossing mid-block; the only local present is not inconvenienced but is affronted; the theory must say whether, and on what grounds, a breach with no material consequence can be criticisable. (3) A local custom has men served before women at a bakery; the theory must say what reason, if any, a visitor has to conform, and must not treat local acceptance as sufficient for obligation. (4) A visitor tips in a city where tipping is taken for charity and thereby insults the recipient; the theory must explain how following one's home convention can itself be the breach. (5) Locals themselves disagree about the customary rate and no majority practice exists; the theory must say whether a convention can exist without convergence.

I have been distinguishing conventions that are load-bearing, where non-compliance shifts a cost onto someone who did not choose it, from those that are ornamental; say whether that distinction does any work in the specification, and whether it or something else should carry the weight.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp; say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, since a specification that rules itself out is the result I most want; and name the single requirement that does most to exclude the popular positions — that conventions are morally inert because merely conventional, that visitors are bound by whatever the locals happen to do, and that only harm to identifiable persons generates obligations. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them rather than on a scorecard of the contenders.
