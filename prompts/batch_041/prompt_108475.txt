My friend lent me this pop-philosophy book (I won't name it, you probably haven't heard of it anyway) and I just finished the chapter on dialetheism, and I honestly cant tell how much of it to trust. Here's the gist as best I remember it:

'Dialetheism is the view that some contradictions are true. It's where you end up if you take the Liar paradox seriously — "this sentence is false" can't be true and can't be false, so the dialetheist (the best known one being Graham Priest) says it's both. But classical logic says that anything follows from a contradiction, so the dialetheist has to build a new logic to work in: paraconsistent logic, which drops the law of non-contradiction and adds a third truth value — true, false, and both — on top of the usual two. The result is a logic that tolerates contradiction: the old proof that takes you from "it's raining and it isn't raining" to "the moon is made of cheese" no longer goes through. Critics say this is fatal, since a logic that refuses to rule anything out isn't a logic at all, and a dialetheist can't even use reductio ad absurdum to argue for their own view. In practice the machinery has been most useful to computer scientists, who use it to hold contradictory information in databases without the whole system blowing up. To be fair to the dialetheists, they don't really mean that the world itself is contradictory; they mean that our theories, or our databases, sometimes have to be, which is the database point in fancier language. So you get things like a cat half on the mat being both on the mat and not on the mat, or Zeno's arrow both moving and not moving. It's a niche position — most logicians treat it as a formal curiosity rather than a serious view about truth.'

If that's right, what is the law of non-contradiction even doing in the story, and are those two things the same thing or not? Are those the same move or genuinely different, and is "a third truth value" even the right way to explain this to a beginner?
