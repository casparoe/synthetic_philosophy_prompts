I have recently become the director of a small program whose continued funding is conditioned on quarterly performance metrics, and a board member has been pressing an argument I cannot answer. He says: (1) every number we report is a proxy for a good we actually care about, and no proxy tracks its target perfectly; (2) whatever we are scored on, we will optimize; (3) optimizing a proxy means exploiting the gap between proxy and good — whether by over-optimizing at the extremes, where the two come apart, or by actively damaging the good in order to raise the number, the logic is the same — and so the proxy's correlation with the good falls; (4) hence a measure that has been made a target is no longer a good measure of what it was meant to track; (5) if our measure is not a good measure of the good, then our evaluation cannot tell us whether the good is being achieved; (6) so any regime that evaluates the program by measured targets is blind to the thing it exists to serve; (7) so we should stop evaluating the program by metrics. I cannot accept (7); it would condemn the ordinary practice of checking whether anything is working, and it would condemn his own argument, which is itself a claim about how institutions should be measured. But I also don't want to wave away the observation in (3) as a truism. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Reason from the structure of the argument; I am not asking for the literature. Begin with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
