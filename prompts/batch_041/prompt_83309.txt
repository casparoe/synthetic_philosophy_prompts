I got into a proper argument at a dinner last week about compulsory voting — one friend is Australian, another is from a country where turnout is voluntary and sliding, and I'm somewhere in the middle and not really sure what I think. But the thing I keep chewing on isn't whether turnout should be higher; it's what a particular set of election rules is committed to. So take a package deal: voting is a legal duty backed by a small fine, small enough that nobody's going to jail over it; the ballot carries a none-of-the-above box, so you can turn up and formally decline to choose anyone; and if you object to the whole business you can register in advance as a conscientious abstainer and be exempt from the fine altogether. Now bolt on some further bits from the same imaginary jurisdiction: 16- and 17-year-olds can vote in municipal elections but not national ones; resident non-citizens can vote in local elections but not national ones; and there's a proposal on the table to give each parent an extra vote for every child under 18, cast on the child's behalf until the child's own franchise kicks in. I know that's a bit of a frankenstien — it's a composite of things that exist in different places plus a proposal or two — but I think that's actually the point, because I want to know what the thing as a whole presupposes.

What view of a vote does that package commit you to? Is a vote a right you may exercise or waive, a duty you owe your fellow citizens, a component of a collective decision that only works if enough people turn up, or something closer to jury service? Does compulsory voting only make sense if you already accept a duty to vote, or can a fairly libertarian view of rights accommodate it — you can be fined for not doing your bit without anyone seriously claiming you were morally obliged to? And notice how much work the small details are doing: a fine so small it's basically a nudge seems to presuppose a weak duty, or maybe no duty at all and just a coordination problem; the none-of-the-above box seems to presuppose that what's required is participation rather than a choice; the conscientious exemption treats abstention as an expression worth protecting, which is an odd thing to protect if voting is a duty. Then the boundaries: if the franchise tracks citizenship and adulthood, why do non-citizens and 16-year-olds get it locally? What is the demos here — residence, citizenship, competence, or having a stake in the outcome? And the parental proxy vote seems to presuppose that a vote represents interests rather than wills, since small children have the former and not the latter.

Could you reconstruct the implicit theory of the whole arrangement — the view of rights, duties, legitimacy and the demos that it only makes sense under — and tell me which rival views it quietly rules out? I'd like you to be careful about the difference between what it strictly presupposes and what it merely happens to fit well with, and to say where it's underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the very same rules. Then tell me what would have to change in the design if we swapped the presupposed view for one of the rivals — say, if a vote were purely a right you're free to waive, or if the demos were defined by residence alltogether. Please write your answer in British English, since I'm keeping everything in one house style for this project.
