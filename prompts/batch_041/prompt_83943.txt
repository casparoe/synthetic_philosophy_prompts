Hi — I'm a second-year PhD student working on value theory, mostly on pain and pleasure. I'm drafting a chapter on what I'm calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I'd really like your comments on the arguement. I don't need citations, reading lists, or anyone else's view — I just want you to engage with the thing itself.

The view, roughly: negative utilitarianism says that reducing pain matters more than producing pleasure, maybe to the point where only pain matters. The lexical threshold version adds a threshold: below it, pains can be traded off against pleasures (a bad headache might be worth a nice afternoon), but above it, no finite amount of pleasure can outweigh the pain. The pain takes lexical priority. I'm using 'lexical' to mean a priority that cannot be outweighed by any finite amount of the other thing.

Here's the draft, in note form.

1. Motivation. Some pains seem to have a floor that no pleasure can compensate. If you can prevent one person's week of severe pain by giving a million people a slightly nicer day, the threshold view says: do it, no matter how many nicer days you pile up. That feels right to some people and monstrous to others. Good.

2. Location problem. Where is the threshold? If it's sharp, it looks arbitrary: why exactly there? If it's vague, the view doesn't give determinate verdicts in the cases that matter. I try to say the threshold can be sharp in value even if vague in our knowledge. But then there are pairs of outcomes where a tiny bit more pleasure flips the ranking from 'pain wins lexically' to 'pleasure wins'. That discontinuity is doing all the work.

3. Aggregation problem. Is the threshold per episode of pain, per person, or per outcome? If per episode, then a billion mild headaches cannot outweigh one severe migraine. If per outcome, then enough mild headaches might cross the threshold and become lexically protected. Neither option looks obviously right. I lean towards per episode, but I can't justify it.

4. Commensurability problem. Below the threshold, pleasure and pain are commensurable. Above it, they are not. What changes at the threshold? If it's just the amount of pain, why does the relation between the two values change? If it's something else about the pain (e.g. it undermines agency), then the view is really about agency, not pain.

5. My current fix. Distinguish value from permission. Value is continuous; the threshold is deontic, a side-constraint on what we may do, not a feature of the value ordering. So we can say: below-threshold pain may be imposed for enough pleasure; above-threshold pain may not. That saves the lexical intuition without a discontinuous value function. But it means the view is not a form of utilitarianism at all. It's a hybrid: consequentialist value plus a deontic threshold. I realise I can't seperate the two as cleanly as I'd like.

6. Worry. If the threshold is deontic, it needs a rationale. Why is it permissible to impose below-threshold pain for pleasure but not above-threshold pain? Could be respect for persons, but then why not all pain? Could be that above-threshold pain destroys agency, but that's an empirical claim and probably false for some pains. Maybe the threshold is just a codification of a moral intuition, not an explanation.

I'm especially unsure about the last move. Is it a genuine solution or just a redescription of the problem? And does the aggregation point sink the whole view?

3. Is the central move interesting? — 200 words, say whether the deontic threshold is a real contribution or a cop-out.

5. What to develop — 300 words, suggestions for strengthening the chapter, especially the aggregation and commensurability bits.
6. Overall verdict — 100 words, whether this is worth pursuing as a chapter or whether I should drop it and write about something else.
