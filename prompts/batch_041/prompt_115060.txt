Quick context before the ask: I'm a 4th-year PhD student (ML, but my thesis is on the eval side) and I'm the student rep on our lab's release review committee. We meet Thursday, I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand, and I've been going back and forth on this one enough that I want the reasoning checked.

The case. A team here wants to start a 6-week internal pilot of a small model they trained by distilling a much larger model that was itself built with an iterated amplification loop. Roughly how the loop works: human contractors (not researchers — contractors, working to about a 10-minute-per-question budget) answer very small questions; a model is trained to imitate those answers; that model then handles somewhat bigger questions by breaking them into smaller ones; the cycle repeats for three rounds. The final big model is then distilled down into the small one that would actually be served.

What's bugging me is what showed up in the distillation. On the team's own eval set the small model matches the big model on ~97% of items, and it's at parity or better on every capability benchmark they ran. But when they went looking at items where the big model's answer fell out of a deep decomposition, the small model agrees with the final answer while disagreeing with the reasoning that produced it — and there's a class of items where the big model's correct behavior was to stop and say some version of "this subquestion is malformed, I shouldn't answer it," and the small model just answers. Fluently. Plausibly. Wrong. Their eval set was written by the same team that built the pipeline, which I don't think is disqualifying, but it probably isn't the eval set that would catch this.

Also relevant: the whole pitch for amplification is that you get supervision you couldn't otherwise get, because the human only ever has to judge tiny things. But the distilled model never sees the tree, only the root. So I'm not sure "the small model is as aligned as the big one" is even the right comparison — whatever alignment the big one had came from the decomposition, and distillation is exactly the step that throws the decomposition away.

The team's position, which I do take seriously: distillation is how every deployable model here gets made, the pilot is internal-only with full logging, refusing it just means the safety work doesn't happen and someone less careful ships something worse, and if you demand a proof that alignment survives distillation before anyone ships anything, nothing ever ships.

I need to write the committee's opinion — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation and a time frame. Their training run for the next version starts on the 14th, so if we want an eval in place before then it has to be cheap enough to build in a week. Please write it as the memo I'd circulate: reasons the team can actually respond to, and the strongest version of their side rather than a strawman. I'd rather be argued out of my position now than have them nod along Thursday and do it anyway.

Two things I keep getting stuck on and would like the memo to actually take a position on. First: if the small model matches the big one on the distribution but not on the trees, is that a competence gap, an alignment gap, or is the distinction not doing any work here? Second, and maybe the same question: the pipeline's weak link looks like the contractors' judgments on the small questions, so if they're the ones who are wrong in some systematic way, does amplification preserve that too, and does distillation make it better or worse? And whatever you end up recommending, say what would change your mind.
