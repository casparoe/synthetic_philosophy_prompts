I am writing a novel set in Detroit in the mid-1970s and have reached the chapter in which a mid-level engineer — decent, not heroic, a man who has spent his whole career inside the logic of cost accounting — is asked to defend his division's handling of a rear-end fire problem in a subcompact. I want the moral argument in that chapter to be exact rather than gestured at, and I have settled on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism as the thing he cannot answer. What I need from you is not a summary of that objection but a genuine two-way analysis of a real episode through it: the Ford Pinto affair, with the fuel tank mounted behind the rear axle, the fatal rear-end fires, the internal cost-benefit document that assigned dollar values to burn deaths and burn injuries and weighed the cost of a design change against the value of the lives it might save, the recall that came years late, and the product-liability litigation that followed. Take the standard facts as given; you need not look anything up. But where the standard facts are contested — and I know that a revisionist reading of the episode holds that the document was prepared for a regulatory comment period rather than as the basis for the design decision, and that the car's overall safety record was comparable to that of its subcompact competitors — say so, and say what the analysis looks like if that reading is right, rather than quietly assuming the mythic version.

Then do the analytical work. Map the case onto the objection with discipline: whose interests are aggregated, what exactly is traded against what, and who bears the loss. Distinguish three things that are usually run together — utilitarianism as a criterion of rightness, cost-benefit analysis as a decision procedure, and the corporate fiduciary structure that made a private firm the site of the calculation. Tell me whether the separateness objection lands on the first, the second, both, or neither, and whether the analogy that carries it from one to the other is sound. I expect the hardest part to be this: the classic statement of the objection concerns an identifiable person sacrificed for the greater good, whereas a safety decision of this kind spreads a small increase in the probability of death across everyone who buys the car while the savings accrue to each buyer as well. Say whether that ex ante, probabilistic structure defuses the objection, relocates it, or reveals that the objection was always narrower than its proponents claim. Bring in the alternatives the objection is supposed to motivate — a contractualist requirement that principles be justifiable to each person, a prioritarian weighting of the worst off, a rights-based prohibition on using persons merely as means — and tell me which of them handles these facts better, and where each must make a tradeoff it would rather not name. Finally, run it the other way: what does this episode show about the separateness objection that its standard formulations do not anticipate? Does the case confirm it, embarrass it, or expose it as a complaint that bites only when the aggregation is performed by an institution we already distrust? I would far rather have a precise account of where the objection goes silent than a verdict — I need to know what my engineer cannot say, because that is what the chapter is about.
