I'm putting together a feature for the magazine about how much archaeologists can really claim to know about what people believed, and I keep tripping over one line that everyone in the field seems to reach for: a site is a crime scene and the archaeologist is the detective. It gets used to license a lot — that intentions, beliefs, even whole institutions can be read off the objects, on the grounds that a good enough investigator reconstructs not just what happened but why. One archaeolgist I interviewed went further and said a pit full of carefully arranged pots and butchered bones is basically a signed confession, so the ritual reading is pretty much secured; another told me a well-preserved site is a freeze-frame, the moment of abandonment caught in mid-air.

I want to seperate what's doing the work in that from the hand-waving. Can you give me the correspondence table first — what's playing the detective, the crime, the motive, the witness, the chain of custody — then where the thing breaks down, which of the breaks actually matter for the work people are making the analogy do, and whether the conclusion that we can recover beliefs and institutions from objects survives once those breaks are on the table. If there's a better analogy for what's actually going on, I'd rather have that. American English please — my editor is in New York.
