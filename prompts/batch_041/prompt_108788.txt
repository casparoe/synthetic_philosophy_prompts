I'm a journalist who writes long-form features about science, art, and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about authenticity and the strange afterlife of objects. The piece has brought me to a mid-sized municipal museum that has adopted a cluster of policies I cannot quite square, and I'd like you to help me reconstruct the philosophy they imply. The museum owns a celebrated but badly damaged painting. Because the original is too fragile to travel, the museum commissioned a hand-painted facsimile that its own conservators describe as indistinguishable from the original under ordinary viewing conditions; it now lends the facsimile to international exhibitions while the original stays in a climate-controlled vault. When a different work in the collection was exposed as a forgery, painted in the 1920s to pass as a Renaissance panel, the museum did not deaccession or destroy it. It moved the forgery into a small gallery called Provenance and Error, where it is displayed as a historical document of deception, with the forger's name on the label. The museum also permits digital restorations that fill in lost sections of a painting, provided the additions are visible under ultraviolet light, and it has a rule for what it calls ethical de-restoration: it will strip away later repainting to reveal the artist's original surface, unless the repainting was done by a known later artist, in which case the repainting stays and the original is treated as only one layer of the object's history. It will not sell or gift any of these replicas as if they were originals, and its insurer covers the original for a sum with no relation to any recent sale price while declining to insure the facsimile at all.

What philosophical commitments does this constellation of practices presuppose? Does it commit the museum to the view that originals possess a kind of value, call it contact value or authenticity value, that is not reducible to their perceptible aesthetic properties? Does it presuppose a distinction between a work and its material bearer, or between the work as a type and this particular token of it? Or can the whole pattern be explained by a purely aesthetic theory of value supplemented by facts about liability, insurance, and institutional incentives? I am especially interested in the apparent inconsistency: a forgery and a facsimile are both non-original, yet one is treated as a document of deception and the other as a legitimate stand-in. Is that a sign that the museum is tacitly committed to two incompatible theories of value, or is there a principled distinction between them? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license it. Then say what would have to change in the museum's practices if it adopted a thoroughgoing aestheticism, or alternatively a view on which the value of an artwork lies entirely in the experience it produces.

I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the reconstruction and its weak points. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you are least sure about.
