I'm putting together a long-form piece on how two intellectual traditions have separately tried to explain the same human spectacle — the tendency of people and institutions to keep pouring resources into a losing course of action — and I want your help working out how the two accounts actually relate, rather than assuming they are two names for one thing.

On one side is Martin Shubik's dollar auction, set out in a short 1971 article in the Journal of Conflict Resolution, in which a dollar is auctioned under the rule that the highest bidder wins the dollar and the second-highest bidder forfeits their bid as well. On the other side is the research programme that Barry Staw launched in 1976 with his paper about escalating commitment to a chosen course of action, and which grew into the organisational-behaviour literature on escalation of commitment, the sunk-cost fallacy, and self-justification.

What I want is a careful comparison of the two as explanations, not as metaphors. Specifically:

First, in the dollar auction, once two players are engaged, continuing to bid can be the locally rational move even though the pair of them is heading for a collectively ruinous outcome; the trap lies in the payoff structure, not in anyone's psychology. Does that mean the escalation literature's standard explanatory apparatus — self-justification, the sunk-cost fallacy, the need to save face — is not merely unnecessary for explaining dollar-auction behaviour but actively misleading about it? And conversely, does the game-theoretic account of the auction explain the findings of the psychological literature better than that literature explains itself, or is it silent about the cases that matter most to it: wars of attrition with no fixed prize, research programmes with uncertain payoffs, careers invested in a single project?

Second, the two traditions use the word commitment very differently, and I want that disentangled. In game theory, commitment is usually something you do to yourself in order to alter someone else's expectations — burning bridges, making withdrawal costly or impossible, and so on. In the escalation literature, commitment is closer to a psychological state: having invested so much that withdrawal becomes unthinkable. In the dollar auction, does anyone actually commit in the first sense? It looks as though the bidders never restrict their own future options at all; they simply face a structure in which every available option is bad. If so, is the dollar auction really a model of escalation of commitment, or a model of something adjacent that the shared label obscures?

Third — and this is the part I care most about, because I suspect it is where the comparison gets interesting — consider a modified auction. Suppose the second-highest bidder forfeits only a fraction of their bid, say a half or a tenth. Or suppose the auctioneer offers, at a price, a one-off amnesty allowing a bidder to withdraw without paying. Ask what happens to escalation as that fraction falls, and what happens when the amnesty is available but costly. My hunch is that the psychological story predicts escalation should persist quite far down, because the invested stake still feels wasted, whereas the structural story predicts a fairly sharp threshold beyond which bidding stops making sense and the game unwinds. Which hunch is right, and what would the disagreement between them look like as a comparison of equilibria or as an experiment worth running?

Finally, tell me what each account cannot say about the other's paradigm cases. The dollar auction has a determinate prize, a fixed and known value, and a single auctioneer who profits from the carnage. Real escalation is usually messier: the prize is vague, its value shifts as the campaign proceeds, the investors are also the prize-holders, and there is often no auctioneer at all — nobody who designed the trap and benefits from it. Is the dollar auction therefore best understood as a limiting case, an idealised pathology showing how far escalation can run with no psychological error whatsoever, rather than a general theory of escalation? And if so, what does the escalation literature have left to explain that the auction cannot?

I'd like the piece to land on a verdict: are these two theories rivals, complements, or descriptions of different phenomena that have been confusingly given the same name? Give me your reasoning rather than a summary of the two literatures, and flag the points where you are genuinely uncertain.
