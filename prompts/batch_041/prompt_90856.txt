I chair the professional standards panel of a regional public-health agency. Months ago, before any concrete case existed, we approved in principle a protocol allowing the incident commander during a declared emergency to depart from the agency's public information plan where full disclosure would foreseeably cost lives. We now have our first case under it, and a live complaint. During the port chlorine release, the commander announced that the gas was being carried north and that the coastal road was unsafe, directing residents of the two southern districts inland; the release was in fact being carried south, straight down the coastal road, and the inland route was the only one clear of it. Her after-action statement says she knew the announcement was false and would make it again. The same bulletin named a harbour contractor as the probable source of the release, on the strength of a preliminary reading later shown to be wrong; he lost his operating licence and his business, and he is the complainant before us. My colleagues split three ways. One would dismiss the complaint: the broadcast was the right call, and the contractor's losses were an unavoidable cost of a necessary act. One would uphold it and censure her, precisely because the call was right — bless it and we license every future commander to lie. One would take no action against her at all, on the ground that in authorising the protocol we absorbed the wrong into our own procedure, so that there are no dirty hands left in this case but ours; he calls this the textbook dirty-hands problem and treats the verdict as settled by the label, which I am not willing to accept as a reason.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before we vote. It should recommend one disposition of the complaint, with a date by which we should act and a decision on whether the finding and her name go into the public minutes; say which of the three positions is closest to right and where each of the others fails; be explicit about whether her justification for the broadcast carries over to the naming of the contractor; and give the strongest case for whichever position I end up recommending against, rather than a caricature of it. One page; our counsel will handle the legal framing, so do not spend space on it.
