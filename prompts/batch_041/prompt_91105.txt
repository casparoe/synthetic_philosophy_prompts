Last night a friend and I argued for hours about moral foundations theory, and I came away unsure whether I'd been defending the evidence or just my own politics. The fight kept circling back to a magazine explainer she'd read, which she paraphrased roughly as follows:

"Haidt's moral foundations theory shows that our moral judgments are driven by gut feelings rather than reasoning — he demonstrated this in experiments where people could not give any reasons for their judgments and just kept insisting that something was wrong. He identified five foundations — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity — that are universal, hardwired, and present in every culture. Liberals have only the first two, which is why they find conservative morality incomprehensible, whereas conservatives draw on all five. And because the theory has been empirically validated across cultures, it shows that conservative morality is in fact the fuller and more complete morality, and that moral disputes are ultimately just clashes between different evolved modules, with no fact of the matter about who is right."

I suspect there's something sound in there and something badly off, but I can't tell which is which. Please go through it clause by clause and tell me what a moral psychologist working on this would accept, what they'd wince at but grant as a forgivable simplification, and what is simply wrong. I especially want to know whether the normative conclusions in the last two sentences follow from the descriptive findings, or whether they are smuggled in, and whether the theory as such commits its defenders to any view at all about who is morally right. My instinct is that the psychology is broadly right and the metaethics tacked on at the end is bogus, but I'd rather not be told that simply because it's what I already believe.
