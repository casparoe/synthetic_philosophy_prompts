I'm the ethicist on the clinical ethics committee at a mid-size teaching hospital — I do consults, but what's eating my time lately is that our patient-communication office now sends me their campaigns for sign-off before anything goes out. It's three or four a month: reminder texts, letters, a script for the front desk, a change to the default on the patient portal, occasionally a video. They're not asking whether the campaign is legal — compliance already checks that. They're asking me whether it's manipulative, and I keep giving answers I can't defend consistantly: I waved through something last month that I'm now pretty sure I should have flagged, and I blocked something that, on reflection, was just a well-made argument. The people running these campaigns are nurses and comunication staff with no philosophy background, the decision has to be made in about a week, and both I and the communications director have to sign off, so I need something I can hand them that they can run without me in the room. What's at stake is whether patients are genuinely agreeing to things or being worked, and these are often one-shot decisions made by people who are sick and tired, so I'd rather the procedure be a bit overprotective than clever.

A friend from grad school gave me a list of ways people distinguish manipulation from ordinary persuasion, and I want to know which of them can survive being turned into a test that a nurse can apply. Roughly: (a) manipulation bypasses or subverts someone's reasoning rather than giving them reasons; (b) manipulation is covert, so it's the hidden intent that makes it manipulation, and disclosure fixes it; (c) manipulation exploits a vulnerability or a known irrationality — fear, shame, sunk cost, scarcity — instead of addressing the patient's actual concern; (d) manipulation aims at a result the person would disavow if they thought about it, so what matters is the counterfactual reaction rather than the technique; (e) manipulation treats the patient as a means to our targets rather than as someone whose own ends count. I suspect some of these are the same idea in different clothes, at least one is circular, and at least one is unusable by anyone who isn't me, but I can't tell which is which. You're a philosopher who works on manipulation, autonomy and consent — think carefully here, I'll be using this on real patients — and I'd like you to start by brainstorming that sorting-out part out loud: which distinctions are doing real work, which collapse into each other, where they conflict, and which should win when they do, before you commit to any procedure.

Then write the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a clear no, where it hands the decision to a human, and — the part I care most about — where a step is really just a value judgment wearing a lab coat, since I'd rather flag those than pretend they're neutral. Run it on four things: (1) a text reminder for an overdue diabetic eye exam that says most patients find it quick and painless, aimed at a patient who has told staff she's frightened of having her eyes touched; (2) pre-scheduling patients into a screening program so they're enrolled unless they opt out, with a clear and easy opt-out; (3) a patient testimonial video chosen partly because the speaker is unusually warm and convincing, when the actual benefit is modest and the speaker is one of the lucky ones; (4) a front-desk script where staff are trained to ask about a patient's grandchildren and then connect the screening to being around for them. Include at least one verdict I won't like, and finish by telling me what this procedure can't do and which of the four cases I should expect it to get wrong.
