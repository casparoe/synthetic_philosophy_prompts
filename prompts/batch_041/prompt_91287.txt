You are a philosopher and speechwriter with a gift for making ancient ideas speak to ordinary lives. I need you to draft a speech for a specific occasion, and I want the philosophical content to do real structural work—not to be decoration.

The occasion: The Willows, a residential community for retirees, is holding its annual Long Table Dinner, this year marking its twenty-fifth anniversary. The speaker is a resident—a retired surgeon, not a professional philosopher—who has been asked to give the after-dinner speech. The room will hold about eighty people. Some are retired academics, including one classics professor who knows Cicero's De Senectute well; many others are residents with no philosophical training, and some are adult children of residents who are there mostly out of love and duty. The theme of the evening is "The Long View." The speech should run about 1,200 words.

The speaker wants to engage Cicero's dialogue De Senectute (On Old Age), in which Cato the Elder defends old age against four standard complaints. The four complaints are: (1) old age withdraws us from the conduct of affairs; (2) it weakens the body; (3) it deprives us of pleasures; (4) it is close to death. Cato's replies, in brief: (1) old age need not withdraw one from worthwhile activity, since counsel and judgment remain, and great affairs are often guided by the old; (2) the body's decline can be met with moderation, and one should not demand of old age the strength of youth; (3) the loss of sensual pleasures is a liberation, not a deprivation, and the pleasures of the mind—conversation, learning, memory—remain; (4) death is either nothing to us or a passage to something better, and in any case its nearness is no reason to resent the time one has. Cato also offers images of old age as a harvest, as a ship coming into harbor, and as a season for planting trees one will never sit under.

But the speaker has an argument to make, and this is where the speech must do more than summarize. The speaker believes Cato misses a fifth complaint, one that is not on the standard list and that Cato's framework cannot answer. The fifth complaint is this: old age is not merely the loss of this or that good, but the loss of the self that could enjoy them. One's past becomes a foreign country; one's future stops being a field of possibility and becomes a diminishing corridor. Cato's reply—that virtue and wisdom remain—assumes a continuous self that can own its past and project its future. But that continuity is precisely what old age, at its hardest, takes away. The speaker's proposed remedy is not Cato's self-sufficiency but a deliberate practice of beginning again: cultivating new attachments, new skills, new forms of attention, and accepting that the self is not a fortress to be defended but a garden to be replanted. The speech should build toward this idea, not announce it at the start.

Before you write, brainstorm briefly: how to open with a concrete image or anecdote that earns the audience's attention; how to lay out the four complaints and Cato's replies fairly and vividly without turning the speech into a lecture; how to introduce the fifth complaint so that it feels like a discovery rather than a thesis; and how to land the remedy in a way that is warm, honest, and not saccharine. Then write the speech.

Constraints: Do not quote Cicero or any other ancient text directly—paraphrase. Do not cite editions, line numbers, or scholarly works. Do not mention debates among classicists. Keep the philosophy accurate but accessible. The speech should be speakable: short sentences, clear transitions, room for pauses. It should feel like a person talking to neighbors, not a paper being read aloud. It should acknowledge the mixed audience—never condescending to the non-specialists, never showing off for the classicist. And it should be honest about the difficulty of old age; the remedy should not sound like a slogan.
