I've been meeting with two friends every week to try to get to the bottom of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and at this point I can finish everyone's sentences before they start, which I take as a bad sign. Here's the shape of it, compressed. Lottery case: a hundred tickets, exactly one wins, for each ticket you seem justified in believing that it lost, but you can't be justified in believing all hundred of those at once, since you know one of them is false. Preface case: an author writes a book, is justified in believing each claim she makes, and is also justified in believing that at least one of them is wrong. We have four positions taped to the whiteboard and none of them has budged in three months. (1) Keep closure — justification is closed under conjunction — and give up the idea that high probability is enough for justified belief, so you never get to believe of any single ticket that it lost. (2) Keep the high-probability rule and give up closure; justification just isn't closed under conjunction, live with it. (3) Say both cases are artifacts of conflating graded credence with binary acceptance, and that nothing should ever be outright accepted on merely probabilistic grounds. (4) Say they aren't one paradox at all — the lottery is about what an observer can believe about a random draw, the preface is about a fallible author's attitude toward her own output — and that lumping them together is what manufactures the illusion of a single problem. The friend who holds (4) keeps pointing out that in both cases you have a set of attitudes each of which looks fine on its own and which can't all be true together, and says that's the only real commonality, and that it's too thin to do any work.

My own suspicion — and I want you to test it rather than agree with me — is that (1) through (3) are largely talking past each other, that "justified belief" and "rational acceptance" are being used as a norm-governed speech act by some of us and as a credence-threshold by others, and that a chunk of the apparent disagreement would evaporate if we said which we meant. But I don't trust that diagnosis, because it feels too cheap, and because the four of us genuinely do seem to care about different things: two of us want an account of what you may assert, one of us wants an account of what you may believe, and one of us mostly wants to know whether a preface is ever rationally assertable at all. So: don't give me a survey of the literature, give me a diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute, a dispute about which cases count as data, a dispute about what the target phenomenon even is, or several questions wearing one name? Ground the diagnosis in what each position actually commits you to — tell me what (1) has to say about the preface, what (4) has to say about a lottery you bought a ticket in yourself, and so on. And then tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, a decision to stop asking one of the questions, or nothing — maybe it's just hard and we should stop meeting.
