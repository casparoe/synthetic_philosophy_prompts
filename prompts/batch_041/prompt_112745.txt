I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm putting together a feature on a historic ship that's been rebuilt so many times over the centuries that people argue over whether it's really the same ship. A classics-major friend sent me this bit from Plutarch and said "this is where your story actually starts," and I'll admit I'm in over my head:

"The ship on which Theseus sailed with the youths and returned in safety, the thirty-oared galley, was preserved by the Athenians down to the time of Demetrius Phalereus. They took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places, so that the vessel became a standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth, some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel."

Can you walk me through it? The stuff I can't get past:

1. He calls it a question about "growth." I always assumed this was a puzzle about identity -- same ship or not. What's "growth" doing there, and why would swapping out planks count as a case of growth at all? Is this a translation thing? If the underlying idea is more like "increase" or "getting bigger," I'd love to understand why that's the frame instead of just "is it the same thing or not."

2. He dates the preservation to "the time of Demetrius Phalereus." Who is that, and is it meant to be a real terminus -- like, the ship stopped being kept up around then -- or is it just a fancy way of saying "for a very long time"?

3. "Some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel." Who are the some and the others, and what was actually riding on the answer for them? My hunch is the fight wasn't really about boats.

4. My friend keeps insisting the ancient version is a cousin of an older argument about parts and wholes -- something like, if you add a piece to a thing, is it the same thing that just got bigger, or is it a new thing? I have no idea if that's right. Does it actually fit what Plutarch is describing?

5. Finally: how did this turn into the "Ship of Theseus" that everyone hauls out for personal identity and brain transplants? Did the modern telling keep what the ancient one was really about, or did something important get dropped along the way?

I'm not looking for a reading list or anything like that -- I just need to actually understand the passage well enough to write two paragraphs for general readers without mangling it. Where you're filling in a gap with a guess rather than something solid, please flag that, because I've definately been burned before by confident-sounding glosses. And if any of my assumptions up there are off, say so.
