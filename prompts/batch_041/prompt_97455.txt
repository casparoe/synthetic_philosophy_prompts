QUESTION (20 marks)

A student of cultural evolution claims that a nursery rhyme spreading through a daycare is a Darwinian process: the tune exists in variants, some variants get sung more than others, and singers learn what they hear. A critic objects that the process is not Darwinian at all, since nobody copies the tune. Each singer reconstructs it from a partly forgotten memory, and a singer who has heard two versions will often produce a third that blends them. Using the concepts of replication, heritability, and selection, assess the critic's objection. Is the spread of the tune a case of evolution by natural selection? And does the meme's-eye view illuminate this case, or merely redescribe it?

ANSWER SUBMITTED

For a process to count as evolution by natural selection it needs three things: variation, differential reproduction, and heritability, so that the offspring of the more successful variants resemble those variants rather than the population average. The tune case supplies the first two easily. Variants exist, and some of them — the catchier ones, presumably — get sung, and therefore heard, and therefore learned, more often than others. The interesting question is heritability, and here I think the critic is right.

The critic's objection is really the old problem of blending inheritance, the objection that Darwin's critics pressed against him. If a singer who has heard two versions produces a blend of both, the resulting variant is intermediate between its two "parents," and averaging of this kind reduces the variance in the population with each generation. Selection can accumulate change only if variance is preserved; blending eats the fuel that selection runs on. The student's claim that the tune is a Darwinian process therefore fails: what we have is variation without heritability, that is, change without cumulative selection. Reconstructive memory makes matters worse, since the "copy" is not a copy at all but a fresh composition, and where there is no copying there is no replication, and where there is no replication there is nothing for selection to act on.

There is a further reason to resist the Darwinian label. Cultural transmission is Lamarckian: what a singer acquires during her lifetime she can pass on, and this inheritance of acquired characters is precisely what Darwinism denies. A process whose inheritance is Lamarckian is not a Darwinian process, whatever the surface similarities, so calling the tune's spread Darwinian is a category error.

The meme's-eye view, finally, adds little here. It invites us to see the tune as using the singers in order to get itself copied, but since, on the argument above, nothing is being copied, the perspective has no application to this case; at best it is a vivid metaphor. The critic's reconstructionist picture — culture as a process in which each generation remakes what it receives — is both more accurate and less mystifying.
