I'm a software engineer who works on the data side of a consumer subscription app, and I have no formal training in philosophy. My team keeps having the same argument whenever we try to interpret an experiment, and I'd like the argument to be had properly rather than badly.

The two sides are these. The choice-constitutive view: a user's preference just is her choice. A preference ranking is a summary of the choices a person makes, and any talk of "what she really wants" that goes beyond her choices is either a restatement of them or empty. The choice-evidential view: choices are evidence of preferences, not the same thing as them. Preferences are states of a person that her choices can track well or badly, so there can be a genuine gap between what she chooses and what is good for her — and that gap is what makes paternalistic product decisions intelligible in the first place.

Please draft the joint statement these two positions could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, restricted to the question of how welfare claims may be licensed from choice data. The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what choice data can establish, what it cannot, how self-reports and stated preferences should be treated, and whether any part of the dispute is merely terminological.
2. A table of the residual disagreements, each row stating its own side's position in wording that side would accept.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, together with what each side predicts about that case.

Two pieces of scaffolding I want the document to engage with explicitly, because they are what my team can never get past:

- Menu relativity. Every choice is a choice from some available set, and any product change alters that set. So the choice-constitutive view appears to owe an account of which menu's choices are the preference-revealing ones, and it is not obvious that such an account can avoid appealing to something other than choice.
- A notion I am stipulating, "audit-stable": a choice is audit-stable for a person when, if she were shown a plain description of the mechanism that produced the choice and of the options it displaced, she would still make it. Treat this as a candidate criterion; say whether either side can accept it, and on what reading, and what each side predicts about choices that fail it.

Anchoring case, if it helps: our "play next episode automatically" setting is on by default; the large majority of users never touch it, while in a survey a majority say they would rather it were off; an experiment that turns it off cuts viewing time by about a third.

No citations or reading lists, please — I care about the argument, not the bibliography. Keep the whole document to at most 900 words.
