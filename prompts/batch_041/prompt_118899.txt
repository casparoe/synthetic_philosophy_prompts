I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of mind module on whether the unity of consciousness is the same thing as the unity of the person, and I want to construct one case that does a precise job. I need two views to be driven to their atypical verdicts simultaneously. Call the first the experience-first view: subjects of experience are individuated by phenomenal fields, so a single integrated field means a single subject. Call the second the subject-first view: subjects are individuated by unified agency, so a single deliberative standpoint means a single subject. In the ordinary split-brain case the experience-first view counts two subjects and the subject-first view counts one; I want a case in which the experience-first view must count one and the subject-first view must count two.

Construct it. It should be a single realistic clinical history: a patient after surgical separation of the hemispheres who retains one narrow residual pathway between them. You may stipulate the bandwidth of that pathway, but it must be a single channel and not a bundle of contrivances, and it must be wide enough to integrate phenomenal content while being too narrow to integrate deliberation. No invented surgery, no exotic physics, and keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph. The verdicts must fall out of the two views rather than being written into the setup.

Then state each view's verdict and the reasoning that generates it, identify the one structural feature doing the work, say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and give the strongest line each view's defender would take to resist the verdict it dislikes. Finish with a table with columns: view | verdict on the number of subjects | criterion producing that verdict | why the verdict is atypical for that view | the defender's best objection.
