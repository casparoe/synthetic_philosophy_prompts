I am a quantitative demographer by training: I spend my working life on Lexis diagrams, age–period–cohort models, cohort-replacement decompositions, and the identification problem that follows from the exact linear dependence among age, period and cohort. I am fluent in identifiability, design-matrix constraints, exchangeability, potential outcomes, latent-class and mixture models, and variance decomposition; I can read a hazard function without flinching, but I have never taken a course in metaphysics and I know the phrase 'natural kind' only from overheard seminars. A colleague in sociology keeps insisting that the interesting question about generations is whether they are real kinds — whether 'Boomer', 'Millennial' and 'Gen Z' name anything that does explanatory work, or are merely administrative cut-points imposed on a continuous variable. I would like the philosophical debate about generations as real kinds restated in the vocabulary I actually use — cohorts, periods, ages, the identification problem, cohort replacement, synthetic cohorts, latent classes, exchangeability, variance components, interventions and counterfactuals — as faithfully as you can manage, followed by a residue report.

Take the positions one at a time, so far as they can be distinguished: the realist view that generations are kinds with something like a shared causal structure, a common formative experience or a mechanism that makes members alike in ways that outrun their birth years; the nominalist or conventionalist view that they are partitions drawn for convenience, any apparent clustering being an artefact of where the cut-points fall; and the intermediate view that generations are real but socially constructed — real because the classification loops back and alters the people classified, not because it tracks a division already present in nature. For each, say what translates cleanly into my vocabulary, what has to be distorted to fit, and what finds no expression there at all. I expect the identification problem to be carrying some of the load, but I want you to specify precisely where, and to resist treating a cohort effect as though it were the same thing as a generation.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about most, with two items in particular. First: the sociological notion of a generational unit — a subgroup that works out a shared response to the same historical predicament, as against the whole birth band — looks to me like a claim about second-order clustering within a cohort. Can it be expressed as such, or does it require something my vocabulary cannot supply, and if so, what exactly? Second: my own working hypothesis is that generation membership can be modelled as a latent class whose mixing proportions drift across periods, with cohort effects identified only relative to whatever constraint I impose. I want to know whether the realists' claims survive that reconstruction, or whether the reconstruction quietly converts their position into the nominalist one; if a claim looks substantive only because it has smuggled in the target framework's assumptions, say so plainly.

Close with a verdict on the residue. Is it a defect of my vocabulary — is there something about generations that the demographic apparatus is structurally unable to say — or is it a sign that the philosophical claims were less clear than they appeared, the real disagreement being about which constraints to impose rather than about what generations are? Please write in British English, and do not give me a reading list, bibliography or citations; I want the argument, not the references.
