I am in my final year of sixth form and writing an extended essay on Stoicism. The essay has to compare two things, and I have reached the point where the comparison is no longer the easy part. The first is the Stoic distinction between what is up to us and what is not — the claim that only our own judgements, impulses and the use we make of our impressions are properly ours, while our bodies, reputations, possessions and circumstances are not. The second is the circle of control as it is used in the contemporary self-help and productivity writing that has grown up around the modern revival of Stoicism, where the point of the distinction is to tell you where to spend your attention and effort.

Please compare the two and give me a verdict on which, if either, I should be defending in the essay. I would rather the comparison did not collapse into the ancients were subtle and the moderns are shallow, so here is what I have already worked out and would like tested rather than merely confirmed.

First, I suspect the two views are answering different questions. I want to distinguish two senses of control. Authorship: an action is genuinely mine to own, and so can be credited or blamed to me. Leverage: my effort makes a noticeable difference to the outcome. The Stoic line looks like a claim about authorship, whereas the modern circle looks like a claim about leverage, and therefore an empirical one, revisable as we learn more about how much difference effort actually makes. If that is right, then the modern version is not a diluted Stoicism but an answer to a different question — and the interesting question becomes whether a claim about authorship can really support the practical advice the modern writers want to draw from it, or whether they are borrowing a distinction that was never meant to do that work.

Second, please deal with the awkward middle category rather than ignoring it: things that are neither wholly up to me nor wholly outside me — whether a friend forgives me, whether an illness responds to treatment, whether my revision pays off. Several people have suggested adding a third category to the dichotomy to accommodate these. Does adding one save the picture, or does it destroy the point of it?

Third, the Stoic habit of pursuing something while adding the proviso that obtaining it is not up to one: does that idea survive translation into the modern version, or is it one of the things the modern version simply cannot say?

Argue it out from first principles.
