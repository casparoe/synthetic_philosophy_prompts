You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a strong grounding in the metaphysics of causation, and you have a talent for building diagnostic instruments that sort people by what they actually believe rather than by the labels they already use. Work carefully: the value of this deliverable lies entirely in the questions, so begin with a scratch section in which you brainstorm the commitments that could separate reasonable people here, mark which of them are independent of one another, and only then draw the tree. Do not invoke any literature; there is no bibliography, reading list, or named author or work to produce.

Context: I am preparing a handout for an interdisciplinary reading group, half philosophers and half cognitive scientists, that keeps arguing past itself about whether the symbolic or representational level of description earns its keep in explaining what a system does. I want a decision tree that carries a reader from a starting commitment to a named position.

The fixed case, which every question in the tree must be about, and the only example you may use: the careenium. Picture a large vessel packed with tiny magnetic marbles — call them simms — whose only relevant properties are local dispositions to attract and repel their immediate neighbours. Out of purely local encounters the aggregate settles into stable clumps and travelling patterns; call the clumps simmballs. An observer finds it indispensable to describe certain simmballs as carrying information, as standing for features of the environment, and as steering the aggregate's subsequent behaviour. The quarrel is whether those descriptions do any causal work — whether the simmballs push the simms around — or whether the simm-level dynamics already tell the complete causal story and the symbolic description is a mere convenience.

Requirements:

- Six to nine branch nodes. Each node is a single question with two or three mutually exclusive answers, phrased so that a reader can answer it without knowing where it leads and without having read any particular author.
- Leaves carry the positions, each named (or marked unnamed if nobody holds it), with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The following must appear: eliminativism about the symbolic level; reductive identity; non-reductive physicalism in which higher-level properties inherit their causal powers from their realizers; causal exclusion or epiphenomenalism; realism about real patterns; instrumentalism or stance-dependence; and at least one position that dissolves the question rather than answering it. Add any further leaves the space contains.
- Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of positions: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the positions within that space.
- Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say so, and diagnose whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what additional question would separate them.
- Keep the nodes honest about levels: no node may presuppose that the simm-level description is the privileged or ground-floor one, and no node may presuppose that causing means transferring force rather than making a difference. Where the difference between those two readings of causation does real work, make it an explicit node.
- The instrument must sort by answers, not by self-description: no node may take the form of asking whether someone is a reductionist, a realist, or the like.
- Close with a short note on the two or three nodes where you think most actual disagreement lives.
