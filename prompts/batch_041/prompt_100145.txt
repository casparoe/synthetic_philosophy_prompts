Hey, so I'm on my school's Puzzle Day commitee — I'm a junior, we run this big logic competition every spring, and we've got a mess I have to write up before our meeting Thursday. Here's what happened. The final puzzle this year was one of those string-rewriting ones: you start with a little string of letters, you've got a few rules for adding or swapping letters, and the goal is to turn the starting string into some target string by applying the rules. Nobody solved it. But one team turned in something totally different: instead of a sequence of steps, they gave an argument that the target can never be reached no matter what — they found a property of the starting string that every single rule preserves but that the target string doesn't have (basically like the number of one kind of letter always staying even, so you can never get to a string where it's odd). So they proved the puzzle is impossible. Now half our judges want to disqualify them, because the rules literally say the answer has to be a series of moves that ends at the target, and they gave zero moves. The other half says this is obviously the smartest answer anyone turned in and the puzzle was secretly unsolvable, so they should win. There's a deeper worry too: is this even the same kind of thing as solving a puzzle? They stepped out of the system — stopped playing by the rules and started reasoning about the rules from the outside — and some of us think that's kind of cheating, or at least that it answers a different question than the one we actually asked. I read somewhere that people call this move jumping out of the system, and that it's a real thing in logic, not just kids being clever.

Can you write the decision memo I'll bring to the committee? I need a clear recommendation (award them, don't, award with conditions, whatever you think) plus the actual reasoning for it, and I really want it to take the other side seriously instead of just dunking on it — like genuinely explain why someone would say a proof that the puzzle is impossible isn't a solution to the puzzle, and why that might still be wrong. Also throw in something about what we should do with the rules for next year so I don't have to have this fight again. Keep it short enough that I could actually read it out loud at the meeting without dying of embarrassment.
