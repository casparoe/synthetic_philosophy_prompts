I've got no academic background, I just read a lot and argue with my mates, and there's a line I keep bumping into that's apparently from some nineteenth-century German historian — the gist being that a historian's job is just to show how things actually were and not to dress them up or pass judgement. My mate used it on me last week when I said a documentary we'd watched seemed to be pushing a particular story rather than just giving us what happened: he said that's the whole point of history, you tell it how it actually was and leave your interpretation out of it. Part of me agrees but it definately feels too easy. I've half-heard that the German word in the original can mean either 'actually' or 'essentially', and that people like Carr and Hayden White reckoned the whole ideal was a bit of a swindle.

What I'd like is a menu rather than a verdict. What are the seperate precise things that one line could be claiming, going from the totally harmless up to the really strong, and for each one, is it obviously right, is it genuinely up for dispute, or is it just false? Then I want to know which of those readings would actually get my mate what he wanted — the licence to lay out a pile of facts and no interpretation — and whether the original bloke even meant it the way he thinks. I've a suspicion the phrase has drifted a long way from whatever it started out meaning.

Could you lay it out like this:
1. 'The line, unpacked' — a numbered menu of the distinct claims hiding in it, from trivial to radical, a couple of sentences each. This should be the bulk of it.
2. 'Verdicts' — one line per reading, saying whether it's a platitude, a live dispute, or plainly false.
3. 'What my mate actually needs' — a short paragraph on which reading would do the work he wants it to do.
4. 'What the original fella probably meant' — a short paragraph.

Please write it in British English.
