I'm a science journalist and I've somehow agreed to give the commencement address at my old j-school in a few weeks, and I'm stuck. Here's the idea I want to build the whole thing on. There's a distinction I've found genuinely clarifying between two ways of being badly informed. An epistemic bubble is when you simply never get exposed to the other side — nobody lied to you, the information just never reached you, and the fix is exposure. An echo chamber is a nastier thing: you've been taught that everyone outside is a liar, a shill, or acting in bad faith, so outside voices get discredited on sight, which means exposure doesn't help and can even reinforce the chamber. Bubbles pop when the news gets in; chambers are largely immune to that. I want the speech structured around this, aimed at a room of brand-new journalists who mostly believe their profession is the cure for both — and I don't want to hand them the comfortable version where they're the immune system of democracy. So use the distinction to press on where journalism itself breeds bubbles, where it has historically helped build actual chambers, and make them sit with the awkward fact that a commencement address is itself a room full of people who already agree with each other. It has to work for the professors in the audience too, some of whom know this stuff cold and will catch me if im fudging it. About six or seven minutes read aloud. Give me the speech itself, no preamble and no commentary around it.
