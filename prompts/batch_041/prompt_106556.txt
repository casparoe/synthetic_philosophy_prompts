I am a retired chemist who has only recently begun to read philosophy in earnest, and I have been attending a reading group led by a scholar of Kant whose judgements I have come to trust. Over the past few months she has given verdicts on a number of cases involving truthfulness and deception, and I should like to know what she would say about the case we are to discuss next week. I am interested in the rule her own verdicts reveal, rather than in what Kant himself is supposed to have said.

Her verdicts so far are as follows. A painter asks a friend whether he admires her new canvas; he finds it dreadful but replies that it is very striking, which is true though intended to mislead; she says he has lied. A doctor tells a patient's spouse, who has a right to know the patient's condition, that the patient is comfortable, when the patient is in severe pain, intending to spare the spouse's feelings; she says this is a lie. A woman refuses to tell a stranger on the street where she lives; she says this is permissible. An actor on stage declares that he is the King of France; she says this is not a lie.

Next week's case is a variant of the famous murderer at the door. A murderer asks where your friend is; instead of denying it, you answer with a statement that is literally true but intended to mislead him. For instance, you say that you have not seen your friend since breakfast, which is true, and you know the murderer will infer that your friend is not in the house.

What verdict would she give on this new case? What rule or rules is she implicitly running, and what other rules fit her earlier verdicts just as well? Which verdict would you predict, and why? Finally, which further case would best distinguish the candidate rules from one another? Please answer in British English.
