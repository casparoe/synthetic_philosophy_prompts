I'm in Year 12 and I've got to write an essay for our school philosophy club. It's not for marks but there's a prize and a smug trophy, and I actually care about this one. We did a session on a game called the centipede, where two players take turns and on each turn you can either take what's on the table (which ends the game, and the other person gets a bit less than you) or pass, which makes the pot bigger for the next person. The longer you both keep passing, the more money there is, so if you both cooperated all the way you'd both walk off with a fortune compared to the few quid you get for taking on turn one. The catch is the last turn: the person whose go it is can take a huge pile and you get left with less than them. Our teacher told us the 'correct' answer is that you take straight away, because you reason backwards -- at the last turn the player takes, so the player before them knows that and takes first, and so on right back to the start, so a rational player grabs the money on move one. Everyone in the room agreed that's what a rational player does, and then everyone in the room also said they'd definately never do it, which I find suspicious. I've been argueing about this with my mate Priya for three weeks (she does A-level Maths and is insufferable about it) and I want to write my essay on it, but I've got three half-arguments from different people and no idea which one actually wins.

First, Priya reckons the backwards reasoning is quietly assuming that at every stage you're certain the other person will be coldly rational at every later stage, and that this kind of mutual certainty basically can't hold in a real game, so the conclusion is only about a situation that never actually happens. Second, another friend says the fix is just to decide at the very start that you're going to keep passing no matter what, and then never revisit the decision -- and suddenly passing is the rational thing, because your plan is consistent as a whole even if each individual step looks daft on its own. And third, someone in the club said that if there's even a tiny chance the other person is the sort who never takes, or who doesn't get the logic, then it can be worth passing for a good while to find out what kind of person you're up against, which sounds like it turns the whole thing into a game about reading your opponent rather than a maths problem at all. I can't tell if those are three versions of the same objection, three separate objections, or two of them are just cope.

So can you write me an essay that sorts this out? I want the arguments on both sides actually laid out and pressed on, not a summary of what famous people said -- I want to know where each of the three objections is strong and where it falls over. And I want you to come down somewhere: in this game, does a rational player take straight away, or pass, or is the honest answer 'it depends' -- and if it depends, then say precisely on what and why, and defend that. Please don't end on a shrug, my teacher will kill me. Also write it in British English, she's weird about that (she circled 'color' in my last essay and just wrote 'no' next to it).
