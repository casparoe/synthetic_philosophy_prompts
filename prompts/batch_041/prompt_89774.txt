I serve on the board of a small housing co-op — twelve units, a set of written bylaws, and, because we are all academics and slightly insufferable about procedure, a standing argument about our amendment clause. I would like to know whether the thing we keep colliding with has a name. The bylaws provide that they may be amended by a two-thirds vote at a special meeting, and that the secretary decides whether a proposed amendment is in order. Last year we passed an amendment, by the required two-thirds, that changed the amendment rule itself: from then on, amendments would pass by simple majority, and the chair rather than the secretary would rule on whether a proposal was in order. We thought we had been clever. Two objections followed. The secretary, in what I concede was a pointed gesture, ruled our amendment out of order on the ground that under the rules in force at the moment of the vote, the proposal to strip her of the ruling power was itself subject to her ruling. And another member argued that if the old clause really is gone, then every amendment we have ever passed was authorized by a rule that no longer exists, so all of them lapse. Nobody could say which objection was right, and nobody could say what rule would settle it, since the rule that would settle it is precisely what is in dispute. What struck me is that in each case we reached for something rule-shaped that is not a rule: we said the amendment really did pass because the members accepted it, or because it is what the founders would have wanted, or because holding otherwise would be absurd.

I gather I am circling a known neighborhood. I once played a game, Nomic, I think, in which the only legal move is to change a rule, and I have the impression that legal philosophers have written a great deal about whether a rule can amend itself and about the sort of thing — acceptance, custom, force, an unamendable presupposition — that has to sit at the bottom and stop the regress. But the puzzle I keep reading about seems to be whether self-amendment is possible at all, and that is not my situation: ours went through, everyone has been operating under the new clause for a year, and the live question is what makes the act of changing the rule valid, and what that act does to everything authorized by the rule it replaced. So the label matters less to me than the carving, and a bare name will not satisfy me. Tell me whether there is an established concept that covers exactly this — the validity of the act that alters the very rule determining validity — or, if there is none, coin one and define it, and then do the work of marking its edges. In particular: how does it differ from the general paradox of self-amendment, from the idea of a foundational norm or a master rule that is accepted rather than valid, and from ordinary retroactivity? Is the retroactive-invalidation objection part of the same phenomenon or a distinct one? Is the rule-shaped non-rule we reach for — acceptance, custom, "the founders' intent" — a fourth thing, or just the familiar regress-stopper under a new name? And how do the boundary cases fall: an amendment that abolishes the authority that passed it, one that makes future amendment impossible, one that was passed under the old clause but purports to bind votes already taken under it? Please write the answer in American English, and where you introduce a term of art, define it in plain words before using it.
