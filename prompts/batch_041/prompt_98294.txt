I have no training in philosophy, but I have been turning over the toxin puzzle on my own for some time, and I have arrived at a view I would now like you to attack.

The puzzle, as I understand it: a wealthy stranger will pay you a large sum tonight if and only if, by midnight, you genuinely intend to swallow tomorrow a substance that will make you thoroughly ill for a day and leave no lasting harm. The money is transferred tonight. Tomorrow, nothing forces you to drink, and once the money is safely yours, drinking looks pointless. The usual question is whether you can rationally form the intention at all.

My view is that the puzzle's real lesson is not about intentions specifically but about a split within practical rationality itself. I think there are two independent tiers of appraisal that need not agree: the rationality of the state you are in at a moment — the dispositions, commitments and intentions you carry — and the rationality of what you then do. On my reading there is no single what-should-I-do question that both tiers are answering; the tiers can return opposite verdicts about one person at one time and both be correct. The person who sincerely intends tonight and declines to drink tomorrow is not failing at rationality in either tier; they are answering two different questions, and the answers differ because the questions do. I take this to be the general shape of rational agency rather than an artefact of this particular payoff structure.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed the view is. Which arguments, cases, intuitions or formal results would count against it, ranked from those that would be decisive to those that would merely be awkward? I would particularly like to know what someone who holds my view ought themselves to concede as counting against it, rather than only what a critic would say. Please separate the two, and do not spare the view.

A few things I have been chewing over, and I would like your judgement on whether each tells against me or for me:

- An altruistic variant: the payment goes to a third party I care about and I receive nothing. If my two-tier verdict survives unchanged — intending rational, drinking not — does that support the general claim, or does the change in who benefits expose the view as a disguised appeal to self-interest? And if the intuition shifts, what does that show?

- A paralysis variant: I must form the intention tonight, but I know that tomorrow I will be physically unable to drink at all. Does intending remain rational? If it does, my two-tier picture seems to apply so trivially that the puzzle loses its bite; if it does not, then the rationality of the state depends on the availability of action, which is exactly what my view denies.

- An absurdity worry: if states can be appraised independently of the actions they produce, what stops the view from licensing me to make myself believe anything, or to cultivate any disposition whatever, for a sufficient reward? Is that a reductio, or can the view draw a principled line between states that may be rationally cultivated for their own sake and those that may not?

- An iterated variant: the reward in each round is paid only if I also intend in the next round, and so on without end. Does the two-tier picture make the whole chain of intentions rational all the way down, and if so, is that a consequence my view can live with?

Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether the view is doing any real work or has been qualified into something that can accommodate every possible outcome — and if it has, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or simply the price of a general one. The thing I would personally find most persuasive against me would be a demonstration that the two tiers cannot come apart in principle: that any sensible notion of a state being rational just reduces to a claim about the actions it would produce in the agent's actual situation. If that reduction works, I would want to know where it bites and what is left of the puzzle afterwards.
