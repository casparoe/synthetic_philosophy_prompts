I keep noticing a pattern in people who work with AI assistants, and I cannot tell whether it already has a name. Two instances. A radiology trainee reads every scan alongside a model's pre-read; when she overrules the model she is right often enough to keep trusting her own eye, but the cases she wins are the ones the model is weak on, and the cases she loses are the ones she would not have flagged in any event. A graduate student writes code with an assistant and still catches the obvious defects, so his confidence in the output is high, but the defects that survive are the subtle ones — the ones that would have required a fluency he never had to build, because the assistant built it for him. Generalizing: as the tool improves, the user's capacity to verify does not merely fail to keep pace, it erodes, since verifying was the practice that produced it; the residual failures are not a random sample but cluster where the user's independent grip is weakest; and confidence rises rather than falls, because confidence tracks the failures the user can still catch, and those are precisely the ones that are disappearing. At the limit there is no fixed point: checking the output would require the competence the tool displaced. Is there an established concept for this configuration? If so, I want to know which candidate fits best, and where the near misses — relying on testimony, losing a skill one stops practising, trusting a system one does not understand — each fall short. If not, name and define it, and mark its edges, above all the line between it and ordinary, healthy reliance on a tool.
