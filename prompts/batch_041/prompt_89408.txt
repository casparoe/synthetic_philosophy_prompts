In my aesthetics reading group we've been going round in circles for about a year on a hypothetical I thought would take ten minutes. A forger in 1900 makes a canvas that's indistinguishable, to any eye and any test we can run, from a 1650 orginal, sells it as the original, and it hangs in a museum as the original for fifty years until the fraud comes out. Now, a century later, the forgery has its own history, its own scholarly literature, and the forger is collected as an artist in his own right. Did anything of value get lost when the truth came out? We're split and stuck: one camp says yes, the old canvas was a work and this is only a copy; a second says no, every perceptible feature survived and the history the first camp keeps appealing to wasn't destroyed, just swapped for a different one; a third says the question is confused because 'value' is doing two jobs in the sentence. We agree on every fact about both canvases, and we all agree the museum should label it honestly. So what kind of disagreement is this actually -- a verbal one, a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether a work's past is part of the work, or two different questions wearing one word? My hunch is that it's a metaphysical discomfort about duplicates that no further art criticism will shift, but I want a diagnosis with reasons and a straight answer about what, if anything, would count as progress here.
