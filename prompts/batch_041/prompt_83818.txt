You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics, with a research focus on the theory of promising and a reputation for mapping debates as structured logical spaces rather than summarizing received views. I need a diagnostic instrument for an advanced ethics seminar: a decision tree, equivalently a taxonomy, of the positions on why promises bind.

Two constraints on what you may rely on. First, you cannot search the internet, so do not cite authors, titles, dates, or page numbers, and do not attribute views to named people. Refer to each position by a descriptive label and define it in your own words; where a view has a familiar name, you may use the name, but you must characterize it yourself in a sentence. Second, do not include a literature review; I will supply references myself.

Before you draft anything, spend some time brainstorming and mapping the logical space. Do not begin from a list of famous views and then hang questions on them; begin from the questions and let the positions fall out of the answers. Think carefully about whether the tree genuinely separates the leaves you place on it, and be honest where it does not.

Build the tree under the following specifications.

Each internal node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. No node may be of the form "do you accept the practice theory?" Instead, each node should be a verdict on a concrete case or a commitment about the structure of obligation, phrased so that the reader discovers their position by answering rather than by labeling themselves.

Fix these diagnostic cases, and use them as branch points wherever positions genuinely diverge: the release case, where the promisee sincerely and with full authority over their own interests tells the promisor not to bother; the silent non-performance case, where the promisor simply fails to perform, offers no explanation, and the promisee never relied on the promise and does not care; the secret promise, where no one knows and no expectation is created; the promisee's death before performance, where a third party would have benefited; the promise to someone incapable of claiming performance, such as a young child, a person who has died, an animal, a deity, or one's own future self; the promise extracted by duress or deception; the promise to do something mildly wrong; the case where the promisor judges, on balance, that the promise should not be kept; and the vow, oath, or private resolution addressed to no particular person.

I want the tree organized, at least in part, around a seam that you should treat as the spine of the exercise: a promise can be understood as an arrangement that relocates discretion over whether the promised act is performed. For each position, ask where the discretion ends up. It may stay with the promisor, so that the promise is a self-directed constraint defeasible by her own judgment of the balance of reasons; it may pass to the promisee, so that the promise is a transfer of authority and the promisee may demand, waive, or release; it may be held jointly, so that neither party can unilaterally cancel; or it may rest with no one, so that a social practice fixes the terms. Then ask what each answer predicts about the release case and the silent non-performance case, and use those disagreements as branch points.

A second seam should run alongside the first: whether the source of the bindingness is the promisor's act, understood as an exercise of a normative power; the promisee's interest, whether in assurance, reliance, or authority; or a social institution that assigns the power and defines its limits. Cross this with whether the account treats breach as a directed wrong owed to the promisee, an impersonal wrong against a practice or against the value of trust, or no wrong at all but a reason for regret.

At least the following must appear as leaves: a self-legislation or normative-power view; a transfer-of-authority view on which the promisee holds the discretion; a practice or convention view; a reliance or assurance view; a non-conventionalist wrong-making view on which the promise binds because breaking it would use or mislead the promisee, independent of any practice; a joint-commitment view; a relationship or trust view; a perform-or-justify view on which the duty is to perform or else answer for not doing so, rather than an outright duty to perform; a deflationary view on which promising generates no distinctive obligation and binds only through ordinary moral reasons; and a self-regarding view on which promising binds chiefly for the promisor's own sake, through integrity or resoluteness. Add further leaves if the logical space demands them, and mark any leaf no one defends as unnamed.

For each leaf, give the label, one sentence defining the position, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, that is, the case it handles worst, and its verdict on the release case and the silent non-performance case.

Then do three things. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and explain why they collapse into one another. Note which combinations of answers the tree leaves unoccupied, and diagnose each: is no one there because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored? Finally, state plainly which of your own branch questions you are least confident actually discriminates, and what evidence would settle it.

Size and format: eight to twelve internal questions and twelve to sixteen leaves, for an advanced undergraduate and graduate audience. Present the tree as an indented numbered outline, with each node written as a question and each leaf carrying its label and annotations, followed by the flagged pairs, the empty branches, and your statement of uncertainty.
