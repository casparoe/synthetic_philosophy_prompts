Consider two rival criteria for personal survival through a change of physical substrate.

Criterion A — organizational invariance. A person survives a procedure just in case the procedure preserves, at every moment, a functional organization sufficiently similar to the original's. By "functional organization" I mean the pattern of causal dependencies among the system's components: which component's output depends on which other components' outputs, and in what way. On Criterion A, the material that realizes the pattern is irrelevant, and so is whether the successor's states are causally descended from the states of the particular original components; what matters is that a system instantiating the pattern is present at every stage. Note two consequences in advance: a perfect reproduction built from a scan qualifies as the person, and a system whose organization drifts far enough from the original's ceases to be the person even if its causal history is impeccable.

Criterion B — token-tracking. A person survives a procedure just in case there is a single, unbranched, temporally continuous chain of causal state-transitions running from the original system's states to the successor's, each transition carrying the state of one and the same system forward. On Criterion B, a causally disconnected but organizationally perfect copy is not the person; a moment at which two successor systems both stand in the right causal relation to the original is either fatal to survival or requires a tie-breaking rule; and organizational drift is not fatal, since the chain may wander.

Treat both criteria as stipulated for the purposes of this exercise. No citations or references to the literature are needed; work out the verdicts from the criteria as stated.

Apply the two criteria to the following five procedures:

1. Stepwise substitution. A surgeon replaces the patient's neurons one at a time with silicon chips, each a functional duplicate of the neuron it replaces and wired into the same pattern of connections. Each original neuron is destroyed the instant its chip takes over. The patient is awake and conversing throughout, and nothing about the patient's behavior, memories, or cognitive profile changes at any point.

2. Scan and rebuild. The patient is scanned, and a new system is built from the scan whose organization matches the original's. At the instant the new system is switched on, the original is destroyed. Nothing passes from the old system to the new one except the scan.

3. Drift. The procedure is as in (1), except that the chips are not functional duplicates: each differs slightly from the neuron it replaces, and the differences compound. Within a year the patient's tastes, memories, and temperament have changed beyond recognition; the patient reports feeling like a different person, and observers agree. There is no interruption, no copying, and no branching at any stage.

4. Duplication. The scan and rebuild of (2) is carried out, but the new system is switched on while the original continues to run. For a year two systems run in parallel, both claiming continuity with the patient and both behaving alike; the original is then destroyed.

5. Bulk replacement under inactivity. The patient's brain is brought to a complete metabolic standstill — no neural activity of any kind for several hours — the neurons are replaced in one operation by functional duplicates, and activity is then restored. Memory, personality, and behavior are unaffected.

For each procedure, determine what Criterion A and Criterion B say about whether the patient survives. Where a criterion does not yield a determinate verdict, say so and identify precisely what additional stipulation would settle it.
