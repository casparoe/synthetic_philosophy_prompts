Hey, I'm a second-year econ PhD student who's gotten weirdly obsessed with the foundations of iterated elimination, and I keep banging into the same wall, so I figured I'd just ask. Here's the setup as I understand it — correct me if I've got the framing wrong, because figuring out whether I'm even asking the right question is half the point. A strategy is strictly dominated for a player if some other strategy of theirs — possibly a mixture — does strictly better against every pure choice the others could make. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is order-independent: no matter what order you delete in, you end up with the same survivors, which feels like evidence that you're tracking something real. But switch to weak dominance and the order starts to matter — different deletion orders leave different survivors — and now I can't tell whether that's a deep fact about the procedure or just proof that weak-dominance elimination was never a requirement of rationality in the first place. Then there's the mixed-strategy wrinkle that really gets me: a pure strategy can be strictly dominated by a fifty-fifty mix of two others even when no single pure strategy dominates it, so "a rational player never plays a dominated strategy" seems to quietly assume the player is willing to randomize — and people disagree about whether a mixed strategy is an actual act of deliberate randomization or just a bookkeeping device standing in for the opponents' uncertainty about a pure choice. And hovering over all of it is the epistemic stuff: one crowd proves theorems to the effect that common knowledge of rationality implies nobody plays a strategy that iterated elimination would have deleted, and another crowd says those theorems only go through because "rationality" has been watered down to something like "doesn't play a strictly dominated strategy whatever they believe," which is not what any of us means when we call a person rational.

What gets me is that this fight has definately been running for decades. As far as I can tell nobody disputes the theorems themselves — the math is settled — and yet nobody switches sides, which is exactly the signature of a disagreement that isn't about the math. My hunch, and I'd like you to attack it rather than just nod along, is that the two camps are using "rationality" for two different jobs: one side wants a decision-theoretic constraint on what a single agent may choose given fixed beliefs, the other wants a characterization of what follows when a whole profile of agents have beliefs about each other's beliefs, and the impasse is at least partly the word doing double duty. If that's right, is there a substantive question hiding underneath — some formal choice about which notion of dominance, or which knowledge operator, is doing the real work — or is the whole thing two questions wearing one name? And if it's partly verbal, does that deflate the debate, or does it actually matter for how we build solution concepts?
