ok a friend in my reading group dropped this on me and i cant decide if its profound or just a fancy way of giving up. its about the whole why-are-some-countries-rich thing. here's the argument as he wrote it on a napkin:

(1) To know that X causes Y you need either a comparison of cases that differ in X and nothing else, or some way of holding the other candidate causes fixed.
(2) Any two countries that differ in institutions also differ in geography, culture, colonial history, and plain luck. You never get the clean comparison.
(3) You can't run the experiment. You can't reroll a country's history with the institutions swapped and everything else held fixed.
(4) The natural experiments people lean on -- settler death rates, borders drawn straight through ethnic groups by colonizers, a sudden oil find -- only license a causal claim if the thing doing the instrumenting reaches prosperity through one channel and no other. But institutions shape geography too: they shape where people settle, what gets built, which land gets conserved, who leaves and who stays. So the channel is never clean.
(5) Therefore every proposed cause of prosperity is just a variable that happens to correlate with it.
(6) So we can never know why some nations are rich. The honest answer to the question is that we don't know and cant.

part of me wants to accept the conclusion, because every book i read on this obviously overclaims. but the argument feels like it eats everything: if (1)-(5) are right, dont they also show we cant know that smoking causes cancer, or that some germ causes a disease, even though we can never reroll a person's life either? and i do think we know those.

so tell me where it breaks. is it a step, or is it the word know? and whichever one i drop, what do i pay -- do i have to admit these books are all just storytelling, or is there a weaker kind of causal claim that survives and is actually enough to answer the question? i'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (6), if that matters.
