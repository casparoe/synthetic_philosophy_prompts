I am drafting a paper on epistemic deference to one's own future self, and I want a credence from you, with its derivation, rather than a rehearsal of the standard positions. Treat everything you need as stipulated below; do not go looking for literature.

The principle under discussion. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle (Journal of Philosophy, 1984), in the form in which it is usually stated: if an agent is certain that at some later time her credence in a proposition A will be x, then her credence in A now should be x. In the conditional form: her present credence in A, on the supposition that her later credence in A will be x, should equal x. The principle is defended as a coherence requirement binding an agent's credences at different times, and it is criticized precisely because it seems to mandate deference to a later self who is in no way epistemically better than the agent is now — a self that has forgotten evidence, or whose credence has been shifted by a drug, a mood, or an argument she would now reject.

The case I want the number to be about, stipulated in full. Ada has read a long series of pre-registered trials on a class of propositions in which human credences are systematically overconfident. The trials establish, to her satisfaction, that a certain pill, which adds no evidence about any particular proposition and removes none, reliably moves takers' credences in that class toward the truth: takers are better calibrated afterwards than before. Ada knows that at midnight she will take the pill. The pharmacological model is good, and she knows that after taking it her credence in a particular proposition A of that class will be 0.6. At 11:59 her credence in A is 0.9. She has no evidence about A that the pill will take away, no reason to think the pill will behave differently in her case, and no way of adopting 0.6 at 11:59 by an act of will: recalibration is not something she can perform deliberately, and the only thing she knows is that a process she has good statistical reason to trust is about to alter her credence. Nothing in the case tells her which way the truth about A lies; she does not thereby learn anything about A.

The proposition I want a credence in:

R*. The Reflection principle should be restricted to evidential successors. If an agent is certain that her later self will have credence x in A and that this later self is her evidential successor with respect to A — differing from her present self only in the evidence acquired and in what follows from it — then her present credence should be x. But no deference is required, and none is required even as a matter of coherence, to a later self whose credence in A differs for reasons that are not evidential: a known reliability-improving or reliability-destroying intervention, forgetting, a mood, a pill.

Sub-questions I expect to be relevant; add, drop, or replace them as you see fit.
