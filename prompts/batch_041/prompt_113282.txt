Hey — I'm a third-year PhD, working mostly on demandingness and the Williams stuff around integrity. I've read the drowning-wife case and the usual moves a hundred times and honestly I've started to think everyones answering the wrong question with it. Here's what's bugging me.

The standard reading goes: impartialist theories, utilitarianism above all, are objectionable because they make the agent treat her own deepest commitments as just one input among others, and to have one thought too many — the extra justificatory thought — when she saves her own wife. Fine. But now take the committed utilitarian whose deepest project just is maximising welfare. On the standard reading the alienation complaint should evaporate: nothing about her is being subordinated, the theory and the life coincide. And yet a life organised entirely around the impartial standpoint still seems off to me in a way I can't quite articulate. Then flip it: a guy who saves a stranger over his spouse and has no extra thought at all, just does it, is obviously a monster — and he's exactly the case the extra-thought framing says is clean. So the objection can't really be about whether the thought is present. It has to be about something else: which thoughts are available to the agent, or what the theory does to the agent's own standpoint, or the relation between the reasons that make an act right and the reasons the agent can actually act on.

So: given that reframing, which of the open questions in this area actually matter most, and in what order should someone with about 18 months and no appetite for empirical psych spend them? Things like whether the objection is genuinely distinct from the demandingness arguement or just a vivid way of stating it; what work integrity is even doing (moral value, psychological constraint, or rhetoric); whether the objection generalises to Kantian and contractualist views or only bites consequentialism; whether the one-thought-too-many complaint has any normative force or is just moral psychology dressed up; and whether any of it really threatens the idea that there's a distinctively moral standpoint at all. I want a defended ordering — which of these is upstream of which, which is tractable for a philosopher working alone, which are overrated or settled enough that I'd be wasting my time, and what would move something up or down the list.

Also tell me how confident you are in the ranking overall, and flag the single claim in your answer you're least sure of.
