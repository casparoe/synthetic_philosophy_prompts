I'm a third-year undergraduate, and alongside my coursework I sit on the student committee that runs our college's small grantmaking fund — a few thousand pounds a term, distributed to local causes and student projects, with half a dozen members and a chair who has to sign off. In my moral philosophy seminar we've just spent two weeks on moral uncertainty, the predicament of an agent who is unsure which moral theory is correct and still wants to act well, and it struck me that our committee is that agent writ large: we never converge on a theory, we just muddle through by vote and call the result "consensus." I'd like you to turn what the literature on moral uncertainty actually supplies into a procedure the committee can run on each application, not a seminar summary. Concretely: what steps, in what order; how to elicit and record each member's credences across a small set of moral views without demanding more numerical precision than people can honestly give; how to compare options when the theories don't share a common scale of value — I gather this is the hard part, and I want the procedure to face it rather than quietly assume it away; how to treat options that some views regard as impermissible rather than merely suboptimal, since we can't fund something half the committee thinks we must not do; and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision back to a vote or to a person, because the remaining question is a value judgment dressed up as arithmetic. Assume roughly forty minutes per application, members who are sharp but not philosophers, and a chair who will not accept a procedure that pretends to be more determinate than it is.

Then run it end to end on the three applications actually in front of us: (a) funding a free legal-advice clinic for asylum seekers in the city; (b) funding a scholarship that would go to a single outstanding student from a low-income background; and (c) buying and distributing bed nets through a well-known international charity. I want at least one of these to come out against the option the committee is emotionally attached to, and I want you to name the exact step that produced that result. Finally, be explicit about the failure modes: where the procedure will manufacture a confident-looking number that means nothing, what we should do when a member's credences are unstable between meetings, and what happens when two views carry equal weight and the aggregation is effectively a coin flip. Take a definite position on the design choices rather than laying out a menu for me — if you think straightforward expected-choiceworthiness aggregation needs to be modified to be usable at this scale, commit to the modification and defend it, and tell me plainly which parts of the procedure are honest machinery and which are just the committee's values wearing a formula as a costume.
