I write about philosophy and religion for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a piece on how the phrase "the problem of evil" conceals several quite different arguments. Before I simplify anything for readers, I want the underlying map right, and I would rather have the version that would survive scrutiny from someone who works on this than the version that reads well in a paragraph.

Please explain, with that degree of precision, three distinctions the popular discussion tends to blur. First, the difference between the logical problem of evil and the evidential problem of evil, and why the failure of the first is generally thought to leave the second untouched. Second, the difference between a defense and a theodicy — between showing that the existence of suffering is not logically incompatible with the existence of a good God and claiming to know God's actual reasons for permitting it. Third, the conceptual issue at the center of skeptical theism, the reply that we have no good reason to suppose a good God's reasons for permitting evil would be available to us, together with the charge that this reply, if it succeeds, proves too much: that it disables the very moral judgments the theist relies on elsewhere.

I also want to test an idea of my own, which I would place in the mouth of a character in the piece. Call it the asymmetry corollary: the claim that we are entitled to trust our moral judgments about human conduct precisely because we are not entitled to trust our judgments about divine conduct, so that the humility skeptical theism recommends toward God's reasons is a ground for confidence, rather than doubt, about our own. Explain whether this is a stable position or whether it collapses back into the objection that skeptical theism undermines moral knowledge, and say which reading you find more defensible. I want the argument, not merely a verdict — but I do want the verdict stated.
