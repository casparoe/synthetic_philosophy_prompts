ok so I want a dialogue — two people, roughly equal in skill, both smart, going at each other about whether a government that delivers prosperity and order has earned the right to rule even when it isn't democratic. Not a democracy-good / democracy-bad shouting match. Both of them should be able to land punches and both should give ground somewhere.

Set it up like this. One of them — call her the pragmatist — thinks legitimacy is basically earned output: if the state keeps the streets safe, grows the economy, houses people, and the population by and large goes along with it, that isn't a lesser species of legitimacy, it's the thing itself, and elections are just one delivery mechanism among several. The other — call him the proceduralist, but don't make him a stiff, he should be the kind of person who'll happily admit that democracies produce rotten outcomes — thinks output can buy compliance, and even genuine gratitude, but never the right to rule, and that the interesting question is whether "the population goes along with it" is evidence of legitimacy or evidence that there's no safe way to register dissent.

Now the stuff I actualy want them to fight about, and please let them get in the weeds:

1. The comparison problem. Performance legitimacy always smuggles in a comparison class. Better than what — the previous regime, the neighbouring country, the counterfactual where this country had gone democratic, the worst plausible alternative? Make them disagree about which comparison is the right one and whether the pragmatist's answer can be anything other than arbitrary. I want the proceduralist to push the line that the honest comparison is the one the citizens would pick from behind a veil, not knowing whether they'd turn out to be the ones jailed, censored, or left behind — and the pragmatist to reply that this is a philosopher's fantasy no actual population uses, and that this tells you something about the theory rather than about the population.

2. Whether order is an output or an input. The pragmatist lists order as a deliverable. Let the proceduralist argue that order is partly constitutive of the measuring stick — a regime that suppresses the people who'd contest the evaluation has corrupted the very data being used to call it legitimate — and let them work out whether that's a knockdown, or just a reason to discount, and whether it applies equally to democracies that manufacture consent. Related: make them try to say whether legitimacy is a property of the regime or a relation between regime and citizens, and whether "performance legitimacy" can even be a relation without some mechanism of consent in it. The pragmatist should try to seperate legitimacy from justification from mere stability and accuse the proceduralist of conflating all three; the proceduralist should come back that the pragmatist is conflating legitimacy with not currently being overthrown.

3. The indispensable-person case. Have the pragmatist introduce a small state whose performance rests on one extraordinary leader plus favourable external conditions — a port, a strategic patron, oil, whatever. Let them argue about whether the legitimacy survives the leader's death and the conditions turning. The pragmatist should bite a bullet somewhere, and the proceduralist should have a bullet of his own to bite — say, a democracy that performs terribly for a generation and keeps its legitimacy anyway, or a population that actively wants to trade political voice for stability.

4. At least one real concession. I'd like a moment where one of them grants a genuine point and the other's position shifts a little, so it isn't two monologues. If they end up disagreeing about something narrower than they started with, even better.

Singapore and China can come up as cases, but don't let anyone turn into a Wikipedia summary, and nobody cites books or articles — argue from cases and from the structure of the concepts. No quotes from real texts. A few pages, long enough that they can recieve each other's actual points and respond to them rather than talking past each other.
