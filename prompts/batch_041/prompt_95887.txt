I have been trying to settle what I think about killing in war, taking as my starting point Anscombe's argument that Truman's bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder rather than a regrettable necessity. She is plainly relying on a distinction that has to do with intention, and I want to know what principle, if any, fits the verdicts I actually hold. Here they are.

Bombing a munitions plant at the edge of a town, foreseeing that some of the workers and residents will be killed though my aim is only to destroy the plant: not murder.

Bombing a city in order to kill its inhabitants and so break the government's will to keep fighting: murder.

Hiroshima and Nagasaki: murder, even granting that they shortened the war and saved lives.

The area bomber who insists that he intended only the surrender, not the deaths he knew the bombing would produce: his disclaimer does not change my verdict; murder.

Shooting a conscripted soldier who is trying to kill me: not murder.

Killing a civilian who is filling shells in a factory, where his death is the means by which I stop the shells: murder, even though he is sustaining the war no less than the soldier is.

The case I keep going back and forth on: blockading an enemy's ports so that its civilians starve, foreseeing the starvation, in order to force a surrender.

The principle I would offer for these is roughly this: a killing is murder when the victim's death is intended or is the means by which the act achieves its purpose; a death that is foreseen but neither intended nor used as a means is not murder, provided the act is aimed at something else and the harm is proportionate to the good. I can see that this faces at least three problems. It lets intention do the work in the city-bombing cases but lets proportionality do the work in the factory case, and I am not sure those are the same kind of consideration. It seems to leave the area bomber free to redescribe himself as the factory raider, since he too can say the deaths are not what he wants but only what he foresees. And I cannot tell whether the good consequences I concede in the Hiroshima case are the very sort of thing that made the factory raid permissible, which would force me either to permit Hiroshima or to withdraw my permission for the factory raid.

Give me the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that does best by these verdicts. Say which of my verdicts the best candidate forces me to give up, and why I should give up the verdict rather than the principle. Say how it decides the blockade case, and one further case I have not mentioned. If the relevant distinction is between those who are attacking me and those who are not, rather than between the morally innocent and the morally guilty, tell me what that does to the munitions worker and the conscripted soldier. Say also whether the principle is one Anscombe herself could have endorsed or one she was arguing against. Lead with the principle in your first paragraph, then the cases. No more than 650 words.
