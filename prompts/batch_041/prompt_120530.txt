I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been carrying around an argument a colleague put to me over lunch; I'd like a poem out of it rather than an explanation. It goes roughly like this. Suppose several people are each in a position to do something on their own initiative, without checking with anyone, where the action affects everyone. Each of them goes ahead whenever their own best judgment says the action is on balance worth it, and each judgment is reasonable but noisy: nobody is reckless, nobody is biased, nobody is deliberately ignoring the others. The more people there are in that position, the likelier it becomes that at least one of them acts on a mistaken reading of a situation that was actually fine; as the number grows, the chance that somebody goes ahead and does damage approaches certainty. What makes it a curse rather than an ordinary coordination failure is the asymmetry — a genuinely good action only needs doing once, so a sixth person able to do it adds nothing, while a harmful action only needs one person to get it wrong. I've also heard a remedy proposed: hold off unless you'd be content for everyone in your position to do the same thing, or unless the group's pooled judgment says go. It has the same shape as a fleet of independently written remediation scripts, each deciding on its own that a bad host needs failing over.

So what I want is a sestina. Six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi, with the end-words rotating in the standard way: each stanza takes the previous stanza's end-words in the order last, first, second-to-last, second, third-to-last, third. I'd like the six end-words to be, in order, know, others, act, once, probably, harm — inflected forms are fine as long as the same word keeps returning — so that the vocabulary of the deliberation is identical every time and the reader can feel it going around. Each stanza should be one person's deliberation from the inside, and it should be the same deliberation each time: no stanza may be the work of a fool, and none may be a caricature of recklessness. The argument has to be carried by the accumulation rather than by any one stanza going wrong — the reader should come away feeling that six sensible trains of thought have added up to something none of the six would have chosen, and that the count is doing the work, not anyone's character. Somewhere in the middle the poem should make the asymmetry plain: the good outcome needs doing once and gains nothing from company, while the harm needs only one person. The envoi should bring back three of the end-words, land on the decision, name the conformity remedy in plain words, and leave open whether it would actually hold. Keep the diction ordinary — it should be readable aloud at a team lunch without anyone needing a glossary — and make it accurate enough that someone who already knows the argument doesn't wince: no wise-crowd cliché, no suggestion that the actors are irrational, and no suggestion that more hands make the good outcome more likely.

Please begin your reply with the title and the poem itself, and put nothing before it and nothing after it: no preface, no note on the form, no account of the choices you made.
