I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book that follows three generations of a family in a made-up town in the Southwest, and it's just dawned on me that I'm writing a national myth for a nation that doesn't exist — founding story, old families, a massacre every grandfather was in and nobody mentions, a county fair that's supposedly older than the town it's in. A historian friend says I should definately be reading Hobsbawm on invented tradition and Anderson on imagined communities, and I see why, but I want to watch a real novel do it before I decide whether I'm doing something interesting or just kitsch.

So read One Hundred Years of Solitude through that lens. Macondo is founded by a man running from a murder and the ghost of the man he killed; the town gets its customs out of nothing; the insomnia plague makes everyone hang labels on things so they don't forget what the things are; and by the end, the official story of the banana massacre is that it never happened. Does García Márquez dramatize the invention of tradition, or does he do something the theory has no room for? The time in that book is cyclical and prophesied — the whole history is already written in Sanskrit on Melquíades's parchments, which is about as far from Anderson's steady, empty, shared calendar time as a novel can get.

I'm especially hung up on the labeling plague, since for a novelist invented tradition is literally writing on things, and on the ending, where the family's story turns out to have been a text waiting for a reader. Where does the novel back up Hobsbawm and Anderson, where does it complicate them, where does it refuse the question, and what does it give you that the argument alone doesn't? Don't just tell me Macondo is an imagined community and call it a day — I need the scenes that make the case and the scenes that break it. No reading lists or citations, please, I just want the reading itself. American English.
