I retired from a career in chemistry a couple of years ago and have spent most of it since reading philosophy, mostly on causation, and I've gotten stuck on omissions. Can I just give you my gut verdicts case by case and have you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to?

Not watering my neighbour's ficus while they were away, so it died: I don't think I caused it's death, I just let it happen, even though nothing else was in the way. Leaving the stirrer off during a reaction I was running, so the batch came out as the wrong crystal form: that one I do feel I caused, even though all I did was not do something. A referee not blowing the whistle while a player gets hurt: not a cause of the injury, I think, just a failure of the job he's paid for. A man who knows a child is drowning in the pond behind his house and stays in his chair because he'd rather the child were gone: definitely a cause of the death, feels almost more causal than if he'd pushed the kid in. Me not taking a statin and then having a heart attack at 63: I can't bring myself to say my not-taking caused it, it just left the odds where they were. Me not telling my brother about a nasty thing that runs in our family, and his kid getting it: that one I do feel I caused, even though it's the same shape of thing -- I did nothing and the odds went up. Not sending fifty quid to a charity that would have saved a kid I'll never meet: not a cause of that kids death at all, as far as my gut is concerned, and yet the pond case is the same structure.

The half-baked principle I keep coming back to I've been calling the default setting idea: an omission counts as a cause only when it's a departure from what the situation was set up to do. That handles the stirrer and the referee nicely, since the safe state of a rig is something someone engineered, and the referee's job is to intervene. But a machine you have to keep holding down to keep it safe -- a dead-man's switch -- means doing nothing is the lethal option, and then my rule flips and says the operator caused the deaths by letting go, which feels right but for a reason I can't state. And nothing was set up at the pond, so the rule either says the man in the chair didn't cause anything, which I won't accept, or it says every setup is somebody's, which seems like a stretch.

So: is there a principled story that gets most of these, or do I have to give some of them up, and which ones? Also, two seperate questions I'd like answered if you can. What does the best version of the principle say about two people both standing at the pond, each able to jump in, each not jumping because they assume the other will? And what does it say about a case where the omission doesn't produce an outcome but selects among ones that were already going to happen anyway -- say, not adding a seed crystal, so the solution crystallizes one way rather than another? That last one I can't even get an intuition about, so if your principle has a verdict there it's probably doing real work.
