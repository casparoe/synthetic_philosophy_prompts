I'm the tech lead on a small team building an agent that handles insurance claims end to end — it can approve, deny, or settle, and mostly nobody looks at what it does. Legal keeps asking "what stops it from screwing people," and I dont want a values statement, I want something my engineers can put in the spec. So: write me a decision procedure the agent runs before it denies or lowballs a claim, as an ordered list of checks, each with a clear pass/fail and a note on where it stops with a hard no and where it has to hand the file to a human. Build it around the one idea I actually believe here, that you mustn't treat someone merely as a means — I care about the "merely", i.e. what separates using someone as a means (fine, that's what a claim is) from treating them merely as one, and what counts as a person's real consent to a settlement versus consent the system has worn down. I've seen a version of this where the test is whether the person could go along with what you're doing given only a true description of it — if that's usable, build the procedure on it; if not, tell me what to use instead. Then run the procedure on three cases: a claim the model flags as probably fraudulent but the policyholder swears is real; a settlement offer to someone who doesn't know the claim is worth triple; and a routine denial of a claim that's denied only because the person missed a deadline they couldn't have met. For each, say what the procedure spits out and, honestly, where it's just dressing up a judgment call. One of the cases should come out in a way I won't like — tell me which and why I should live with it. And tell me what the procedure can't do.
