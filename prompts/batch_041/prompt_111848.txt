I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with an old friend about whether the Germany of today can be said to carry guilt for what was done before most of its citizens were born, and the argument kept snagging on a distinction neither of us could state cleanly: she insisted that if a nation can be liable for reparations then it can also be guilty, and I kept feeling that this was a category error without being able to say why. I have since volunteered to lead the next session of a small reading group — eight or nine people, none of them philosophers: a couple of historians, a lawyer, a retired teacher, someone who works for a foundation — and I want to use Jaspers's fourfold distinction among kinds of guilt as the spine of the session. Please do not explain the distinction to me; I know it well enough. What I need is a plan for teaching it in ninety minutes to people who will arrive holding the ordinary intuition that a collectivity can simply be guilty, and who will leave, if I do this badly, with the impression that Jaspers was either excusing individuals or else smuggling in a mystical notion of inherited sin.

So: what has to be established before the four categories can even be heard as four rather than as one idea said four ways? I take it that some prior separation of guilt from liability or responsibility must come first, and that the categories then have to be introduced in an order such that each next one answers a question the previous one leaves open; tell me whether that is right, what the order should be, and why that order rather than another. Then the example that carries the whole session. I had been inclined to use the figure of the low-level civil servant who joined the party for career reasons and did nothing dramatic, but I suspect that is the example everyone reaches for and that it collapses the very distinctions I need it to hold apart, so tell me what the carrying case should be, what features it must have and must lack, and how far I can let the group's own historical cases take over from it. Give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — my guess is that the first will be that Jaspers is defending collective guilt, when he is doing something closer to the opposite — and say exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it run. I also want to know how the session should end: the one question I can put to the group that will show me whether they can now use the distinction themselves rather than merely recite it, and what I should openly tell them we are leaving out at this level (I am thinking of the metaphysical category, and of the whole apparatus of collective memory, but I would like your judgment). Assume the group has read a short excerpt and no secondary commentary, and that they will push back hard on anything that sounds like it lets the perpetrators off.
