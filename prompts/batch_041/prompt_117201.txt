I'm on the ethics comittee at a mid-sized hospital and we're rewriting the part of our policy that says when a patient is dead. What's driving me up the wall is that every draft I've looked at smuggles in a different answer to the question of what the definition is actually for — the transplant people want one thing, families want another, and the nurse charting at 3am with no physician in the building wants a third. So: please rate and then rank the candidate criteria we're considering by how well they'd work as hospital policy, not by how good they sound in a seminar.

Here's the list:
- whole-brain / brainstem death, established by the standard bedside tests plus an apnea test
- permanent loss of circulatory and respiratory function (the 5-minute version, no autoresuscitation)
- higher-brain or cortical criterion: the person is gone once the capacity for consciousness is gone, even if the body keeps going
- loss of integration of the organism as a whole: death is when the body stops being one coordinated living thing and becomes a collection of parts
- one of our intensivists wants a criterion she calls 'zero net contribution' — you're dead when nothing left in your body is being done by you anymore, i.e. everything still happening is something the machine is doing to you
- a purely procedural one: you're dead when a properly authorised person, following the agreed procedure, says so; the biology doesn't settle it, the procedure does

Give each a score out of 10 for how well it would work as policy (one number each, no ranges), rank them, and for each say in a sentence or two which edge case breaks it. Assume the policy sometimes has to be applied by a single nurse at night with no specialist in the building, and that we can't make the family's agreement a condition of anything.

Then tell me what would change your mind: which argument or case would move one of these up or down a tier, and where in this ranking you think you're most likely to be wrong.
