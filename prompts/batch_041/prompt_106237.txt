For about three months now, a running argument in my house has been whether any of us is entitled to criticize the others. It started small: I told a housemate that leaving the kitchen in ruins for the third time that week was thoughtless, and she replied that I had done exactly the same thing a fortnight earlier, so I had no standing to say it. I backed down in the moment, but I have never stopped wondering whether she refuted me, silenced me, or simply changed the subject. The question has since metastasized — I keep noticing how often arguments die not because anyone denies that the act was wrong but because someone questions the critic's right to be the one saying so — and I have found that philosophers keep a whole apparatus, standing to blame, that captures the mess far better than I had expected. What grips me is the gap between a criticism's being true and my being the person who gets to voice it, and the suspicion that if you press the hypocrisy objection hard enough it swallows everything, including itself.

I would like you to turn that apparatus into an institution and satirize it from the inside. Write the paperwork of a fictional Office of Standing to Blame — the government body that decides, case by case, whether a particular person is authorized to blame a particular other person for a particular act. Give me roughly 700 words in the flat, unblinking register of real administrative documents: a numbered set of application fields, each with the evidence it demands, followed by a short FAQ. No winking, no jokes announced as jokes, nothing that breaks the form — the comedy has to come entirely from taking the logic of standing seriously and letting it run a bureaucracy.

The satire must run on the actual structure of the problem rather than on invented absurdities. So the packet should have to handle: the recognized grades or categories of standing and what qualifies an applicant for each; whether standing can be forfeited, transferred, inherited, or merely suspended, and for how long; the complicity case, meaning the applicant who is in some sense part of the problem; the office's own standing to adjudicate other people's blame; and an appeals procedure. A reader who has never encountered the phrase standing to blame should still be able to recover, from the forms alone, the difference between a blame's content and the standing to voice it, the regress that looms if standing requires the blamer to be blameless, and the thought that standing might be a matter of the relationship between blamer and blamed rather than of the blamer's record — and should be able to tell exactly which practice is being sent up. Someone who does know the literature should find it fair rather than a strawman. Please do not name any real philosophers or cite any works.
