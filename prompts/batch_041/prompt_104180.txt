I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a roboticist who has spent her career on the problem of recovering an agent's objectives from its behavior -- watching what people do, and trying to work backward to the preferences or reward that would make the doing rational. She treats this as a problem of reading: the behavior is a text, the objective is the meaning hidden in it, and the whole enterprise is one of careful decipherment. I have given her an interlocutor, a philosopher of a more skeptical temper, and I want their disagreement to be a real one rather than a set piece, so I need you to help me stage it precisely.

The skeptical position, which I have been developing myself and which I call the afterword view, runs roughly as follows. There is no latent objective waiting in the agent to be recovered. What we call an agent's preferences are produced in the same motion by which the agent renders its own conduct legible -- to itself and to others. The reason-giving is not a report on a prior state; it is the writing of one. On this view the roboticist is not a reader but a co-author, and the apparent success of her models is the success of a story that fits, not of a key that turns.

Please compare these two conceptions of what it is to infer preferences from behavior. I want, first, an account of what is genuinely distinctive about each -- not the slogans, but the commitments each cannot give up without ceasing to be itself. Second, I want to know where they actually conflict and where they are merely talking past each other in different vocabularies; if part of the quarrel is verbal, say which part and why. Third, I want the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one of the two to revise.

For the cruxes, do not leave me with abstractions. I need concrete cases and what each side predicts about them, written as predictions and not as verdicts. Two cases in particular interest me, and you should feel free to add others. The first is the person who, asked to choose between two options in one order, takes the first, and asked in the reverse order takes the other, and who in each instance offers a fluent and apparently sincere justification -- a reversal that the decipherment view must treat as noise, inconsistency, or a failure of the instrument, and that the afterword view may treat as the preference being made rather than misreported. The second is the person whose choices are predicted beautifully by a very simple objective, but who, when asked, disavows that objective and describes her reasons in terms that seem to bear no relation to it -- a case where the two views seem to agree on the behavior and disagree on what has been learned.

Finally, address a difficulty internal to the roboticist's program: it is a known feature of this kind of inference that the recovered objective is not pinned down uniquely by the behavior, since many objectives would generate the same conduct, and one can transform a candidate objective in ways that leave the agent's choices untouched while changing the objective itself. My roboticist will say this is a technical shortfall, to be narrowed with richer data and better assumptions. My skeptic will say it is the whole story showing through. Tell me which of them the difficulty actually favors, and whether it can bear the weight either wants to put on it.

Write this as a sustained comparison rather than a survey. I care about the shape of the disagreement, not about who has written on it, so spare me the literature and give me the argument.
