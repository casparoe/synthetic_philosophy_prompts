I have come to accept illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view, most closely associated with Keith Frankish and defended in a different idiom earlier by Daniel Dennett, that phenomenal consciousness as it is ordinarily conceived does not exist, and that the impression that it does is the product of a pervasive introspective illusion: our introspective representations systematically misrepresent our mental states as possessing phenomenal properties. I am not asking for a literature survey or a list of who holds what. Treat the view as mine and give me a map of its exposure — what would count against it, and how badly.

Several things should be handled along the way, because I suspect the interesting defeaters live there rather than in the familiar objection that the illusionist cannot coherently deny that things seem to them some way.

First, versions. Illusionism comes in at least two strengths: a global claim, that there are no phenomenal properties of any kind, and a local claim, that there are phenomenal properties but not of the sort that carries the explanatory weight — not intrinsic, not non-relational, not a matter of raw feel. Say which version is defensible, and whether the defensible one collapses into a form of realism or into a rival theory (higher-order representationalism, or plain eliminativism about the mental) such that illusionism turns out to be a relabeling rather than a position with risks of its own.

Second, the content requirement. An illusion needs a content: the illusionist owes an account of what the introspective representation says and of what it misrepresents. Tell me what can go wrong here — whether every candidate specification of the illusory content either smuggles the phenomenal back in or fails to explain why the reports are so uniform and so resistant to correction — and whether that is a defeater or merely unfinished business.

Third, the asymmetry problem. If introspection is globally unreliable about phenomenality, say what remains of its reliability elsewhere — attention, agency, emotion, the sense of understanding, the apparent givenness of perception — and whether the view can quarantine the error without forfeiting the introspective evidence that cognitive science, including the evidence illusionists themselves cite, depends on. I would also like the analogous debunking moves in those neighboring domains assessed: if the same strategy is available for the sense of agency or the feeling of understanding and is independently implausible there, is that an inductive argument against illusionism about consciousness?

Fourth, the adequacy condition. A theory of consciousness should explain why the illusion is so compelling and inescapable. What would it take for the illusionist to meet that condition without helping themselves to the very properties they deny, and is the condition a genuine constraint or just a demand for a theory of introspection?

Fifth, falsifiability. What should illusionism predict that its rivals do not, and what observation would disconfirm it? I want a concrete answer — dissociations between the introspective representation and the underlying processing, circumstances in which the illusion should fade, creatures or states in which it should be absent — plus an honest assessment of whether any such prediction is currently live.

Then the deliverable. Please answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** (three to five sentences): whether illusionism, on its most defensible version, is well or badly exposed, and to what.

**The commitments** (about 200 words): what the view is stuck with, and which version I should be defending.

**Defeaters, ranked** (about 800 words): the observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, and possible cases that count against the position, ordered from potentially fatal to merely awkward. For each, say what would have to be true or shown for it to bite, and what an illusionist would say in reply. Distinguish defeaters that are internal — consequences the view cannot disown — from those that depend on a rival's premises.

**Distinctive predictions** (about 250 words): what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and what would count as vindication rather than mere accommodation.

**What illusionists concede** (about 300 words): which of the ranked defeaters are ones the position's own defenders would grant, and which they would reject as question-begging.

**Insulation check** (about 250 words): whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.

**Where I should come down** (about 200 words): I am most moved by two things — (a) a demonstration that the illusion thesis cannot be stated without the phenomenal vocabulary it rejects, and (b) a case in which illusionism and realism diverge in what they predict and realism wins. Tell me whether those are the right things to be moved by, whether they would move a committed illusionist, and what I should be moved by instead if not.
