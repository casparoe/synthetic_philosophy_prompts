A colleague of mine — moral psych guy with a foot in the sociology dept — keeps saying that honour is just a credit rating and a duel is a debt collection. Once you see honour cultures as reputation economies, he says, the whole who's-morally-superior question dissolves: they're not savages, they're running a different ledger, and our dignity culture is just the same bookeeping with better manners. He'll extend it to the victimhood stuff too — being wronged is just another way to bank moral capital, same currency, flipped sign. You know the typology I mean (honour = standing you can lose and must defend before an audience; dignity = equal, unconditional, not forfeitable; victimhood = status claimed through the recognition of having been wronged).

I think the analogy is carrying more than he admits, but I can't quite tell wether that's fatal. Can you audit it? Correspondence table first — credit bureau, collateral, interest rate, default, repo man — then the places the mapping breaks, and I care much more about the breaks that bear on his conclusion than the cute ones. In particular: fungibility; whether honour is positional or zero-sum in a way a credit score isn't; the fact that honour needs an audience and often the wrongdoer's acknowledgment; and that it sticks to you and your kin in a way a FICO score doesn't. Then say which of those, if any, kills the no-culture-is-superior conclusion, and whether the credit model can even get a grip on the victimhood case or whether that's a different kind of good altogether. If there's a better analogy — honour as a tournament, a title, something you can't alienate — tell me what it buys.

No lit review, no citations, just the audit.
