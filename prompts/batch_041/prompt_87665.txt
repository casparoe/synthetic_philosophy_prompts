I am an undergraduate taking a course in moral psychology, and I have been trying, without success, to find a name for a pattern that keeps recurring in the literature and in our seminars. A study reports that people's moral judgements shift when an apparently irrelevant factor is present — a foul smell in the room, the attractiveness of the person being judged, the language in which the question is put — and the shift is reported as a bias, as noise the judgement ought to have screened off. Another study reports that judgements shift when people are given longer to reflect, or hear the affected party's testimony, or are made to consider consequences — and that shift is reported as reasoning, or as a proper sensitivity to reasons. Nothing about the mechanism seems to distinguish the two cases: in both, some feature of the situation causes the judgement to move. What distinguishes them is whether we already believe the feature is the kind of thing that ought to make a difference. The same ambiguity seems to run through the distinction between a preference and a bias: an attitude that forms part of someone's moral outlook reads as a preference when we find it intelligible and as a bias when we do not, yet the label is presented as a finding rather than as a verdict.

I know there is a substantial literature on debunking arguments and on the genetic fallacy, and my pattern is plainly in that neighbourhood, but it does not seem to be the same thing. Those debates take it for granted that an influence can be identified as distorting, and then ask whether a belief so caused is undermined. What puzzles me is the step before that — how an influence gets classified as distorting rather than as tracking in the first place, and whether that classification can be made without presupposing the very normative commitments it is meant to support.

Is there an established term for this pattern, or for the circularity I am describing? If there is, please say which concept it is, how it differs from the nearest alternatives (debunking arguments, the genetic fallacy, and whatever else is closer), and whether anyone has argued that the classification can be made non-circularly. If there is not, I would far rather have a careful definition than a neat label: tell me exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — in particular, whether it genuinely applies to the preference/bias distinction as well as to the reason/bias one, and whether it collapses into ordinary moral disagreement.

Please answer in British English.
