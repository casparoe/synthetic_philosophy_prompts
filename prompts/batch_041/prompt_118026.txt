A friend of mine who is finishing a dissertation in logic and I have now spent three consecutive Saturday afternoons arguing about baldness, and I have decided that the argument is better conducted in the form of a document than in the form of my voice, which is getting hoarse. The disagreement is this. She is an epistemicist: there is a fact of the matter about whether a man with 3,412 hairs on his scalp is bald, and we cannot know it — not merely because nobody has counted, but because knowledge requires a margin for error, so that a belief landing exactly on the true boundary would be true only by luck. I am a supervaluationist: there is no fact of the matter, "bald" is neither true nor false of the borderline cases, and yet the predicate is not defective — whatever holds on every admissible way of making it precise is true, including the connections between our predicates (no one is both bald and hirsute survives every precisification), and I am even willing to accept the consequence that "there is some exact number of hairs at which baldness ends" comes out true while no number is the number. She regards that last admission as fatal; I regard it as merely odd. We have made no progress, and I would like to see what our two positions look like when somebody has to run an office with them.

So write me the public FAQ page — a citizen's guide, roughly 700 words — for a fictional agency that has to adjudicate these cases in real life. Say it administers a hair-restoration subsidy and a customs rule about what counts as a heap of grain, and that the statute defines neither term. The agency's official position should be epistemicist, and it should give the margin-for-error rationale rather than a shrug: the sealed annex recording the exact thresholds is not withheld from the public, it is unknowable, and that is the whole reason the office exists. Its appeals division should be supervaluationist, granting appeals on the ground that no verdict was ever required, while conceding that it must certify that a cutoff exists somewhere and refusing, on principle, to say where. The FAQ must also cope with higher-order vagueness — a third division that hears appeals about whether the second division's findings of indeterminacy were themselves determinate, plus a footnote explaining why there is no fourth — and with penumbral connections, enforced even in cases where both classifications are indeterminate. Every joke should be traceable to a real feature of one of the two theories; the piece should be legible to someone who has never heard either word, and a logician should find it fair to both sides rather than a strawman of one. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and no narrator who knows better.
