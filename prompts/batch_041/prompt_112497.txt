I have spent enough years in rationalist blogs and forums to have watched a particular argument fail to terminate many times, and what I want now is a diagnosis rather than another round of it.

The argument is about weakness of will — or akrasia, or present bias, or procrastination, or self-control; the participants cannot agree on the name, which I suspect is itself a symptom. The canonical case is an agent who judges, sincerely and all things considered, that she ought to do A, and then does not do A, without having changed her mind. One side treats this as a near-paradox: if the judgment were really her considered judgment, and the action were really intentional, there would be no gap left for weakness to occupy, so the real work is to say which of the two was not quite what it seemed — the judgment was not quite endorsed, or the action was not quite intentional. The other side, mostly economists or the economically minded, sees no paradox at all: the agent's preferences are inconsistent across time, the discount function is not exponential but falls steeply near zero, and "weakness" names the shape of the curve and nothing further. Each side can redescribe every case the other offers in its own vocabulary, and in all the time I have been watching, neither has conceded anything.

My hypothesis — please test it rather than adopt it — is that these are two questions wearing one name. The first is synchronic and about the will: whether there can be action contrary to one's own best judgment at the moment of action. The second is diachronic and about the shape of a preference ordering: whether an agent's preferences can be inconsistent across time. If that is right, the two frameworks need not conflict at all, and the deadlock is an artifact of a shared word.

To make the diagnosis bite, consider two cases. In the first, an agent judges at t1 that she ought not to smoke, forms the intention accordingly, and at t2 smokes while still holding that judgment unchanged, repudiating her own action as she performs it. In the second, an agent judges at t1 that she ought not to smoke, and at t2 prefers to smoke, holds that preference without embarrassment, and would not describe any part of what she does as a failure; nothing is repudiated, the ranking is simply not what it was. Is the second case a case of weakness of will at all? And, more pointedly: does the discounting framework have the resources even to state what is happening in the first case as distinct from the second — not to explain why it happens, but to represent the difference between acting against a judgment and having a different preference? If it cannot, that looks like a formal limitation rather than an empirical disagreement. Symmetrically, does the philosophical framework say anything about the second case beyond "not incontinence, next question"?

There is a further wrinkle I keep returning to. In the discounting framework a preference is a disposition to choose, revealed in choice; in the philosophical one a judgment is a mental state that may be entirely absent from choice. If the two sides take different things as their data — choices for one, first-person deliberation for the other — then they may disagree about which cases count as evidence before they disagree about anything else, and no case can settle the matter, because each side's central cases are not evidence for the other.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A verbal dispute about a word; two questions under one name; a substantive disagreement about what an action or a preference is; a hidden empirical question about whether a single mechanism underlies both; a clash of standards about what counts as data; or a stalled research programme kept alive by a division of labour in which each side is rewarded for answering its own question? Support the diagnosis from what each position actually commits to, and say whether my two-questions hypothesis survives the cases above or is too neat. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a representation theorem, an impossibility result, a stipulated vocabulary, an experiment — or argue that none should be expected, and say why.
