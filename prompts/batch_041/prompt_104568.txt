I'm presenting a paper at a small welfare-economics workshop in a few weeks and I need to prep for the discussion. Audience: mostly micro theorists who work in revealed preference / choice theory, plus two philosophers of econ and one behavioral person. My thesis is that the revealed-preference apparatus can only ever pin down an ordinal ranking of choices under a *fixed menu*, and that every attempt to read "utility" or "welfare" off it quietly assumes what I'm calling the menu-stability assumption -- that a person's ranking doesn't depend on what else is on the menu. Once you drop it, I argue, the standard welfare comparisons (including the ones behavioral welfare econ leans on) are either underdetermined or smuggle in a normative claim about which menu is authoritative. My go-to illustration is a person whose ranking between two options flips depending on whether a strictly worse third option is present. I'm not claiming I have a clean fix -- I mainly want to show the assumption is doing way more work than anyone admits, and that the usual patches just relocate the problem.
