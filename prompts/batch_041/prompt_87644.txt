I am preparing a systematic treatment of causation by omission and want the specification fixed before I assess any candidate account. Write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of how an omission can be a cause must deliver. Justify each requirement by the case or consideration that generates it, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which merely reflect the commitments of a particular camp, and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — that negative result is the part I care about most.

Organize the list under four heads.

Cases the theory must get right. At minimum: (a) a safety system holds a valve closed only while an operator keeps a switch depressed; the operator's hand slips, the valve opens, harm follows — the theory must say whether this is an action, an omission, or both, and on what basis; (b) two clinicians independently withhold a drug, and either withholding alone would have sufficed for the patient's death, so neither is but-for necessary — the theory must handle redundant omission; (c) two bystanders fail to intervene in identical circumstances, one under a duty to rescue and one not — the theory must say whether the causation itself differs or only the responsibility; (d) a warning alarm that never sounds. Add any further cases you think a serious theory must accommodate.

Distinctions the theory must not collapse. Between omitting and acting; between causing and merely allowing; between causation, causal explanation, and attributability or blame; between an omission treated as an entity (an absence, a negative event, a fact) and an omission treated as a mode of describing a relation; between singular and general causal claims; and the contrast or baseline problem — against what alternative is the omission a deviation, and does the theory fix that baseline descriptively or normatively?

Results the theory must remain consistent with. That legal and medical practice treat certain failures — failure to warn, withdrawal of treatment, failure to rescue — as causes; that the very same absence counts as a cause in one setting and as mere background in another; that prevention and double prevention are commonplace; and the standing intuition that nothing comes from nothing, if that intuition is worth preserving at all.

Finally, name the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise plausible accounts, and identify the pair of requirements that does the most to make a unified theory of omission-causation look hopeless. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular theories; I will do that assessment myself.
