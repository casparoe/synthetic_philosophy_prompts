I'm a long-time reader of rationalist blogs and forums — the sequences, the decision-theory threads, the interminable arguments about whether 'rational' names one thing or several — and I've lately been sitting in on a small game-theory reading group that a friend runs, mainly to see whether the formal machinery lines up with the decision-theoretic intuitions I already have. Last week two of the others, whom I'll call R and S, had a long argument about the iterated elimination of dominated strategies, and specifically about the versions of it in which the strategy doing the dominating is a mixture rather than a pure strategy. I read philosophy as an undergraduate, so I can follow the arithmetic, but I don't know this literature well enough to referee, and I've been chewing on it since. They were working from a three-by-three game, with rows Top, Middle and Bottom and columns Left, Centre and Right, and payoffs given as (row player, column player): Top–Left (3, 0), Top–Centre (0, 3), Top–Right (0, 0); Middle–Left (0, 3), Middle–Centre (3, 0), Middle–Right (0, 0); Bottom–Left (1, 1), Bottom–Centre (1, 1), Bottom–Right (−1, −1). Neither of them had a textbook to hand, since we were in a pub, so everything was from memory; I suspect that is part of why it went round in circles.

R opened by claiming that Bottom is strictly dominated: mix Top and Middle fifty-fifty and you get 1.5 against Left, 1.5 against Centre and 0 against Right, which beats Bottom's 1, 1 and −1 column by column, so a rational row player never plays Bottom. He added that Right is dominated for the column player in exactly the same way, by the fifty-fifty mix of Left and Centre, and he stressed that no pure strategy does the job — Top loses to Bottom against Centre, Middle loses to Bottom against Left — so the mixed-strategy notion of dominance is earning its keep, and an iterative procedure that deletes on the strength of it is not a mere convenience. S replied that a mixture is not an act: when your turn comes you play Top or Middle or Bottom, you don't play a coin flip, so all R has shown is that a coin flip he could commit to would have done better, which is a fact about a counterfactual rather than about whether playing Bottom is irrational. R answered that mixed strategies are acts in the only sense that matters — you can in fact flip a coin, and since the dominance holds against every column strategy it makes no difference whether the column player knows your policy — so S was conflating the way mixtures are interpreted in equilibrium with the role they play in the dominance relation. S then asked why, on R's account, the line stops at strict dominance: weakly dominated strategies are also beaten-or-matched by available acts, and it is common knowledge that iterating the weak version is a mess, being order-dependent and capable of deleting strategies that are played in Nash equilibrium. As a parting shot he added that once there are three or more players the mixed-strategy criterion comes apart from the simpler one that deletes whatever is never a best response, and that the two coincide only in two-player games, so the extra strength is precisely the part that depends on lotteries counting as acts. R replied that the strict/weak line is not a technicality: a strictly dominated strategy is worse against every belief the player could hold about the other side, whereas a weakly dominated one is worse only against beliefs that give weight to particular strategies, and a rational player is not obliged to hold those, which is why the weak version needs an extra assumption and the strict version does not.

What I would like is a move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on who 'won', since R and S each asserted several separable things and I am sceptical that either of them was wholly right. Is Bottom strictly dominated as claimed, and is the parallel claim about Right correct? Is S's 'a mixture is not an act' objection a real objection to the elimination, or does it misfire? Is R's coin-flip reply sound? Does the strict/weak distinction carry the weight R puts on it, or is S right that it is doing less than it appears to? Is the three-or-more-players point correct as stated, and does it support the conclusion S drew from it? And what should I make of the position the deletions leave you in — you end up with a two-by-two sub-game whose only equilibrium is mixed, and I cannot tell whether that tells against R or against S. Where you can, please separate what is settled, in the sense that anyone who knows the area would accept it, from what is a live interpretive dispute among people who do, and flag your own confidence rather than smoothing everything into one confident line; I would rather have an answer that marks its uncertainties than one that pretends the disagreement is closed. I don't want a reading list — I can't look anything up at the moment, so references would just be guesses to me. Please write in American English.
