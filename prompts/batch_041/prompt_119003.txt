I'm mid-draft on a paper about suffering-focused ethics and I need someone to settle an arguement for me, because the two people who had it are both sharp and I've stopped being able to see who's right.

The disputed bit is a proposal I put in the draft mostly to see if it would survive. The proposal: suffering and enjoyment are both real values, but they don't trade off at one rate. Below a certain intensity they're commensurable — a big enough pile of mild pleasure can be worth some mild pain, and vice versa. Above that intensity, suffering gets lexical priority over enjoyment: no amount of enjoyment, however large, justifies imposing it. The point was to keep the part of negative utilitarianism that seems obviously right — agony isn't outweighed by enough ice cream — without buying the conclusion that it would be good to wind down the world, since only suffering past the line is protected from being outbid, and ordinary life mostly sits below it. I called it the two-tier view in the draft, which is maybe a bad name, whatever.

Mira and Aleks went at this for about forty minutes at writing group. As best I can reconstruct:

(1) Mira: If the line is real it has to come from somewhere. Either suffering has a special axiological status — in which case it can't just switch on at some arbitrary intensity — or it doesn't, and then you're asserting that very bad things are infinitely bad, which isn't a theory, it's a stipulation. And look at what the commensurable zone buys you: if mild pains and mild pleasures really do trade off at a rate, then a large enough quantity of mild pain adds up to the same disvalue as the agony you're so worried about, and a large enough quantity of mild pleasure outbids it. So your lexical priority protects intensity, not quantity, and everything leaks out the bottom. Also, where do you set the line? If the answer is "wherever it has to be to avoid the implications I don't like," that's reverse engineering, not discovery.

(2) Aleks: The line isn't arbitrary if you ground it in the shape of a life instead of a number on a scale. Suffering counts lexically when it's the kind that takes a life over — when it destroys the person's capacity to care about anything else. That's a real joint in nature, and it's about the sufferer's relation to her own future, not about how many hedons we're adding up. The aggregation worry, meanwhile, is a problem for any axiology that adds up experiences, not for mine in particular.

(3) Mira: Then you've stopped doing axiology and started doing deontology with extra steps. What you've described is a side-constraint on what may be done to a person, and once you've admitted side-constraints you don't need the suffering-focused apparatus at all — ordinary rights talk does the same work without pretending to be a theory of value. And it isn't even about suffering anymore: some lives are wrecked by things that aren't suffering, and some people endure terrible suffering without being wrecked. So your suffering focus has quietly turned into a claim about life-structure, which is a different paper.

(4) Aleks: I'd rather have a view that's true to what makes agony bad than one that's pure about its primitive. But your first objection is the one worth answering, so here it is as a dilemma. Take linear trade-off everywhere and the many's mild pleasures can buy the few's agony. Take lexical priority everywhere and any world containing any suffering is worse than one with none, which slides toward saying no one should ever be born. The two-tier view is the only option that avoids both horns. That's not an arbitrary parameter — it's the price of not being impaled.

(5) Mira: Avoiding two bad implications isn't evidence that you've found the actual structure of value. You can dodge any pair of horns by adding a knob. And notice which zone your moderate, sensible half covers: chronic mild misery, marginal harms, the stuff that's actually everywhere. If mild suffering is fully commensurable with mild enjoyment, then suffering-focused ethics has no particular grip on the most common kind of suffering and total grip only on the rare kind. That seems backwards for a view whose whole selling point is that suffering is what we should be focusing on.

Now I've got to decide whether to keep the section, and I keep flip-flopping. I don't beleive either of them is obviously right, which is the problem. Go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, where they're talking past each other, and what both of them are missing. Then give me a verdict: does the two-tier view survive, and if it survives only in modified form, what's the modification? Be committed — I don't want a survey of the positions ending in "it depends on your metaethics," I want to know what you think is true and what I should do with the section. But flag the places where you're least confident, since I need to know which of your claims are load-bearing.
