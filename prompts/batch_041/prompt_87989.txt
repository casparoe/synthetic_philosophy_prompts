A journal has commissioned a joint statement from two philosophers of science who have argued for a decade about whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. The first holds a monist view: there is one privileged, mind-independent partition of the world into kinds, and the predictive success of the special sciences is evidence that inquiry is converging on it. The second holds a pluralist view: the divisions we draw are answers to the questions we ask, incompatible partitions can each be legitimate, and no partition is privileged except relative to an interest or a practice. Write the document the two of them could both sign.

It should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both accept: what a joint would have to be, what it would mean for one partition rather than another to be privileged, what the success of a science shows and does not show, and what both would count as evidence for or against convergence. Each claim must be substantive — it must rule out something at least one of the parties would otherwise have been tempted to say. Second, a table of their remaining disagreements, one row per question, with a column for each party's own formulation of its position, worded so that its defender would accept the wording as fair, and a note on which part of the dispute, if any, is merely verbal. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. Fourth, for each crux, a case or argument that both agree bears on it, with each party's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance.

The commission has asked that the statement not be settled by appeal to any historical episode, and the parties have instead agreed to test their cruxes against four stipulated cases, which they accept as coherent: (i) a classification that is perfectly projectible and stable for every instrument and organism we could build, but that a well-confirmed physical theory describes as an artifact of the size-scale at which observers like us must operate; (ii) a domain admitting two partitions, each licensing more successful predictions than any partition other than itself and the other, neither outperforming the other; (iii) a social kind whose extension is caused in part by the classification itself, so that its boundaries track the classifier's interests; (iv) a kind whose members are all extinct and whose characteristic mechanism is unrecoverable, leaving only statistical traces.

For each case, say whether each party's view predicts that it carves at the joints, and in which sense — the monist's, the pluralist's, both, or neither — and why the case does or does not discriminate between the views. Where a case fails to discriminate, say so plainly rather than forcing a verdict. Close with a short statement of what would change each party's mind.
