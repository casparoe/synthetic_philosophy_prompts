I am preparing a session of a graduate seminar on whether money-pump arguments establish anything about diachronic rationality, and I need a case study that will not be recognised on sight as a stock example. Construct a money pump to the following specifications, then demonstrate that it meets each of them. Work out the construction yourself rather than reporting anyone's published version of it; I want the case, not a summary of the literature.

The victim is to be an ordinary adult in an ordinary setting — a household budget, a loan, a subscription, a payroll arrangement — not a figure in a fable. She must satisfy all of the following simultaneously.

1. Full information and full foresight. She knows the entire sequence of offers before the first one is made, and she correctly predicts every choice she will later make. She forgets nothing, deceives herself about nothing, and learns nothing in the course of the sequence that she did not already know at the outset.

2. Transitivity at every moment. At any single time, her preferences over the options then before her are complete and transitive. The case must not turn on a cycle among the options she is weighing at one time.

3. Fixed values. What she wants does not change: at the end she wants the same things, in the same amounts, as at the start. The one thing permitted to shift is how much she cares about when she gets them — her discounting of the future may be inconsistent from one moment to the next, and this is the only lever the case may use.

4. No way out. At every point she may refuse any offer at no cost, and no external constraint binds her; but she has no commitment technology whatever — no escrow, no enforceable contract, no trusted third party, no rule or resolution that would in fact stop her later self.

5. An ordinary counterparty. The other party is no cleverer than a shrewd human, cannot read her mind, and knows only what she has said or shown. That party must end up with a genuine profit.

6. No process preferences. Nothing may turn on her enjoying or disliking the act of trading as such, or on her caring about sunk costs, past losses, or regret.

Deliver the following.

(a) The case itself: the counterparty's opening move and the full sequence, in no more than four transactions.

(b) The node-by-node audit: at each point, the comparison she faces and why accepting beats refusing for her, then and there, by her own lights at that moment.

(c) The sense in which she loses. State precisely in what respect she ends up worse off than if she had never met the counterparty, and equally precisely in what respect she could have avoided it. Since she can always refuse, the loss cannot be unavoidable without qualification, and I want the qualification stated rather than papered over.

(d) The load-bearing feature, with removal tests: for each of conditions 1 to 4, show what happens when that condition alone is relaxed; and show that giving her a single consistent way of discounting the future either destroys the case or converts it into something that is not a pump.

(e) The strongest defence of the victim: the argument that she is not irrational — that the sequence merely exhibits a conflict between what she wants at each moment and what she wants overall, and that she is entitled to resolve that conflict by holding to a plan rather than by yielding to each successive temptation — together with your honest assessment of whether that defence succeeds and what it would cost her to make it.

(f) The smallest change to her circumstances, as opposed to her character, that defeats the pump, and whether an ordinary person could actually bring that change about.

A case that stipulates the loss rather than generating it from her dispositions does not meet the brief: every payment must be traceable to a comparison she herself makes at the moment she makes it.
