So I chair the clinical ethics committee at a hospital network in a country with a large immigrant workforce, and I have to circulate a written opinion before next Thursday's vote. Here's the mess.

We're adopting a protocol for a scarce therapy — limited slots, way more demand than supply, so the protocol has to rank patients. The draft in front of us, written by our allocation office, gives a modest preference (they insist it's only a tiebreaker, not a veto) to citizens and permanent residents over non-citizens. Their reasoning: members of a political community owe each other something they don't owe outsiders, and people who've been paying in should get something back when it's their turn to need care. Clinical need still dominates; the preference only decides cases that are otherwise dead even. Their own numbers say it would flip maybe one decision in forty a year, which is either a reason it's harmless or a reason we shouldn't bother, depending who you ask.

Two things make it hard. First, the case that will actually get decided by this tiebreaker in the next month is a guy — call him Mr. A — eleven years here, undocumented, worked as a care assistant through the worst of the pandemic, payroll taxes the whole time through a borrowed number (which is its own problem), and right now he's dead-even with a citizen patient on every clinical criterion. Second, our network adopted a statement a few years back saying we don't let immigration status affect clinical decisions, and the allocation office says the new protocol doesn't contradict it because "tiebreakers aren't clinical." I don't buy that and I definately don't think the board will either. Three of my members hold the citizen-preference sincerely, and one wants us to declare the whole thing outside our competence and punt it to the ministry.

There's also a consultant who keeps pushing a framing I can't tell is brilliant or sophistry. She says special obligations to compatriots only earn their keep if membership is voluntary in a real sense — if people can actually leave. So the right question isn't "who's a member" but "who could exit." She wants us to replace the citizen/resident line with an exitability line: the preference goes to whoever is stuck. That flips the protocol here, since Mr. A can't safely go back and the citizen patient obviously can go anywhere. She calls it the two-door view — doors in and doors out — and says a community that won't let you out has no standing to claim you owe it anything extra.

What I need from you is the opinion I'll circulate. It should (a) actually recommend something the committee can vote on — adopt as drafted, adopt with a specific amendment, or reject — and state the amendment in operative language we could paste straight into the protocol; (b) give the reasoning, including the best version of the citizen-preference case rather than a strawman, since three members hold it in good faith; (c) take a position on the two-door thing on the merits, because if it's right the protocol is backwards and if it's wrong I need to be able to say why in front of the people who'll ask; and (d) say what we tell Mr. A's team and the citizen patient's team in the meantime, because both cases are live now and we can't wait for the perfect policy.

Don't hand me a reading list or citations — I'm not chasing down papers before Thursday. Just the argument, in a form I can read out loud without embarrassment.
