I am a secondary-school student in the eleventh grade, and I am writing an extended research paper on the ethics of institutional influence for a philosophy elective. Last term our school changed its course-selection procedure so that any student who does not return the form is placed by default into the more demanding track; the administration defended this on the grounds that it rescues students who would otherwise undersell themselves, while still permitting anyone to opt out with a single click. Several of my classmates found the change unsettling rather than helpful, and our debate club has now split into two camps that argue past each other. One camp holds, roughly, that it is permissible for institutions to steer people toward choices that advance those people's own welfare, provided the steering is transparent, cheap to reverse, and free of false information; the other holds that even well-intentioned steering is objectionable because it succeeds by bypassing the person's own deliberation, so that a school which quietly engineers its students' choices has wronged them even when the students end up better off.

I want to write the document the two camps should have produced instead of carrying on like this, and I would like you to draft it. What I am after is the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: the claims both sides can sign; the precise points on which they still differ, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own; and the cruxes, understood as the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Please build the document around three analytic tools I have been developing, because I want to see whether they earn their keep: first, a distinction between interventions that work by exploiting a cognitive bias and interventions that work by repairing a gap in attention or information; second, what I call a second-order endorsement test, which asks not whether the person would endorse the resulting outcome but whether, on reflection, they would endorse being influenced in that particular manner; and third, the suggestion that influence may be directed at what people believe and not only at what they do, so that a school's framing of a careers assembly is itself a candidate act of steering. I suspect that one genuine crux is whether legitimacy should be assessed nudge by nudge or whether individually benign interventions can compose into an environment that is objectionable as a whole; I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected. A second case I would like worked through, alongside the default placement, is a reminder message that informs students that most of their classmates have already submitted the assignment.

Please structure the result as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table, with a column for the crux, a column for the case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, and a column recording each side's prediction in advance. If you conclude that some part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, that the two sides attach different meanings to manipulation or to autonomy — say which part and why, rather than announcing that the whole disagreement is terminological. Please do not include reading lists, citations, or appeals to what particular authors have written, since the argument needs to stand on its own; and where the two sides cannot agree even on what would count as evidence, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a test.
