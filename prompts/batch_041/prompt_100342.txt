My friend is really into rationalist blogs and he keeps telling me decision theory is basically a solved subject. When I pushed him on it he sent me this, and honestly it sounds pretty tight but something feels off. Here's his summary:

'Coherence arguments prove that anyone who doesn't maximize expected utility is irrational. The idea is dead simple — if your preferences don't obey the axioms, someone can money-pump you: they'll offer you a sequence of trades, you'll accept each one because each is an improvement at the time, and by the end you've paid money and ended up with nothing. Ramsey and Savage already proved this, which is why expected utility is the only game in town. Any alternative — letting your probabilities be non-additive, caring about risk in some non-standard way, having preferences that aren't totally ranked — has to be exploitable, so it's just irrational, full stop. And it explains all those experiments where people violate the axioms: those subjects aren't just quirky, they'd literally be driven to bankruptcy by a clever trader.'

So is he right? I mostly buy the big picture, but I can't shake the feeling he's squeezing more out of the argument than it actually gives him — like the money pump is doing way more work than it can really do. Where does this summary get the actual argument right, where is it quietly overstating things, and does the conclusion that expected utility maximization is the uniquely rational choice really follow from the exploitability point? I'd especially like to know if the jump from "you can be pumped" to "you're irrational" is as airtight as he makes it sound.
