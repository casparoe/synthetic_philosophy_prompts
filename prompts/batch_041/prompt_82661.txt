I am assembling a set of prompts for a benchmark on philosophical and decision-theoretic reasoning, and I would like you to write a satirical piece in the form of a corporate FAQ page for a fictional company, Acausal Casualty & Indemnity, which sells insurance policies against what it calls counterfactual liabilities. The piece should run around 700 words, formatted as a numbered list of questions and answers, in the deadpan voice of a customer-service document that never acknowledges how strange its subject matter is. The target is not any particular person or company but the cluster of decision-theoretic puzzles around counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail: the fact that a perfect predictor's threat can be causally inert yet decision-theoretically relevant; the difference between paying because of a causal effect and paying because of what your willingness to pay reveals about the predictor's prior prediction; the role of precommitment and the question of what an agent should do if it failed to precommit; and the way these scenarios are often discussed as if the right answer were obvious when the formal theories disagree. The humor should come from getting the decision theory right—so that someone who knows the literature on causal, evidential, and updateless decision theory recognizes that the document understands the puzzles—rather than from inventing absurdities. For example, the FAQ might explain what happens if the policyholder's decision is already known to the blackmailer, whether the policy covers a threat that is conditional on the policyholder's not having bought the policy, and how the company's actuary determines the counterfactual branch in which the policyholder would have paid.

Please make the piece self-contained: a reader who has never encountered counterfactual mugging or XOR blackmail should be able to follow the jokes from the FAQ alone, so the document should weave in enough of the setup—a predictor who asks for a small sum in one branch and rewards you in another; a blackmailer whose threat is conditioned on a prediction of your choice—without pausing to lecture. At the end, include a short author's note, no more than 150 words, that states which decision theory the FAQ implicitly endorses, where it takes liberties with the standard formulations, and which parts of the piece lean on a contested reading of the puzzles. I want the piece to be committed rather than evenhanded: pick a definite stance—for instance, that the company's underwriting is coherent only under an updateless or functional decision theory, and that a causal decision theorist would find the whole business fraudulent—and let the satire follow from that stance, rather than surveying all the options and leaving the reader to choose.
