I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, and I want to work through a defence of psychiatric classification that a colleague in the medical school put to me, because I could not answer it cleanly on the spot and I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. His position is that diagnostic categories are instruments of measurement, and that measurement is the reading-off of a magnitude that exists independently of the instrument. A thermometer warms the room it sits in, he conceded, but that perturbation is a known quantity: it is the sort of thing a serious science of measurement models and corrects, and it is no objection to the quantities being measured. So too, he says, with the fact that patients read their own diagnoses, that advocacy groups contest definitions, that symptom presentations drift across decades, and that a category can be extended to a population that did not previously fall under it. These are instrumental artefacts and problems of endogeneity, to be handled with better instruments, tighter criteria and statistical techniques for feedback — not evidence that the categories fail to track anything real. The upshot he wants is that the enterprise is sound and only its instrumentation needs improving.

What I want from you is an audit of that analogy rather than a verdict on the politics of diagnosis, and I do not want a recitation of Hacking's looping effects; I want to know whether they can be represented within the picture my colleague is relying on. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly — a table would be welcome: what plays the role of the thermometer, the mercury, the room, the temperature, the scale and its calibration, the observer who reads the dial — and then tell me where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he wants from those that are merely picturesque. Three features of the psychiatric case strike me as candidates for the decisive ones, though I would rather you tell me which, if any, is decisive. First, that the mercury can read the thermometer. Second, that the scale is stipulated rather than discovered, so that moving a cut-off — as happens whenever a threshold for a common condition is revised downward — converts the healthy into the ill without any change in the underlying magnitude. Third, that the loop iterates: the category is revised in response to those it classifies, who then present differently in response to the revision, so that the classification and the classified are each other's causes over time rather than a signal and its noise. My colleague's fallback is that all of this is endogeneity, which is a familiar problem with familiar remedies, so I would also like your judgement on whether there can be an instrument — in the econometrician's sense, something that shifts how a person is classified without shifting what the classification is meant to measure — for a category that people take up and live inside. Finally, consider whether any of the rival analogies in circulation does better: the thermostat, the field guide, the legal code, the self-fulfilling prophecy, the measure that becomes a target, or one you construct yourself; and say whether his conclusion — keep the categories, improve the instruments — can be supported by an analogy of this family at all, or whether the thermometer picture has been carrying it from the start. No references or reading lists, please; I want the argument, not the bibliography.
