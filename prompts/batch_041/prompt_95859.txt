I work as a software engineer at a small forecasting startup, and I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, so I may well be mangling the vocabulary here. While building an internal feature that aggregates the probabilistic forecasts of several analysts into a single pooled estimate, I kept colliding with a family of results that all seem to rest on the same underlying assumption: that rational agents share a common prior probability distribution over the state of the world, so that any disagreement between them must ultimately be traced to differences in the private information they hold rather than to genuinely different starting beliefs. When I described this to a colleague, she gave me two reactions that I cannot reconcile. The first was that the assumption is essentially a bookkeeping convention rather than an empirical claim: if two people truly do start from different priors, she said, you can always redescribe the situation by enlarging the space of possible agent types and letting each person's prior be a function of which type they happen to be, which means the assumption can never be shown to be false. The second was that when the assumption is combined with common knowledge of rationality and of the agents' initial holdings, it delivers a result that strikes me as plainly absurd: two traders who openly disagree about an asset's value will nevertheless decline to trade with each other at any price, because each treats the other's eagerness to trade as bad news about their own information.

I would like you to write an essay that reaches a verdict on the following question: should the common prior assumption be understood as a substantive claim about how rational agents actually are, a claim we have independent reason to think is false, or as a harmless normalization of the way we describe belief disagreement, in which case its strange downstream consequences, such as the no-trade result, are artifacts of our description rather than genuine discoveries about rationality? Please weigh the strongest arguments on both sides. On one side, consider the thought that real people plainly do begin from different priors, shaped by different upbringings, training, and models of the world, and that a theory which rules this out by construction is simply stipulating away the phenomenon it was meant to explain; on the other side, consider the thought that the assumption is a modeling choice about how to represent disagreement, not a psychological hypothesis, and that it would be a category mistake to call a representational convention false. Do not end on a fence-sitting note. Defend a definite position, and make clear what work the common prior assumption is and is not doing once you have taken your stand.

To keep the essay from becoming a survey, please also do three things. First, explain as carefully as you can what the difference is supposed to be between two agents holding different priors and two agents holding the same prior but different private information, and say whether you think that distinction survives your verdict. Second, take seriously the objection that if the assumption is unfalsifiable it is therefore empty, and either defuse it or concede it. Third, say what you think follows for someone like me, who wants to combine the forecasts of analysts who genuinely seem to disagree: does your verdict mean that pooling their estimates is illegitimate, that it is legitimate but for reasons other than the ones usually given, or that the question is simply orthogonal to the common prior debate? Assume your reader is technically competent but, like me, has never taken a course in decision theory, so define terms when you lean on them.
