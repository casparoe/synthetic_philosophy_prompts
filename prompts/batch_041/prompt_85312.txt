Ok so I'm an ER nurse, 22 years at a county hospital, and somehow I've been asked to give the pinning ceremony address for the nursing school's graduating class next month. I said yes before I thought about what I'd actually say, and now I'm stuck. The one thing I know for sure after 22 years is that who lives and who dies often turns on something completely stupid — whether a bed was free, whether someone swapped a shift, whether the family got there in time because the bus was late. I've sat with families at 2am while somebody told them everything happens for a reason, and I've never once been able to say it back, because I don't believe it and I don't think it's kind either. But a pinning ceremony is a happy occasion, half the room is parents and grandparents, some of them have buried a kid, and the faculty will be sitting right up front. I don't want to hand these graduates the usual stuff — you can do anything, the future is yours, this was always meant to be — and I also don't want to stand up there and tell a room of 22-year-olds that nothing they do matters and it's all coin flips.

The way I've been thinking about it, and I want the speech to actually do this rather than just mention it: a friend of mine does complex systems modeling for a living and he gave me a distinction I keep using. Some small events are sparks — the fire was already loaded up and any match would've lit it, so the particular match is nothing special. Other small events are hinges — take that one away and the whole thing swings a different way and nothing else takes its place. His point was that you almost never know which one you're looking at, not at the time and usually not afterward either, and that cuts both ways: it means you can't predict anything, but it also means a small thing done early can move a lot, way more than it should be able to. I'd like the speech to sit on that without pretending to resolve it — the honest version of encouragement, where their effort is real and load-bearing but isn't doing all the work by itself, and where nobody gets to claim credit for the hinge they happened to be standing on.

So: no everything-happens-for-a-reason, no false comfort, no pretending I know why any particular thing happened. Please don't quote anybody — every quote I try to remember I get wrong, and the dean of nursing teaches a history of science course and will absolutely catch me. If chaos comes up, use it the way a physicist would, sensitive dependence and all, not as a word for messy. The audience is mostly first-generation families, a fair amount of Spanish spoken at home, and a handful of nurses who've worked the same floor as me for fifteen years and will know if I'm faking it. Six minutes, so maybe 800 words, and just give me the speech — start with the speech itself, no preamble about what you're doing, no notes at the end.
