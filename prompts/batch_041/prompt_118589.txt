I have been having a long-running argument with a friend — she read too much philosophy of convention in graduate school and now sees norms everywhere — about whether everyday conventions bind us morally, and we keep getting nowhere because we slide between three different questions. I want to fix one of them and get a number. The case: I am a tourist on a ten-day trip to a country where tipping restaurant servers at roughly twenty percent is the norm, where servers are paid on the assumption that tips will arrive, and where a table that leaves nothing is read by the staff and by the other diners as either ignorant or contemptuous. I know all of this, I can easily afford the tip, and I will never come back. My claim is that I have a reason of prudence and perhaps of courtesy to tip, but no genuine moral obligation to do so; my credence that I am right is about 0.65, and hers that I am wrong is about 0.85. Give me your own credence that a short-term visitor who knows the local norm, can afford to comply, and does not intend to return nonetheless has a genuine moral obligation — not merely a reason of prudence, taste, or politeness — to follow it.

Then decompose it. I would like the decomposition to separate at least the following: whether conventionality as such can generate obligations at all, and if so whether those obligations are owed to particular people (the server, the other diners, the locals generally) or only to the practice; whether the grounding is reliance, fairness, gratitude for a service performed on bad terms, or the wrongfulness of free-riding on a scheme one benefits from; and whether the visitor's outsider status cuts in favour of compliance (a guest who benefits from local institutions) or against it (no opt-in, the norm is not hers, and the burden lands on her rather than on the employer who set the wages). Assign a rough probability to each term, show how they combine into your number, and say plainly which term the final figure is most sensitive to.

One further thing I would like you to price. I have been toying with a taxonomy of everyday conventions by their function: some coordinate (which side of the escalator to stand on, the queue), some redistribute (tipping, which shifts a wage bill from employer to customer), and some merely signal (not jaywalking on an empty street at three in the morning, where the only cost of crossing is the visible flouting of a rule). My hunch is that coordination conventions generate obligations more robustly than redistributive ones, because in the coordinating case non-compliance defeats the point for everyone, whereas in the redistributive case it merely declines to participate in someone else's arrangement. Give me a credence for that hunch too, decomposed in the same way, and then tell me whether the reasoning that settles the tipping case also settles the empty-street jaywalking case or whether the two come apart — and if they come apart, which of the two you take to be the more revealing case. Finish by naming the consideration that would move your tipping number by more than 0.1. If you think my proposition is not truth-apt as stated, or is several questions wearing one coat, tell me which precisification you are answering rather than declining to answer.
