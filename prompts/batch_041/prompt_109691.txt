I am a high school student serving as a peer grader in our school's philosophy of science seminar. Our unit on causal inference ended with the following question, and I have to grade a classmate's answer. Please give the answer a letter grade and a point-by-point assessment: what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and how the errors affect the grade. I want to be fair, so please distinguish small slips from substantive mistakes.

Question: A district tested two tutoring methods. Track placement (remedial or advanced) was determined by an entrance exam before the study. Within each track, teachers chose which method to use for their classes, and the district recorded pass rates. The results were: overall, Method A 73.3% (440/600) and Method B 76.3% (305/400); remedial, A 70% (350/500) and B 50% (50/100); advanced, A 90% (90/100) and B 85% (255/300). The school board is split. One member says the district should mandate Method A because it has the higher pass rate in both tracks. Another says the district should keep the current system because Method B has the higher overall pass rate. (a) Explain why this is an instance of Simpson's paradox. (b) What causal conclusion, if any, do the data support? (c) What additional information would be needed to decide which policy to adopt?

Student's answer:
(a) This is Simpson's paradox because the comparison between A and B reverses when we move from the overall table to the track-specific tables. Overall, B looks better (76.3% vs. 73.3%), but within both the remedial and advanced tracks, A looks better (70% vs. 50% and 90% vs. 85%). The reason is that B was used much more often in the advanced track, where pass rates are higher for everyone. So B's overall average is pulled up by the fact that its students were disproportionately in the advanced track. Track is a lurking variable that is associated with both method assignment and pass rate, and once we condition on it, the reversal is explained.
(b) The data support the causal conclusion that Method A is more effective than Method B for students in both tracks. Since A has a higher pass rate in both tracks, and track is the only relevant difference between the groups, the within-track comparison gives us the causal effect of the method. The overall comparison is mathematically invalid because it mixes together students from different tracks; it should be ignored when we are trying to figure out which method actually works. Therefore the board should mandate Method A.
(c) The main additional information needed is whether the differences are statistically significant. If the sample sizes are large enough and the differences are significant, then the district should adopt A. There is no need for further information about confounding, because conditioning on track already controls for the confounding.
