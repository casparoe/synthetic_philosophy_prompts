I read a fair bit of philosophy blogs and I keep running into an argument that I can't tell whether it's one argument or three, and I'd like to know if it has a name.

Here's the move. Somebody wants to justify a rule for distributing something — money, medical care, exposure to risk, whatever. They say: pick the rule you'd choose if you didn't know which of the affected people you were going to turn out to be, and treated each of them as equally likely. Then they show that the rule maximizing your expected wellbeing from behind that ignorance is the one that maximizes the sum of everyone's wellbeing. Conclusion: add up everyone's wellbeing and take the policy with the biggest total. I've seen this used for tax rates, for who gets a scarce drug, and — the one that got me thinking — for a proposal to site a hazardous facility by lottery, on the grounds that since everyone had the same chance of being picked to host it, the result was fair whoever ended up with it.

Two things bother me.

First, it seems to prove more than people want it to. If I really am equally likely to be any of the affected people, then I should be indifferent between everyone getting a modest share and a lottery where one person gets a fortune and the rest get nothing, as long as the expected amount comes out the same. Nobody believes those are equally good. The person who draws the short straw has a real complaint, and telling them the draw was fair doesn't seem to answer it. The argument seems to make equal chances and equal outcomes interchangeable, and I don't think they are.

Second, it seems to prove something about a single person that we wouldn't accept either. As one person facing a 50/50 gamble, fine, maybe I should maximize expected value. But the argument treats the whole group as if it were one person facing a gamble over which body they wake up in. That's either just a way of talking or it's smuggling something in, and I can't tell wether it's the latter.

And a third, smaller worry: the conclusion depends on there being a fact about how much better off one person is than another that doesn't depend on who they are or on any arbitrary choice of scale. If you rescaled one person's wellbeing differently, the sum would rank the policies differently. I can't tell if that's a real objection or just the usual grumbling about measurement.

So: does this have a name? Is there a name for the formal result and a different name for the informal version people actually argue? What are the nearest established ideas — I assume this is somewhere near the Rawlsian buisness of choosing principles without knowing your place in society, but I don't know how the two relate — and which one fits best? Please don't just hand me a label. What I'm after is the carving: which steps of the informal argument are licensed and which are doing extra work, and whether what I'm calling one thing is actually two or three things people keep running together.

You're a philosopher who works on decision theory and social choice. Before you answer, brainstorm: write out the informal argument as numbered steps, and note at each step what it assumes. Then give me the answer.
