I write about economics and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am in the last week of a piece on what my beat calls price discovery — the fact, which I have now watched happen twice with my own eyes, that a market can move a few minutes before a news story breaks, as though the price had read the wire before I did. Over lunch, a source of mine, an economist I have quoted for years and who is not given to provocation, offered me an argument that I have not been able to put down, chiefly because it quietly dissolves the subject of my article. He was careful with it, and I have transcribed it as carefully, resisting the temptation to soften any step:

(1) A price is nothing over and above the point at which one person agrees to sell and another agrees to buy. (2) No one sells what he believes to be worth more than the price, and no one buys what he believes to be worth less; a trade that both parties expected to regret would not be a trade. So every trade rests on a difference of opinion about what the thing is worth. (3) The thing has one value, so buyer and seller cannot both be right; at the moment of every trade they hold contrary beliefs about the same proposition. (4) Two people who are both rational and equally well informed cannot hold contrary beliefs about the same proposition; if they do, at least one of them is making a mistake. (5) Therefore every trade is evidence that one party has erred, or that one party knows something the other does not. (6) If your counterparty knows something you do not, you should expect to lose by trading with him; you can work this out in advance, so you should decline, and he should expect you to decline, and so on until no trade that rests on private information can survive being thought through. (7) So the only trades that can happen are ones in which neither party is better informed, which is to say trades that teach the participants nothing; and since a price is just a completed trade, no price can teach anyone anything he did not already know. His gloss: markets do not aggregate information, they merely display the opinions of whoever was already best informed, and my price that moves before the news is either luck or a story I am telling about noise.

The conclusion proves too much for me to accept. It would mean that insider trading cannot exist, that price discovery is a phrase without a referent, and that a bubble is impossible by definition, since a bubble requires people to buy at prices above what they privately think the thing is worth. It would also, I notice, be self-undermining in a way I cannot quite articulate: the argument opens by insisting that a trade is informative — it tells you your counterparty thinks he knows something — and closes by insisting that trades are not. I have pressed my friend on the obvious escape, that people trade to raise cash, to hedge, to shed risk, to match a liability, and he waves it off: his case, he says, is two people, one binary question, cash on both sides, no other motive, so that the only reason for either to take either side is a difference of view. He calls the underlying result the no-trade theorem and says the rest is arithmetic; I have since seen the name attached to more than one pair of authors and will not try to pin it down here. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on markets. Tell me which numbered step, or which seam between two steps, I should reject, and tell me why it looked so obviously fine until I stared at it — including which of the steps is quietly carrying an assumption it never states. Then price the repairs. If I give up the step you nominate, what else goes with it, and what does the remaining position fail to explain that the original argument explained? My friend would sooner lose (1) than (7); I would sooner lose almost any premise than the fact that prices sometimes move before the news, which is not itself a numbered step. We are each defending our own conclusion, so I would like you to say plainly whose step is cheapest to give up.
