Okay, I've basically settled on Lakatos's picture of science — hard cores, protective belts, progressive vs degenerating problem shifts, positive and negative heuristics, and the rule that you never judge a research programme by itself but only against its rivals, and only over the long run. It just feels right to me: it explains why scientists keep grinding away on a theory the data is battering (you don't touch the hard core, you patch the belt) and why Popper's drop-everything-the-moment-it's-falsified picture is wrong.

But I want you to treat it as a target. What would count against it? Not "Kuhn said something different" — I mean what would the history of science have to look like for Lakatos's account to be the wrong one, and which episodes, arguments, or internal problems would do the damage. Rank them from "this should make me drop it" down to "annoying but survivable," and be clear about which ones a Lakatosian would actually concede versus wave away as a misunderstanding.

Things I'm already suspicious about:

How do you identify a programme's hard core except by looking at what its defenders refuse to give up? That looks circular, and if any episode can be reconstructed as a progressive problem shift after the fact, is there anything the account actually forbids? I.e. has it been insulated into unfalsifiability by the "only in hindsight" and "only comparatively" caveats — and if so, is that a legitimate feature of a historiographical methodology or the sign that it's quietly stopped making claims?

Novel prediction is doing a lot of work in the account (a programme is progressive if it predicts new facts). Novel in what sense — novel in time, novel relative to what the rival programme already said, or novel relative to the programme's own heuristic? Depending which one you pick, verdicts on real cases flip. Is there a version of the criterion that isn't just "whatever makes this particular case come out right"?

Timing. Take supersymetry and the naturalness programme: the LHC hasn't turned anything up, people have been calling it degenerating for a decade, others say it just needs a belt-patch. Does Lakatos's machinery actually deliver a verdict, or does it license both answers forever? Same question for string theory, and for the amyloid cascade hypothesis in Alzheimer's research.

The comparative move: if "degenerating" is always relative to some rival, what does the account say when every programme in an area is degenerating? Does it just go quiet?

Self-application: is the methodology of research programmes itself a research programme? Has it been progressive? What would its degeneration look like? And is "it's meta, the criteria don't apply at that level" a defence or an evasion?

Last thing: what does it predict that Kuhn or Popper doesn't — anything you couldn't also tell from the outside, after the fact, once you already know how things turned out? If the honest answer is "nothing," say so, and then tell me whether I should keep beleiving it anyway.
