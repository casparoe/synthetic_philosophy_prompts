An essay I read recently gave the following account of Buridan's ass:

'Buridan, the medieval logician, asked what happens when an agent is confronted with two options it regards as exactly equal. His famous illustration is a donkey placed midway between two identical piles of hay. The donkey has no reason to prefer either pile, and since the will cannot act without a reason, it cannot choose at all, and starves. Buridan's lesson was that choice requires a preference, and that in the absence of any preference rational choice is simply impossible. This is why decision theory insists that a rational agent's preferences be complete, and it is why, whenever you are genuinely torn between two equally good options, the sensible thing to do is to invent an arbitrary tie-breaker before you end up like the donkey.'

I have no academic background and had only met the phrase 'Buridan's ass' in passing before reading this, but the passage troubles me, and I would rather not carry away a garbled version of it. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a specialist in medieval philosophy or in decision theory would accept as accurate, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong? I would especially like to know how much of this is Buridan himself and how much is later tradition fastened onto his name, whether the advice about tie-breakers is the moral of the story or a reply to it, and whether the closing claim about rational choice being impossible without a preference follows from anything true in the summary.
