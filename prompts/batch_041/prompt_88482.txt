Im working on a paper about whether neural networks really have representations, and I want you to tear apart the draft below. Fair warning: I'm not a philosopher by training, I do mech interp stuff, so if I'm reinventing a wheel or committing a category error I'd rather hear it now. Here's the draft.

The question of whether neural networks have representations is usually treated as one question but it's at least two. First: do the internal states of a trained network carry information about task-relevant features of the input, information that some downstream part of the network actually consumes? Second: are those states contentful in the sense that matters when we say a state represents something — do they stand in for the thing in a way that supports the normative talk of getting it right or getting it wrong?

Call the first the thin question and the second the thick one. Most of the interpretability literature answers the thin question and then quietly treats the answer as an answer to the thick question. Someone trains a linear probe on activations, gets ninety-five percent decodability for some feature, and concludes the network represents that feature. But decodability is cheap. Almost any high-dimensional state will let you decode almost anything if you pick the right probe, and the probe may be doing the work rather than the network. So thin representation is nearly free, which is exactly why it can't be what the realists and the skeptics are actually fighting about.

My proposal is to define the interesting notion causally, as a two-way test. A feature is represented in the thick sense, for a given network and task, when you can both read it out and write it in. Read-out: from the state alone you can recover the feature better than chance, and the network's own downstream computation is sensitive to the same direction, so that the read-out direction and the direction that actually moves the logits agree. Write-in: you can intervene on that direction and the behaviour changes in the specific, systematic way the content of the feature predicts — flip the feature, flip the behaviour, and not just any behaviour, the right one.

The hard part is that write-in success is gameable. If you add a big vector to the residual stream, the network downstream will often just do something. You can get a behavioural change and call it a successful intervention when really you've shoved the state off the manifold the network was trained on, and it's responding to the weirdness rather than to the feature. So the write-in test needs a condition: the intervention has to keep you on the manifold of states the network actually encounters, or at least the behavioural change has to be predicted by the content of the feature and not just by the size of the perturbation. I dont have a clean formal version of this yet.

The payoff, if it works, is that it lets us say what's at stake between realism and skepticism about interpretability without either side begging the question. The skeptic says decodability proves nothing; the realist says the circuits prove everything. They talk past each other because neither has said what a representation has to do. The causal two-way criterion is a proposal for what it has to do.
