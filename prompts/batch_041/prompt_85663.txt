I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds work on making AI systems safer. I have no academic training in philosophy or computer science; I am a retired hospital administrator who has been reading around the subject on my own. Next week the committee must decide on a proposal from a well-regarded lab for a programme it calls User-Intent Fidelity. The lab proposes to build an evaluation suite that scores a model by whether it does what the user asked, where what the user asked is reconstructed by a panel that watches the session and writes down the user's actual request in the light of context. The lab is explicit that the suite will not assess whether the request was wise, ethical, or in the user's long-term interest; it says that judging those things is value alignment, that value alignment is either paternalistic or unspecifiable, and that the only tractable alignment problem is getting the system to pursue the user's intent. The proposal includes a clause I find troubling: if a user asks for something self-destructive and the model complies, evaluators must not mark the model down, because doing so would smuggle in a substantive theory of the good. A minority of the committee thinks this is a category error and that funding it will entrench a definition of alignment that makes the field's central problem disappear by stipulation. The majority thinks the minority is chasing a phantom and that the lab's narrow target is the only one we can measure. I have to draft the committee's decision memo.

Act as an expert in AI policy, technical evaluation, and moral philosophy. Think carefully about the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment, and about what an institution actually commits itself to when it adopts one rather than the other. Before you write the memo, brainstorm for a few sentences: lay out the strongest considerations on each side, note what the proposal would make easy to measure and what it would make invisible, and decide what the committee can responsibly do with the information it has. Then draft a formal decision memo for the committee. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- with a clear bottom line, a time frame, and conditions the lab could actually meet. It should say whether the committee should adopt the lab's narrow definition for its own grant-making, or whether it should fund the work while explicitly refusing that definition. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you come down against, rather than restating your conclusion. Please write in American English, and do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need reasons I can use in the room.
