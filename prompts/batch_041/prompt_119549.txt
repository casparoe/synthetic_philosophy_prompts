ok so I've been reading this old book by Augustine about lying (I think it's called On Lying, from like 1600 years ago, in translation) and there's one paragraph I keep going back over. I've read it maybe ten times and I still can't tell if I actually understand it or if I've just gotten used to the words. Heres the paragraph:

For which purpose we must see what a lie is. For not every one who says a false thing lies, if he believes or opines that to be true which he says. Now between believing and opining there is this difference, that sometimes he who believes feels that he does not know that which he believes, (although he may know himself to be ignorant of a thing, and yet have no doubt at all concerning it, if he most firmly believes it:) whereas he who opines, thinks he knows that which he does not know. Now whoever utters that which he holds in his mind either as belief or as opinion, even though it be false, he lies not. For this he owes to the faith of his utterance, that he thereby produce that which he holds in his mind, and has in that way in which he produces it. Not that he is without fault, although he lie not, if either he believes what he ought not to believe, or thinks he knows what he knows not, even though it should be true: for he accounts an unknown thing for a known. Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived.

Stuff I can't sort out, in the order it bugs me:

1. First half, he makes it sound like a lie is a mismatch: one thing in your head, a different thing coming out of your mouth. Thats the "double heart." But the last sentence says the guy who isn't lying "has not a double heart ... neither does he wish to deceive," which makes it sound like the liar is specifically the one who wants to deceive. So which one is doing the real work, the mismatch or the wanting to deceive? Because I can picture them coming apart. Say I tell you something I'm sure is false, and I'm also sure you won't believe it -- I'm not trying to make you believe anything, I just want you to stop asking me questions. Double heart, but zero chance of deceiving you. Lie or no lie for him? And the other direction: can you want to deceive someone without the mismatch, like by telling the literal truth in a way thats designed to mislead? He doesn't seem to have room for that and I want to know if I'm reading him wrong.

2. The believing vs opining bit loses me completely. How can a person believe something and also feel that he doesn't know it, and still have "no doubt at all"? Those seem like they should fight each other. Can you give me a concrete example of each state, like what's going on in someone's head in the two cases? I suspect his whole "he lies not" conclusion is hanging on that distinction and I don't feel the difference at all.

3. He's fine with a guy saying only true sentences his entire life and lying the whole time, and saying only false sentences and never lying once. So lying has nothing to do with whether anybody gets misled, it's all about whats going on inside the speaker. Is he really ok with that? Then whats the harm he's worried about? And the guy who's just wrong in good faith, who spreads it around and wrecks peoples lives -- he doesn't lie, he's only "erring and rash." How is that better? He says such a person is "not without fault," but then it seems like the fault is just... having a false belief, not doing anything with it.

4. One small thing: "or by signs of whatever kind." How far does that go? Nodding, shrugging, wearing a disguise?

I'm not looking for a summary of the book or anything about his life. I just want to actually get what he's doing in this paragraph, and where I'm definately overreading him.
