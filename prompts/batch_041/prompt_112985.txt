I run a small team that builds semi-autonomous software — our two live products are a warehouse robot and a customer-support chatbot — and my engineers and I keep having the same fight about how "smart" each new behavior needs to be. I want a procedure we can actually run, not a philosophy seminar.

Here's the ladder I'm working from, as I understand it. Level 1: the behavior is just built in — no learning, no deliberation; the design did the adapting, not the agent. Level 2: the agent tries things in the world and keeps what gets rewarded, so it has to actually act and get feedback. Level 3: the agent builds some internal model of the situation and tries options inside that model before acting, so bad options die in simulation instead of in the real world. Level 4: the agent offloads part of the thinking onto external stuff — notation, checklists, language, a lookup table, another agent or a person — and the tool carries some of the cognitive load. I think Dennett called these Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, in that order, but I've probably mangled the details, so correct my summary where it's wrong, because the procedure has to be right about the ladder even where it simplifies it.

Write me a decision procedure my team can run every time we're about to add a behavior. It should be a short ordered list of questions, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, tell us where the procedure stops with a no (i.e. don't build this at all), and mark the points where the call is a seperate value judgment that has to come back to me rather than being settled by the procedure. Assume the reader is an engineer who doesn't know the theory. Keep the distinctions that actually do work and tell me which ones I can throw out. Constraints: the robot has a weak onboard computer and a hard 200ms budget on anything that touches the wheels; we cannot let it learn by trial and error in an aisle full of people; the chatbot can log everything and retrain nightly but has to answer within a couple seconds. Anything that changes how the robot moves has to be signed off by me, and I have about twenty minutes to review.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (a) the warehouse robot's collision avoidance, where a mistake can hurt someone; (b) the chatbot deciding whether to promise a refund above its standard limit; (c) the robot picking which aisle to take on an ordinary day. At least one of the three should come out in a way my engineers won't like — say so plainly if it does, don't soften it. Finish by telling me what this procedure can't decide and where it will misfire.

Start the response with the procedure itself, no preamble and no restating my question.
