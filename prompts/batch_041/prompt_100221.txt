Suppose Otto Neurath, the Vienna Circle philosopher who died in 1945, could have lived to see the emoji keyboard. From the 1920s onward he worked on ISOTYPE, a system of simplified, standardized pictograms for displaying social and economic statistics, designed to be legible to an international public that shared no common language; he was also the Vienna Circle's most uncompromising opponent of metaphysics and of any attempt to found knowledge on a first philosophy, and he held that the repair of our conceptual language always has to be carried out from a ship already at sea.

Emoji are in some respects the closest thing the world has actually built to a general picture language: a finite, standardized inventory of pictographic characters, fixed by an international committee and carried inside ordinary text. But the standard fixes code points rather than drawings, so the same character can be drawn very differently by different vendors; and the committee's stability rules mean that a character, once assigned, cannot be withdrawn or reassigned even as its use drifts. Nor are emoji used to state anything: they signal tone, soften a demand, and are read differently across generations and cultures.

What would Neurath have made of this? Would he have seen emoji as the belated realization of the international picture language he wanted, as a cautionary case that reveals what such a language actually requires, or as evidence that the whole program was misconceived? Address both the technical question — what does an unambiguous picture language demand of its signs? — and the political one, given that his picture statistics were meant to inform citizens rather than to decorate their messages. Argue for whichever reading you find most defensible, rather than the one easiest to attribute to him. Limit yourself to 300 words and begin with a single sentence stating your verdict.
