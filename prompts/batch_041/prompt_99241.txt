ok so decision theory reading group, and "you can't money-pump a rational agent" is the line everyone keeps using to win arguments. Last week it got dropped to shut down a debate about a friend's preferences over three job offers — she ranks A over B, B over C, but also C over A — and someone said "well, she can be pumped, so shes irrational, done." I don't think it's that simple and I want to see all the machinery instead of just the verdict.

Lay out as a menu the different precise claims "you can't money-pump a rational agent" could actually be making, ordered from the near-trivial to the very strong, and for each give me a verdict — true, false, or defensible-but-contentious — plus whether it's really a constraint on rationality or just a claim about what happens to be exploitable. Then tell me which of the readings actually gets the guy his conclusion that her cycle is irrational.

A few things I specifically want pulled apart:
- what the "can't" is doing — she won't ever get pumped, she can't be forced into it, or there simply exists no sequence of trades that leaves her worse off
- whether the pump needs her preferences to stay fixed while her holdings change, or whether it can run on preferences that shift over time (and if it can, is that even the same argument anymore?)
- the difference between "her choices are exploitable" and "her preferences are the wrong shape" — same claim or two different ones?
- the old objection that an agent who sees the whole sequence coming can just refuse the first step, and whether that rescues her or only relocates the problem
- synchronic vs diachronic: the pump unfolds over time, so is this a constraint on how you choose at a moment or on how you're allowed to change?

and at the end, which reading is the reading group most likely invoking when they use the line as a conversation-stopper? I have a feeling it's the flattest one.
