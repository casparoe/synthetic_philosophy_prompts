I've been drafted to run the February session of a monthly discussion group that spun out of a reading group a few of us kept going after the pandemic — about a dozen people, mostly working programmers plus two policy analysts, all of them argument-comfortable and none of them historians — and the topic I've been handed is how to use historical analogies when reasoning about transformative AI. I don't need anyone talked into the subject; the group reads the same blogs I do and has already absorbed the outside view, base rates, and the general suspicion of narrative. What I need from you is a plan for teaching the skill itself in seventy-five minutes with a whiteboard, and the plan is the deliverable — don't run the argument for me, tell me how to run the session.

Here is the material I want to build on, and I'd like you to tell me whether it survives contact with an audience. A friend of mine has been pushing a three-way distinction I've found genuinely clarifying: an analogy can function as a base rate (it tells you what usually happens in cases of this kind), as a frame (it tells you which features of the present case are worth looking at), or as a repertoire (it tells you which moves were available to the actors, so you can ask which of them are available now). On her account, 'is this analogy apt?' is a malformed question, because the same analogy can be first-rate in one mode and worthless in another — nuclear weapons are plausibly a decent repertoire for thinking about deterrence and a poor base rate for thinking about the frequency of catastrophe. I also want to lodge a point I've only half worked out: the outside-view move, which this group already trusts, presupposes that a reference class has been chosen, and choosing the analogy is exactly choosing the reference class, so the analogy has done its work before anyone consults a frequency. My provisional carrying example is the printing press, because it's the case everyone believes they already know and it forces the question of what the analogy is supposed to do; I keep being tempted by nuclear weapons and suspect that's mere familiarity, and the Industrial Revolution feels too diffuse to anchor anything — tell me whether that instinct is right. I'd also like the audience to leave with one disanalogy lodged firmly in mind: the press spread through matter, metal and paper and a trade in books, whereas the thing we're actually worried about can be copied at approximately zero marginal cost, which changes what containment could even mean.

Plan the session: the order in which the pieces must go so that the three modes land as a working tool rather than a taxonomy, what has to be in place before my carrying example can do any work (and whether you'd replace it, and with what, given that the audience knows no history and I have no time to teach any), and in either case what the example has to have and has to lack for the three modes to be visible in it. Then the misconceptions they'll arrive with or develop, in the order they'll actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off — my guesses are at minimum 'an analogy is an argument whose conclusion follows if the mapping holds,' 'apt versus inapt is binary,' 'one disanalogy kills it,' and 'this technology is unprecedented, so no analogy applies,' but tell me what I'm missing and which of my four is really the same mistake wearing a hat. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so out loud. Finish with the single question I should ask in the last five minutes that would show whether the tool took, and what a wrong answer to it would look like. Lead with your bottom line — the one sequencing decision the whole session turns on — in your first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
