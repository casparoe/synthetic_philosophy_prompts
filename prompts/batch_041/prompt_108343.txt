I am opening a two-day workshop on the norms of assertion next month, and I would like you to write the opening remarks I will deliver. The room is about thirty people: roughly half are epistemologists who have argued about this material for years, and the other half are people I badly want to keep in the room — two of our funders, a retired appellate judge who has read her way into the subject on her own, and four graduate students from other departments. The remarks need to be worth the ten minutes they take, which means they cannot be thanks and housekeeping with a paragraph of philosophy bolted on.

The provocation I want the remarks to carry is this. The debate has been conducted largely on cases in which one person tells another how things are and very little hangs on it, whereas the cases that matter are ones in which the audience will act on what is said and the speaker knows it. I have been calling the first sort report cases and the second sort stake cases; please use those two terms. I want the remarks to argue that moving from the first to the second is not a change of subject but a change of pressure on the very same question, and to make that argument through the two cases below rather than in the abstract.

Case one. A mountain-rescue coordinator, working from radio reports and a snowpack test taken that morning, tells a team on the ground that the ridge is safe to traverse. Her evidence is good but not conclusive — she would put it near ninety percent — and she knows the team will go if she says so. They go; a slab releases. Was the assertion permissible, and which of the candidate norms — knowledge, truth, or belief — should be doing the work in answering that? If the answer is that she should have said something weaker, say what she should have said and why that is not a dodge.

Case two. A clinician learns, from an unblinded result given to her in confidence, that the trial drug is harming patients in the control arm, and the protocol bars her from telling the participants. She knows. Does anything in the standard accounts explain why it would still be wrong of her to say so? If nothing does, say so plainly, and say what that shows.

Please build the remarks in three movements, in this order: first, the difference between report cases and stake cases, stated crisply enough that the outsiders catch it on one hearing; second, the two cases used to show that each of the three accounts says something true and something insufficient, with the insufficiency located precisely rather than gestured at; third, a single well-formed question put to the room — one the two days are meant to answer — in place of a declaration of a winner. One brief honest aside is welcome, once and no longer: nearly everything an opening speech does is thanking, promising, hoping, and predicting, so the norms we have convened to discuss are oddly idle in the very genre that opens the discussion. Do not turn that into a stunt or a running gag. Do not close on uplift.

For the audience: gloss any technical term in the sentence where you use it, and do not rehash material that half the room could recite in their sleep. Do not attribute views to named philosophers and do not refer to particular books or papers. I would rather the remarks be about the positions than about who holds them, and a misattribution in an opening speech is worse than no attribution at all. Keep it to about nine hundred words, roughly six minutes at the lectern, in a register I could actually deliver: measured, dry, mildly amused, never solemn.

End the response with a final line beginning Summary: followed by one sentence stating what the remarks argue.
