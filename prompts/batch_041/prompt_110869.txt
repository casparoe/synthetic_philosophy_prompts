I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading in moral and political philosophy. I am preparing an essay for a seminar on Rousseau, and I have been struck by how much of his case against the theater is a claim about the audience rather than the stage: that the theater trains us to feel virtue vicariously while leaving us passive, that it makes a spectacle of passion and so weakens our capacity to act on it, that the actor's art is one of simulated feeling and that a republic which prizes such performances may come to prefer appearance to reality, and that the theater replaces the shared festival, in which citizens are both participants and spectators, with a darkened room in which each person is alone with an image. I would like to test that argument against Hamlet.

Read the play through Rousseau's critique of the theater. Does Hamlet confirm Rousseau's diagnosis or refute it? On the confirming side, Hamlet himself seems to be the perfect Rousseauian spectator: he is moved to self-reproach by the player's speech about Hecuba, yet he cannot act; he stages a play to catch the king's conscience, but the effect on Claudius is ambiguous, and the play-within-the-play may be as much a substitute for action as a spur to it. On the refuting side, the theater in Hamlet does seem to have real effects -- on Claudius, on Gertrude, on Hamlet himself -- and the play is full of characters who perform, dissemble, and watch. Which reading does the play support?

I would like you to take both Rousseau and Shakespeare seriously, in both directions. Where does Rousseau's argument illuminate a scene or a character's choice -- the Hecuba speech, the play-within-the-play, the closet scene, the graveyard, the final duel? Where does the play resist the Rousseauian reading, and where does Rousseau's argument need to be qualified to accommodate it? I am particularly interested in the difference between the actor's simulated passion and Hamlet's real grief, and in whether Hamlet offers any alternative to the theater other than more theater. Does the play's own status as a theatrical spectacle complicate its critique? Does it make the audience complicit in a way Rousseau would have predicted, or does it produce a kind of self-knowledge that Rousseau thought the stage could not produce?

Finally, tell me what Hamlet adds to the philosophical debate about theater and politics that Rousseau's argument alone does not. I do not need a survey of criticism or a reading list; I want a sustained reading of the play in Rousseau's terms, and a judgment about whether the play vindicates him, refutes him, or does something more interesting.
