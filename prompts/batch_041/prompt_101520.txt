So I'm in this ethics seminar and last week we did the whole "consequentialist cluelessness" thing — the idea that if you judge actions by their consequences, the consequences keep rippling out forever (you give a stranger directions, they make their train, they meet someone on the train, etc.), so there's no way to ever know whether what you did was actually for the best. I've been arguing about it nonstop with two people in my dorm. One of them thinks it basically demolishes consequentialism as a practical decision procedure — like, if you can never know the score, what's even the point of trying to maximize it? The other one thinks that's way too dramatic and that consequentialists can just evaluate the consequences you can reasonably foresee and call it a day.

Could you write a dialogue between these two? Make them both sharp grad students who've read the same stuff, neither one a dummy, and have them actually respond to each other's points instead of talking past each other. I'd love it to get a bit in the weeds rather than just trade slogans. A few specific things I want one of them to bring up: (a) that the ripple effects don't just fade out, they multiply, so a tiny probability of some astronomically huge downstream outcome could in principle dominate the expected value of even a trivial act, like being rude to a barista; and (b) that if you try to write the total expected value of an action as a sum over all its downstream effects, that sum might not even be well-defined — it could depend on the order you add the terms up — which would mean "the expected value of this action" isn't a determinate number at all. Have the other one take both of those head-on rather than dodging them.

Also let them land on a concrete case at some point, because I keep thinking about whether my roommate was right to spend $400 on concert tickets instead of donating it, and whether cluelessness makes that question unanswerable or just harder than it looks.
