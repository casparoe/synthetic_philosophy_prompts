So I'm a TA for an intro grad seminar and I've got a stack of short-answer responses to grade by Friday. Here's the question I put on the assignment plus one student's answer. Can you give me a letter grade and then the specific comments that justify it, point by point? I want to be neither too harsh nor too generous, and I don't want confident prose to carry a weak answer or vice versa. Also don't grade on style.

The question:

"In lecture I split apart two questions we tend to run together. (A) The metaphysical question: what computation, if any, constitutes a state's being a morally considerable experience? (B) The attribution question: given our evidence, when are we licensed to treat a system as morally considerable? A very common move is to answer (B) with some marker -- reportability, global broadcast, recurrent processing, whatever -- and then quietly let that marker also answer (A). Pick any one system (a purely feedforward image classifier, a recurrent net trained on next-token prediction, a human, a honeybee) and explain what each question demands of it. Then say whether the move I just described is a mistake, and if so, exactly what kind of mistake it is."

The student's answer:

"Two questions. (A) is about what consciousness IS; (B) is about what we can KNOW. Take a feedforward image classifier. It has no recurrence, no global workspace, no reentrant loops, so by the main computational theories (global workspace, recurrent processing, higher-order) it probably doesn't have experiences. That's the metaphysical verdict -- the computation it runs is just the wrong shape.

Now (B). We can't rule out that it's conscious; the problem of other minds never fully closes, and behavioral indistinguishability wouldn't settle it even if we had it. So the honest position is uncertainty. Here's where the professor's move comes in. If the classifier can't report anything and can't broadcast to a global workspace, then we have no way to attribute experience to it. But since we also can't prove it isn't conscious, and the cost of wrongly denying moral status is potentially enormous, we should give it the same moral weight as a system we're sure is conscious. A nonzero probability times a huge stake is still a huge expected cost, so symmetry of stakes gives us symmetry of treatment.

Is the professor's move a mistake? I don't think so. There's no sharp line between a feedforward classifier and a recurrent one -- you can interpolate continuously between them by adding layers and connections. Since nature draws no line, there's no fact of the matter about which side a given system is on, so (A) has no answer and (B) is all we have left. The marker doesn't replace the metaphysics; it shows there was never a metaphysics to replace."

For the grade: assume a standard A-F scale where an A- means the central claims are right and the slips are minor, B-range means one real substantive error, C or below means the answer defeats its own thesis. Tell me which band you'd put it in, then list the specific sentences that drive the grade and what's wrong or right about each. I care most about whether the answer actually keeps (A) and (B) apart the way the question demanded, so please address that head-on.
