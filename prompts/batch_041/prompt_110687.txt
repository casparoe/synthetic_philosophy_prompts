I've been trying to work out why the law seems to care so much about actual lies and so little about misleading people, and I can't get it to add up.

Here's the pattern that bugs me. If I'm under oath and I say something I know isn't true, that's perjury. But if I answer every question with statements that are all literally true, chosen and arranged so the jury walks away believing something false, that's normally not perjury, and usually not fraud either. Someone told me there's a word for that move — paltering, using true statements to plant a false impression — but the law seems to shrug at it. Selling stuff is the same story: between two private people the default is that if you don't ask whether the engine block is cracked, I don't have to bring it up. But then consumer law in lots of places goes the other way and bans misleading people even when every single word was true, and advertising regulators will go after a whole campaign for being misleading overall without any one false sentence in it. And obviously the true-but-misleading answer is the house style in politics and in lawyerly non-denials.

So one legal system, three lines: false statement bad; true statements arranged to fool you fine (under oath); true statements arranged to fool you bad (business selling to a consumer); true statements arranged to fool you fine (me selling you my car). I want to know what view of lying is baked into all this. My hunch is it's not really about whether anyone ends up believing something false — it's about the act of asserting, of putting a falsehood into words — but I can't tell if that's the law's actual commitment or just something I've talked myself into.

So: what theory of what's wrong with lying does this practice presuppose? Does it rule out the idea that the wrong is the damage to the hearer's beliefs, or can it live with that too? Which parts of the practice really need that theory, and which parts are just about what courts can practically prove or about where we've decided to put the burden of asking questions? Where is it underdetermined, so that several different theories would license the same rules?

Then push on it: a friend says we should scrap the assertion line and just say you're liable whenever you meant for me to end up with a false belief, true words or not. Would the current practice survive that, and if not, what would actually have to change — and would we lose anything worth keeping?
