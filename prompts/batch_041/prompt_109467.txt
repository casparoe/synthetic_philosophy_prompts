I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty years in accelerator physics — who has spent the last three years sitting in on a metaphysics discussion group, where I am reliably the least qualified person in the room. Last week the group discussed David Lewis's modal realism: possible worlds are concrete totalities of the same kind as our own, each is a maximal whole whose parts are unified by spatiotemporal interrelations, no two worlds share a part, and actuality is indexical, like here or now. Two members, Anna and Ruben, argued their way into a standoff I could not resolve.

Ruben opened by saying that modal realism should look far less extravagant to a physicist than to a philosopher, because the Everettian reading of quantum mechanics already commits us to many concrete worlds which diverge from one another at measurement events. The branches share a common past, he conceded, but that is a detail of the physics rather than a difference in kind, and it disarms the incredulous stare.

Anna replied that the shared past is exactly what ruins the analogy. Lewis's worlds do not overlap, and branches share their entire history: each branch contains the same earlier events as parts of itself. She added that the relation meant to unify a world must partition reality — worlds are maximal sums of mutually related things, and worldmatehood has to be an equivalence relation for that to work — whereas in a branching structure spatiotemporal relatedness is not transitive: an event in one branch and an event in another are each spatiotemporally related to the event they share in the past, but not to each other. On her reading, Everettian quantum mechanics gives us one world with a branching causal structure, not many worlds in Lewis's sense.

Ruben answered that she had identified the wrong requirement: what the analysis of modality needs is some relation that partitions reality into maximal wholes, not spatiotemporal relatedness in particular. If branching is fundamental, we may take the worldmaking relation to be the branch-internal one, and the branches come out as the worlds.

Anna said that relation is not transitive either — two branch events are each related in that way to the shared past event, but not to each other — so it is not an equivalence relation, and taking its transitive closure simply merges the branches back into one world. Ruben said that if transitivity is the only obstacle, then so much the worse for transitivity. Anna said that is not a repair but a change of subject, since worlds were introduced to do a job.

Neither would give ground. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who is right about what, tell me which of them is more right overall, and tell me what both of them are missing.
