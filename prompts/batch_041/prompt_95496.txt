I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a mid-level official at a global health foundation, a man who has talked himself into a version of consequentialism that I find both attractive and alarming. The position he holds—and which I have come to half-believe myself—is this: consequentialist cluelessness, understood as the fact that the causal and evaluative consequences of our acts ramify indefinitely so that no finite agent can trace them, does not undermine consequentialism as a guide to action. Expected-value reasoning remains the right response to that cluelessness, because uncertainty is precisely what decision theory is for; the cluelessness is an epistemic predicament, not a refutation, and a consequentialist can act with integrity while knowing the full ledger will never be available. I want to know what would count against this position, not as a slogan but as a serious map of its exposure: which observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or historical developments should move someone who holds it, which would be decisive, which merely awkward, and which the position's own defenders would concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as misunderstandings.

To keep the inquiry from collapsing into generic debates, please treat two varieties separately. The first is ramification cluelessness: the causal pathways are in principle traceable, but their branching is so vast that any finite calculation is arbitrary. The second is category-drift cluelessness: the evaluative significance of later outcomes may be transformed by the very act being evaluated—say, by changing what future agents value, or by making a practice obsolete—so that the standard is not merely uncertain but partly constituted by the choice. My character believes expected-value reasoning can absorb both. Tell me what would have to be true for that belief to fail. I am especially interested in whether the view can be distinguished from rule consequentialism or from a virtue-ethical insistence on practical wisdom; whether it predicts anything its rivals do not; and whether it can avoid the conclusion that, under sufficiently radical cluelessness, all available acts have equal expected value and moral choice becomes arbitrary. I would personally be most moved by a case in which the view's own expected-value machinery recommends acts no reflective agent could endorse, or by a formal result showing that the relevant expectations are undefined rather than merely difficult to compute.

I do not need citations, reading lists, or names of theorists; I want the arguments and the defeaters themselves. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, say explicitly which ones a committed defender would have to concede, and end with an honest assessment of whether the position is well insulated and whether that insulation is a defect. The character in my novel will have to act on whatever remains; I need to know what he would be rational to believe, and what would rightly shake him.
