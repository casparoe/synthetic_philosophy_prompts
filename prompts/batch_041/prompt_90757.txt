I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book about a priest who loses his faith and then has to keep showing up for people anyway, and I've gotten weirdly obsessed with Rawls's religion problem. Not the famous public reason stuff, or not only that — the whole arc. The kid who was so devout he thought about the priesthood and wrote that undergrad thesis where he goes after Pelagianism, the idea that we can save ourselves by our own efforts, that grace is a nice extra if you can get it. Then the war, the capricious deaths he saw, the Holocuast, and the providence thing just dies in him. And then he spends his career building a political philosophy that has to be freestanding, that has to work for people who don't share a faith — and he keeps insisting it isn't a secular comprehenisve doctrine either, it's just politics, and later he even softens the public reason thing so you can bring your faith in as long as you eventually cough up the public reasons. Fine.

Here's what I can't stop chewing on. I want to read The Brothers Karamazov through that arc — not the standard Ivan's-problem-of-evil reading, but the whole thing: Ivan's rebellion, the Grand Inquisitor, Zosima on grace and universal guilt, Alyosha's faith, Dmitri's suffering. Does the novel actually dramatize the Pelagian question Rawls was chewing on as a kid — that we can't save ourselves, that it has to be given? Zosima seems to be saying something like that. And if he is, what does that do to Rawls's later move, where religion becomes one comprehensive doctrine among others that the political conception has to be neutral between? Is there something in Zosima that resists being filed under "comprehensive doctrine" — something about grace that a freestanding political liberalism can't even name without thinning it out? Or does the novel actually vindicate Rawls: Ivan's rebellion is unanswerable, the Inquisitor is right that most people can't bear the freedom, and the only way to live together is to bracket the whole thing?

I want the honest version — where the novel resists the Rawls reading, where Rawls's categories make the novel look smaller than it is, and the other way round. And I want to know what the novel gives you that Rawls's argument can't. Is there a scene or a character that shows the thing about faith a political conception has to leave out, or is that just me being a novelist and wanting religion to be more than a doctrine?
