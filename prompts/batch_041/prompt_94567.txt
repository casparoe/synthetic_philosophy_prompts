I'm a journalist writing a feature for an ideas magazine on how popular films handle counterfactual history. I've been thinking about the climax of Quentin Tarantino's Inglourious Basterds, where Hitler is machine-gunned to death in a Paris cinema. It's a satisfying revenge fantasy, but it also seems to endorse a particular view of history: that killing one man would have changed everything. That's the Great Man theory in its purest form. Yet historians of Nazi Germany have long debated whether the regime was driven by Hitler's personal will (intentionalism) or by the chaotic, competitive structures of the Nazi state (functionalism). I'd like you to write an essay that reads the film through that debate. Does the film's counterfactual logic commit it to intentionalism, or does it inadvertently reveal the limits of the Great Man view? What would a functionalist say about a world without Hitler? And does the film's aesthetic of revenge—its pleasure in violence—distort or illuminate the historical question? I want a reading that takes both the film and the historiography seriously, and that ends by saying what the film gets right or wrong about how history works. Please don't just summarize the plot; I need an argument.
