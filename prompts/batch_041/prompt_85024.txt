ok, I need a consistency check on my take on grounding before a reading group tears me apart. By grounding I just mean the in-virtue-of relation -- the thing holding between a fact and whatever makes it the case, like a whole holding in virtue of its parts. Here's the package:

1. Grounding is a real, objective relation out there in the world, not just a projection of our explanatory habits. When I say the whole is grounded in its parts I'm tracking a joint in nature.
2. Grounding is the worldly correlate of metaphysical explanation -- "A grounds B" just is "B obtains in virtue of A," no gap between the relation and the explaining.
3. Necessitarianism: if A grounds B, then necessarily, if A obtains then B obtains. Grounds necessitate what they ground.
4. Grounding is transitive: if A grounds B and B grounds C, then A grounds C. I treat this as non-negociable; it's what makes grounding a genuine ordering.
5. Explanation, on the other hand, isn't transitive. Standard cases where A explains B and B explains C but A doesn't explain C -- the relation just doesn't carry across chains.
6. There's no fundamental level. Whatever's grounded is grounded in something that's itself grounded; the regress never bottoms out in ungrounded facts.
7. But a real metaphysical explanation has to be well-founded. An infinite descending chain of "in virtue of" doesn't explain anything, it just defers the question forever -- if nothing is ungrounded, nothing has actually been explained.
8. And most of the things that do the grounding are contingent anyway -- this table's being made of these atoms, this set's having these members.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each real clash? Rank them by severity, tell me for each one the cheapest fix and what it costs me, then tell me the single thing to drop if I want the fewest repairs. Please don't tell me they're all fine, or that it's just "different notions of grounding" -- I want to know where the actual friction is.
