I would like to know whether the following has a name, and if it does not, I would like a name with a definition sharp enough to have edges.

Two women stop being members of a church. The first lives where the church is financed by a surcharge on the income tax, collected through the payroll, so that membership is a fact entered in a government register. She was made a member as an infant, by a baptism that was reported onward; she becomes a non-member only by presenting herself at a government office, signing a declaration, and in some places paying a fee, after which the change has a date, an official, and a receipt. The second lives where no such register exists. She was received into membership by a vote of a congregation, and at some point she stopped attending; her name sits on a roll that neither she nor anyone else has examined in a decade. If you asked her when she left, she could not tell you. In the first case the act required of a person is an act of leaving; in the second, the act required is an act of joining, and leaving is not an act at all.

The point I am trying to isolate is not that the state is entangled with religion, and not that a tax gives people a financial motive to depart. It is that the machinery of record-keeping settles what kind of thing membership is. Where affiliation is a legal status, belonging is a discrete condition that one occupies and then vacates; where it is a practice, belonging is a matter of degree that one fades out of. Three consequences follow, and I cannot tell which of them are real and which are my own invention.

First, the official statistics of such a country measure the register rather than the religion. Because the surcharge makes departure financially worthwhile for some who still believe and merely tedious for others who do not, the count is wrong in both directions at once, and there is no way to correct it from the inside.

Second, because exit is a dated act rather than a slow fade, it becomes available as a gesture: performable in a bad year, after a scandal, or out of irritation. The decline therefore arrives in visible waves rather than as a slope, and each wave is legible as a cause.

Third, and this is the part I most want a word for, the institution's revenue depends on the register, so the institution acquires an interest in the register's categories and will defend a form of membership it has every reason to believe is hollow. The hollow category is not a failure of the arrangement; it is the arrangement's product.

I am aware of the usual arguments in this neighbourhood, and I do not think they are what I am describing. The usual ones concern how much religion there is: whether an official church makes a country more or less observant, whether competition among churches raises or lowers participation, whether falling attendance means falling belief. Those are questions of quantity, or of who is winning. Mine concerns the form the affiliation takes. I have a half-memory of a distinction between a church that encompasses a whole population by birth and a body that consists of the voluntarily converted, but that seems to be about a congregation's own stance toward the world rather than about who keeps the list, and I cannot tell whether it is the same idea in different clothing.

What I am asking for is not the nearest label but the carving. If an established concept fits, I want to know which one, how it is defined, and where it diverges from what I have described. In particular: does the phenomenon belong to the state, or merely to the keeping of a list? A congregation with a strict covenant and a formal roll might exhibit some of the same effects with no state involved at all. Does a state that recognises a church without funding it or recording its members produce any of this? Does it extend to union dues check-off, party cards, and professional licences, and if so, is the religious case merely an instance of something more general about statuses with exits and practices with fades? If nothing established fits, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I can say what falls outside: what would count as a clear case, what as a case that merely resembles one, and what would have to be true of a country for the phenomenon to be absent.
