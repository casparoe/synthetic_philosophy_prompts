I'm in a legal theory seminar that keeps circling back to Hart and Devlin, and the lecturer has a favourite picture for Devlin's side of it: a society's shared morality is all one piece, a single fabric. Pull one thread — let a bit of private immorality go unpunished — and the weave starts to come apart, and eventually there's no society left for the law to protect. On that picture the law is a kind of loom-keeper: it punishes immorality the way it punishes subversion, not because anyone was harmed but because the cloth has to hold.

I want to know how far that picture actually holds. Start with the correspondence table — what's supposed to map onto the fabric, the threads, the weave, the tear, the loom, the weaver — then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some breaks seem obvious to me (cloth has no majority opinion, it doesn't argue back, it can change its weave and still be the same cloth, nobody ever says how big a tear is too big) but I don't trust my own list, so tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for what Devlin needs the picture to do and which are just decoration.

Two things I really care about. First, the picture seems to need a threshold, some amount of pulling that unravels the whole thing, and the argument never says where it is — is it any good without one? Second, does the picture quietly smuggle in that morality is homogeneous and that deviation spreads mechanically, and if we drop those, does the conclusion (criminalise the private immorality) survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? The whole thing started over the Wolfenden report on homosexuality and prostitution, and the predicted unravelling didn't happen — does that refute the picture or just one empirical premise attached to it?

Also: someone in the room says the fabric picture is really doing expressive work rather than causal work, that the law's job is to declare what the cloth is rather than stop it coming apart. Does the analogy support that reading better than the one the argument needs? And if the picture is bad, what's a better one — morality as a language, as a market, as an ecosystem — and does the better picture flip the conclusion? No reading lists please, I just want the argument audited.
