Last week a friend and I spent an evening arguing about Friedman's essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics" — the one where he claims that the realism of a theory's assumptions is beside the point and only the accuracy of its predictions matters, the view Samuelson later nicknamed the F-twist. The argument deadlocked over a distinction we improvised and then could not agree on: between assumptions that are load-bearing, in the sense that the result depends on their being false and would dissolve if they were relaxed, and assumptions that are mere scaffolding, removable without disturbing the conclusion. He holds that the F-twist is blind to this difference and therefore licenses bad model-building; I suspect the difference is real but that it does no work where he thinks it does. Please lay out the most important implications of the F-twist for how these two kinds of false assumption should be treated — in particular, what follows for when "but the assumptions are false" is a legitimate objection to a model, and what follows for the larger question of whether predictive success gives us any reason to think a theory is true of the world. If the F-twist cannot draw the distinction my friend wants, say so, and say precisely what that costs it.
