I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy and I've somehow ended up leading next week's reading group session on incommensurability and hard choices, and I'm realising I definately don't have the view straight. The problem is my flatmate, who's a lawyer and has no patience for any of this, keeps asking me questions I can't answer. His line goes: if two things are incommensurable — like taking the job that means moving abroad versus staying near my mum while she's ill, or the usual career-versus-kids thing — then neither is better than the other, right? And if neither is better, there's no reason to pick one over the other, so it's a coin flip, and anyone who agonises over it for weeks is just being sentimental. He reckons the whole 'hard choice' literature is people dressing up the fact that they can't have everything. I keep feeling this has to be wrong, but whenever I try to say why I just end up repeating 'but they're not comparable on a common scale', which is the very thing he's already saying.

What's thrown me is that someone in my seminar said something that sounded like the opposite: that a hard choice isn't one where reason runs out but one where you have to make a call, and in some sense make the reason yourself by committing to one option rather than the other — and that a comparison only ever gets made relative to some 'covering' consideration, some respect under which the two options are being compared at all. If that's right, then 'they can't be compared' can't mean 'there's nothing to think about'. But I can't tell whether that's a real position or just a way of rescuing the vocabulary, and I'm not sure how it's supposed to square with the idea that the values really are incommensurable rather than just very close together.

So how do I actually answer him? I don't want the two-minute version where incommensurability just means 'really difficult'. I want to know what work the term is doing, where the line falls between incommensurability and plain incomparability, what if anything can still count as rational in a hard choice, and what to say to someone who thinks that if there's no common measure then anything goes. And if you can tell me how regret is meant to fit in I'd be grateful — he thinks regret in those cases is obviously irrational, since you took the best option available, and I don't think that's obviously right either.
