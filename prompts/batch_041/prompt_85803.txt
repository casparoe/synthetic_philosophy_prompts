I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I'm drafting the final exam now. The closing unit is the two-envelope paradox, and I have grown tired of reading essays that dispose of it by announcing that the argument presupposes an improper prior over the smaller amount and then stopping; that diagnosis is not wrong, but it has hardened into a canned answer that lets students skip the only part worth testing, namely what work the expected-value calculation is actually doing and when it is entitled to do it. So I have built the question around two variants of my own construction, and I want the canned answer to visibly fail on one of them. I have already had a shouting match about this with a colleague in the economics department, who insists the whole thing is a bookkeeping error that a competent probabilist would never make, and with a doctoral student who thinks the paradox survives every attempt to formalize it; I would like a question that forces a student to take a side and defend it.

Here are the two variants, exactly as I want them stated to the students. The capped ladder: the host chooses an integer n uniformly from 1 through 20, places 2^n dollars in one envelope and 2^(n+1) dollars in the other, and hands you one of the two at random. The endless ladder: the host draws n from a geometric distribution with parameter p, so that the probability that n equals k is (1 − p)^k times p for k = 0, 1, 2, …, and again places 2^n dollars and 2^(n+1) dollars in the envelopes and hands you one at random. In both variants you open your envelope, see an amount, and are permitted one swap. I have worked out the following and want it treated as given, so that nobody loses the thread on arithmetic: in the capped version the posterior probability that you hold the smaller envelope is exactly one half for every amount you could possibly see except the two ends of the range; in the endless version that posterior probability is constant in the observed amount for every amount except the very smallest one; and the endless family turns over at some critical value of p, which I suspect is also the value at which the expected number of dollars in your own envelope stops being finite, though I want the students to establish that rather than take my word for it.

Please write me a single essay question, suitable as one of three on a three-hour exam, with the following parts: first, the student must determine the optimal swapping rule in the capped version and say precisely where and why that rule falls short of always swapping; second, the student must determine the optimal rule in the endless version as a function of p, including what happens at the critical value; third, the student must explain why the critical value in the second part coincides with the value at which the expected amount in your own envelope ceases to be finite, and what that coincidence shows about the standard improper-prior diagnosis, in particular whether a proper prior can reproduce the paradox; and fourth, the student must say what all of this implies about the principle of indifference as it is used in the original argument and about whether swapping in every state of information can be a coherent rule at all. Give me a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually performs the derivations rather than gesturing at them, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain, with the marks attached to each, and the specific errors that should cost marks — I especially want to catch the student who writes improper prior and stops, the student who believes that merely bounding the amounts above is what rescues the argument, and the student who treats the two variants as though they raised the same problem. Write everything in American English.
