**Predictability is a relation, not a property: three grades of unpredictability in deterministic systems**

1. *The received view.* Since chaos theory reached the philosophy of science, the standard lesson has been that determinism does not entail predictability. The usual gloss is epistemic: chaotic systems have positive Lyapunov exponents, so errors in our knowledge of the initial state grow exponentially and the horizon of useful prediction shrinks to nothing — but nothing in the world is thereby undetermined, and a Laplacean demon with exact initial data and unbounded computational power would forecast everything perfectly. On this account unpredictability is a fact about us and not about the world, and the philosophical work consists in keeping the two apart.

2. I want to argue that this gloss is true but incomplete, and that the incompleteness matters. There are at least three grades of unpredictability, and the received view runs the first two together.

 (1) *Precision-limited unpredictability.* What the Lyapunov exponent measures: the rate at which an uncertainty about the present is converted into an uncertainty about the future. It is relative to the resolution of the predictor and it vanishes as that resolution improves.

 (2) *Structural unpredictability.* Unpredictability that survives perfect data and unlimited computing power. Three routes lead to it: (i) dynamics that are not computable at all, so that no algorithm generates the future from the present; (ii) dynamics that are computable but computationally irreducible, so that the cheapest way to obtain the system's future is to let the system run, and no predictor can be reliably ahead of what it predicts; (iii) self-reference — a predictor that is itself part of the system whose future is at issue cannot in general return a complete prediction of that system's future, because the prediction would have to contain a representation of the prediction.

 (3) *Level-relative unpredictability.* In chaotic systems it is individual trajectories that are unpredictable while statistical summaries are robust: a numerically computed trajectory, though not the true one, tracks some true trajectory of the system, so the invariant measure, the attractor and coarse-grained averages are preserved. What is lost is the particular history, not the pattern.

3. The claim I want to press is that unpredictability, in the grade that matters, is not a property of a system but a relation between a system and a predictor — more precisely, a relation between the predictor's information-processing resources, including its place in the system, and the rate at which the system generates distinctions. It follows that the question whether the world is predictable is ill posed until one says who is predicting, and from where. The Laplacean demon is not a limiting case of an observer but an incoherent idealisation: it must stand outside the world it predicts and run ahead of that world's own dynamics. Route (iii) shows that the second condition fails for any predictor inside the world.

4. *A thought experiment to fix the idea.* Consider a deterministic universe U containing a machine M that prints a complete description of U's future, with one exception: the description omits whatever depends on M's own output. Nothing about M is obviously impossible; given enough of U's resources it can be built to simulate U's dynamics. But a stable residual remains: the future of M, and of everything causally downstream of M's printout, is not contained in the printout, and any further machine added to close the gap generates a fresh gap of the same kind. Call this the ledger regress. If it holds, a deterministic universe can contain facts about its own future that no inhabitant can have in advance — not because of measurement error, and not because anything is undetermined, but because of the structure of prediction from within.

5. *Pay-off.* The familiar argument that determinism would make deliberation pointless — the future is already fixed, hence already knowable, hence deliberating is a charade — trades on an equivocation between grade (1) and grade (2). What would embarrass deliberation is knowability in advance by the deliberator. Determinism at most offers knowability by an ideal outsider, and I have argued that the ideal outsider is not a coherent posit. The thesis worth defending is therefore not the trivial one that determinism does not entail predictability, but an indexical one: predictability is always predictability-for-a-predictor, and every predictor sits inside some system that outruns it.

6. *Where I am least sure.* (a) The step from (iii) to §4 may be too quick: a machine can predict a great deal about itself without predicting its own prediction, so impossibility attaches only to total self-prediction, and the ledger regress shows only that no single machine does the whole job — a second machine might recover the residual facts afterwards. The phrase "in advance" needs a clock, and the clock is the problem. (b) The phrase "in principle" carries too much weight: given unlimited time, the demon's forecast does arrive, but too late to be a forecast. Whether that is a genuine limit on prediction or a pun on "in principle" is, I think, the crux of the whole essay.
