Last month I covered a small workshop on the metaphysics of causation, and a twenty-minute exchange near the end has been nagging at me since. I would like you to referee it, point by point, in the manner of a careful commentator.

The case under discussion was this. A lamp is wired to two switches in parallel, so that closing either switch on its own is enough to light it. Both switches are closed at once and the lamp lights. The question was whether the closing of the first switch caused the lamp to light.

One of the panellists — call him the difference-maker — said no. Causation, he said, is a matter of counterfactual dependence: an event causes an outcome only if, had the event not occurred, the outcome would not have occurred. Here, had the first switch not closed, the second would still have closed and the lamp would still have lit. So the first switch's closing is a condition of the lighting but not a cause of it. He added that this is not an embarrassment but the whole point: causes are the differences that make a difference, and where the outcome would have happened anyway, no difference was made.

The other — call her the interventionist — said that criterion is a recipe for deleting causes. The right test, she said, is not what would have happened had the candidate not occurred, holding everything else at its actual value, but whether there is some well-defined way of changing the candidate — an intervention — under which the outcome changes, with the remaining variables held at some values or other, not necessarily their actual ones. In this case, hold the second switch open and intervene on the first: the lamp's behaviour changes. So the first switch's closing is a cause of the lighting after all. The reason the difference-maker's test finds nothing, she said, is that he has quietly frozen the second switch at closed, and that choice, not the metaphysics, is what makes the first switch look inert.

He replied that this lets in too much. If the background may be set to anything, then not only the switch but the lamp's being plugged in, the wiring's being intact, and the bulb's not being burnt out are all causes of the lighting, because for each of them there is some background under which changing it changes the outcome. A theory that cannot separate the striking of the match from the presence of oxygen has stopped distinguishing causes from mere conditions.

She answered that the distinction he wants is real but is a matter of degree rather than of kind, and that her framework can capture it: causes are difference-makers relative to a specified contrast and a specified background, and the striking of the match differs from the presence of oxygen in being invariant — it goes on making a difference across a much wider range of interventions and background conditions. That, she said, is a principled line, whereas his account simply fails on the case in front of them: it implies that neither switch caused the light, which is absurd, and the repairs he then reaches for — chains of counterfactual dependence running through intermediate events — do not work here, because there is no intermediate event on the first switch's path whose occurrence the lighting depends on.

He came back that invariance is no escape, since it is itself defined in terms of what would happen under interventions, so she is grounding causation in counterfactuals and then claiming to have replaced them. He also said his chain-based repair is not a fudge but the standard treatment of cases of this shape.

I have no stake in who wins. What I want from you is an adjudication of the exchange as it actually went: which of the moves above are sound, which are mistaken, and where the two of them are talking past each other. In particular, I want to know whether the difference-maker's original verdict on the two-switch case can be defended, whether the interventionist's reply genuinely rescues the first switch or merely re-describes the case, and whether the proliferation objection has real force or is answerable. Where a party is right only under a qualification, state the qualification. And tell me what both of them are leaving out.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your verdict, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
