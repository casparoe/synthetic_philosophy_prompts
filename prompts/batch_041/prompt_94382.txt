I am writing a position statement on the St. Petersburg paradox for a graduate seminar on rational choice, and I would like an audit of it before I circulate anything. The position compresses into seven commitments.

(1) The correct normative standard for choice under risk is expected utility maximization, the expectation taken over a real-valued utility function. I accept the standard sure-thing/independence principle for lotteries, and I take an ideally rational agent's preferences to be complete and transitive. Expected utility theory is the standard for ideal agents, not a useful approximation to something else.

(2) For the St. Petersburg game with a fair coin, the expected monetary payoff really is infinite. The divergence is not an artifact of careless summation.

(3) The verdict of the correct theory is that refusing to pay more than a modest sum — I put the figure at about $25 — is rational. The refusal is not a limitation of my psychology, a bound on my computing power, or a failure of nerve; it is what the theory delivers, and I take it to be a fact about rationality rather than a report of my particular tastes.

(4) The familiar appeal to the diminishing marginal utility of money is not the real explanation of (3). It succeeds only because dollar payoffs happen to grow more slowly than the relevant probabilities decay; describe the same game with the stakes denominated directly in utility and the paradox returns unchanged.

(5) The repair is to require the utility function to be bounded. With bounded utility the game's expected utility is finite, which gives (3) the shape it should have — some finite ceiling on what it is rational to pay rather than none at all. The same constraint also disposes of the alternating variant, in which the payoff on the nth toss is 2^n/n, won when n is odd and lost when n is even, and whose expectation is not even well defined, the series being only conditionally convergent.

(6) The infinity does not strike me as robust to the coin: if the coin is biased so that heads is more likely than tails, the expected payoff becomes finite. The fair coin is the extremal case in which the game still has infinite expected value, and my sense is that the paradox is a phenomenon of that limiting case rather than of the game as such.

(7) Decision theory is about ideal agents, so I will not be moved by the objection that the game cannot actually be played — that no coin can be tossed arbitrarily many times, that no casino has unbounded reserves.

What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial. Go through the commitments in pairs and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look that way; I would rather have the real conflicts identified sharply than every pair annotated. For each pair you report on, say what the conflict consists in — the shared assumption that cannot be kept, or the step that fails — how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most, and whether any commitment is shaky on its own terms rather than only in combination with the others. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the argument.

Give the audit as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "pairs": an array, ordered by severity, most severe first, with apparent tensions last. Each entry is an object with "commitments" (an array of the commitment numbers involved), "verdict" (either "genuine" or "apparent"), "severity" (an integer from 1 to 5, where 5 means one of the pair has to go), "conflict" (a few sentences naming the shared assumption or the failing step), "cheapest_revision" (what would have to give, stated as a change to a numbered commitment), and "cost" (what that revision costs elsewhere in the set).
