I'm putting together a grad seminar on the free energy principle and I keep getting the same fight every week: half the room thinks it's the next great unification, the other half thinks it's the kind of framework that can't be wrong because it can't be tested. Before we start scoring camps I want the spec on the table. So write me the desiderata.

What does a theory of this kind actually have to deliver to count as an adequate unified theory of brains and behavior — and, if it's claiming them too, of life and self-organization more generally? I want the full list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results it can't contradict, with the case or consideration sitting behind each requirement. I'm not asking for a survey of what people have said; I want your list.

Some things I want either on the list or explicitly argued off it, and I want you to take a side on each:

- the dark room problem and its relatives. If a system can drive prediction error down by doing nothing at all, what exactly does the theory owe us? Is that a bug, a feature, or a non-question?
- the triviality worry. A colleague in my department insists active inference is just control theory and Bayesian filtering with a new vocabulary. Is it a genuine desideratum that the theory be more than a re-description of existing engineering, or is that just a demand for novelty dressed up as rigor?
- Markov blankets. Does the theory have to tell us what's real about them, or is it allowed to be a modelling convenience all the way down? I think this is where the whole thing lives or dies, so don't punt.
- scope. Does it have to cover a candle flame, a slime mold, a bacterium, and a person in a coma? What does each extra case cost the theory, and what does dropping them cost?
- the technical bookkeeping. Does it owe an account of why the quantity being minimized is a bound rather than the thing itself, and of which quantity is actually the target when a system acts rather than just perceives?
- falsifiability. Does it have to say what would count as evidence against it? And is that even the right demand to make of a framework rather than a theory — I genuinely don't know, so decide for me.
- experience. Does it owe us anything about the phenomenal side, or is that legitimately outside its brief?

Then, and this is the part I actually want: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, flag any pair of them that I can't have together (I'd bet money there's at least one), and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the main contenders — predictive processing as usually stated, the free energy principle itself, and whatever you take to be the strongest minimal alternative.

No citations, no reading list, I know the literature. I want your argument. And be committed: take positions, don't lay out options for me to pick from. I'd much rather you be definite and wrong in places than balanced and useless. American English, and keep it tight.
