Write a joke about the value of information.

Build it on the material below, so that you need not rely on any particular paper, author, or notation. In decision theory there is a result, usually called Good's theorem, to the effect that an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates her beliefs by Bayes' rule can never lose expected utility by receiving free information before she acts: she is always free to ignore it and act as though she had not received it. Two features of the result are worth exploiting. First, the guarantee is about the expectation taken before the information arrives; once it has arrived the agent may be worse off in fact than she would have been in ignorance, so that she can be sorry she looked and still have been right, ex ante, to look. Second, the guarantee is conditional, and it lapses if the agent will not in fact update as a Bayesian, if the information reaches her only after she has already acted, or if the information enters her preferences directly, so that knowing is not merely a means to acting well but a good or a bad in its own right.

Write a joke — setup and punchline, at most about 200 words, one concrete scene, no narrator explaining the theory afterwards — whose punchline turns on one of those two features rather than on a generic joke about economists, or about ignorance being bliss, or about fortune-tellers. A reader who knows decision theory should find it funny and should be able to see exactly which feature it turns on; a reader who does not should still be able to follow the scene. Do not presuppose knowledge of any specific author, title, or formalism, and do not let the punchline consist in simply naming the result.

Begin with the joke itself: no preface, no framing sentence, no announcement of what you are about to do. After the joke, add one sentence naming the feature of the result that the punchline turns on.
