The half-idea: everyone treats those three as a menu of stances you can take toward the past, and everyone breezes past the fact that Nietzsche diagnoses a disease for the first two and then, when he gets to critical history, just... stops. Monumental history breeds people who imitate a greatness they can't touch; antiquarian history breeds people who hoard and embalm; and critical history shows up as the thing you do when the past is crushing you and you need to cut yourself loose from it. Where's its pathology? Nobody seems bothered by that. I think the asymmetry is a symptom that the trio isn't a menu of stances at all — that there's some other organizing axis (my guess: what each mode does to the present's capacity to act, or the direction in which it leaves you indebted) and that once you've got the axis you can either derive the missing fourth slot or show that critical history can't have one, because it isn't a mode of history at all but a mode of self-overcoming — which would be a much bigger claim and would change how the whole essay hangs together. There's a second thread I'd want folded in if it fits: the criterion the whole essay runs on is health or life, and Nietzsche never says what that is, so part of the question is whether pathology there is a medical metaphor doing rhetorical work or a real functional claim that could be wrong.
