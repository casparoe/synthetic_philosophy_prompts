ok so i'm in this philosophy club at my high school and our teacher wants us each to write a satirical piece for the end of term zine, and i got assigned (lucky me) the williams thing about "one thought too many." You know the setup: a guy has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger from drowning, and he saves his wife, and the impartialist says fine, thats allowed, BUT he has to have the thought that "she's my wife" as his justification/reason for doing it. And williams says even needing that thought is already one thought too many — that if you need a general reason to prefer your own wife you've already got a kind of alienated, soulless picture of what morality is, and it wrecks the idea of integrity, because the whole point of your deep commitments is that they're just yours, not that they pass some impartial test. (At least thats my understanding, correct me if i've mangled it.)

Anyway can you write the piece for me. I want it to be a mock advice column in a glossy lifestyle magazine — a reader writes in with a dilemma and the columnist answers. The columnist should be some kind of "moral efficiency consultant" / life-coach type who sells a service auditing how many thoughts you're spending on your loved ones, and who has fully swallowed the impartialist demand without noticing anything is wrong. Deadpan, relentlessly upbeat, no winking. Around 500-600 words, letter plus reply, maybe a headline.

The jokes need to come from getting the actual structure right, not just "haha utilitarians are cold." Like: the difference between having the thought and needing it as a justification; whether the objection is really to impartialism or to a picture of moral thought where everything has to be licensed by a reason; the integrity point about your projects being *yours* and not just one item among many. A philosophy grad student should read it and think "yeah, ok, they get it," not wince. And it should still be funny to my mom who has never heard of any of this.

One more thing, cause the teacher will ask: at the end, after the column, add a short note (like 100 words) where you take a definite position on what williams's objection is actually targeting — a committed reading, not "some interpret it this way, others that way" — and say where the satire leans on a contested reading or exaggerates for the joke. Pick a side and stick to it.
