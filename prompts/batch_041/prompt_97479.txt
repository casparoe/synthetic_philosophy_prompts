I am a junior at a mid-sized public high school, and I run the philosophy club, which meets once a week after school. We usually get fourteen to sixteen students, ages fifteen to eighteen, and a session runs about an hour. I have been given one session to lead on a topic of my own choosing, and I want to build it around an idea I have been trying to work out: the thought that the good and bad things that happen to a person are deserved, because they are the results of that person's own past actions.

What I want is not a lecture. I want an hour-long activity in which the members of the club, simply by playing it, run into the strangeness of that idea themselves, and only learn at the end what the idea is called and who has defended it. The feature I find most striking, and the one an abstract explanation would hide from them, is that the idea works as a ledger. Whatever a person suffers, the ledger has already recorded a reason, so the suffering is deserved and no one owes them anything; whatever a person enjoys, they have earned. The activity should let the group feel the pull of that ledger — how satisfying it is, how it seems to explain everything — and then let them feel what it costs.

I have a rough picture of how it might go, and you may keep, change, or discard it. Early in the session, before any framing, members take part in a short game that hands out unequal circumstances — some get advantages, some get burdens — through some mixture of their own choices and plain luck. Then comes the turn: they are told that their circumstances were actually settled in an earlier round of the game, a round that has already happened and that none of them can remember, and that whatever they are holding now is exactly what they earned then. They should not be told what to make of this; they should simply have to live with it for the rest of the session, while the game continues and they keep making choices under it. I want the moment when someone who is badly off is told it is their own doing, and cannot prove otherwise, to land on the group before anyone has heard the word for what they are doing.

Please design the game itself: the rules, the setup and materials, how circumstances and stakes are handed out, the length of the rounds, how the earlier round is revealed, and how the remaining time is spent. The stakes should not be real money. Keep it runnable in an ordinary classroom with nothing more exotic than index cards or a whiteboard, and make the rules simple enough that I can explain them in two or three minutes.

Then give me the debrief, which is the part I care most about. I want the group to end up seeing a specific set of difficulties, and I want you to show me how to get them there from what actually happened in the game.

First, that the idea seems to answer the problem of undeserved suffering by denying that any suffering is undeserved, and that this is exactly why it is attractive and also why it is dangerous: it can be turned into a reason to be indifferent to the suffering of others, since they are only getting what the ledger says they owe.

Second, that the idea rests on something the players themselves will have experienced — the earlier round cannot be checked, and neither can its connection to the present. Ask the group what evidence would count against the ledger, and let them discover that they cannot name any. I want them to feel the difference between a claim that is merely hard to test and a claim that is arranged so that nothing could test it.

Third, and this is the part I most want the activity to reach, that two very different ideas are hiding under the same name, and the game should put the group in a position to see the difference. On one reading, the ledger is a matter of desert: someone is keeping account, the person who suffers has it coming, and so the suffering is just. On the other reading, there is no account-keeper and no debt at all — actions simply produce later conditions the way one event causes another, and the point of the idea is not to justify what happens but to describe the machinery that keeps a person trapped in it, so that the machinery can be escaped. I want the group to see that the first reading is what the game has been tempting them into, that the second reading is not a softer version of the first but a rival to it, and that some of the traditions which use this idea reject the first reading on the grounds that there is no permanent self for a debt to attach to. The game will not be able to show them that last point on its own, so tell me plainly what I will have to supply in the debrief, and how to phrase it without turning the debrief back into a lecture.

Finally, include two more things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do when it does; I am especially worried that the group will treat the whole thing as a joke once they work out that the earlier round was invented, and that the person who ends up badly off will feel personally attacked rather than philosophically provoked. And tell me which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to say out loud and what I should not pretend the game accomplished.
