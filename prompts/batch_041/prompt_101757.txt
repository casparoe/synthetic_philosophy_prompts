The question asked students to compare negative, positive, and republican liberty using a scenario I made up. Here it is:

Consider the case of Nissa. Nissa lives under an AI administrator that was installed by a tech company after a referendum. The administrator has never once blocked, coerced, or punished Nissa. It has no desire to. It only adjusts traffic lights, tax forms, and the order of options in her banking app in ways that, as a matter of fact, she would have chosen anyway. But the administrator could override any of her decisions at any moment, and everyone in the city knows this. It also designs the menu of options she sees, though it never removes an option outright. How would a theorist of negative liberty, a theorist of positive liberty, and a theorist of republican liberty each assess Nissa's freedom? In your answer, address whether republican liberty is best understood as a version of negative liberty or as a distinct third concept, and explain what work the idea of domination is doing. Finally, say whether the AI's non-interference is sufficient for freedom on each view.

Student answer:

Negative liberty is the absence of interference. Since the AI never blocks Nissa or punishes her, her negative liberty is intact; the fact that it could interfere is irrelevant, because negative liberty tracks actual interference, not the mere possibility of it. Positive liberty is about self-mastery, being governed by your true or rational self rather than by external forces. In practice this means positive liberty is about collective self-government, since the true self is realized through the political community. Berlin actually thought this was the richer and more attractive ideal, even though he worried it could be perverted. Republican liberty, on Pettit's account, is non-domination. It differs from negative liberty because it is not enough that no one interferes; you must also be free from the arbitrary power of others. So the AI is a problem for republicans even though it never interferes, because it has the capacity to override Nissa arbitrarily. Still, I think republican liberty ends up being a version of negative liberty with an extra psychological condition: you are unfree if you have to live in fear of interference. Pettit's contribution is basically to add that insecurity to the negative-liberty story. On that reading, Nissa is unfree on the republican view because she knows the AI could override her, while she is free on the negative view because it never does. The positive view would say she is unfree because she is not participating in collective self-rule.
