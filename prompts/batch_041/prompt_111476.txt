I'm a third-year PhD student working on population ethics and the aggregation of small goods and harms — my advisor keeps telling me to pick a lane, but here we are — and I've got two people in my reading group who have been fighting for about a year and a half now, in seminar, over email, once at a conference bar at 1am, and it's stopped being productive. One is a total-utility true believer who thinks the only real problem with trading one person's fifty years of agony against an astronomical pile of eye-specks is that you'd run out of specks; the other thinks some harms are just not the kind of thing that any number of trivial harms can outweigh, full stop, no matter how you do the arithmetic. I want to make them write the document they should have written instead of arguing: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both of them would genuinely put their names to. So: a numbered list of agreed claims first (what aggregation is even claiming, what each side concedes about the other's view, what the numbers do and don't settle). Then a disagreements section — a table is fine — with each residual disagreement stated in wording that its own side would accept as a fair statement of their position, no strawmen, no "so what you're really saying is". Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would force at least one of them to move. For each crux, name the argument or case they both agree actually bears on it, and record what each side predicts about it in advance, before anyone runs it. And if part of this fight turns out to be about words rather than substance — I have a suspicion about "better than" and about "harm" quietly doing two different jobs — say which part and why.

Some things I want them forced to deal with, because these are the spots where I think the usual back-and-forth goes soft. First, the original case is one person's agony against a colossal number of *other* people's specks; I want each side to say what it does when you move the specks inside a single life — same person, one speck a day, for as many days as it takes to reach the same total — since that seems to strip out the separateness-of-persons move the anti-aggregationist leans on, and I want to see whether they both agree it does. Second, a version where the agony is fantastically improbable instead of certain (a one-in-a-power-tower lottery, everyone else still gets their speck for sure), because I genuinely can't tell whether their disagreement is about aggregation or about risk, and neither can they. Third, I'll hand them a little ladder I built myself: a chain of cases where each rung is only a hair different from the one below it — slightly worse harm, slightly fewer people — starting at something both agree is completely fine and ending at something both agree is monstrous, so the bottom and top rungs *are* the original two options and no single step looks like a big deal. Are they willing to say "better" at every step, or does one of them have to break the chain somewhere? If so, exactly which rung, and what makes that rung special rather than just the first one they couldn't stomach? That last one is what I actually care about, so I want the rung-by-rung commitments written out, with each side's prediction for what their own view implies at each step.

Don't worry about citing literature or naming who said what — we all know the papers and half the point is to get something neither of them could have gotten by quoting. Just make it a document that a reader who disagrees with both of them would still call fair, ending with a short list of things each side now agrees would count against its own position. Roughly 1200-1800 words, and keep the tone like a working paper, not a peace treaty. (Also, if it turns out they can't actually sign the same document, tell me that instead of fudging it — that would be a more interesting result anyway.)
