I belong to a small reading group that has spent the past term working through the special composition question — the question of what conditions must obtain for some things to compose a further thing — and we keep colliding with a slogan that I suspect is carrying far more weight than it can bear. At our last meeting a colleague reasoned roughly as follows: a whole is greater than the sum of its parts; therefore a composite object is a genuine entity over and above its components; therefore any view on which, say, a table is nothing over and above its atoms (or some simples arranged in a table-like way) stands refuted. I wanted to object that the slogan had been smuggled in to do the real work, but I couldn't put my finger on where the sleight of hand lay. Matters got worse when a second member pressed the very same slogan into service on the opposite flank, arguing that it showed a bronze statue to be something distinct from the lump of bronze that constitutes it, and a third proposed reading "greater than the sum" as a claim about explanatory priority — that facts about wholes cannot be derived from facts about parts and their arrangement — which she took to settle the composition question against the deflationist outright. I should like a disambiguation I can take back to the group.

Please take the sentence 'a whole is greater than the sum of its parts' and lay out, as a menu rather than a single preferred interpretation, the distinct precise claims it could be making: which are trivial or close to analytic, which are substantive and defensible, which are dubious or plainly false, and — for each — whether it would actually deliver my colleague's conclusion. I am especially interested in two matters. First, whether any reading of the slogan can do genuine work on the special composition question itself, or whether the slogan only ever speaks to the separate and easier question of emergence, so that its appearance in our debate is a change of subject. Second, whether the slogan covertly presupposes that composition occurs at all, in which case using it to argue that composites are real would beg the question. Please also comment on the hidden labour done by its talk of a 'sum' (is a sum a further object, or merely the parts taken together?) and of being 'greater' (greater in what respect — mass or size, causal power, explanatory irreducibility, or sheer existence?), and say which reading you think people who repeat the slogan generally have in mind.

I would rather you commit to definite verdicts — telling me which readings you would yourself defend and which you would reject, and giving your reasons — than merely catalogue the options and leave me to choose between them. Please write in British English.
