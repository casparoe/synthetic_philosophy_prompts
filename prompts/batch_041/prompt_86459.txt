I'm a science journalist — I write a weekly column about minds and machines — and I've somehow agreed to be the opening speaker at a two-day workshop on AI and the mind next month. The room is about half philosophers of mind (a couple of Dennett people who'll tell me I'm doing a bad cover version of the intentional stance, plus two who think content is fixed by causal contact with the world) and half ML people — evals engineers, a couple of interpretability researchers — who think all belief-talk about models is anthropomorphic noise. And one animal-cognition person who's spent a career on bees and keeps saying we went thru all this already.

Here's the argument I'm putting on the table, and I want you to prep me for the Q&A. My line is that the fight over whether LLMs "have beliefs" is really two fights that got fused together. There's a content sense: the output carries a content, you can say what it's about, you can call it true or false the way you can call a sentence true or false. And there's a commitment sense: the system is on the hook for the content — it'll stick up for it, drop it when the evidence turns, notice when it contradicts something else it said, be the kind of thing for which the content is a liability. Models have the first and not the second, I say, and nearly everything people actually care about — lying, hallucinating, being honest, revising, "the model thinks X" in any sense that matters — needs the second. So "the model believes X" should be cashed out as "the model's output carries the content X," full stop. Most of the shouting in this area is people sliding from one sense to the other mid-sentence, and the slide is nearly always in the direction of commitment.
