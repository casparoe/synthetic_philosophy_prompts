I write a monthly column on decision theory and rational choice, and I keep dragging readers back to the old Death in Damascus setup — the one where Death tells you he's coming for you tomorrow, you bolt for the next city, and it turns out he knew you'd bolt, so running is exactly what did you in; pick either way and the pick is the fatal one. A friend who teaches this stuff says the juicy part isnt the paradox itself, it's what the formal theories do with it: causal decision theory can't land on a stable choice (every option, once you've actually taken it, turns out to look like the wrong one), while evidential decision theory just shrugs in the textbook version, because both options are certain death and so there's nothing to choose between them. I want to run a cleaned-up version in the column, but the textbook case is too symmetric and too thin to show readers anything. So build me a variant to spec — and actually run the machinery rather than just asserting the answers.

Specs: keep the flavor but lose the personified all-knowing Death. The "predictor" has to be an ordinary mechanism — a well-validated statistical model, or at most a very shrewd human — and you state its reliability explicitly. Real places, real hazards, no magic, and the whole scenario in one paragraph. The design goal is that the two theories come apart not by recommending different actions but by one of them failing to give an answer at all: causal decision theory should end up with no self-ratifying choice (and say whether bolting on a mixed strategy really rescues it or is just an ad-hoc patch), while evidential decision theory should land firmly on one option. To get that second part you'll probably have to break the symmetry — give me a genuine preference about where I die, say, rather than flat indifference.

After the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, name the single feature of the case that's doing all the work, say what happens if you remove it, and then tell me how a defender of whichever theory comes out worse would try to argue the case is rigged. No need for citations or further reading — I just want the thing worked out on the page.
