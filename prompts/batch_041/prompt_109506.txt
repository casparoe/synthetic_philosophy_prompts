You are a behavioral ecologist who has spent a career studying animal communication and who is sometimes asked to write speeches for academic occasions. I need you to draft an address that I will deliver, and I want you to think carefully before you write: the intellectual problem at the center of this address is real, and the room will contain people who will notice if I smooth it over.

The occasion. Ines, my friend and colleague of thirty years, died in March, and I have been asked to give the memorial address at a gathering next month. She was a behavioral ecologist herself. What almost no one knew until her executor opened the file was that for the last three decades she had been paying the tuition of students who could not otherwise have afforded it: through a lawyer, with no name attached, the recipients told only that a private donor had chosen them. Several of those students are now scientists and will be in the room. So will her brother and his family, who are religious and who expect a memorial address to sound like one. So will a dozen colleagues who know the literature on costly signaling as well as I do.

The problem. The account of generosity that most of us in the field work with runs roughly as follows. Generosity is a signal, and signals are worth attending to only if they are hard to fake. What makes a gift credible is that it costs something the giver cannot easily get back, so that a giver who could not afford the cost could not have sent the message. On this account the audience is essential: the point of the expense is what it demonstrates to someone. Ines's giving, as far as anyone can tell, had no audience at all. There was no reputation to be won, because no one knew; no gratitude to be collected, because the students never learned whom to thank. So either the theory is incomplete, or there was an audience I have not found, or my question is the wrong one. I do not know which, and I am not willing to pretend in front of her family that I do.

Here are some materials you may use, revise, or set aside. First, not every honest signal is a handicap: some are unfakeable because they are physically inseparable from the thing they reveal, the way the pitch of a voice is inseparable from the size of the body that produces it, and some are unfakeable only because they are expensive. The difference matters for what a gift can demonstrate. Second, the receiver of a signal need not be another organism; it is possible to act in order to demonstrate something to one's own later self. Third, anonymity may be not the absence of a cost but an additional one: she gave up the reputation the gifts would have bought, which makes the act more expensive, and therefore, if the logic holds, a stronger signal — but stronger to whom? Fourth, it may be that the theory does not reach this case at all, because a signal with no receiver is not a signal, and some costly acts are not communications but something else.

What I need. First, a planning note of no more than two hundred words, in bullet form if you like, setting out what the address will claim Ines's giving was, what it will refuse to claim, where it will leave the question open, and which of the four materials above you find weakest and why. Second, the address itself, about five minutes when spoken, roughly seven hundred to eight hundred and fifty words.

Constraints. The theory should govern what the address is allowed to claim; it should not be the address's subject, and the delivered text should contain no jargon at all — the biology stays under the surface, and the ideas are carried by concrete detail. Nothing in the address should be a claim a careful behavioral ecologist would have to retract the next day, and nothing should be a comfort the theory in the room cannot support. Do not resolve the difficulty by discovering a hidden vanity in her, and do not explain her giving away by saying what it was really about; she was entitled to her act without our translation of it. The address should be warm to her brother and his family without asserting anything about an afterlife or about her continuing to be present. You may invent small concrete particulars of the sort a memorial needs — a habit, a phrase, a field site, the weather on a particular day — but not events that bear on the ethical question at the center. No scripture, no quotations, no citations, and no reading list; the room is not a seminar and I will not be handing anything out. Write for the ear: short sentences, no piled-up subordinate clauses.
