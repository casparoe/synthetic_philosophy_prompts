I've been going round in circles trying to work out what I actually think about imprecise credences, going purely on gut reaction to cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle -- if there's one -- is actually doing the work. My verdicts, roughly in order of how sure I am of them:

A coin from the Royal Mint that looks and feels normal: 0.5 is simply the right credence. Anyone who says "somewhere between 0 and 1" here is being obtuse, not scrupulous.

A coin fished out of an unlabelled jar at a car boot sale: here I genuinely do think "no idea, could be anything from nearly always heads to nearly always tails" is the honest answer, and that plumping for 0.5 would be a fiction. There's no reference class I can point to, so there's no fact of the matter I'd be guessing at.

Two urns. The first has 50 red and 50 black and I've counted them. The second has 100 balls, each red or black, and I haven't looked. I'd rather bet on red from the first urn. I think that preference is rational and I'm not going to be argued out of it -- it isn't a bias to be trained away, it's tracking something real about the second urn.

Two propositions, P and Q. For P my credence is a range, [0.3, 0.7]. For Q it's exactly 0.5. I'd rather bet on Q, even though P's range contains 0.5 and P might be far better than 0.5. So whatever the range is, it doesn't seem to reduce to "some point inside it, unknown".

Someone told me about a result where you've got a range for X, you then learn something you're quite sure is irrelevant to X, and the range is supposed to widen. That strikes me as a reductio of the formalism, not a surprising theorem. Learning irrelevant things shouldn't make me less sure.

But then, on the other hand: I'll happily pay over the odds for a warranty on an ambiguous risk, and I think that's sensible rather than weak-willed. And if you force me to act right now -- one shot, no hedging, no waiting for more evidence -- I think I should just pick a number inside my range and go, and that the range was never anything over and above my indecision. Which is awkward, because "the range is just book-keeping for indecision" is the only account I've come up with myself, and it seems to make the urn preference irrational, and I don't want that.

A friend of mine says an imprecise credence is just an ordinary probability distribution over probabilities. I don't think that's the same thing at all, and I don't think it gets the boot-sale coin right -- I don't want to say there's a fact about how likely each bias is, that I'm merely uncertain about. But I can't say precisely why that's different from the urn.

So: is there one account of what an imprecise credence *is* that gives me the urn preference as rational, keeps the ranges where I want them (mint coin vs boot-sale coin), refuses to let them dilate on irrelevant news, but also says that when I'm forced to act I collapse to a point? State it as precisely as you can, tell me which clause each of my verdicts is buying, and be honest about which verdict it can't accommodate -- I'd rather drop one than keep a Frankenstein principle. And say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: two people with exactly the same evidence ending up with different ranges, both of which strike me as perfectly fine. If your account can't allow that, tell me, and tell me what it costs.
