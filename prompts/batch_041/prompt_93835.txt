I'm a novelist — three books, none of them mathematical — and through a chain of favors I've agreed to lead a ninety-minute session in a continuing-education series for adults, the sort of room where everyone has read a great deal of history and fiction and no one has touched a probability textbook since school. The theme I've been handed is "how we decide what is equally likely," and what I actually want them to leave with is the principle of indifference together with the shock of Bertrand's paradox: the thought that where we are wholly ignorant we should spread probability evenly, followed by the discovery that "wholly ignorant" can be cashed out in several equally blameless ways that disagree. I am not asking you to explain the material to me; I have read enough to have gotten myself into this, and I can hold the ideas in my head. What I want is a plan for the explanation — the order the pieces have to arrive in and, more importantly, why each depends on the one before it, since I suspect the whole thing collapses if I let the conflicting answers surface before the students have felt the pull of the innocent rule.

For the example that carries the session I have been using, when I try this out on friends, a small scene: a point is dropped at random onto a circular pond, and we ask the chance that it lands nearer the middle than the rim. Dropped evenly over the water, the answer comes out one in four; but if you instead stand at the centre, spin to a random direction and then walk a random distance out, the answer comes out one in two, and both procedures feel like exactly what "at random" ought to mean. Tell me whether that is the right load-bearing example or whether I should reach for something else — and if you keep it, what it must have and what it must not, and why I should not simply fall back on the old business of the random chord in a circle, which every summary of this topic reaches for and which I fear my audience may have half-met already. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, and mark precisely where in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than merely corrected afterwards: I already anticipate that half the room will conclude the paradox proves probability is subjective or arbitrary, that a stubborn few will insist one of the two answers is simply the right one and the rest are mistakes, and that someone will ask whether indifference is just a polite word for guessing.

Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end that would tell me whether the idea has actually landed, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to explain at this level — the measure-theoretic machinery, the invariance arguments, the whole subsequent literature — and how to say so without making the refusal sound like evasion.
