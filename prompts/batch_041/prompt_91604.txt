I spent my working life as an experimental physicist and have come to philosophy only in retirement, so I may not have the vocabulary right; I would rather you correct my terms than defer to them. I am not asking for references, and a reading list would be of no use to me if the concept itself has not first been pinned down. What I want is a correct carving.

I have been working through the difficulty that philosophers of mathematics call Benacerraf's dilemma: roughly, that an account of mathematical truth which treats mathematical sentences as true in the same way as any others sits badly with any credible account of how we could come to know them, while accounts that make the epistemology easy tend to surrender the truth. The dilemma itself I can follow. What I cannot find a name for is a smaller move that recurs inside the debate, and that I suspect is not peculiar to it.

The move, as I see it, has three parts.

First, someone objects to a class of entities on the ground that we have no credible account of how our beliefs about them could be reliably connected to the facts about them, so that the beliefs would be true, if at all, by accident. In the mathematical case the complaint is that nothing in our commerce with the world could explain why our arithmetic should match the arithmetic of objects we never touch.

Second, the defender does not deny the gap. She points out that the same gap opens for something the objector is plainly committed to: the meanings of the words she uses, or the propositions her sentences are supposed to express, or the relation of logical consequence she is relying on in order to argue at all. None of those is something we bump into either.

Third — and this is the part that interests me — the objector does not withdraw. She keeps the argument and narrows it. She explains that the cases are different after all: that numbers are supposed to be independent of us in a way that meanings are not; or that meanings come free with the very capacity to mean anything, whereas numbers would have to be discovered; or that the real trouble was never existence but reference, and that the words of mathematics stand differently from the words of our own theorizing with respect to what they latch onto. A line gets drawn which separates the objector's own commitments from the ones she is attacking, and which was doing no work in the argument until the parity reply appeared.

I have seen the middle step called a companions-in-guilt argument, and that name seems right as far as it goes, but it appears to name only the parity reply, and it is normally offered as a way of defeating an objection outright. What I am describing is the case in which the objection is not defeated but relocated. I am also told that the first step is simply the access problem, and that the third step is nothing but the drawing of a legitimate distinction; I do not know how to tell whether that is so. I have noticed the same shape, as far as I can judge, in arguments about whether moral properties could be the sort of thing we come to know — where the reply is that mathematical properties are equally unlike anything in nature, and the narrowing is that mathematical properties are not supposed to move us to act.

I would like three things. First, tell me whether the three-part trajectory, or the third step on its own, has an established name; if it does, say precisely how it differs from the nearest neighbours I have already mentioned, and from the tu quoque, and from the ordinary and respectable business of distinguishing cases. Second, if it has no name, give it one and define it, with a boundary case on each side: a pattern that resembles it but should not count, and a case you consider borderline, with a ruling and a reason. Third, tell me whether the third step is ever defensible — whether there is a principled way to redraw the line after parity has been conceded, or whether the honest response is always to drop the objection. Work the answer through concrete cases in the philosophy of mathematics; a survey of the literature is not what I am asking for.
