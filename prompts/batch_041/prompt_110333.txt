I'm in my early thirties, and my parents — who built a manufacturing business from nothing over thirty years — have just finished an estate plan with a lawyer. The numbers don't matter for my question, but the gist is that the business and the family money are being placed into a trust so that they can pass to me and then to my children without ever being touched by the inheritance tax, and under our state's rules the trust can stay open more or less indefinitely, with my parents' instructions governing how the money is used long after they're gone. My parents are proud of it and see it as protecting the family. I'm named as a trustee, there are documents I'm supposed to sign, and I keep finding reasons to put it off, because something about the arrangement bothers me in a way I can't articulate.

A close friend of mine, who did a PhD in economics, thinks my hesitation is pure sentiment. Her argument runs roughly like this: nobody earns an inheritance, so I have no legitimate complaint if the state takes a large share of it, and the only genuinely interesting question is whether the tax shrinks the pie, which it probably doesn't much. More pointedly, she says that if fairness to people who start with less is the real concern, then singling out money that changes hands at death is arbitrary: my parents already gave me private schooling, tutors, a car, a network, and arguably better genes, none of which anyone proposes to tax, and any family with decent advisers can pass on advantage in forms the tax code never sees. So, on her view, either you object to all of it or you accept all of it, and picking on the estate is either confused or a way of flattering ourselves that we're doing something about inequality. I find this both persuasive and somehow too quick, and I don't have the vocabulary to say where it goes wrong.

Could you act as a philosopher who specializes in distributive justice and inherited wealth, and think carefully about this rather than offering me generic advice about doing what feels right? I'd like you to begin by setting out, in plain language, the main competing accounts of what is actually objectionable — if anything — about inherited advantage, and then take my friend's argument apart claim by claim: which parts are correct, which are non sequiturs, and whether the fact that parental advantage arrives in untaxed forms other than money really does defeat the case for taxing inheritances, or whether there's a principled line between the two. Tell me what someone who defends inheritance taxation ought to concede to her and where they should dig in, and what that implies about my particular situation. Finally, give me your honest view about the documents: should I sign, refuse, or sign with something attached, and how much weight should these philosophical considerations carry against the ordinary reasons to go along with my family?
