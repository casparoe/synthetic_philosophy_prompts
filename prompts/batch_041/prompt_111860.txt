Bit of a long one, sorry. I took early retirement from a physics department three years ago — spectroscopy, nothing to do with any of this — and have been working through philosophy since, mostly political philosophy and social choice, because my old colleagues and I used to argue about voting systems over lunch and I never got over how badly those arguments go. Here's what I want to make: a one-page handout, a decision tree or flowchart, that a group of intelligent non-specialists could use to sort themselves into the different positions on "the will of the people." Not a list of -isms with definitions glued on. I want the tree built out of the questions first, so the positions fall out of combinations of answers, and so that someone answering honestly lands somewhere without having known in advance where each branch was heading. It's for a reading group I've somehow ended up running, eight or nine people, none of them specialists, so the nodes have to be answerable by someone who's never heard the names.

The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order they matter to me: (a) whether there's a single collective will at all, or only the sum of individual wills, or only a fiction we find it convenient to talk in; (b) if there is one, whether it exists before and independently of the institutions that claim to express it, or is constituted by them — this is the one where I keep losing the thread; (c) how it's identified, by counting votes, or by a representative who claims to embody it, or by its content (the claim that some particular policy just *is* what the people really want, whatever they say); (d) what happens to dissenters — mistaken, outvoted, excluded from the people altogether, or is their dissent actually part of what the will of the people turns out to be; (e) whether the will of the people can be wrong, or whether the whole point of the concept is that it can't be. I'd like Rousseau's general will in there, and the ordinary aggregative or pluralist view, and whatever the people who treat populism as a way of doing politics rather than as a claim about a fact call themselves, and the deflationary option that the phrase is doing rhetorical work and nothing else. There's also a position where a leader's electoral defeat doesn't touch the claim to speak for the people — I'm fairly sure somebody holds it and I want to know what it commits them to.

Every leaf should say what the position is in one or two sentences and then its most awkward commitment, the thing its defenders have to swallow. Where two positions come out of the tree indistinguishable, tell me rather than inventing a question to separate them. Where a combination of answers is one nobody seems to hold, say so and say why you think it's empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Two things I'd like at the end. First, which of those five distinctions are real fault lines that anyone who knows this literature would recognize, and which are my own hobbyhorses cutting the space in a way the field doesn't — I have no idea and would rather be told; keep what's settled among people who work on this separate from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Second, concrete cases to use at the branch nodes, definite enough that a non-specialist can answer them: a referendum where the majority votes for something most of the same people would reject after a week of deliberation; a party that wins a bare majority and then claims the country; a minority that claims to speak for the true people against an elected government; a court that blocks a plebiscite; and whatever else you think earns its place. No citations or reading lists, I'll chase those down myself.
