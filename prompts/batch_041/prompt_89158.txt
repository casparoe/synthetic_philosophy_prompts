I was rereading the Odyssey with my kid and it struck me that the sirens episode is a hyperbolic discounting case study -- Odysseus at t1 wants to hear the song knowing that at t2 he'll want to jump, so he gets himself tied to the mast, and then at t2 he's begging to be untied, which is a textbook preference reversal. What I want is a real reading of the poem through the akrasia/discounting lens. Two things I think the theory misses and want you to test. First, the episode actually has two different devices in it: the wax plugs the crew's ears, which just deletes the temptation, while the rope keeps the option open and only overrides Odysseus's later preference -- and I suspect the poem knows these aren't the same fix, whereas the discounting framework mostly treats "commitment" as one thing. Second, the whole thing only works because someone else is holding the rope and refuses to honor his current preferences, so maybe the poem is saying self-control is a social/technological achievement rather than a personal one; the Helios cattle bit later (crew breaks its oath while Odysseus is asleep, nobody holding anything, everyone starving) would be the control case. Where am I overreading, where does the poem genuinely resist the discounting picture, and what does it add that the models of preference reversal definately don't have?
