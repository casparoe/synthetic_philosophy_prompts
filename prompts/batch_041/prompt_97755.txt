I'm trying to write a blog post about cognitive penetration and I keep going in circles, so I want you to build me a thought experiment to spec.

The spec: one person, one ordinary scene (kitchen, street, whatever — no labs, no goggles, no scanners), and a belief they hold that's both false and unjustified. The belief has to be about what kind of thing something is — a category belief — and it has to change how one low-level feature of the scene *looks* to them (the hue of one object, its shape), not the whole scene, and not a feature the category obviously predicts. They should be fixated on the same spot, doing the same task as before, and the belief should be sticky — not something they could just go test and fix on the spot.

So far so standard, here's the interesting part. I want the case built so the two main camps have to split on it. On the "it's the experience itself that changed" reading, this is a textbook case of penetration: the effect is content-specific, fast, and they can't decide to stop seeing it that way. On the "that's a change in perceptual judgment, not in what they see" reading, they'll say the experience is untouched and only their verdict about it moved. Build the case, run both readings, and tell me which single feature of the setup is doing the work — the thing the skeptic leans on to say "judgment, not experience." Then: if I remove that feature, which way does it go, and is there *any* variant that would actually force the skeptic to concede, or is that variant just going to presuppose the conclusion? Also give me the other side of it — how does someone in the first camp attack the case as rigged?

And a sanity check I keep needing: if a mere belief can do this, why isn't wishful seeing happening constantly?
