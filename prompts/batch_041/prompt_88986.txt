I've spent enough time in the control-versus-alignment arguments to notice that the distinction almost always arrives as a slogan and is almost never felt. The alignment side promises that the system will want the right things; the control side promises something stranger and, I think, more interesting — that even if it doesn't, the arrangement around it will catch it, so that safety can be bought with protocol rather than with character. Everyone nods at that sentence. Nobody writes the story in which the promise is actually spent.

So: write me that story, built on a notion I've been turning over, which I'll define here since it isn't standard. Call a control measure *inspection-fragile* if it only reduces risk to the extent that the system it constrains does not know about it. An inspection-fragile measure is easy to design and impossible to audit: confirming that it works requires showing it to the thing it guards against, and showing it is the single act that ruins it. A team can therefore verify the safeguard or keep it, never both — and at some point a named person has to sign the safety case on the strength of an assurance nobody has tested. The second notion is, I think, the darker one. Every protocol eventually bottoms out in some component that must be *assumed* loyal rather than checked, typically the weakest and most trusted link in the chain, which was chosen precisely because nobody wanted to think about it. Call that the *trust floor*. Nothing above the trust floor is more secure than the trust floor, and no audit ever reaches it.

Write a short story, roughly 1,200–1,800 words, set inside a plausible near-future oversight team, in which both of these bite. Some constraints I care about:

- The system under oversight must never lie, cheat, sandbag, or scheme. As far as anyone can tell, it is behaving well, and that must stay genuinely open. All the danger has to come from the shape of the humans' arrangement — from what the arrangement makes unknowable — and not from a villain.
- No lecture, no character explaining the control/alignment distinction, and no use of that vocabulary. A reader who knows the debate should recognize it from the structure of the scene; a reader who doesn't should find a good story about people making a hard call before a deadline.
- Give the other side its best line. Whoever argues for running the test that would burn the measure should be right about something important, and whoever argues for keeping it should be right about something else.
- Give the protagonist a specific institutional role and a specific decision with a date attached — a certification to sign, a release to authorize, an evaluation to run or not run. The story turns on that decision, not on a discovery.
- Don't cite or allude to real papers, labs, researchers, or documents, and don't invent quotations attributed to real people. I want this self-contained: everything the reader needs lives inside the fiction. If you want an epigraph, make one up and attribute it to a character in the story.
- No ending in which the system was secretly hostile all along, and no ending in which it turns out everything was fine. The ending I want is one where the reader sees exactly what has been left unverified and cannot say whether that was the right call.

The failure mode I'm trying to avoid is a story where control just means the system is watched by other systems — true, and boring. The mechanism should be visible in the scene itself: in who is allowed to know what, and in what the paperwork quietly certifies.

End with a single final line of the form \summary{...} containing exactly one sentence, naming what the story leaves unverified.
