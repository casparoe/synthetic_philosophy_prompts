I have been asked to open a reading group on Kelly betting and what is sometimes called geometric rationality — the idea that a rational agent should maximize the geometric mean, equivalently the expected logarithm, of their wealth rather than its arithmetic mean, and that this is a genuinely different proposal from expected-wealth maximization with a little risk aversion sprinkled on top. The room will be mixed: a handful of economics and philosophy graduate students who are comfortable with expected utility theory and basic probability, and two or three serious sports bettors who use the Kelly criterion as a bankroll rule and have never once been asked why log wealth, of all things, is the quantity to maximize. I have five minutes before the technical material starts, and I would like to spend them on a joke — partly as a warm-up, partly because I think the central contrast lands better as a joke than as a definition.

Would you write me one? The constraint that matters most is that it should be funny to somebody who genuinely knows this material, which means the punchline has to turn on the actual content of the theory rather than on the general fact that quants are nerds, that gamblers go broke, or that Bayesians enjoy updating. I want a joke I can tell out loud to that room, and one that nobody there is likely to have heard before: a short dialogue or compact scene with two or three speakers at most, under about a hundred and fifty words, nothing that has to be drawn on a board to work, and no dependence on a particular paper, author, or textbook, since the audience will not have read what I have read. Please do not append an explanation of why the joke is funny; if it needs one, it is not the joke I am after.

To push you off the stock material, I would like the punchline to turn on one of the following features of the position, and I would like the details to be right. First: a bet that doubles your money on heads and halves it on tails is worth one and a quarter times your wealth in expectation, and yet an agent maximizing expected log wealth is exactly indifferent between taking it and declining it, while an agent who takes it over and over, staking everything each time, has a long-run growth rate of zero. Second: no bet that carries any chance at all, however small, of leaving you with nothing is ever worth taking to a log-wealth maximizer, because the log of zero is not a small number but minus infinity, so an arbitrarily large payoff cannot buy back an arbitrarily improbable ruin. Third: the Kelly stake depends on the price as much as on the probability, so a Kelly bettor asked whether they believe something tends to answer with a question about the odds rather than with a number between zero and one. Use whichever of these gives the best joke, or, if you can find a sharper feature of the same material, use that instead — the only firm requirement is that the joke turn on something true about Kelly betting or geometric rationality rather than on the atmosphere of the subculture that surrounds it.
