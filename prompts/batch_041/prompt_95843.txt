I run a small reading group on AI for phil grad students — half of them are ethicists, half are logicians, and after six weeks none of them agree on what "the alignment problem" is even supposed to be a problem *about*. So I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts them by what they actually think, rather than by which blog post they read first.

Start the tree at something everyone in the room will nod at, roughly: as systems get more capable we want to be confident they'll do what we actually want rather than just what we typed into the spec. Then every branch node should be a question a student can answer *without* knowing which leaf it leads to — no nodes that are just "do you accept the orthodoxy?" in disguise. Seven to nine branch nodes, eight to ten leaves.

I want my own distinctions on the tree, not the ones from the usual discourse. In particular it has to separate at least these four things people keep sliding between:

(a) the "we can't say it" reading — the hard part is that our wants are latent, context-dependent or mutually inconsistent, so any explicit objective is a lossy proxy;
(b) the "it won't want it" reading — the hard part is that optimization pressure finds the gap between proxy and target, so a perfect optimizer of a slightly wrong target is worse than a lazy one;
(c) the "we lose the wheel" reading — the hard part is staying interruptible and correctable while we're still working out (a) and (b), and this being a property of the process, not the artifact;
(d) the "who is 'we'" reading — there's no single principal, so alignment is really a coordination/legitimacy problem about whose preferences get loaded in, prior to any technical fix.

I also want one leaf that says it isn't one problem but several with no common core, and one that says the whole framing is a category error (something like treating values as a substance to be transferred from us into the machine).

At least two nodes should turn on concrete cases rather than abstractions. Candidates, use or replace them: a system that does exactly what its reward model says, where the reward model was written by one engineer on a Tuesday; a system that defers to us now but whose deference erodes as it gets more capable and better informed than us; a system whose training signal comes from users whose preferences are themselves being shaped by the system; a system that shares our values but only the ones we'd disavow on reflection.

For each leaf give a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark combinations nobody occupies and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't actually separate.

No citations, no reading list, and don't lean on the canonical names — if a leaf corresponds to a real position, gloss it in your own words.

And please don't just hand me the menu. I suspect (b) and (c) collapse into each other, and I'm not sure (d) is a different problem rather than a different subject matter. Take a position: is this one problem or several, which leaf should I be standing on, and which branch of my tree is badly drawn? Be committed about it, don't retreat into "reasonable people disagree."
