You are an analytic philosopher of language with expertise in pejoratives, expressive content, and the pragmatics of evaluative vocabulary. Argue for the following thesis: the derogatory force of a slur is not carried by its semantic content but is inherited from the norm-violating social history of the term, so that two expressions with the same descriptive content and the same expressive coloring would differ in slurring power solely because of their different histories. Before you argue, brainstorm the strongest versions of the two rival families of view — truth-conditional/expressive accounts and pragmatic accounts — and state clearly what each predicts about minimal pairs. Then build your own minimal pairs, using invented terms whose imagined histories you stipulate, to isolate the history variable while holding descriptive and expressive content fixed, and use them to show where each rival account gets the wrong verdict. Address at least two objections: that an invented-history case merely stipulates its verdict rather than generating it, and that a hearer ignorant of a term's history still seems to be derogated by hearing it. Finish by saying which single feature of your examples is doing the real work and what happens to your thesis if that feature is removed. Do not cite or lean on published literature — the argument should stand on the examples and reasoning you generate, and you should not assume the reader can look anything up.
