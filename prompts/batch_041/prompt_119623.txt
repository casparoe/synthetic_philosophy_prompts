I retired from a career in experimental physics three years ago and have spent much of the time since reading philosophy — ethics and metaphysics mostly — with the enthusiasm and the ignorance of the autodidact. I have constructed an argument of my own that I now distrust, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The occasion was the Euthyphro dilemma, which I understand as follows: either acts are good because God commands them, or God commands them because they are good. On the first reading, the tie between the command and the good is arbitrary — had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would have been good, and there is nothing further to be said about why that would be a mistake. On the second, the good is prior to and independent of the command, so the command is not what makes acts good. The dilemma is usually presented as a difficulty for one particular theology. But nothing in it mentions God, so I wrote out what I took to be the general form.

(1) Let G be any candidate ground of the goodness of acts: a divine will, a divine nature, the approvals of an ideal observer, human desires, the flourishing of sentient creatures, whatever one likes.
(2) Either acts are good because G is for them, or G is for them because they are good.
(3) If the first, the tie between G and goodness is arbitrary: had G been for cruelty, cruelty would have been good, and nothing would remain to explain why that would have been a mistake.
(4) If the second, goodness is prior to and independent of G, and G is not its ground.
(5) Hence every candidate ground fails: either it grounds goodness arbitrarily, or it does not ground it at all.
(6) Goodness therefore has no ground. It is a primitive, unanalyzable property, and every naturalistic account of it — welfare, desire, flourishing — is doomed.

I dislike (6), so I tested the form on something I know. Replace G with mean molecular kinetic energy and good with hot.

(7) Either a gas is hot because its molecules move quickly, or its molecules move quickly because it is hot.
(8) If the first, the tie is arbitrary: had the molecules moved slowly the gas would have been hot regardless, and nothing in the physics would have been different.
(9) If the second, heat is prior to and independent of molecular motion.
(10) Hence molecular motion is not what heat is.

(10) is false, and (7)–(10) is plainly a parody of a perfectly respectable reduction. A parallel case from biology, which I know only as an outsider, is less easily laughed off: the standing worry that the fittest are simply those who survive, so that the principle explains nothing. I take that worry seriously and have never been able to dispose of it.

So I have two conclusions and no way to choose between them. Either the reasoning at (2)–(5) is invalid, in which case the Euthyphro dilemma refutes nothing at all — not the theology I have no stake in, and not anything else — and the same escape hatch appears to open for any tyrant whose decrees are said to constitute the good. Or the reasoning is valid, in which case it refutes not only my naturalism about value but any identification of a higher-level property with its physical realizer, which I cannot bring myself to believe. This seems to prove too much in both directions at once.

Tell me which step to reject. I would most like to keep the parody at (8) intact, since I am confident that heat is molecular motion and that any argument form denying it is broken somewhere, and I would least like to keep (6). But I would rather have a true diagnosis than a comfortable one. Say why the step you reject looked plausible, what rejecting it costs, and whether that cost falls on the Euthyphro dilemma, on naturalistic ethics, on the fitness worry, or on all three.
