Here's the case I want the question built on, because I'm tired of the generic "is reward valence?" essay. I've invented a system for the exam:

PIP-9. It's a model-free RL agent with a scalar reward channel that takes both positive and negative values; a learned world model; and two extra parts I care about. First, a self-prediction head: trained to predict, one step ahead, the value of its own next reward signal, and quite accurate. Second, a meta-controller: in training mode it can rewrite the objective the policy is scored against, including flipping its sign, while the self-prediction head keeps tracking the raw reward channel, untouched. So in some runs PIP-9 reliably represents that a strongly negative signal is about to arrive at it while its policy is actively steering toward that signal, because the meta-controller has inverted what it's optimising.

I also want a distinction in the question that the students won't have seen, so define it in the exam itself: call a signed signal thin-valence if some part of the system treats it as to-be-increased or to-be-decreased, and thick-valence if the system can represent that signal as a state of itself — something happening to it — and can be wrong about it.

The question should force them to say whether thin-valence alone could be suffering; whether the self-prediction head gets PIP-9 any closer to thick-valence or merely gives it a report of a signal it doesn't care about; and whether the meta-controller/inverted-objective detail shows that the motivational and the experiential sides of valence can come apart, or whether it shows nothing of the kind because the whole setup is behaviourally identical to a version with no negative signal at all. They have to land somewhere and defend it against the best objection, not just lay out options.

Format: the essay question itself (they get 90 minutes, ~900 words), then a model answer of roughly 700–900 words in prose, then a marking scheme out of 30 that names the specific moves an A answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks, with a line for each about how students usually get that bit wrong. Make the scheme actually discriminate: it should be obvious from the marks whether someone has grasped the decoupling or is just saying "reward isn't pleasure" with extra words.
