At a residency last winter I watched two friends, a novelist called Ines and a translator called Paul, argue for the better part of an evening over a claim Ines had made in a lecture on narrative, and I have not been able to settle the matter since. I write fiction myself and have some stake in the answer, which may be why I could not adjudicate it. I would like you to referee, and to do so point by point rather than by declaring a winner at the outset.

(1) Ines: Every story presupposes that what happens in it can be made sense of as somebody's doing something for a reason. That is not a fact about the stories we happen to write; it is a condition of the possibility of narrating at all. A sequence of events that could not be understood as anyone's acting for a reason is not a difficult story; it is not a story.

(2) Paul: You have confused a fact about texts with a fact about minds. You have shown, at most, that you cannot read certain sentences as a story. Someone trained differently might manage it; your inability is an empirical fact about your habits, and no amount of reflection on those habits will convert it into a necessity.

(3) Ines: It is not a fact about difficulty. I cannot make this out as a story is not like I cannot read Russian. It is a refusal of a form of description, and where a form of description cannot be applied at all, the fault lies with the concept of a story rather than with me. Show me a story in which an arm's rising is described only as contractile tissue and the thing is still a story, and you will have refuted me. You cannot.

(4) Paul: Then your conclusion is either trivial or unearned. If all you mean is that our concept of narrative is tied to our concept of action, very well — but that carries no metaphysical weight, and it will not deliver the claim you actually want, that reasons are not causes and cannot be reduced to them. And your argument proves too much: by the same form, a person could not be described both as an agent and as a body, and a science of decision would be impossible. Neither of those is impossible, so something in the inference is broken.

(5) Ines: A science of decision is no counterexample, because I am not denying that mechanism is true. I am saying that a description of a life in the vocabulary of mechanism is not a narration of it, and that a writer who tries to have both at once — a character whose acts are at once fully accounted for by causes and genuinely done for reasons — has not produced a difficult story but an incoherent one. That is a claim about what can be said, not about what exists.

(6) Paul: Then you have retreated from every story presupposes to our stories presuppose, which is the empirical claim I made at the beginning, dressed up.

Go through the exchange move by move, from (1) to (6), rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. I want to know, in particular, whether Ines's claim in (1) can be read in a way that gives it the force she claims for it; where Paul's objection in (2) lands and where it misses; whether the move in (5) is a concession, a clarification, or a change of subject; and which distinctions neither of them names that would settle much of the dispute if it were made explicit. Then tell me who has the better of the argument overall, and why.
