I am a retired physical chemist — thirty-four years in atmospheric research, the last decade of it running a laboratory — and since retiring I have been working my way through philosophy, mostly philosophy of biology, at a rate of perhaps a book a month plus a graduate seminar I audit at the local university. For two years I have sat as the outside member on the Environmental Release Review Committee of a research foundation, the body that must approve or refuse field trials of self-spreading genetic constructs. On Thursday we vote on the first proposal of this kind to come before us, and the chair has asked me to circulate the written opinion that will frame the discussion. The proposal would release a gene-drive construct — an engineered element that biases inheritance so that it sweeps through a population within a few generations — into the house-mouse population of a sub-Antarctic island, in order to collapse that population and protect a seabird colony whose chicks the mice now kill in enormous numbers. The applicant's benefit case is stated almost entirely in terms of "the recovery of the species" and "the health of the ecosystem"; the risk case is stated, candidly and at length, in terms of individual animals and their suffering. One of our members, a philosopher of biology, has been circulating notes arguing that the benefit case is not merely unproven but incoherent: nothing in that population is being selected for the recovery of the species, she says, and a committee that admits species-level goods into its ledger has smuggled in a unit of selection that does not exist. If she is right, and that whole side of the ledger is struck, my own reading of the applicant's numbers is that the balance flips and the answer is no. I suspect she has half a point and is using it to reach a conclusion the other half does not support, but I cannot yet say which half is which.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve subject to named conditions — with a time frame, and it should be a document the committee can act on rather than an essay: the recommendation first, then the reasoning, then the strongest consideration on the side I end up rejecting, treated as its proponents would wish it treated rather than as a straw position. The crux is the units-of-selection question, and I want the memo to settle it in a way that actually moves the decision rather than gesturing at it. Two distinctions I have picked up in my reading and am not confident I am wielding correctly should be brought to bear if they are apt: first, the difference between a group's being selected and a group's having properties that are selected for — I gather one can have the former without the latter, and that this is where a great deal of loose talk about the good of the group goes wrong; second, the difference between something's being a unit of selection and its being an object of value or moral concern, since it seems to me a committee could coherently care about a species without committing itself to any view about what selection acts upon, though I may be conflating two questions my colleague would insist on separating. I would also like the memo to say what the committee should do with the applicant's language — strike it, require it to be restated in terms of individual organisms, or accept it as a compressed statement of human-valued outcomes — and to be honest about how much of the decision genuinely turns on the metaphysics rather than on the ecology, which several of my colleagues will suspect is nothing at all.

Three further details that may matter. Our terms of reference instruct the committee to weigh "benefits to biodiversity and to the affected ecological community," language I have never seen litigated in a meeting and which I would rather not be the first to litigate badly. The applicant's modelling assumes the mouse population collapses within eight to twelve generations, and our own reviewer's memo suggests that figure is optimistic but not absurd. And I should say plainly that my training is in physical chemistry and that my population genetics is that of an interested amateur, so where the memo leans on a technical distinction I would prefer it be explained in a clause rather than assumed; the applicant has a right of reply at the following meeting, and the chair has asked me to keep the opinion to roughly twelve hundred words.
