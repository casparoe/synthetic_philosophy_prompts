I run a mechanistic-interpretability team at a company that deploys large models, and we keep hitting the same disagreement. A probe finds a high-level feature—say, a direction in activation space that tracks whether a document is a legal contract—and one engineer says the model "uses" that feature, while another says the feature is just a summary of lower-level activity and the real causal action is in individual neurons or attention heads. Hofstadter's careenium is the picture we keep arguing about: a vast arena of tiny magnetized marbles whose local pushes and pulls make clusters that drift and merge, and the question is whether describing those clusters as symbols that push the marbles around is legitimate causal talk or a convenient fiction layered on the microphysics. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run, on a deadline, to answer the question for a given feature: does this high-level pattern push the substrate around, in a sense that should change how we intervene, or is it epiphenomenal with respect to the behavior we care about? The procedure should ask its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, say where it stops with a no and where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is interpretive rather than empirical, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls in procedural clothing. Assume we can run ablations, patching, and causal tracing, but not retrain the model from scratch. Then run the procedure on three cases: a feature that passes cleanly; a feature that fails; and one where the procedure gives an answer my engineers will not like—for instance, telling us to treat as causally real a feature that is ugly, unstable, or not the one we wanted. Close by saying what the procedure cannot settle, and whether the careenium picture actually earns its keep here or whether we should just talk about interventions and be done with it.
