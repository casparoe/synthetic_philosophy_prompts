I'm doing a master's unit on the politics of welfare and we keep circling back to the deserving/undeserving poor thing -- the idea that the argument is never really about whether someone is poor but about whether they deserve to be, and that the practical upshot is always conditionality plus a claimant who's suspect until they prove otherwise. I get the idea in the abstract. What I want is to watch it do some work on a case that wasn't built for it, so I've been reading about robodebt in Australia and I'd like you to run the lens over it.

Here's the shape of the case as I've got it (correct me if I've got it wrong): from mid-2016 Centrelink replaced its manual overpayment checking with an automated system that took a person's annual income from the tax office, chopped it into fortnights, and compared it against what the person had reported fortnight by fortnight; where the averaged figure came out higher, a debt was raised automatically, no human in the loop. Averaging a year of income into fortnights invents income that nobody actually recieved in those particular fortnights, so a great many of the debts were simply wrong -- hundreds of thousands of them, as it turned out, with the scheme scrapped in 2020 and a royal commission in 2023 that was about as damning as these things get. The mechanics that interest me: the debt arrived as a fait accompli, the onus was on the recipient to disprove it, they had a few weeks and an online portal that wouldn't show them the tax office numbers they were supposed to argue with, and the people it caught were mostly on part payments -- casual and seasonal work, incomes that go up and down -- rather than anyone who'd been hiding money. It survived the ombudsman, two senate inquiries and a lost court case before anything moved.

So: run the deserving/undeserving lens over that. Who gets sorted where, and by what -- the algorithm, the letters, the portal, the appeals queue, the ministers, the press? What does the lens explain better than a boring story about budget targets and a badly built IT system: why the error rate didn't seem to matter, why making the recipient prove the debt wrong looked like sensible design to the people who signed off, why it lasted so long? And then the other direction -- what does the lens miss or misdescribe about this episode, and is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the framework never anticipated? I'm especially interested in whether the category gets manufactured rather than discovered (nobody's conduct changed, only the state's model of it) and in what it takes for someone to be reclassified as deserving after the fact. Keep it under 700 words, lead with one sentence saying whether the lens holds up, then four short sections: the mapping, what it explains, what it misses, verdict. No general essay on welfare please -- I want the mapping disciplined, and I want you to say where the lens goes quiet.
