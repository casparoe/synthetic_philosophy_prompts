For my school's philosophy magazine I'd like you to draft a satirical piece, and I'd rather you not simply recycle the usual lines about robots taking jobs. The form is an Onion-style corporate press release, about 450 to 500 words, issued by a fictional firm called Surplus Labs, announcing that it has fully automated its own operations and is now selling its former employees' newly freed time back to them as a monthly subscription. The comedy should come from the actual content of Marx's account of machinery rather than from generic corporate jargon: that machinery under these relations does not shorten the working day but turns the worker's own accumulated knowledge into a rival standing over them — the skill that once lived in the labourer now sits inside the machine as capital — so that automation makes the worker redundant rather than free, and their very time becomes a commodity they must buy back. Make the jokes land for a reader who knows this material (the machine as a means of extracting surplus rather than of easing labour, the worker's knowledge hardening into the dead labour of the apparatus, free time opened up as a new frontier for accumulation) while staying legible to a reader who does not, and keep the irony deadpan throughout: never wink at the reader and never state the critique in the open. Include at least one straight-faced quotation from a fictional executive who is delighted by the arrangement.
