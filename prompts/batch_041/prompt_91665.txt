In a seminar on eighteenth-century economic thought, the lecturer keeps settling arguments with Berkeley's one-liner that money is a ticket or counter. She quotes the query from The Querist asking whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, and whether 'the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter'; then she draws conclusions from it in two directions — that Berkeley had seen, long before anyone else, that money's value comes from convention or law rather than from metal, and, on another day, that he took money to be a fiction, so that anxiety about paper credit is superstition. I cannot tell whether those are one claim or two, or whether either follows from the line she quotes. Please lay out as a menu the distinct precise theses 'money is a ticket or counter' might be expressing — about what money is (ontology), what it does (function), what the word means (semantics), and who authorizes it (politics) — and mark each as a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or self-undermining. Note where Berkeley's own neighbouring queries pull against each reading: he also asks whether things might not still be rated, bought, and sold were the bullion gone, and whether money is useful only so far as it stirs up industry. Finally, say which readings would actually support each of the lecturer's two uses, and which of them the ticket metaphor, read strictly, rules out.
