I write about logic and the foundations of mathematics for a general-interest magazine, and I am commissioning a short story for an issue built around the question of whether mathematics describes one universe or many. The story I want only works if the reader has absorbed Skolem's paradox, and I want the paradox to be the load-bearing beam of the plot rather than a lecture that a character delivers to the reader. Let me set the stage so you know exactly what machinery I have in mind, and then tell you what I need from the fiction.

The paradox, stripped down. First-order logic has the Löwenheim–Skolem property, so any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model, set theory included, has a countable model. Suppose the axioms of set theory have such a model. Inside that model there is a set the model certifies to be uncountable, its version of the real numbers, say. And from where it stands the model is right: there is no bijection, internal to the model, between that set and the model's own natural numbers. Yet from outside, the model's entire domain can be laid out in a single countable list. Both verdicts are true. Uncountability is not the same property seen from inside and from outside, because the bijection that would witness countability is not itself an element of the model; the model cannot see the very object that would refute it. Nothing is broken, and the disagreement is about the range of the quantifier rather than about the facts.

Now the fiction. I want a story set inside a mathematical community that lives in such a universe and has proved, to its own satisfaction, that a particular collection is uncountable, a collection that matters to them and that they cannot enumerate or traverse. Give that collection a concrete role in their lives, so that its uncountability is a felt fact rather than a theorem on a page. Then bring in an outsider who, from a vantage the insiders cannot occupy, can exhibit the enumeration, the list that lays their vast collection out in a row. I do not want this resolved by revealing that one side was simply wrong; both are right, and I want the reader to feel the vertigo of that. I also do not want the outsider to be merely a god or a trickster; make the asymmetry of vantage points the engine. If you can, let the ending turn once more: let the outsider's own comfortable enumeration be countable within a still larger frame, and leave it there rather than drawing a moral.

A few things to avoid. No character may explain the theorem to another character; if the paradox needs to surface, let it surface through what the characters can and cannot do. Do not lean on the usual furniture of such stories, the infinite library, the map that contains itself, the dream within a dream. Do not dissolve the tension by proving that one model is the intended one. And keep the mathematics honest: the only facts you may assert are the ones I set out above, and you should not invent set-theoretic results. Roughly two thousand words, and it should read as literary fiction rather than as a thought experiment with names attached.
