I am a high school senior, and next month I will present a proposal to my district's school board asking it to rewrite the student speech policy. My thesis is that the district should stop disciplining students for off-campus speech that is offensive, shocking, or demeaning, provided it does not threaten a particular person, incite violence, or amount to sustained targeted harassment; and that on campus it should restrict speech only when that speech leaves a captive audience unable to take part in class. My argument runs through Mill's harm principle: the only legitimate ground for restricting someone's speech is to prevent harm to others, and being offended is not the same as being harmed. I then argue that a school is not a public square, because students cannot leave the room to which they are assigned, so the harm that matters on campus is what I call access harm — speech that actually prevents a student from using the school's educational goods, for instance by driving them out of a class or making it impossible for them to participate. Offense, however intense, is not access harm. I concede that the line is harder to draw off campus, where there is no captive audience, and I am willing to accept that my test will sometimes be under-inclusive.

The board is not a philosophy seminar, and I need to be ready for the room I will actually face. Its members are the superintendent, whose chief concerns are legal exposure and the district's reputation and who will frame everything as risk management; a retired attorney on the board who will attack the vagueness of my test and ask who decides what counts as access harm; the district's equity director, who believes that demeaning speech inflicts real psychological harm on marginalized students and will treat my offense/harm distinction as a way of dismissing that harm; a civics teacher who has read Mill and will press me on whether the harm principle was ever meant to govern an institution like a school rather than the state; two student representatives who mostly want less policing of their social media; and a parent who will ask what happens the first time a student is hurt and the policy I wrote is blamed.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet for this meeting. I want the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones a philosophy professor would raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each does to my proposal. Phrase each objection the way that particular member would phrase it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Please do not give me a general essay on free speech; I have the argument, and what I lack is a defense of it against these specific people.
