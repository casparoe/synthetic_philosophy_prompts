I have been going back and forth with two friends in a philosophy of value reading group about a view one of them calls threshold lexicalism, and I want to pin down my own credence rather than keep talking in circles. The view, as she states it, is a hybrid: there is a threshold of suffering such that any pain at or above it is lexically prior to pleasure, so that no finite quantity of pleasure, however large, can make a world containing that pain better than a world without it; whereas pains below the threshold are commensurable with pleasures on a single ratio scale, so that enough mild pleasure really can outweigh a mild pain. Her main argument is an intuition pump: we are invited to compare one episode of agony against N episodes of mild contentment, with N set at 10^30, and she claims that the pull toward saying the agony still dominates is not a failure of imagination but a datum about the structure of value. My other friend thinks the threshold is a fudge — either the scale is Archimedean and the agony is eventually outweighed by enough mild pleasure, or the priority is lexical all the way down and the threshold is doing no work except to save the appearances.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but your own credence in the following proposition, stated precisely enough to be wrong: there is a fact of the matter about the axiological structure of the pain-pleasure comparison, and the correct structure is threshold lexicalism as she defines it, with the threshold located at a principled and non-arbitrary point on the intensity scale rather than merely stipulated. Decompose that into whatever sub-questions you think it actually rests on — among the candidates I can see: whether pain and pleasure are commensurable at all, that is, whether there is a single cardinal scale that both can be located on, or whether the comparison is merely ordinal or not even that; whether, given commensurability, the scale is Archimedean or admits lexical priority; whether the relevant magnitudes are felt intensities, preference-satisfactions, or something else, and whether the answer changes under each; whether the threshold could be principled rather than a stopgap; and whether the whole question is well-posed enough to have a truth value, or whether it collapses into a question about how we should talk. Give me a number between 0 and 1 for each sub-question, one line of justification each, say how they combine into the headline credence, and then tell me which single sub-question the headline number is most sensitive to and what argument or consideration would move it most.

Two further things to fold in. First, I am not sure the comparison is even the same question when the pains and pleasures belong to different subjects rather than to one life, so tell me whether your credence changes if we restrict the proposition to the intrapersonal case, and say separately what you think happens interpersonally. Second, do not simply report what you take the consensus to be, and do not defer to how the debate is usually framed; I want the decomposition and the number, and if you think a sub-question is not well-posed, say so and treat that as a reason the corresponding probability is unstable rather than silently assigning it a value. End with a final line in the form \answer{X}, where X is your headline credence as a decimal to two places.
