In my paradoxes seminar I've been working on the surprise exam, and what puzzles me is less the puzzle than the literature's failure to converge: the flaw in the student's reasoning has been located, by different writers, at the base step (that an exam on the last possible day could not surprise), in the inductive step from one day back to the previous, in the student's willingness to treat the teacher's announcement as a premise at all, and in the very idea that "surprise" could be a coherently announced condition. My hypothesis is that these are not four answers to one question but answers to different questions under one heading — some about the validity of a backward induction over knowledge, some about the consistency of the announcement, some about what "surprise" means — and I'd like to test it. I'm particularly interested in one sub-question I keep running into: whether the announcement is best read as a prediction about the student's epistemic state, in which case the student's own knowledge of the announcement is part of what would make it true or false, and whether that reading is what separates the camps or merely restates one of them. Please give me a diagnosis rather than a solution: which of these disagreements is substantive and which verbal, whether the base-step dispute is a genuine disagreement about knowledge or a stipulation in disguise, whether a hidden formal question is doing the work, and what would actually count as progress here — a formal result, a distinction, or an argument that the puzzle is ill-posed and no progress should be expected. Defend the diagnosis by engaging the positions as they are actually stated.
