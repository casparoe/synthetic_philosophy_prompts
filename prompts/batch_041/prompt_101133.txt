I am a doctoral student working in philosophical logic, and what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The literature on the probabilities of indicative conditionals has, so far as I can tell, been stuck for decades in a way that the technical progress does not explain. The triviality results are no longer themselves in dispute: it is agreed on all sides that one cannot simultaneously hold that an indicative conditional expresses a proposition with an ordinary probability and that the probability of that proposition is the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, at least not if the identity is required to survive supposition and conditionalization rather than to hold only for the initial credence function. Yet the field cannot agree on what the results mean, or on which of the jointly incompatible commitments should give way.

Four responses seem to be in play, and I want them distinguished by what they actually maintain rather than by who maintains them. One camp treats triviality as a reductio of the propositional view altogether and concludes that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions, so that the conditional probability is not a stand-in for the probability of a proposition but the whole content of the conditional. A second keeps the truth conditions and denies the identity, treating the conditional probability as a fact about what one is entitled to believe or how assertable the conditional is, not as the probability of the proposition expressed. A third accepts both the truth conditions and the identity and locates the culprit in an auxiliary assumption, either the way a conditional's probability behaves under conditionalization or a principle governing how conditionals embed inside other conditionals, and then disagrees with the rest of that camp about which assumption is guilty. A fourth denies that the results bear on the question at all, on the grounds that they deploy a notion of probability that was never the right one for conditionals.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than assumed, is that this is not really a disagreement about any of those premises but about what a semantic theory is supposed to deliver: a compositional assignment of truth conditions, or a theory of the credences a speaker is committed to. If that is right, then the problem of the probabilities of conditionals is a settled theorem plus an unsettled question about which of those two jobs a semantics of conditionals is doing, and the apparent dispute over the premises is a symptom of that. I can equally imagine the opposite diagnosis: that it is a substantive disagreement in formal semantics, with a fact of the matter about which auxiliary premise is false that the disputants simply have not found the argument for, or that it is verbal, in that the probability of a conditional is being used by different parties to mean the credence of the proposition expressed, the credence one ought to have in the consequent on learning the antecedent, or a measure of assertability.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Is it substantive, verbal, a clash of pre-theoretic intuitions about whether conditionals are truth-evaluable that no argument could settle, or two different questions travelling under one name? If you conclude that some of the parties are answering different questions, say exactly which question each is answering and what evidence in their stated commitments shows it. If you think a hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work, identify it. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or argue that none should be expected and why. I am not asking for a reading list or a survey; I want the diagnosis and the reasoning behind it.
