I spent thirty-six years as a condensed-matter physicist and retired in 2019, and since then I've been doing philosophy in a fairly amature way — an adult-ed course, a lot of reading, and the weekly reading group that the philosophy department here runs. Next month it's my turn to lead the session, and I've somehow ended up not just presenting Hofstadter's later book about the self but actually defending its central claim. That's more than I meant to sign up for, and I'd like to be properly ready for the questions.

The claim, as I understand him (and I'm aware I may be flattening him): the self isn't a thing but a pattern — a self-referential loop that a brain runs — and a rough copy of that loop can be running inside somebody else's head if you know them well enough. Hofstadter pushes it further: he says each person's I is spread over numerous brains rather than confined to one, so that when someone dies, part of what they were is still running, in the people who knew them. He insists this is not just a way of speaking. Selves come in degrees, and so does being dead. I think he's broadly right and I'd like to hold the line for an hour. My own instinct, from physics, is that this is far less alarming than they'll make it sound — we're perfectly happy to say the same wave pattern is in two places at once — so I'd also like to be told where that analogy breaks down.

Here's who's in the room, roughly a dozen people. Two are proper philosophers of mind. One is a hard functionalist who will want to know why a pattern realised twice is the same self rather than two things that merely resemble each other. The other is convinced the self is a folk illusion and thinks Hofstadter is smuggling it back in under a scientific-sounding name. There's a woman who works on Buddhist no-self material and will probably come at me from the opposite direction — that degrees of self is still a self, and a self is precisely what she thinks we should give up. There's a retired psychiatrist who is sympathetic but keeps asking what would count as evidence for any of it. Three or four grad students who do logic and will go straight for the metaphysics of patterns: what individuates one, whether a pattern can be in two places, whether it can be more or less itself, whether two people with similiar loops are one self or two. And the convenor is a Kantian who will ask about the unity of apperception, whatever that amounts to.

So what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not an essay. What are the objections I'll actually get from this lot, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each one written the way that person would put it (a functionalist's objection doesn't sound remotely like a Buddhist's), with a two-minute answer for each and an honest note on where the answer is thin. Tell me what to concede straight away and what to refuse to concede at all, and finish with the one question I'm dreading — the one I'm hoping nobody asks, because I don't have an answer to it. Don't bother with a reading list or citations, I'm not writing a paper; I need things to say out loud. And please write it in British English — I've been here long enough that 'analyze' looks wrong to me now.
