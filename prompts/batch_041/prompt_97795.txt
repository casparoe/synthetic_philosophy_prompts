I sit on a citizens' advisory panel for our county environmental health department, and next month we have to hand in a short written recommendation on whether to let a small biotech outfit field-test a genetically engineered soil microbe that's supposed to chew up PFAS at an old industrial site. The problem is nobody can give us a number -- not the company, not the state tox folks -- for the chance that the microbe spreads, or trades genes with native soil bacteria, or does something nobody thought of. The chair of our panel keeps settling every worried comment with the same move: "c'mon, we all buy fire insurance on our houses even though we can't say what the odds our house burns down are. Being careful here is just insurance. Nobody refuses insurance because the acturaries don't have numbers yet." About half the room nods along; the other half just rolls their eyes and mutters about technophobia. I'd rather actually audit the thing instead of trading vibes, and I'm the one drafting the recommendation, so I want it done properly.

So: take the analogy the chair is leaning on -- precaution here is like buying insurance -- and lay out what maps onto what. Who or what is the insurer, what's the premium and who pays it, what's the payout, what's the insured event, what plays the role of the actuary, the pool of policyholders, the contract you sign? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I care most about the breaks that are load-bearing for the conclusion she's drawing, which is that we should impose a moratorium or at least heavy conditions even though we can't quantify the risk: insurance, after all, works because the loss is definable, the probabilities are at least estimable, and the whole arrangement is voluntary and priced -- so what, if anything, is left of the analogy once we're in a regime of ignorance rather than measurable risk, and does her conclusion actually need the analogy or could she reach it on grounds that don't depend on it at all? A table of correspondences is fine, and please separate the disanalogies that sink the argument from the ones that are just interesting. If you think some other comparison does the job better -- someone at the last meeting said it's really more like Pascal's wager, betting on the safe side -- say what it is and why it earns its keep where the insurance one doesn't.
