My father died in March, and I am to give the eulogy at a secular memorial. He read the Zhuangzi for fifty years and left written instructions: nothing about him watching over us, nothing about a better place, and nothing about how he lives on in our memories or in his work — he said that last one was the same comfort with better manners. What he did want said is harder: that dying is one more turn in the process that made him, that nothing of him is left over to be missing anything, and that this is less the loss of a person than the world continuing to do what it has always done. Grief, he thought, was entirely reasonable; grief that assumes he is somewhere waiting was not. He also insisted that mourning is for the living, so none of this was an argument for feeling less or for skipping the rites — he would have called a stiff upper lip its own kind of falseness.

Two complications. He was frightened through his last months, and my mother has been telling people that he died calmly, in the spirit of the book; I will not lie about it, but I also don't want the room to hear his fear as a verdict on the philosophy he lived by. And the room is split: relatives who need comforting, and friends who have read the Zhuangzi closely and will wince at anything that turns it into uplift — including, several of them, the tub-drumming story my cousins are expecting to hear as a joke.

Write the eulogy, about five minutes spoken. It must not say anything he would have called false, and it must not leave the room with nothing.
