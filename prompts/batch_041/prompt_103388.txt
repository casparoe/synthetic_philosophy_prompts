A reading group I sit in on has generated the following argument, and I have not been able to say cleanly where it fails. (1) Social facts — a recession, the persistence of a norm, a pay gap — are nothing over and above facts about individuals: their beliefs, actions and interactions. (2) If a fact is nothing over and above facts of some other kind, then an adequate explanation of it must be given in terms of facts of that kind. (3) So an adequate explanation of any social fact must be given entirely in terms of individual beliefs and actions. (4) Yet the explanations we actually give cite things like a central bank's decision to raise the rate, or the fact that a norm is enforced by third parties, and these are not facts about any individual's beliefs and actions. (5) Therefore such explanations are not, strictly, explanations at all; they are placeholders until microfoundations are supplied. (6) So the only genuine social explanations are individualist ones.

Two things bother me. The conclusion seems to prove too much: by the same reasoning it would condemn an explanation of residential segregation that runs from individuals' mild preferences to an aggregate pattern, even though that story is told entirely about individuals. And I would rather keep (1) than (6), since I have no wish to posit social wholes. So: which step should I give up, why did it look compelling, and what does giving it up cost? If the trouble is with the move from (1) to (3), tell me what a defensible account of higher-level social explanation looks like once supervenience is no longer doing that work, and what that account has to concede to the holist.
