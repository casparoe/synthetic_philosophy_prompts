Rank the following six Pascalian theses about justice and force, each stated below in my own paraphrase, according to how much of a coherent position they leave Pascal with once all six are on the table at once. In other words, rank them by how much they can each be retained without forcing you to abandon or radically reinterpret the others. Put the theses into tiers (I'd like at least three tiers, with ties allowed inside a tier), and for each thesis give one or two sentences explaining what it contributes and what it costs the rest of the position.

The theses, as I have reconstructed them from the Pensées:

(A) Justice by itself has no power to secure compliance, so it is impotent unless force is behind it.

(B) Force by itself has no legitimate claim on us, so it is tyrannical unless justice is behind it.

(C) What people actually obey is established custom, and a law is treated as just for the reason that it has been received and stands, not because it satisfies any standard of justice we could state independently.

(D) Justice is perpetually contested while force is not, so the only thing that reliably settles a dispute is the stronger party, which is why the appearance of justice is best guaranteed by being backed by the multitude.

(E) There is a hierarchy of orders -- the body or force, the mind, and charity or the will -- and greatness in one order is invisible and incommensurable with greatness in another, so force's rule over the world of bodies says nothing about its authority over minds or wills.

(F) The requirement that the just be obeyed and the requirement that the strongest be obeyed are both binding, and they are not the same requirement.

I am particularly interested in whether (A) and (B) can both be kept, and whether (C) and (D) can both be kept, without collapsing into either a purely descriptive account of power (in which "unjust" is just a word for "not yet enforced") or a purely moralizing one (in which force is always in need of a justification it can never supply). If some subset of the six has to go, say which, and what the remaining position looks like.

This is for a seminar paper, so I want the reasoning to be usable in prose, but I also need to be able to pull a single verdict out of it at the end. After your discussion, end your answer with a line of the form

\answer{...}

where the braces contain a comma-separated list of the tier numbers in order of most to least central, e.g. \answer{T1: A, F; T2: B; T3: C, D, E} if you put A and F in the top tier, B alone in the second, and C, D, and E in the third.
