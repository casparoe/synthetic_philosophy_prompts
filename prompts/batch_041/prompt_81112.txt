I'm taking a course on the philosophy of punishment and writing a paper on Camus's case against the death penalty. Rather than the usual deterrence debate, I want to test his argument against a real episode: the Eichmann affair — the Jerusalem trial of 1961, the survivor testimony, the conviction, and the execution in 1962. I know the familiar banality-of-evil reading and don't need it rehearsed. I'd like the analysis to run in both directions.

First, break Camus's case into the strands that do the real work. As I read him: (i) irreversibility — the state's killing is the one act that can never be recalled or made good, and it forecloses any amendment on the condemned man's part; (ii) asymmetry — the murderer typically kills in a passion he cannot govern, whereas the state kills coldly, in advance, and by procedure; (iii) non-exemplarity — executions fail to deter and coarsen the society that stages them. For each strand, say whether the episode confirms it, pressures it, or falls outside its scope, and flag where you are attributing a reading to Camus rather than finding it in him. Strand (ii) interests me most: administrative genocide looks like the case in which the asymmetry collapses, since Eichmann's crime was colder and more deliberate than the hanging that answered it, and I want to know whether that damages the argument or only identifies which strands were load-bearing.

Second, run it the other way. What does the episode expose about what his framework cannot see — the trial as a forum for survivors rather than a determination of guilt, and the execution as the political community's expression of condemnation rather than a deterrent or a punishment in kind? Where he is silent, say so rather than extending him on his behalf, and if another account handles the case better, say what it captures that his does not.
