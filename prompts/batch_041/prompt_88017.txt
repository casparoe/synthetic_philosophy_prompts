I'm a retired entomologist — 30-odd years in ag research, mostly insecticide resistance — and I've somehow ended up on the technical advisory committee of our county mosquito control district. I also started reading moral philosophy after I retired, which was probably a mistake, because now every agenda item looks like a trolley problem with extra steps.

In two weeks we vote on the districts next five-year plan and I have to circulate my written opinion beforehand. The mess: right now we larvicide the salt marsh with Bti, which is supposed to be fairly targeted, and when trap counts pass a threshold we fog from trucks with a pyrethroid. The fogging is broad-spectrum — beekeepers are complaining, and the state says our marsh holds the only remaining population of a certain tiger beetle in the county. The university wants us to run a pilot release of gene-drive mosquitoes instead: it could crash the Aedes population for good, but it would probably drift into the next county and there's no calling it back. Whatever we put in the marsh also ends up in the estuary, where the oyster growers already hate us. Our charter says we exist to control mosquitoes of public health importance, full stop, which may or may not settle anything.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate before the vote. I want an actual recommendation for this season with a time frame, not just principles. I'm leaning toward the gene drive, mainly because I figure it kills fewer insects overall than years more fogging, but I don't trust that instinct and you should take the strongest case against it seriously. And please don't just tell me insects don't matter: half the board thinks they matter a great deal, one member keeps saying the sheer number we kill makes the whole program monstrous, and another, an ex-wildlife biologist, says reversibility should decide everything — the beetle can't come back, the mosquitoes can, so never pick the irreversible option. I'm not sure either is right. I'd rather you be honest about whats genuinely uncertain than pretend the science settles the ethics.
