My dad's been on the transplant list for a bit now and I've been reading up on where the organs actually come from, and something's been bugging me. The usual setup: someones dying on a ventilator, the family says stop, the doctors turn the machine off or stop the drugs holding the heart up, and once the heart stops and they're declared dead the surgeons take the organs. Fine, great. But the rule everywhere — the "dead donor rule," they call it — is that you can't give someone a drug that stops their heart *in order to* get the organs. That's killing, that's murder, doesn't matter how much everyone wants the organs or how clearly the patient wanted to donate. Meanwhile switching off the machine, which is the thing that actually causes them to die, is just "letting them die" and nobody blinks. So what view of killing vs. letting die does this practice actually presuppose? Is it really resting on the act/omission line, or is the real work being done by something else — the intention, the idea that the disease is what's "really" killing them, the requirement that death happen by a certain clock for the organs to still be usable? And are the two halves of the practice even consistent with each other, or is one of them just a fig leaf for the other? Last thing: if you replaced the doing/allowing picture with a straight view where all that matters is whether the patient dies with or without their consent, does the whole thing fall apart or would it survive more or less intact? I want to know whats carrying the weight here.
