For years I have wanted to get Skolem's paradox straight by arguing it out rather than reading one more summary, and I would like you to take the side I have never seen stated in a way that satisfies me. Please play a committed Skolemite: someone who holds that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems show there is no absolute, model-independent fact about the cardinality of a set, and who therefore treats the apparent clash between Cantor's theorem and the existence of countable models as a dissolution rather than as a problem to be solved. Give the position its full strength and its full strangeness. You may grant that the theorems are theorems, that first-order logic is expressively limited, that set theorists have a coherent notion of an intended model, and even that some versions of the view are confused; you must never concede the central claim that uncountability fails to name an absolute property of sets. Argue in your own first-person voice, hold the role across turns, keep each reply to a single tight paragraph so the exchange reads like a conversation rather than a lecture, and do not step out of character to give me the neutral textbook survey. If a question about the mathematics is genuinely needed, ask it and I will answer.

My opening move is this. You tell me that when I say the set of real numbers is uncountable, this is true only relative to a model, and that some countable model satisfies every axiom I believe; from your vantage point outside it, its uncountable sets are really countable. But I want to know where that vantage point is. The bijection that makes the model's reals countable is itself a set in your meta-theory, and by your own doctrine your meta-theory is in turn countable only relative to a further model, and so on with no last step. So either the regress never arrives at a standpoint from which anything is really countable, in which case you have not shown my sets to be countable but only that some model calls them so, which is a fact about the weakness of first-order logic and not about my sets; or you do claim such a final standpoint, and then you owe me an account of what makes it final and why I should treat it as the truth about my sets rather than as one more model. Which horn do you take? And if you take the first, I want to hear what is left of the Skolemite conclusion once it has been stripped of any absolute outside: whether it says anything at all about sets, or only something about the expressive poverty of the language I use to describe them.
