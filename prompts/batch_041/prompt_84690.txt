I've been in a reading group that's working through the demandingness literature — Parfit, Singer, the usual — and last week one of the members circulated a one-page handout summarising Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints, the 1982 one that gets cited whenever effective altruism comes up. Having spent a fair few years in the rationalist blogosphere reading about moral demandingness and the "morality is one project among many" line, the shape of the argument felt familiar to me, but the handout's version doesn't quite line up with the bits of Wolf I've seen quoted elsewhere, and I'd rather not go on repeating the handout's version in conversation until someone has audited it. Here is what it says, roughly as written: 'Wolf argues that a morally perfect person — someone for whom morality is the overriding, all-consuming project — is a bad ideal to hold up for human beings. She means by this people like religious saints or full-time aid workers. She distinguishes two versions. The Loving Saint does the morally best thing joyfully and without inner conflict; the Rational Saint does the morally best thing, but from a grim sense of duty. Wolf's central complaint is that the saint's life has no room in it for the non-moral goods: friendship, hobbies, art, play. Her main evidence for this is that we find such people unbearable to be around, since they make us feel judged and guilty, so the existence of moral saints is actually bad for the moral community. She also suggests that the Loving Saint must be missing a piece of perceptual equipment, since otherwise he would notice how much he is giving up. Wolf concludes that we should therefore be less moral than we could be, and that morality is at bottom just one value among others, with no special claim on us. Her real target is the effective altruism movement, which simply hadn't been named yet when she wrote.'

Could you give me a claim-by-claim audit? I'd like to know which parts a careful reader of the essay would accept as fair, which are common simplifications that a sympathetic defender would wince at, and which are things Wolf explicitly denies or argues the opposite of. Two points interest me in particular. First, does her argument really turn on how saints affect the people around them, or is that doing less work than the handout implies? Second, does the concluding claim — that morality has no special claim on us, and that we should be less moral than we could be — follow from anything in the parts you judge accurate, or is it an inference the handout has added? Where the summary is wrong, please give me the corrected version of the claim rather than just marking it down, and flag anywhere the vocabulary is quietly doing work, for instance if a term of Wolf's is being used in its everyday sense. Please write in British English.
