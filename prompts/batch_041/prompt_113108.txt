I host a small literary-theory podcast — graduate students and curious generalists, forty minutes an episode, and I do all my own prep. Next month's guest is a philosopher of art who has spent a career defending a version of actual intentionalism about literary meaning: the view that what a work of literature means is fixed by the author's intentions concerning the text, with the qualification that those intentions must be successfully realized in the text itself. Where the text cannot bear the meaning, the author's intention does not fix it; where the text can bear it, the intention settles the matter, even against a reader who has found something more interesting in the passage. She is not a naive intentionalist. She distinguishes the author's intentions about what her words are to mean from her intentions about what kind of thing she was making at all — fiction rather than reportage, satire rather than tribute — and holds that only the former do the meaning-fixing work. She is also famously good at deflecting objections with the line that her critics are attacking an "authorial dictatorship" that no serious intentionalist has ever held, and she treats the text's resistance to the author's intention as a matter of degree rather than kind.

I'd like ten to twelve questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question each on: whether the analogy between reading a text and understanding what a speaker meant by an utterance actually supports her view or quietly requires the reader's uptake to do the work; the case of the secret or permanently unrecoverable intention (a private diary meaning, an anonymous text, an author whose letters are lost); irony and other cases where the text's surface and the author's intention seem to pull apart; the Humpty Dumpty worry that on her account the author can make a word mean whatever she likes; collective and posthumous authorship, including whether the view has anything useful to say about a text no single mind intended; and the boundary case of machine-generated prose, where there is no intention to recover at all. Then I want the deflection itself made progressively harder to use: a sequence in which each question concedes the strawman point and then asks what remains of the view once the concession is made, ending with one question about what would count against her position — what observation, case, or argument should move a committed actual intentionalist, and which of those she would herself admit as counting against her rather than dismissing as a misunderstanding.

For each question, give me a one-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so I can keep the exchange moving instead of letting her retreat to the dictatorship line; flag the two you think are most likely to trigger that retreat. Everything should be answerable from the armchair — I'm not looking for citations, reading lists, or names to drop, and neither she nor I will have notes in front of us. Skip anything biographical or anything about the state of the field. I lean toward a more reader-centered view myself, but I don't want a debate: I want questions she'll find genuinely difficult to answer well.
