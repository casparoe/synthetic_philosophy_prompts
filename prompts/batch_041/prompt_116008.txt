I'm midway through a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and we have just spent two sessions on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which I understand as the constraint that my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my future credence in it taking some particular value, ought to equal that value: if I am certain that at midnight I will be 0.85 confident that the experiment will replicate, then I should already be 0.85 confident that it will replicate, and if I am not, something about my present or my future state is defective. A classmate of mine keeps glossing the whole thing with an analogy I find seductive and suspicious in roughly equal measure: on his telling, my future self is simply a better-informed expert, and Reflection is nothing more than the ordinary epistemic duty of deference to expertise — the very same thing that makes it unreasonable for me to keep insisting my knee is fine after the orthopedist has shown me the scan, except that here the expert happens to be me at a later date. He says the principle's intuitive pull just is the pull of that familiar point about trusting those who know more, and that violating it is a species of arrogance; he uses the analogy, in particular, to argue that anyone who sincerely reports something like "I know I'll be convinced by the argument by tomorrow, but I still think the argument is bad" is being straightforwardly incoherent, and that the principle therefore needs no further defence. I can see the appeal — the analogy makes an abstract coherence condition feel like common sense — but I suspect it is doing more argumentative work than it can bear, and I would like an audit rather than a verdict.

Could you set out the correspondence explicitly — what is supposed to answer to the expert, to the expertise, to the act of deference, to the expert's testimony, and to the background conditions that make deference to an expert appropriate in the first place — and then tell me precisely where the mapping breaks down? I would especially like you to work through two sorts of case in which the analogy seems to misbehave: (i) cases in which I already know that my later self will be epistemically compromised, because I have been told I will be given a drug, or will be exhausted, frightened, or simply unable to recall the considerations I now take to be decisive, so that deference to the later credence looks like deference to a bribed or incompetent witness; and (ii) cases in which my later credence is not merely a report on evidence I happen to lack but is itself evidence about the proposition, either because the process producing it is correlated with the truth (my becoming confident is a reliable symptom that matters are going well) or because I can foresee that the credence will be produced by something I now choose. I want to know which of the resulting disanalogies genuinely bear on the work the analogy is doing — licensing the claim that a violation of Reflection is straightforward irrationality — and which are merely decorative, and then whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. If the expert picture is the wrong one, I would also like your best replacement: an analogy that captures what the constraint actually is, whether that is a consistency requirement on forecasts, something about the way evidence is expected to arrive, or something else entirely, together with a sentence or two on what the better analogy makes visible that the expert one obscures.
