I'm preparing a talk for a stratigraphy-and-Earth-system-science audience and need a Q&A prep sheet. Here is the situation.

I am going to argue that the Anthropocene should be formalized, but not as an epoch with a single isochronous base. My claim is that the human transformation of the Earth system is better treated as a geological *event* than as a *time unit*, and that events are allowed a diachronous onset: the boundary is recognized wherever and whenever the human signal first becomes legible in the local record, rather than being pinned to one point in one reference section. On this view the ICS/IUGS would not be shirking its duty by declining to choose a single golden spike; it would be recognizing that the phenomenon it is trying to name is the wrong logical type for a golden spike, and that the existing menu of formal unit-kinds is incomplete because it was built for a world without a globally distributed, self-monitoring causal agent. I will also argue that the *name* "Anthropocene" carries social and political freight that no stratigraphic body can ratify or revoke, and that the ratification of a unit and the currency of a term should be prised apart.

The audience is roughly forty people, and I know its composition. Three of them are Quaternary stratigraphers who sit close to the Subcommission and who hold, as a matter of professional doctrine, that a formal chronostratigraphic unit is defined by its base, that the base must be a single point in a single stratigraphic section, and that the marker defining it must be isochronous — the same instant everywhere. They will regard "diachronous boundary" as close to an oxymoron and will treat my proposal as a category error dressed up as humility. Two are Earth-system scientists who are convinced the real story is the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration and who have little patience for the diachroneity argument; for them the interesting question is whether the signal is global and synchronous, not what kind of unit we call it. One is a historian of science who thinks the whole exercise is an illegitimate grab for authority by a technical body over a concept that belongs to the humanities and to politics. One is a geographer who has been arguing for years that the right name is not Anthropocene at all but something that names the economic system responsible. The rest are agnostics who will mostly follow whichever of the above sounds most confident.

What I need from you is the Q&A prep sheet for the discussion after my talk — not a defense of my position, but a forecast of the questions I will actually get from *this* room, not the ones I would get from a philosophy of science seminar or from the Anthropocene-studies crowd.

Please include:

- The objections in the order they will come, grouped by which faction will raise them, each phrased the way that faction would actually phrase it — the stratigraphers in the vocabulary of unit definition and marker criteria, the Earth-system people in the vocabulary of signal and synchrony, the historian in the vocabulary of authority and disciplinary jurisdiction, the geographer in the vocabulary of naming and political economy.
- For each objection, a reply I could give in about two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is weakest and which follow-up would expose the weakness.
- An explicit ranking by how much damage each objection does to my position, not just by how likely it is; where the two orderings diverge, say so.
- What I should concede up front, before anyone has to ask, so that I am not cornered later.
- The one objection I am hoping nobody in the room thinks of, and why it is the most dangerous one for me.
- A short opening framing (three or four sentences) I can use if the first question is hostile, to move the discussion from whether my proposal is legitimate to what would have to be true for it to be legitimate.

Keep the whole thing practical: I will be standing at a lectern with index cards, and I need to be able to find the objection I am facing in under five seconds.
