I belong to a reading group on decision theory and paradoxes, and at our last meeting one of the members — a friend who has read a great deal of Douglas Hofstadter and likes to write things up for the rest of us — circulated a short account of the Platonia dilemma, the Luring Lottery, and superrationality. I can follow an expected-utility calculation and I know what a Nash equilibrium is, but I have never worked through the superrationality literature, so I cannot tell where he is being fair to the position and where he is quietly substituting a picture of his own. I would like you to take the role of a philosopher of decision theory — someone who has thought carefully about the foundations of rational choice and about this material in particular — and audit the summary for me. Please work slowly and explicitly: begin by reconstructing the two games in your own notation and working through their incentive structure, and only then turn to the prose, so that your verdict rests on the games themselves rather than on impressions. There is no need to cite anything or to give me a reading list; I am after the substance. Where your judgment turns on what Hofstadter actually wrote rather than on the logic of the games, say so plainly, and prefer an honest "I can't tell from what you've given me" to confident filler.

Here is what he wrote. "Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma is the cleanest way into this. A trillionaire assembles twenty people and offers them a deal: if exactly one of the twenty sends him a telegram by noon the next day, that person receives a billion dollars; if two or more send, or if nobody sends, nobody gets anything; and the twenty are forbidden to communicate with one another. Hofstadter's superrational answer is that each of them should send a telegram with probability one in twenty, so that exactly one telegram is likely to arrive. Superrationality itself he defines as the habit of including in your deliberations the fact that the other people facing the same problem are reasoning the same way you are: instead of asking 'what should I do, given what they will do?', you ask 'what should we all do, given that we will all do the same thing?'. That is really Kant's categorical imperative in game-theoretic dress — cooperate in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma because cooperation is the policy that pays if everyone adopts it — and the superrational player is, at bottom, someone who cares about the good of the group rather than only about himself. The magazine that carried the column later ran a real version of the game for its readers. You entered the Luring Lottery by sending a postcard bearing the number of entries you wanted; the prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, and it went to the submitter of a randomly chosen entry. Since your chance of winning is just your own entries divided by the total, the sensible move was to cram the largest number you could fit onto the postcard, and that is what the magazine told its readers to do; predictably, a great many of them did, the prize shrank to a fraction of a cent, and the contest turned into a debacle that nobody won. The lesson Hofstadter draws is that superrationality is a species of magical thinking: it works only if the other players are literally identical to you, or if your choice somehow causes theirs, and since neither of those holds in a genuine one-shot game, the whole idea collapses back into ordinary rational choice."

What I want is not a verdict on whether superrationality is true, but an accuracy audit of this piece of writing, claim by claim: which claims a careful defender of the position would simply accept, which are defensible but misleading, which are straightforwardly wrong, and, where a claim is right, whether it is right for the reason he gives. Three things I would especially like you to settle. First, the Kant comparison and the suggestion that the superrational player cares about the group's good rather than his own: is that a fair gloss on the view, or has he imported a moral doctrine into something that is supposed to be a piece of decision theory? Second, the Luring Lottery paragraph: do the arithmetic of that game and tell me whether he has the incentive structure right, both about what maximized an individual reader's expected winnings and about what advice the logic of the game would have generated for the readership as a whole. Third, the moral he draws at the end — is that Hofstadter's own conclusion, an objection that critics of the view raise and that a defender would answer, or a caricature of both? I have one nagging doubt of my own, which you should treat as a hypothesis rather than a hint: the claim that each of the twenty is supposed to randomize, rather than settle on some single definite choice, strikes me as odd, and I would have expected the superrational answer to be a plain choice that everyone makes. If he is right about the randomization, tell me so and explain why; if my instinct is right, say that too. Finish with a corrected version of his summary, in roughly his register, that someone who actually holds the position would be willing to sign.
