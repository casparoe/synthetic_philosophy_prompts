Ok, weird request, bear with me. I run a small city museum — about 40,000 visitors a year, one gallery anyone actually cares about — and Friday is the opening of a show I let myself get talked into: two paintings, side by side, same room. One is the picture we're known for. We bought it in 1961 as a work by an 1880s Dutch landscapist, and two years ago a lab found a pigment in it that wasn't available until the 1920s, so it's a fake. A very good one. The man who made it died in 1974, we're pretty sure it's him, the canvas matches three others, and these days he's the subject of a whole little industry of his own. The other painting is a genuine one by the same artist, and honestly it's in bad shape and a bit dull; everyone who's seen the two hanging together says the fake is the better picture.

So Friday I have to stand up and give the opening remarks. In the room: our donors, most of whom think I've lost my mind and am spending their money to show off a fraud; the painter's great-grandneice, who's been emailing me for four months and thinks we're turning her family into a joke; a local art historian who wrote to the paper saying the show rewards fraud and has pretty much called me a philistine in print; my staff, who did all the work; and my husband, who thinks this is the funniest thing that has ever happened to us.

I don't want to give the usual isn't-authenticity-fascinating museum talk. I want the remarks to come out of an actual view and let that view do the work. Here's what I think, and I want you to work from it: the wrong in a forgery is the lie, not the paint. If that's right, it matters enormously whether the lie is still running — the second the label says twentieth-century forgery, after X, there's no deception left, and whatever the picture is worth it's worth, and my donors' outrage is really about the money and the embarrassment. But there are two things I can't get past. First, if the fake can hang next to the real one on its merits, then the forger got exactly what he spent his life wanting, which was to be taken for the man he copied — and the niece will hear precisely that. Second, the picture looks identical to how it looked in 1961 and nobody's eye has changed, so if what I see when I stand in front of it is different now, then either the value was never in the looking, or I'm fooling myself about what changed.

Write the remarks. Five minutes, out loud, not a lecture, definately not a survey of the philosophy of forgery. Warm — these are people who gave us money and Saturdays and I like them — but it can't buy their comfort with something I don't believe, and the great-grandniece has to be able to sit through it. And don't end by having it both ways: if the picture is worth showing, say what it's worth showing as.
