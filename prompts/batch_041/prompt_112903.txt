I am producing a small, technically-minded philosophy podcast, and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent several years defending a reconstruction of Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. I want the episode to be about her reconstruction rather than about the objection as it is usually taught, so let me set out her position as I understand it, since the questions need to turn on its details.

Her view runs roughly as follows. The familiar version of the objection — the agent who saves his wife must, on the utilitarian account, add the further thought that the calculus permits him to save her, and this is one thought too many — is vulnerable to the self-effacement reply: a criterion of rightness need not double as a deliberative guide, so the theory can tell the agent to be moved by love while its criterion of rightness stays impartial. She concedes that reply at the level of deliberation and relocates the objection. Even a self-effacing theory, she argues, must in stating why the agent's act was right cite only the consequences; the agent's love for his wife figures in the theory's story as a cause of utility-maximising behaviour and never as a reason. She calls this the Reason-Substitution Claim. Her further move is to derive it from a general constraint, the Reasons-Availability Constraint: any theory of rightness must be able to state the reasons for which the actions it requires may be done. She takes that constraint to indict any theory that operates with a criterion of rightness — Kantian as well as utilitarian — while leaving particularist and virtue-theoretic approaches untouched. When objections come, she has one standing reply: whatever the objection is, it applies to every theory with a criterion of rightness, which she says is precisely her point.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press this position rather than inviting her to restate it. The audience will have read her book; nothing should prompt a summary of Williams's examples or of the self-effacement reply. At a minimum I want: a question on whether the Reason-Substitution Claim survives the sophisticated consequentialist's distinction between the features that make an act right and the reasons for which the agent may act, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; a question on whether the Reasons-Availability Constraint is a conceptual truth about rightness or an artifact of the morality system Williams himself spent his later work attacking, with a follow-up that exploits that tension; a question on whether the Reason-Substitution Claim is a conceptual or an empirical thesis, and what would count as evidence for it either way; a question on whether the nearest-and-dearest problem can be run without the integrity apparatus at all, since if it can, her unification of the two is idle; and a question on why particularism escapes the constraint, when the particularist must also give reasons for what she does.

Give each question its follow-up, keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Order the sequence so that her standing widening reply becomes progressively harder to use, and close with a question about what would count against the Reasons-Availability Constraint — what she would have to see to abandon it. No questions about how she came to the topic, and nothing that asks her to define her terms.
