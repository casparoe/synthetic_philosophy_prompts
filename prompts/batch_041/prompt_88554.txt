A friend of mine has been pressing on me a position she calls tranquilism: that the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and of suffering, and that pleasure, knowledge, achievement, and even affection are at best beside the point and at worst so much fuel for the craving that produces suffering. She is not a scholar of anything; she argues from a single image, which she deploys against every objection I raise. Craving, she says, is like thirst, and the things we crave are like salt water: the more of it you drink, the thirstier you become. From this she concludes that the wise course is not to find better water but to stop drinking — to give up the pursuit of the objects of desire altogether, and to want only the end of wanting.

I would like the analogy audited rather than merely resisted or endorsed. Please set out the correspondence table — what in the image is meant to stand for what — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in four things. First, whether the image can represent the distinction the tranquilist herself needs, between craving and suffering, or whether it collapses the two by making the craving itself the pain. Second, whether it presupposes the conclusion: the image carries the moral she draws only if every object of craving is already salt water, and that is precisely what is in dispute. Third, whether the remedy available inside the image is available in the target; a thirsty person can simply decline to drink, but it is not obvious that one can decline to crave by an act of will. Fourth, whether the image, which implies that fresh water exists and that thirst is a healthy response to it, quietly concedes that some desires have proper objects and are satisfied by them — in which case its lesson would be to seek the right object, not to abandon objects as such.

She reaches for a second image when the first comes under pressure: the mind as a glass of muddy water, which clears of itself if you leave it alone, so that effort and intervention only keep it stirred up. Tell me whether that image does better where the first fails, whether the two in fact support the same conclusion or pull in different directions, and whether either of them smuggles in a positive good — clarity, freshness, relief — into a view whose whole point is that only absences matter.

Finally, if the tranquilist needs an analogy and neither of these will do, what would a better one have to preserve, and what would it look like? I am not after the provenance of these images; I want the mapping, the breakages that matter for her argument, and a verdict on whether her conclusion survives once they are on the table.
