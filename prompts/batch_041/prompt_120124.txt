I have been going to a reading group that is slowly working through the historiography of science, and one of the members — a friend who reads the same blogs I do and has picked up the habit of testing any thesis by asking what else it would take down — has been defending an argument about simultaneous discovery that I cannot get out of my head, mainly because I cannot find the loose thread. He states it like this.

(1) When two people working without contact reach the same result within a few years of each other — Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, Darwin and Wallace, whose sketches were read out together at the Linnean Society, Bolyai and Lobachevsky on hyperbolic geometry, Bell and Gray on the telephone — the result was in the air: the instruments, the accumulated literature, and the state of the problem had made it available to anyone competent enough to look.

(2) If the result was available to anyone competent, then what explains its being found when it was found is the state of the field, not the particular person who found it.

(3) So had A not found it, B or someone like B would have found it at more or less the same time, and had B not, A or someone like A would have. The individual is a courier, not an author.

(4) Credit is owed only for what one's action makes a difference to; nobody deserves credit for the sunrise.

(5) A's action made no difference to whether the discovery was made at that time, since it would have been made anyway. So A deserves no credit for it.

(6) Nothing in (1) through (5) turns on there being two of them. Every discovery, including the one nobody else happened to be working on, is made against a background that made it available; if the background explains the discovery, the lone worker is a courier too. So nobody deserves credit for any discovery.

(7) And discovery is not special. Every painting, reform, and institution is the upshot of conditions that would have produced something like it, so nobody deserves credit for anything.

(8) Including, presumably, the credit he is quietly claiming for having constructed this argument.

He thinks (1) through (4) are as firm as anything in the philosophy of history and that the conclusion at (7) and (8) is a reductio not of his premises but of our attachment to credit. I think he is wrong somewhere, but every exit I try feels expensive. Give up (4) and I have to say what desert could rest on if not difference-making, and I lose the ability to say that a fraud who changed nothing deserves less credit than a researcher who changed something. Give up (3) and I seem committed to a great-man theory on which the field might have waited another century for want of one insight, which is precisely what the evidence of multiple discovery seems to rule out. Give up (6) and I have to explain why the argument works when there are two discoverers and fails when there is one, and I cannot see what the second person is doing. And (7) looks to me like a straight generalization of (6) with the word discovery swapped out.

So tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. I would like to keep (1), since I do not want to pretend that the state of the field is irrelevant or that priority disputes are mostly about genius, and I would like to keep (4), since credit that does not track difference-making looks to me like nothing but applause. But I cannot keep (8), and I do not think I can keep (7). I suspect something happens between (3) and (6) that I cannot name, though it is possible I am simply flinching. If the argument is valid and one of the steps I like is just false, say so and tell me what the rest of my views have to look like afterwards.
