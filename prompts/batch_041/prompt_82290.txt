So i just got elected to my town's library board — weirdest thing that's happened to me, i mostly ran because they were going to cut the children's section — and in my second meeting we burned like 40 minutes on whether one of the other members could vote for a book-removal policy "because my church teaches that this stuff is harmful to kids." Half the room acted like that was obviously out of bounds and the other half acted like telling her she can't say that is itself the outrage, and i walked out realizing i don't actually know what my own view is here, let alone anybody else's. So i want to build myself a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on religious arguments in politics — the kind of thing that could sort a room like that by what people actually think, not by what they call themselves.

Here's the angle i care about, because otherwise this just collapses into a Rawls summary: i want every branch node to be labeled by the question that splits it, and i think the real fault lines are (a) whether the constraint lands on the content of the reason, on the speaker's willingness to also offer a non-religious argument later, or on the outcome — whether the resulting law is one the dissenters could accept; (b) whether it binds ordinary citizens, elected officials, or only the state at the moment it actually coerces somebody; and (c) whether there's any asymmetry between religious premises and secular "comprehensive" ones at all, since someone can always say the secular liberal is leaning on contested stuff too. Start from the logical space — the questions and which combinations of answers they allow — and only then drop the actual positions into it, including the strict "you need a secular rationale before you vote" view, the permissive "religious reasons are just reasons like any other" view, the proviso-style "start religious, translate in due course" view, and whatever the wide / overlapping-consensus versions turn out to be. Each leaf should carry the name if it has one, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one line on the most awkward thing that leaf has to swallow. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually pull apart, and for the combinations nobody occupies, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored — i have a suspicion the empty cells are more interesting than the full ones.

Use concrete cases as the branch questions so a normal person could answer without knowing any of the labels: the board member voting her church's line on the book policy, a citizen at the public hearing who testifies only in religious terms and won't offer anything else, a religious voter picking a candidate mainly for his faith, a legislator who leads with scripture but is happy to also cite the studies, and a case where the law comes out the same either way so the only live question is the reasoning. Ten-ish questions and maybe six to eight leaves, and please don't just hand me author names and dates — i can't look anything up right now and the program i'll eventually feed this to definately can't either, so describe each position by what it says about the cases instead. It's for me and three friends, all adults, all opinionated, none of us trained philosophers, so keep it usable at a kitchen table.
