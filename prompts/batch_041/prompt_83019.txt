My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about the paradox of deontology — whether it can ever be right to do something wrong in order to prevent a larger number of wrongs of the same kind — and we went to bed at two in the morning no further forward than when we started. What I want from you is not a verdict on the debate itself but a prediction about her, together with an account of the rule she is really running. She is not a philosopher by trade, but she is unusually consistent, and over the years she has given me firm verdicts on the following.

- Killing one healthy patient and distributing his organs to five patients who will otherwise die: wrong.
- Diverting a runaway trolley onto a side track, where it kills one person and saves five: permissible.
- Pushing a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path in order to stop it: wrong.
- Diverting the trolley into a closed loop, where it is halted by striking a person who happens to be standing there; his death is foreseen but not intended, and it is his body that does the halting: wrong.
- Killing one innocent person, where that is the only way to stop five other people from each committing one murder: wrong.
- The same case, with a hundred murders prevented rather than five: wrong.
- Killing a person who is at that moment about to kill five others: permissible.
- Lying to a would-be murderer about where his intended victim is hiding: permissible.

I had assumed she was simply tracking what is intended as against what is merely foreseen, until the fourth case, which she calls wrong even though the death there is foreseen and not intended.

The case she has not yet ruled on is this. A person I shall call the Broker has arranged matters so that, unless I kill one innocent stranger, ten thousand people — each of them unknown to me, none acting on any instruction of mine, and none of them yet culpable of anything — will each commit one murder over the coming year. The Broker will not do any of the killing himself, cannot be identified, and cannot be stopped; the only thing available to me is to kill the stranger. If I kill her, the arrangement dissolves and none of the murders occurs. Assume also that the situation is unique, secret and unrepeatable: it will never recur, nobody will ever learn what I did, and my action cannot become a precedent or be imitated. The stranger is innocent, does not consent, and is not otherwise doomed.

What I would like from you: her verdict on that case, and how confident you are of it; the candidate rules that fit the eight verdicts above, and what each of them predicts here; which rule she is most likely running, and why; an honest statement of where the record leaves the new case genuinely undetermined; and the further case, ideally one I can actually put to her, that would settle it. I can see that she is some kind of deontologist, but I cannot tell whether what she is doing is a means principle, a doctrine of double effect, a threshold view, or something else again, so please work that out rather than asking me. And please do not tell me what a deontologist is supposed to say; tell me what she will say, and why.
