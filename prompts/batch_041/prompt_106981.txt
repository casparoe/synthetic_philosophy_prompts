I am assembling a session for an undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I want an activity that lets students arrive at a pair of ideas instead of being told about them. The ideas are the twin inversions of reasoning associated with Darwin and with Turing: Darwin's, that design and apparent purpose can be produced by a process that has neither a designer nor a purpose; Turing's, that genuine intelligent competence can be produced by an arrangement of parts that understand nothing whatsoever. The slogan for the second is competence without comprehension, and the thought behind both is that a regress gets stopped by something mindless — order explained by what has no order in it, thought explained by what does not think.

The setting: sixteen to twenty participants, one hundred minutes, a room with movable chairs, no computers, no money. Roughly half are philosophy students who know the Darwinian material well and the computational material not at all. Design a two-part activity, runnable as written, that lets them feel both inversions in their hands before either is named.

For the first part, I want the group to constitute a mechanism. Participants should be nodes in a fixed network, each holding a private rule that is trivially easy to execute and says nothing about the network's overall task — the rules can be handed out on sealed cards generated in advance from a circuit whose global function I have deliberately not worked out myself. Inputs go in, outputs come out, and the competence of the whole is real: the network does something determinate with the inputs. The sting is that no node, and no participant, can state what the network computes. The group should then be forced into reverse engineering: infer the input-output function from behaviour alone, and discover that they can predict it perfectly while nobody can explain it. Arrange the rules so that at least one node is, in the local sense, doing something that would look deliberate if you watched only that node.

For the second part, I want a breeder with no intention. Give the group a target they are forbidden to aim at directly: they may only copy with error and then let a selection rule decide who propagates, with the fitness criterion hidden from them. Something well adapted to that hidden criterion should appear, and nobody should be able to claim authorship of it. Make the error rate a knob the group can turn, so that they can feel the point where too much variation destroys what has been accumulated — the difference between a population that can still be improved and one that has dissolved into noise.

Neither part should be introduced as an illustration of anything. The vocabulary of competence, comprehension, design, selection and algorithm belongs only in the debrief. In the debrief, map specific moments onto specific pieces of the ideas: node-level ignorance against network-level competence; the reverse-engineering step as the ordinary situation of anyone studying a mind; the regress-stopping move; the error threshold. I also want the students to meet an objection from inside the activity rather than from me — the participant who points out that someone did design the network, whereas nothing designed the biosphere — so build in whatever is needed for that objection to arise naturally and to be pressable.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: the ways a network stalls, the ways the breeding game either collapses into noise or converges too fast, the ways one participant becomes a bottleneck or an oracle, the point where I will be tempted to explain too soon, and what to do at each.

Then tell me, frankly, what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself. I expect the deepest gap to be that my students understand their own local rules, while transistors and cells do not, and that no staging can turn a person into a genuinely non-comprehending component; the timescales and the total absence of a designer in the Darwinian case may be others.

Throughout the debrief notes, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not hand me a contested view dressed as the consensus. I take it as settled that selection acting on heritable variation can produce adaptation with no designer, and settled that the global competence of a network need not be located in any of its parts. I expect the claim that the Darwinian and the Turingian inversions are one idea rather than a suggestive analogy to be contested, and I want that flagged rather than asserted; likewise the question whether such a system literally comprehends anything, as opposed to merely behaving as though it did, is exactly the live dispute, and the session must not pretend to have settled it. Mark which claims in the debrief are safe to state flatly and which need the disagreement made explicit.
