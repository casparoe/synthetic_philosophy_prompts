I sit on my state wildlife commission's listing subcommittee in my capacity as the resident philosopher of science, and we vote on Thursday whether to recommend protection for an isolated population of a spring-dwelling minnow. Let me give you the shape of the case, because the decision turns on a philosophical problem our statute pretends not to have. The population is morphologically almost indistinguishable from the downstream population; it is genetically divergent at mitochondrial and several nuclear markers; and where the spring overflows, the two interbreed along a narrow hybrid zone, so they are not fully reproductively isolated. A genome-wide reanalysis that might settle the relationship is roughly eighteen months away, while groundwater pumping could dry the spring within five. Our enabling statute directs us to protect both species and distinct population segments but defines neither, and the petition is filed under the species heading. Two of my colleagues read the case through a reproductive-isolation criterion and conclude this is at most a population segment; two others read it through a diagnosability criterion and conclude it is a species. Our counsel warns that a species listing which a court later reads as a population-segment listing could be vacated on procedural grounds, leaving the population unprotected for the duration of the litigation. The chair has floated a rule she calls the reversibility test: where a listing is easy to undo and the harm of waiting is not, we should act first.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should say what evidentiary standard we should be applying and why that standard rather than a stricter or looser one. It should state the strongest case for the recommendation I am inclined to resist and answer it rather than dismiss it. And it should be explicit about which parts of the underlying science and philosophy are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, so that the committee does not mistake a live dispute for a consensus. I would rather have a defensible decision than a comfortable one.
