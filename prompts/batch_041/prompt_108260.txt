I spent thirty-eight years as a field ecologist, most of it designing long-term population studies and arguing with referees over whether a difference of two standard errors deserved a sentence, and I have spent the two years since my retirement doing what several colleagues warned me against: reading philosophy in earnest. This term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on transformative experience, and I have arrived at a set of views that I hold with some confidence one at a time and that I now suspect cannot all be held together. I would like a candid audit of them before I present anything to the group.

The views are these. (A) Practical rationality is expected-utility maximisation and nothing more: a choice is rational when it maximises expected utility relative to my credences and my preferences, and where the evidence is thin the remedy is better evidence or a broader credence, never a different theory. (B) The distinction between epistemically transformative experiences, whose character cannot be known in advance without undergoing them, and personally transformative experiences, which change what one cares about at the root, marks something real and pervasive; such experiences are not curiosities but include the choices that have mattered most in my own life and in the lives of everyone I know. (C) I have no patience with the revelation manoeuvre — the suggestion that in these cases one may rationally choose the transformation for its own sake because the discovery is itself valuable; either that is an ordinary expected-utility judgement in costume, or it is not a reason at all. (D) I am a subjectivist about welfare: a life goes well for the person living it exactly to the extent that it satisfies that person's preferences, suitably informed and coherent, and there is no further, preference-independent fact about what is good for them. (E) I do not believe in a fixed evaluative self: after a personally transformative experience the resulting person may hold preferences discontinuous with mine, and I do not think my present preferences have any authority over what is good for the person who emerges. (F) I think a person can be harmed by a transformation that they would, afterwards, sincerely endorse and would not wish undone; when an institution arranges someone's preferences to fit the station it has assigned them, and the person comes to prefer that station, I take it that something has been done to them rather than merely for them. (G) I hold that a medical intervention is permissible only where the patient can genuinely be informed of what they are consenting to, and that a patient's assurance that they will understand it once they have been through it is no consent at all.

Please tell me where these genuinely conflict and how badly, ordered by severity rather than presented as a heap of equal difficulties. I would like to be told which apparent conflicts are only apparent, and why they dissolve, rather than have them flagged along with the rest; and for each genuine tension I would like to know what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. I do not know which of these commitments is load-bearing and which is merely a habit of thought I have mistaken for a principle, so I would rather you work that out than take my framing on trust. Finish with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{(X): N}, where X is the letter of the one commitment I should surrender first and N is the severity, from 1 to 5, of the tension that forces the surrender.
