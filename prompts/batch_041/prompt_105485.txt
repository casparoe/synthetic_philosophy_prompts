I have a draft essay for a graduate seminar on Grice, and I'd like detailed comments on it. I care much less about prose style than about whether the argument is sound, whether the distinction it proposes is genuine, and whether I've represented Grice fairly. The draft follows.

Title: Implicature and Insinuation: A Gap in Grice's Account

Thesis: Grice's account of conversational implicature rests on reflexive intention: a speaker implicates something when she intends to convey it by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention. I argue that this condition cannot distinguish a genuine implicature from what I will call an insinuation. Both can satisfy the intention condition. What separates them is not psychological but normative — whether the speaker can be held to the conveyed content or can disavow it without inconsistency. Because Grice models the maxims as descriptions of rational cooperation rather than as entitlements the hearer may rely on, his framework has no place for this accountability dimension. I therefore propose that insinuation is a distinct illocutionary act, not a species of implicature.

The example I lean on throughout: a hiring-committee chair, asked whether a candidate was passed over for having raised a complaint, replies that the committee weighs many factors and that candor matters a great deal to them. The chair plainly wants the hearer to infer that the complaint counted against the candidate. If pressed, however, the chair can insist she meant only that candor is valued, and this disavowal is not obviously a lie.

Grice, as I read him, would first ask whether the chair intended the hearer to draw the inference and intended the hearer to recognize that intention. If both are present, the utterance is an implicature; if the chair merely foresaw the inference without intending it, it is not. I claim this test cannot do the work asked of it, because the chair can satisfy the full reflexive-intention condition and still retain deniability — and deniability is precisely what distinguishes her utterance from a straightforward implicature such as a pointed remark that some candidates are more candid than others. I conclude that the maxims are better read as licenses the hearer is entitled to use than as obligations the speaker must meet, and that insinuation consists in leaning on such a license while reserving the right to revoke it.

What I want from you:

1. Is the implicature/insinuation distinction genuine, or does it collapse into something Grice already had — for instance into the difference between what is implicated and what is merely suggested, or into his treatment of flouting and exploitation? If the distinction is merely verbal, say so.
2. Does Grice's apparatus in fact lack the resources I claim it lacks? If the normative dimension I describe can be reconstructed from what he already says about calculability, cancellability, or the Cooperative Principle, I need to know before I build further on the gap.
3. Is the hiring-committee example doing the work I need, or is it ambiguous in a way that lets a reader dissolve the case instead of confronting the argument? Suggest a cleaner one if so.
4. Where does the essay overstate? I suspect the move from "the maxims are descriptions" to "the framework cannot accommodate accountability" is too quick, but I haven't located the exact step.

Please be blunt and committed rather than even-handed. If the central distinction is confused, if I have mischaracterized the intention condition, or if the essay conflates speaker meaning with hearer uptake somewhere I haven't noticed, tell me plainly instead of hedging or building on the premise as given. I would rather be told the thesis fails than receive a balanced summary of its strengths and weaknesses. If the task as I've posed it rests on a misreading of Grice, say that directly and explain what the correct reading would change.
