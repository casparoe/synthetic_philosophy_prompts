The question:

Coastal Stormwater Retention Act (hypothetical -- I wrote it for the exam so nobody could look it up)

§ 4(a) No new commercial structure exceeding five thousand square feet in gross floor area shall be issued a certificate of occupancy unless at least fifty percent of the structure's roof surface is covered by a living roof.

§ 4(b) "Living roof" means a roof assembly in which a layer of growing medium at least three inches deep supports live vegetation.

§ 4(c) This section shall not apply to any structure the primary function of which is the collection of solar energy.

§ 4(d) For the purposes of this section, "roof surface" does not include any area occupied by mechanical equipment, skylights, or photovoltaic panels.

Preamble. The legislature finds that urban heat islands and combined sewer overflow are the two principal stormwater problems facing the state, and that dispersed retention of rainfall at the point where it falls is the most cost-effective means of abatement available to the state.

Facts. Ridgeline Logistics wants to build a 6,000 sq ft warehouse. The roof plan is 3,000 sq ft of living roof and 3,000 sq ft of photovoltaic array. The array supplies most of the building's electricity, but the building is a warehouse -- nobody makes a living there by generating solar. The agency proposes to deny the certificate, on the ground that the structure is not primarily for the collection of solar energy and that only half the roof is living roof.

What I asked: Does § 4 require the agency to issue the certificate? Answer once as a textualist and once as a purposivist, then say whether the two methods converge on this case and why.

The student's answer:

The issue is whether the Ridgeline warehouse satisfies § 4(a) or falls within the § 4(c) exemption.

Textualism. A textualist asks what the enacting legislature actually intended the words to do, so the first move is to consult the legislative history -- committee reports, floor statements, and the preamble -- to recover that intent. Here the preamble is clear that the target is stormwater, so the legislature cannot have meant to exempt a warehouse whose roof is only half solar. On the words themselves, "primary function" is vague, but a warehouse's primary function is storage, not electricity generation, so § 4(c) doesn't apply. That leaves § 4(a): the living roof covers 3,000 of the building's 6,000 square feet of roof, exactly 50%, which just clears the "at least fifty percent" threshold. So the certificate must issue.

Purposivism. A purposivist doesn't stop at the words; she asks which outcome does the most good overall, treating the statute as one input among several. The preamble tells us the legislature's real goal is dispersed retention of rainfall, and a living roof over half the building does retain rainfall where it falls, so the purposive analysis favors issuing the certificate. The solar carve-out exists to avoid burdening renewable energy, and here the array is a secondary feature of a warehouse, so it doesn't swallow the rule.

Conclusion. Both methods converge on the same result, so the agency must issue the certificate. The case is easier than it looks: the only real work is noticing that "primary function" is doing all the heavy lifting in § 4(c), and both methods read it the same way. And note that a textualist would refuse to look at the preamble at all, which is exactly why the convergence here is worth flagging -- you get the same answer either way.
