ok so my friend Nadia is an evolutionary biologist, her old labmate Devon does cognitive science, and the three of us ended up arguing about this for like two and a half hours last night and now I honestly can't tell who was making sense. I mostly refilled drinks. Heres the setup, and then the exchange.

The thing they were arguing about: a pre-registered study run in eight small-scale societies, maybe 1200 people total. You play a one-shot anonymous sharing game right after recalling a time you were betrayed. Two versions of the betrayal: by a romantic partner, or by a business partner. After the romantic one people gave away about 15% less. After the business one, nothing — if anything slightly more. Effect held in all eight sites, and it vanished when the betrayer was a same-sex friend. Thats all I know about it, I'm not vouching for the study.

Nadia: this is how it's supposed to be done. She says the mechanism is a mate-directed vigilance system — it reads betrayal by a mate as information about whether investing in relationships pays, and business betrayal simply isn't the input it was built to take. She says the tell is the interaction. A boring bad-mood-makes-you-stingy story predicts everyone gives less, and they didn't. A designed mechanism should be picky about its inputs, and pickiness is a prediction you can flunk. She calls that the signature test. She also said an optimality claim like this owes you three things — what's being maximized, what constrains it, and what the options were — and she thinks the study supplies all three.

Devon: interactions are cheap. You can always find a condition where an effect switches off, and switch-offs are exactly the kind of thing you rationalize after the fact — oh, that input isn't the one the system detects. He says the real problem is that nothing here tells you what the system is for. For a heart you can measure output and load and pressure. For this, the ancestral problem is read off the result itself, so the explanation runs in a circle. When Nadia said the ancestral environment isn't a time or a place, it's just shorthand for whatever selection pressures built the trait, he said great, then name one human psychological selection pressure anyone has measured rather than inferred from the behavior it's meant to explain. He also said universal doesn't mean evolved — eight societies can share a response because they share markets, marriage rules, religions, whatever. Cross-cultural sameness only helps if you find a place where the culture predicts one thing and the adaptation story predicts another.

Nadia: fair about universals, but she said historical sciences do this constantly — geology, cosmology, historical linguistics — and demanding a direct experiment on the Pleistocene is just demanding the science not exist. She said the byproduct move is worse than the adaptation move: calling it a byproduct of reputation management just pushes the adaptation back a step, and if the byproduct fits the ancestral problem that neatly, Devon owes an account of why. She said if you need a measured ancestral pressure for every functional claim you'd have to throw out half of biology.

Devon: fit between design and task is the thing in dispute, so it can't be the evidence for it. And he said if you can't say what would count as the trait not being an adaptation, the claim isn't doing any work.

Then they started fighting about the paper itself and I lost the thread.
