I'm a freelance features writer — science and ideas, the sort of thing that ends up in the back half of a weekend mag — and I've just had a 6,000-word piece commisioned on consent. Not just the sexy stuff either: medical consent forms, the GDPR clickwrap business, NDAs, capacity assessments, the whole spread. My working thesis is that everyone waves the word consent about as if we all agree what makes it valid, and we plainly don't.

What I need from you isn't a survey or a reading list — I can't read forty papers and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. I want to know which open questions about what makes consent valid are actually load-bearing: the ones where, if you settled one, a heap of the others would quietly fall into place, or the law would have to shift. And which ones look meaty but are dead ends that won't change a word of the piece.

The things I keep bumping into: whether coercion is measured against what you're morally entitled to or against what you'd have got in a fair market (which seems to settle whether a lousy job offer is coercive or merely grim); whether consent is really a mental state or a public act, and what we do when the two come apart — someone says yes and doesn't mean it, someone means it and says nothing; how much deception has to matter before the consent is void; and whether competence is a threshold you're over or under, or a sliding scale that depends on what you're consenting to. A philosopher mate reckons the sleeper issue is that consent can be perfectly valid and still not make the thing alright — valid but not licence-granting. Is she right that that's the one to build around, or is that the sort of thing only philosophers care about? I already know the standard checklist of elements of valid consent, so skip that, and skip the social-construction angle — I want the seperate, harder questions.

So: rank the two or three I should build the piece around, and defend the order. What makes a question worth a chunk of my word count — does it move law or practice, does it change what ordinary people ought to do, does it unlock other questions, is it even tractable without a lab? It's for a general-readership quarterly, but I'd love it to be the sort of thing that gets quoted in a law-reform consultation, so weight the practical ones accordingly. And tell me what would change your ranking — what I'd have to see or read in the next year to move something up or down. Flag the red herrings too. Be blunt; I'd rather find out now than 5,000 words in.
