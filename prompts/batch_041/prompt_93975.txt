I'm a first-year PhD student working at the intersection of epistemology and AI, and I've been trying to get clear on the so-called problem of logical omniscience. As I understand it, the worry is that epistemic logic credits agents with knowing the logical consequences of everything they know — an assumption that is plainly false of human beings and, for that matter, of any actual theorem prover or language model. My instinct, then, has been to treat the whole framework as empirically refuted and to look elsewhere. But my advisor keeps insisting the issue is more delicate than that, and I can't quite see why. Here is the view I'd like you to assess, taking the role of an expert in philosophical logic and the epistemology of reasoning: since the defect is that agents are credited with too much, the obvious repair is to build resource-boundedness directly into the semantics — an agent knows φ just in case φ can be derived from its explicitly held beliefs in at most k steps. That seems to me to capture exactly what is missing while leaving the rest of the machinery intact, and I can't think what could be wrong with it.

Could you help me think this through carefully? Please start by setting out, in your own words, what the problem of logical omniscience actually is and what kind of claim it embodies — a false empirical hypothesis about reasoners, a limitation of a particular idealization, or something else altogether. Then evaluate my proposed "k-step" fix: does it do the work I want, or does it bring difficulties of its own? I'm especially interested in whether the trouble lies only in agents knowing too many consequences of what they already believe, or whether there is a further and arguably more awkward difficulty involving logical truths themselves, and in how any of this bears on the distinction between what an agent may be said to know implicitly and what it actually entertains. I'd rather you diagnose where my framing goes wrong than simply reassure me that the literature contains "solutions."
