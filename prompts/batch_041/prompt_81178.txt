Take the post-9/11 FBI stings and run them through Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. The Newburgh Four is the one I mean: the informant comes up with the plot and picks the targets, the FBI supplies the Stinger and the bomb, the four of them turn up with nothing but talk and no money or skills, and they get convicted anyway. The Second Circuit upheld it 2-1, the dissent saying Cromitie was badgered into it and that there was scarce evidence he'd have done any of it on his own. My hunch is that the informant is a Frankfurt intervener who never got the memo about staying offstage: in Frankfurt's story the intervener does nothing, so what happens is down to the agent, which is why we hold him responsible, whereas in a sting the intervener definately does intervene, and yet the law ends up asking the exact question Frankfurt says doesn't matter, namely would he have done it anyway, i.e. predisposition. So work it both ways. First, is entrapment doctrine even intelligible on the actual-sequence view, or does it show that what's really doing the work isn't responsibility but something else, like whether the state has standing to punish someone it more or less built? Then push back the other way: what does the sting do to the Frankfurt machinery? Two specific things I want. (a) The one doctrine that looks at what the government did rather than at what the defendant would have done, the outrageous government conduct doctrine, is basically a dead letter, Russell floated it in dictum and it has pretty much never worked since. Is that a fact about courts or a fact about the theory, i.e. does the actual-sequence view simply have nothing to say when the state is a co-author? (b) The sting defendant could have walked away, so there's a flicker of freedom, and yet the conviction still smells. Does that vindicate Frankfurt, or does it show that responsibility is the wrong vocabulary for whatever is bugging us about these cases? And say where Frankfurt goes quiet instead of stretching it to fit.
