I sit on the research-ethics committee at a neuro-rehab hospital, and we've started getting protocols that don't just stimulate or record from brain tissue, they replace it — a hippocampal prosthetic that takes over a bit more of the job each month, and one proposal to swap out a whole cortical column for a chip doing the same work, in stages over about a year. Next year someone will send us a destructive scan and copy. We meet twice a month, there's two clinicians, a lawyer, a philosopher and two lay members, roughly an hour per protocol, and whatever we sign off on can't be undone. I need a decision procedure we can actually run in a sitting, not an essay about personal identity.

Give me the procedure as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us as a value call rather than a finding. It has to give us two things separately: whether we may treat whoever wakes up as the same person for the purposes we actually care about (consenting to carry on in the trial, advance directives, who signs, who's liable if the chip misfires), and whether what the patient cared about is preserved. I don't assume those come apart in the same place, so don't let the procedure collapse them into one.

Three things I want built in. First, track what I'll call narrow continuity (memory, character, plans, linked causally) alongside wide continuity (same living body, same brain as an organ), because our legal forms lean on the body and our ethics leans on the psychology, and the procedure needs to say what to do when they point different ways. Second, a parity constraint: whatever we conclude about the slow step-by-step case we have to conclude about the one-shot case, unless we can point to a difference that isn't just speed — so make the procedure force us to name that difference or give up the distinction. Third, a two-claimants rule: if there are two successors both claiming to be the patient, identity can't go to both, but the procedure still has to say what each of them is owed. And keep the metaphysical verdict seperate from the practical one, so it can tell us when treating someone as the same person is a legitimate fiction for our purposes and when its just a lie.

Run it on three: 30% staged replacement with the patient awake, lucid and reporting she feels the same; full replacement completed over two years with the last of the biological tissue destroyed at the end; and a destructive scan producing two running copies, original gone, where the question is whether either copy can consent to the next phase. At least one of those should come out in a way we won't like — if it doesn't, you've been too easy on us. Say what the procedure can't settle, and mark every point where we're deciding rather than discovering. Also tell me how confident you are in the claims you make and which single step you're least sure of. Write it in British English. No reading list please, I can't check citations and I'd rather you just argued it.
