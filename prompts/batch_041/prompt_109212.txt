A seminar I am in has been working through the Bayesian case for the claim that information is never bad for you — the result, usually labelled Good's theorem after a short 1967 note of his on the principle of total evidence, that the expected value of a free observation is non-negative for an expected-utility maximiser. The proof, as I understand it, is nearly contentless: whatever act maximises expected utility under your present information, its expected utility is at most the expectation, taken over the possible results of the observation, of the utility of the act that would maximise under the refined information, since you can always ignore what you learn. I have come to hold the conclusion rather firmly, and I now catch myself treating any apparent counterexample as a confusion — as a case where the information was not really free, or the agent was not really an expected-utility maximiser, or an observation with side effects was being smuggled in under the description of information. I would like you to press on this, because I suspect my confidence is doing more work than the theorem is.

So: what would actually count against the claim, and how much? I want the escape routes set out one at a time — which premise of the argument could fail, what the agent or the world would have to be like for it to fail, and which of those possibilities are live rather than merely logically available. Please keep separate the several things that can be meant by the value of information: the value the agent expects from her own prior before the observation, the value she will actually realise once she has it, and the value of the information to someone else or to the group, all of which seem to me to come apart, though I could not say how much turns on it. A classmate maintains that the proof is a trick — that because the agent may always ignore what she learns, non-negativity is built into the setup, and the theorem therefore tells us nothing about when information is worth having; I want your view on whether that is a fair diagnosis of the result or a confusion about what the result is for. The objection that always gets raised in the seminar is the patient who would rather not learn a prognosis she can do nothing about; I suspect this case is doing something other than refuting the theorem, but I want to know precisely what it is doing, and I want the less obvious challenges that do not look like that one.

A few further requests. Say what a defender of the theorem would himself concede as a genuine limitation, as distinct from what an opponent would merely assert. Say something about whether the result survives when other agents are watching — when what you learn is also evidence to them about what you will do — and about the case in which the agent who receives the information is not the agent who will have to act on it, or will not act on it well; I have an inarticulate sense that the theorem's force comes from assuming a single, stable, ideally rational decision-maker at a single moment in time, and I want to know whether that is a fair charge or a misreading. Then rank the defeaters you have assembled from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the position, once it has absorbed all the qualifications a defender would want, still risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated into something that no possible case could tell against — and if the latter, whether that is a defect or simply what a theorem of this shape should be expected to look like. Finally, if you had to bet on one consideration as the one most likely to move someone who currently thinks as I do, which would it be? I am not after a reading list; I want the argument.
