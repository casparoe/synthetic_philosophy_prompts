A recurring annoyance at literary festivals: someone drops 'the paradox of tolerance' — the idea that tolerance taken all the way cancels itself, so a tolerant society has to be intolerant of the intolerant — and it's always being made to do some very specific job. Last week a panelist used it to argue we shouldn't have booked a novelist with vile views, and I couldn't tell wether he was making a logical point (a rule that permits its own destruction is incoherent), an empirical one (tolerant societies that tolerate the intolerant get taken over, Weimar and all that), or a moral one (there's a duty to be intolerant of intolerance, and here's who owes it and to whom). Can you seperate the precise claims hiding in that one line — what 'tolerance' is supposed to mean there, who has to tolerate whom, whether it's about acts or speech or people or whole movements — and say for each whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or just the slogan restating itself? And which of those readings is actually doing the work when someone uses it to keep a person off the bill? I want a menu I can carry around, not a lecture.
