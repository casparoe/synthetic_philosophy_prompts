I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the early days of Overcoming Bias and LessWrong, and the habit I most want to pass on from that material is a refusal to let a word do work it has not earned. I run a small, serious reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics — a dozen people, mostly graduate students in physics and philosophy — and we have just spent three sessions on the ontology of the quantum state. The discussion keeps collapsing into a slogan contest: someone says the wavefunction is real, someone else says it is only information, and the room relitigates the same twenty minutes.

I am setting the take-home exam for the end of the unit. Please draft one essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme.

The question should be self-contained: closed book, 45 minutes, 25 marks, and the examinee should not need to recall who proved what or when. Spell out the content of the no-go result of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph in the question stem — the 2012 argument that, under certain assumptions, two distinct pure quantum states cannot be compatible with the same underlying physical state, so the quantum state must be ontic rather than epistemic — so that the theorem's name is a convenience rather than the answer.

I want the question to force four things, and a student who can manage only the first should not pass.

First, say what the result actually establishes, name at least one assumption whose failure reopens the epistemic door, and say what the resulting view costs — what it has to give up.

Second, produce a view that satisfies the result while denying that the wavefunction is a physical field or substance: a view on which the wavefunction is real in something like the way a law, or a pattern of relations, is real. Then say what such a view is still committed to.

Third, handle a case I will describe in the stem. Suppose someone holds that what is fundamental is the ray in Hilbert space, and that the wavefunction as a vector is merely one representative of it, differing from other representatives only by an unobservable phase. The examinee should say what this view denies, whether it is ontic in the relevant sense, and whether the no-go result has any bite on it at all.

Fourth, say what work the word real is doing at each step. An answer that cannot distinguish the claim that the description is injective into the underlying reality from the claim that the description is itself part of that reality has not answered.

Please make the marking scheme five must-hit points worth 5 marks each, one of which must concern the scope conditions of the result rather than its conclusion, and each with a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss it. Calibrate the scheme so that a fluent answer which says the result shows the wavefunction is real and stops lands in the middle of the range rather than at the top.

Two constraints. Neither the question nor the model answer should turn on names, dates, or titles, and the scheme should not reward name-dropping; where a name appears, the question must already supply the content that the name labels. And the model answer should be honest about what stays open — I would rather it end on the residual difficulty than manufacture a verdict.
