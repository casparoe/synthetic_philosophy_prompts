I teach a 400-level seminar on consent — we do autonomy, the "moral magic of consent" stuff, the mental-state vs. communication split, and every year the last couple of weeks turn into the same fight, both between two camps in the room and between two camps in my own head. On one side: consent is at bottom an attitude, a state of the will — what makes a touch or a transfer permissible is that the person is genuinely willing, and whether they managed to get that willingness across is a downstream, evidentiary matter. On the other side: consent is essentially an interpersonal, public thing, a communicative act, a message sent and received — a willingness nobody ever learns about can't do the moral work, and a yes that's actually said can, sometimes, do it even when the inner attitude lags behind.

What I want to hand the class is more useful than another debate, so I want you to draft the joint statement that an adverserial collaboration between these two camps would actually produce. Give me, first, a numbered list of claims both sides should be able to sign — what consent is for, what it does morally, what makes it valid — being honest where the agreement only holds at a shallow level of formulation. Then the precise points of remaining disagreement, each phrased the way its own side would want it phrased, so neither side is being quietly set up. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody, with each side's prediction written down, ideally as a table. For each crux, give a case both sides agree would bear on it — here are the ones I'd seed: (a) the partner who is entirely willing but says nothing and does nothing, (b) the partner who performs a convincing, enthusiastic yes while inwardly unwilling, out of fear or politeness or habit, (c) deception about something that would have been a dealbreaker, (d) coercion that somehow produces real willingness — the hostage who comes to want it, and (e) the intoxicated person who is, as a matter of fact, willing. Where you think a disagreement is really just verbal, say so and say why.

One thing I genuinely care about here, for the students' sake: don't paper over the state of the field. Where something is settled among people who work on this, say it's settled; where it's genuinely contested, flag it as contested and don't dress up your own preferred answer as the consensus. I'd much rather they see the live disagreement than a tidy fake one.
