For several months a reading group I belong to has been circling a dispute about consequentialism that I suspect has quietly stopped being about consequentialism, and I want you to diagnose the shape of the disagreement rather than to settle it. The dispute concerns what people call cluelessness. One camp holds that our inability to foresee the consequences of our actions is just the ordinary predicament of choosing under uncertainty: the right act is the one with the best actual consequences, we seldom know which that is, and expected-value reasoning on our best estimates is the most we can manage, so the theory of rightness is untouched and only our epistemic position is impoverished. The other camp insists the trouble is deeper than ignorance. Because any act's causal ripples spread through a chaotic world, the consequences of even a trivial choice reach indefinitely into the far future, so the expected value of an act is a sum over an astronomically large collection of effects whose signs and magnitudes we cannot grasp; on this view the expectation is either undefined, or dominated by negligible-probability enormous outcomes, or so sensitive to assumptions we cannot justify that no further information would help. Neither side has budged in a year, and each thinks the other is simply refusing to see the point.

What I want is a diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of rightness — an objective property that outruns what anyone could know, set against an evidence-relative or reasonable-choice notion of what one ought to do? Is it a formal disagreement inside decision theory about whether ill-defined or imprecise expected values can ground an obligation at all? Is it a verbal quarrel about the phrase "the right thing to do" that would dissolve the moment each side said which question it was answering? Or is it a disguised empirical bet about the world — whether consequences really do fan out without limit, or whether in practice they tend to wash out or converge? My own hunch, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the camps are answering two different questions under one heading and that a genuine formal problem about unbounded expected value is hiding beneath the verbal one; I would rather you tell me if that is wrong than simply agree with it.

Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and work it through on the specific example of a small everyday choice — buying one coffee rather than another, or answering this email rather than not — since the dispute only bites if it bites there. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that separates the questions, a formal result about whether the relevant expectation is well-defined, an argument that the two sides are talking past each other, or a demonstration that the impasse is exactly what we should expect and that no progress is available. I don't need a survey of the literature or a reading list; I need the structure of the disagreement and an honest verdict on whether it is doing any work.
