I'm maybe halfway through a novel set in the 1770s — it follows a family of London tea merchants and their cousins in Boston, and the plot basically turns on the Tea Act and the mess that comes after — and for research I've been rereading Adam Smith alot, especially the long stretch in the Wealth of Nations where he goes after the whole colonial enterprise. What keeps tripping me up is that Smith seems to treat the empire as a racket run for the benefit of a handful of merchants, the colonies as a drain dressed up as an asset, and the sensible move as Britain cutting them loose; I think he even floated letting the colonies send members to Parliament. So I want to use his account as the lens for the actual episode: the Tea Act, the Company shipping its tea straight to America through its own consignees, the Boston Tea Party, and then the Coercive Acts. Treat Smith's theory of empire and colonies as the framework and the episode as the test case. Who are the players in his sense — the monopolists, the public that pays, the government chasing grandeur? What does his theory predict they'd do, and what did they actually do? Does the episode confirm him, embarrass him, or land somewhere he never anticipated?

I'm less interested in a summary of what Smith said than in the mapping and the friction. For instance, the Tea Act actually made tea cheaper for the colonists, which seems to cut against a simple monopoly-hurts-consumers story, and I want to know whether Smith's framework can absorb that or whether it's a case he'd have to file under something else. And his account seems to leave almost no room for the things the colonists said they cared about — liberty, consent, not being taxed by a Parliament they didn't elect — or for the Crown's own stake in revenue and honor. Is that a gap in the theory, or does Smith figure all that talk is just the noise monopoly politics makes? And where does he go silent rather than get stretched — say, on why Britain doubled down with the Coercive Acts instead of doing the seperate, letting-go thing he recommended? I'd rather have a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions than a lecture on Smith, and I don't need citations or a reading list. Also my editor keeps asking why the merchants care about the tea at all, and I'm starting to think that's a question for Smith, not me.
