Ok so I keep going around in circles on whether you can naturalize intentional content, and I'd rather see the fight staged than read another summary of it. Write me a dialogue between two people who are both genuinely good at this — neither one is there to lose.

One is a teleosemanticist (the Millikan / Neander line): what a state represents is fixed by the biological function of the mechanism that produces it, where function means what it was selected for, and what it was selected for is settled by how the consumer systems are supposed to use it. The other thinks that's a detour: content is fixed by information / covariation, with the Fodor-style asymmetric-dependence fix for the disjunction problem — a state means F and not F-or-G because the G-caused tokens depend asymmetrically on the F-caused ones.

They're arguing over a few cases I made up, and they should actually chew on the cases and on each other, not swap speeches:

1. A bird has a neuron, call it L. It was selected for: when it fires, the bird dives for cover, and that saved ancestors from hawks. Lab-reared birds that have never seen a hawk still have L, and it still fires to sweeping shadows. So what does L's firing represent — hawk, sweeping shadow, danger, something else? And does the answer change for the bird that's never met the selection pressure?

2. In one population, L's state has been co-opted: the birds learn from their mothers to use L firing as a cue to snap at insects that get flushed by the shadow, and the learned use is reliably adaptive for them. So the same state in the same bird is now serving two uses with different payoffs — dive for cover, snap at insects. Does the content change, split, or stay put? And does a learned/cultural function even count as a selection regime for the teleosemanticist, or does she have to say the learned use is parasitic on the original content?

3. An engineer builds a CO2 monitor out of a component that was originally manufactured as part of a smoke alarm, where it was designed to fire on smoke particles; it's now wired so that its firing opens a vent. Both design histories are real. Whose design fixes what the part's output represents? I know this is the exaptation case in a lab coat, that's the point — if the two views come apart here, say exactly where.

Also get them into: how each handles misrepresentation; the qua / disjunction problem; the objection that teleosemantics explains content by the consumer's use and so needs the consumer to already represent something (the homunculus worry); and the mirror objection that asymmetric dependence is just smuggled-in function talk. Plus: what each has to give up for a molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no selection history at all — and then whether a thing designed from scratch by humans counts as having a selection history, since that connects back to case 3.

I'd rather they each land real hits and leave some stuff genuinly unresolved than have one of them win on points. Don't let them conclude they were talking about the same thing all along.
