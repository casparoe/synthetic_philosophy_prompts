My thesis is that there is a distinct phenomenon I call recursive epistemic akrasia. In ordinary epistemic akrasia, an agent believes some first-order proposition p while also believing that her evidence does not support p, or that she is not permitted to believe p. In recursive akrasia, the higher-order evidence does not merely lower the probability of p; it undermines the reliability of the very faculty or procedure by which the agent would evaluate the higher-order evidence and revise her first-order belief. My central example is a medical resident who has strong first-order evidence for a diagnosis, but then receives higher-order evidence that her diagnostic judgment is systematically distorted by fatigue and by a hospital culture that rewards confident diagnoses. The twist is that the only way she has of assessing whether the higher-order evidence is itself reliable is through the same diagnostic and self-monitoring faculties that the higher-order evidence impugns. She cannot simply bracket the higher-order evidence, because bracketing would itself be an exercise of the discredited faculty; but she also cannot simply lower her confidence in the diagnosis, because the amount by which she should lower it is something only the discredited faculty can tell her. I want to argue that in this kind of case, continuing to believe p while acknowledging that the higher-order evidence is serious can be epistemically rational, or at least not clearly irrational, because the alternative is a regress that has no non-arbitrary stopping point. The essay will distinguish this from ordinary akrasia, from mere weakness of will, and from the familiar problem of the criterion, and it will consider whether the agent should instead adopt a policy of trusting her first-order faculties while treating the higher-order evidence as a reason to seek external checks.

I would especially like comments on four things. First, is recursive akrasia a genuine category, or does it collapse into the standard regress problem for epistemic justification?
