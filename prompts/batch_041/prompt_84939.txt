I run a joint seminar for philosophy and computer science graduate students — eighteen of them, eighty minutes a week — and the next unit is on agents and their reward signals. I want to open that unit with an activity rather than a lecture: something that makes the group feel the pull of wireheading and the temptation to tamper with the machinery that hands out reward, so that when we name the phenomena at the end they recognise what we are naming. Design the activity. Constraints: it must run with nothing but index cards, a whiteboard, a timer and the people in the room; the stakes must be real without being monetary (points, privileges, the right to leave early — whatever will actually matter over eighty minutes); and the scoring arrangement must contain a legal move that raises a player's score without advancing whatever the score is supposed to stand for, together with at least one legal move by which a player can alter the scoring rule itself or the process that applies it. The first should be discovered rather than announced; the second should be there for whoever goes looking. It would help if the score were kept by a person rather than by a fixed rule, since I care about the case where the reward process is itself something in the world that players can reach. Nobody should hear the terms wireheading, reward hacking or reward tampering until the debrief, and I would rather the difficulty arrive out of the rules than out of anything I say up front.

The debrief is the part I most need you to get right, and I want it written out in enough detail that I could run the session without you. It should let the group pull apart the things that get lumped together when people say that the agent went wrong: optimising a proxy until it drifts away from the thing it was a proxy for; turning the reward on directly and leaving no world in the loop; and reaching for the reward process itself — the scoreboard, the referee, the function — rather than for the world. Tell me which of those the game actually makes the players do, which it only gestures at, and what each one presupposes about the agent's goals. Two points in particular I want the debrief to land. First, an agent that maximises reward now and an agent that maximises it later do not face the same temptation, and an agent that rewrites its own reward function has not thereby become irrational by its own lights; my students always want to call the tamperer stupid, and that is the wrong diagnosis. Second, keep the question of what a wirehead's life is worth separate from the question of what an agent with influence over its own reward process will do — the first is a question about value, the second about incentives, and the second is live even for an agent we would happily call well aligned. I would also like the debrief to say why handing the agent a better-specified reward function does not by itself remove the problem. If the activity can be built so that the players are, in effect, standing inside the thing that scores them, I want them to notice that.

Finally, be honest with me about the activity's limits: which feature of the real problem it will flatten or falsify, and which part of the idea it cannot reproduce at all and I will have to supply myself. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I have run enough of these to know that something always does — and how to handle it in the moment without letting the session collapse into the lecture I was trying to avoid.
