I'm putting together a small anthology of poems for a graduate seminar on puzzles in practical rationality, and the session I care most about is the one on Death in Damascus. The version I want the poem to work from is the familiar one: a man in the market at Damascus sees Death look at him and startle, or perhaps flinch, and he runs for Aleppo that same night, only to have Death later remark that she was puzzled to see him in Damascus, since she had an appointment with him that evening in Aleppo. What interests me is not the fatalism — that part is cheap, and any student gets it in five minutes — but the fact that deliberation in this case has no resting place. If I settle on staying, staying looks like the worse bet, because the appointment might be here; so I flee. If I settle on fleeing, fleeing looks like the worse bet, because the appointment might be there; so I stay. Each time I occupy one option from the inside, the other option looks better, and the reasons migrate with me. I want a poem that enacts that rather than explaining it.

Please write two triolets (eight lines each; the standard shape, where line 1 returns as lines 4 and 7, line 2 returns as line 8, and the rhyme scheme is ABaAabAB — I'm spelling that out only so you don't have to guess at the form). The two poems must share exactly two refrain lines, call them A and B, and must swap their roles: the first triolet opens on A and closes on B, the second opens on B and closes on A. That way the last line of the second poem is the first line of the first, and the pair can be read as a loop that never exits. Each refrain should contain a word whose referent shifts depending on where the speaker is standing — "here," "this city," "this side of the wall," something of that kind — so that the very same words, read in the second poem, counsel the opposite of what they counseled in the first. Let the speaker be the man himself, speaking in the first person, and let the two poems be set at the two moments of decision: at Damascus in the first, at Aleppo in the second. Give the pair a title. Do not name any decision theory or use its vocabulary — no "expected utility," no "ratifiable," no names of philosophers — and don't explain the puzzle in the poem; the form should carry it. It should work as verse and not as a diagram: concrete images, audible meter, a chill. Someone who has never heard of the puzzle should finish it uneasy; someone who has should see that the loop is the point and that there is no line of reasoning inside it that terminates.

After the two triolets, add a short prose note, no more than five sentences, on where the poems take liberties with the case: in particular, whether they assume Death's foreknowledge is infallible or merely reliable, whether they assume your choice cannot causally affect where Death will be, and whether the loop is meant to show that neither option can be settled on from the inside, or something else. Then say plainly which consideration would make the structural device the wrong one for this puzzle — what reading of the case, or what alternative diagnosis of what's gone wrong in it, would make the mirrored refrains misleading rather than apt. Write everything in American English.
