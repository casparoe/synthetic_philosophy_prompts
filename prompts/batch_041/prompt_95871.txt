I want your credence in a precisely stated version of the claim that human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves, and I want the reasoning decomposed rather than a bare number.

Take the claim, which I will call Succession-Dependence:

(S) If humanity were to die out in a way that harmed no one — every human who ever lives lives a good life, and no one is killed or injured by the extinction itself — then the projects of people now living would be less worth pursuing and less meaningful than they would be if humanity continued; and this would be so considered as a fact about the projects themselves, not merely as a fact about how people would feel.

Treat 'worth pursuing' as a claim about reasons for action, so that (S) is false if extinction removes no such reasons and true if it removes some; flag it if you think that reading is itself contested. (S) is a sharpened descendant of Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture (Death and the Afterlife, 2013), but I have pressed it in directions that may not match his own formulation, so assess it on its own merits.

Give me your credence in (S), decomposed into the sub-questions you think it rests on. My own list, which you should feel free to amend:

1. The dependence thesis proper: is the worthwhileness of at least some central human projects actually conditional on there being a human future, or is a project's value self-contained in the activity and its achievement?

2. Object versus valuing. I have been turning over a pair of cases I have not seen discussed. Silent Succession: humanity continues for millions of years, flourishing and valuing all manner of things, but nothing of our particular culture, projects, or memory survives — no one has heard of us. Perfect Archive: humanity dies out within a century, but every artifact and record is preserved perfectly in a vault that no one will ever open. If the dependence thesis is true, is the loss in the second case and not the first — that is, does what matters require future agents who value things, or only the persistence of valued things?

3. The content of the continuation. Suppose the future is one of shallow contentment: people continue to value things, but only trivial ones. Does that preserve the worthwhileness of our projects, or does the dependence require specifically that our evaluative practices be carried on?

4. Normative versus psychological. The evidence that people would be demoralized by the prospect of imminent extinction is evidence about human psychology. Does it support a claim about what is valuable, or does it leave (S) as a thesis about what we care about?

5. Person-affecting. If no one is harmed, is there anything left for the badness of extinction to consist in, or does (S) require impersonal value, that is, value not located in any person's good?

6. Substitution. Even granting dependence, must the continuer be human? Would a flourishing post-human or non-human successor that valued things serve as well — in which case what is lost is not humanity but valuing?

7. The metaphysics of hostage value. (S) makes the value of a project depend on events after its completion. Is that a coherent and acceptable feature of value, or a reductio?

For each sub-question give a rough probability and one line of justification, then combine them into a single credence and show the combination. Then tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. Finally, my own credence in (S) is about 0.3 and a colleague's is about 0.75; say where you think that disagreement most likely lives — which sub-question, and which way each of us is probably answering it.
