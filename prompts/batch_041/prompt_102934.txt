A recurring decision in my own life — whether to press on with something when I genuinely cannot tell whether I am at the first or the second such fork — turned out to have the exact structure of the absent-minded driver problem, and I have not been able to set it aside since. I am to present my position at a seminar next week and would like an audit of whether the following commitments can be held together. (Take the driver case in its standard form: at each of two intersections one may exit or continue; exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and continuing past both pays 1; the driver cannot tell the intersections apart.)

(1) Expected utility is maximized by adopting the best policy for the whole situation, chosen from the start.
(2) Causal decision theory is the correct account of rational choice: an act is assessed by its causal consequences, given the information available where one stands.
(3) In the driver case, then, the rational agent randomizes, deliberately continuing with probability two-thirds.
(4) In Newcomb's problem, I two-box.
(5) No rational agent ever needs to randomize; needing a coin flip shows that one has not worked out what to do.
(6) A theory of choice may require of an agent only what that agent can identify and carry out from where it stands.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which merely appear to, and how serious is each real conflict? For each real conflict give the least costly revision and what it costs, and then tell me which single change would repair the most.
