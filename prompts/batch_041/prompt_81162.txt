Over the past few months I have become convinced of pragmatic encroachment — the view, sometimes called subject-sensitive invariantism, that whether a subject knows a proposition can depend on practical facts about her situation, and not only on her evidence and the truth of the proposition. I take the bank-style cases to establish it: two subjects with the same evidence, the same belief, and the same truth of the matter, differing only in what it would cost them to be wrong, and differing, it seems, in whether they know. I do not have contextualism in mind; I am not claiming that the verb shifts with the attributor's standards, but that the knowledge relation itself is sensitive to what is at stake for the subject.

The trouble is that I built the following argument for a seminar and can no longer see where it fails.

(1) Whether a subject knows p can depend on how much is at stake for her in acting on p.
(2) For any p and any subject, there is some possible situation in which acting on p while p is false would be catastrophic for her — she could stake her life, or everything she cares about, on the claim that the bus leaves at six.
(3) What is at stake in a situation is a matter of what could go wrong if one acts on p; in the situation described in (2), what could go wrong is as bad as it can be.
(4) So in that situation the stakes are maximal, and by (1) the subject does not know p.
(5) Nothing in her epistemic position changes between the ordinary case and that situation — same evidence, same belief, same reliability. Only the stakes change.
(6) Therefore for any proposition anyone purports to know, there is an easily described situation in which she fails to know it, and her failure is traceable to the situation rather than to any defect in her evidence.
(7) Nor is there a stopping point: for any level of stakes there is a higher level, so the threshold for knowledge can be raised without limit by describing a sufficiently costly decision.
(8) But knowledge that can be taken away by describing a possible decision, with no change in the subject's evidence, is not knowledge in any ordinary sense; and a predicate that can be withheld from every claim in this way has no positive application.
(9) So either the encroachment thesis is false, or no one ever knows anything. The second disjunct is absurd, so the thesis is false — and the bank-style cases that motivated it cannot even be stated, since they presuppose that someone knows something in the low-stakes version.

I would rather keep (1) than (9). Tell me whether some step is doing illegitimate work — and if so, which word or move is responsible, with the two readings of it spelled out — or, if the argument is valid as it stands, which premise I should give up. In either case I want to know why the offending step looked innocent when I wrote it, and what the price list looks like for the repairs: in particular, whether a defender of encroachment must accept some restricted form of skepticism, or must restrict which stakes count as genuinely at issue for the subject rather than merely possible, or must abandon the idea that knowledge is a threshold that stakes push up and down. If a repair costs the view the bank-style cases, say so. I am not asking whether the argument is harmless or whether both horns are tolerable; I want the step named and the bill itemized.
