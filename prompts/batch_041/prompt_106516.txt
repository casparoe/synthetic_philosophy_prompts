Okay, this is going to be a weird pairing but hear me out.

Two months ago my doctor told me my blood sugar is inching toward diabetic and stuck one of those glucose monitors on my arm — the patch that pings my phone every five minutes — and it has completely taken over my life. So I've been down the rabbit hole. My friend is on one of the new appetite injections everyone's taking, my gym buddy has gone full carnivore, another guy won't shut up about seed oils, and I keep getting ads for a clinic that will sequence my gut bugs and sell me a personalized meal plan. There's also this app a coworker uses that gives him a "vitality score" out of 100 every morning based on his sleep, his glucose, and how inflamed it thinks he is, and it definately makes him miserable.

Around the same time my book club was doing Nietzsche, and I got stuck on the part where he goes on about how much what you eat, where you live, and the climate actually matter for what you're able to think and do — he treats his own bad stomach like a philosophical problem. I know he was dead set against alcohol and against anything that dulls you, I know he thought walking and air and not sitting around reading all day were the real medicine, and I know the body isn't just a vehicle for the mind for him, the body is what's doing the thinking. But I also know he thought comfort was a trap, and that the good stuff comes out of hardship and self-overcoming.

So: drop him into my life tomorrow. The arm patch, the app that beeps when I spike, my friend's injection that turns off the food noise in her head without her lifting a finger, the clean-eating people at my gym who treat a bag of chips like a crime, the longevity guys spending six figures to stay 35, the vegan who's turned dinner into a moral cause. What does he actually make of all of this?

Four things I can't work out. First, is the appetite drug a will-to-power move — engineering your own body — or a decadent escape hatch, since it gets you the result with none of the struggle? Second, is my monitor a tool for self-knowledge or exactly the kind of self-surveillance he'd call sick? Third, does he side with the people who moralize food, or is moralizing food itself a symptom of what he was diagnosing? And fourth, the big one: was he serious that health is a kind of virtue, or would he say the wellness industry just swapped the old ascetic priest for a guy selling supplements?

And the thing that really bugs me — he seems to want two bodies at once, one that's hard and disciplined and trained, and one that's overflowing and spontaneous and doesn't live by rules. Is there a reading where those turn out to be the same thing, and if not, which one is doing the real work? What would he have to say about one of the cases above that would show me which Nietzsche I'm actually getting — is there a case where the two readings give opposite answers?

Don't give me a reading list, I've got enough books. Just tell me what you think he'd say and why.
