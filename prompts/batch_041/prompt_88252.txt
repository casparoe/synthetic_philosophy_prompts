I teach an upper-level seminar on Chinese and comparative philosophy — it's cross-listed with the logic side of the department, so I've got a room that's half philosophy majors who've had a bit of Frege and Russell and half students from Chinese studies who read classical Chinese better than I do, and I'm writing the final exam now. I want one essay on Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue (白馬論), but I got burned last year: I asked them to reconstruct the argument that a white horse is not a horse, and every single answer was the same three paragraphs — horse names shape, white names colour, so white horse is a compound of two names and therefore isn't the same thing as horse — followed by a paragraph saying it's obviously false anyway. Nobody looked at the actual moves. This time I want a question that can't be answered by reciting that gloss. The bits I want them stuck on are the opening, where the advocate grounds the thesis in what the two terms designate, and especially the middle stretch, where everything turns on what you can and cant get when you ask for something: ask for a horse and a black or a yellow one will do, ask for a white horse and it won't, and the advocate treats that as showing that what's being sought in the two cases isn't the same thing. The dialogue then wanders off into coloured and colourless horses; they can bring that in if it helps them, but I don't want the question to hinge on it.

What I'd like you to build the question around, and please put it in the question stem itself as a short supplied reconstruction so they're arguing with something rather than reciting: treat a term as a rule for answering requests, and call two terms interchangeable when every request the one can be used to answer, the other can too, and every request it can't answer, the other can't either. Horse and white horse plainly fail that test, so on this reconstruction the advocate wins — but only, it seems, for the much weaker claim that the two terms aren't synonyms, and the very same test would show that a brown horse isn't a horse and a horse isn't an animal. So: does the argument as reconstructed actually deliver the thesis, or does it deliver something the objector could cheerfully grant? If the weaker claim is all we get, what does 非 have to mean for the original thesis to be a real thesis and not a pun, and what happens to the objector's comeback — if there's a white horse you can't say there's no horse — under that reading? And what should we make of the fact that the same test proves far too much?

Give me the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built on five must-hit points worth 5 each, with a one-line note for each on how candidates usually miss it. Also tell me what a this-is-just-a-pun-about-Chinese-not-having-articles answer should score and why — I get two or three of those every year and I want the scheme to be honest about it. It's closed book, no internet, 75 minutes for this question out of a three-hour paper, so nothing can depend on knowing who in the secondary literature said what; I'd much rather they argue it out than name-drop. Third- and fourth-year, mixed backgrounds, and the logicians in the room are the toughest audience I've got.
