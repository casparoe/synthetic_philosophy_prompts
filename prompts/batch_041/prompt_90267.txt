I have spent the past year reporting on the argument that the suffering of animals in the wild dwarfs anything in human experience, and that anyone who takes pain seriously has at least to ask whether something should be done about it. My interest is not in the argument itself but in a move I keep meeting when I put it to people, and for which I have never found a name.

The move runs roughly as follows. My interlocutor accepts the premise without much resistance: yes, the fawn dying of exposure is bad, bad for the fawn. But the question of what follows produces a shift of subject. The answer stops being about the fawn and becomes about us — that we would be the ones doing it, that we do not understand what we would be disturbing, that it is not our place, that good intentions have a poor record. Three features of this seem to me worth separating. First, the objection is about the interveners — their knowledge, their motives, their standing — whereas the concern that prompted it was about the animals. Second, it is applied unevenly: the same person who insists that we cannot know what is good for a system will approve of vaccinating an endangered population, of hauling a whale off a beach, of eradicating rats from an island, of releasing a gene drive to crash a mosquito species. The epistemic humility appears to be reserved for interventions that would benefit the animals themselves. Third, the baseline moves. When the point is that we should not interfere, the wild as it now stands is treated as the state to be preserved; when the point is that some interference is obviously right, the wild as it stood before we arrived is the state to be restored. The privileged baseline seems to be whichever one licenses the verdict already reached.

What I want from you is not a label but the shape of the thing. Is there an established concept that fits it — something in the neighbourhood of the distinction between doing and allowing, or status quo bias, or the appeal to nature, or the precautionary principle? My difficulty is that each of these seems to catch a part and miss the whole: the doing/allowing distinction says nothing about why the epistemic objection should scale as it does, and the appeal to nature would not explain why the objection is so often framed as a fact about the intervener's standing rather than about the animal's prospects. If nothing existing fits, I would like a term proposed and defined, with the boundaries marked: what it covers, what it excludes, and how it differs from each of those near misses. Does it require that action be genuinely available, or can it also describe the case of someone who could act and simply declines to think about it? And is the assumption that the natural state carries moral weight part of the phenomenon, or a separate error that happens to accompany it?
