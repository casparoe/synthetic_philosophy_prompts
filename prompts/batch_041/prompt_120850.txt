I am convening the final meeting of a reading group on the unity of consciousness, and we have spent three sessions on the split-brain cases: the patients whose commissures were cut, the experiments in which a picture is delivered to one visual field and the hand acts on it while the speaking half of the patient sincerely reports having seen nothing, the patients whose two hands work at cross purposes on the same shirt, and the underlying question of whether the operation leaves behind one subject or two. I would rather open the session with a poem than with a summary, so that the group has to feel the problem in the act of reading before they start arguing about it. I do not want the familiar philosophical moves restated in verse, and I do not want a poem that lectures me about the confabulating interpreter or about personal identity under fission. I want the form to carry the argument, and I would prefer that the poem never use the words brain, hemisphere, surgery, split, left, or right, and never explain its own device.

Here is the constraint, which is the whole point of the exercise. Write twenty-eight lines of blank verse, no stanza breaks, arranged as fourteen couplets in which the two lines of each couplet belong to two different voices. The first line of every couplet is the body's: it reports an action — hands, feet, eyes, an ordinary afternoon of physical doings — and it contains no reason, motive, or explanation, and no connective of the because kind, the since kind, or the in-order-to kind. The second line of every couplet is the talker's: it supplies a reason, a motive, a justification, a scrap of self-commentary, and it contains no physical action whatsoever. The poem has to survive three readings. Read straight through, it should be one continuous, plausible, first-person account of an ordinary stretch of time that no one would suspect of anything. Read as odd lines only, it should still scan and cohere as a poem about a body doing things for no stated reason. Read as even lines only, it should still scan and cohere as a poem of pure rationalisation. The catch is that the odd-line poem and the even-line poem must be telling incompatible stories about the same hour: the body must be somewhere, or doing something, that the reasons quietly presuppose it is not, so that a reader who takes the two streams apart discovers that the seamless straight-through reading was manufactured by the very act of reading it straight through. For that reason it must not rhyme, since a rhyme would leave orphans when the streams are separated; the single shared meter is the only thing coupling the two voices, and nothing in the poem may adjudicate whether there is one person here or two. One further beat, if you can manage it without violating anything above: somewhere in the middle the talker should deny, flatly and in good faith rather than as a lie, something the body has just been shown doing on the page, and the final couplet should have the talker give a reason for something the body never does, so that the last line of the poem is a piece of plausible invention. It should be a real poem — concrete, with a scene, a sound, and a temperature — not a demonstration. Someone who knows the experiments ought to be able to point to the exact line where the denial happens and the exact line where the invention happens, with neither ever named; someone who has never heard of any of this ought simply to find it a good poem.
