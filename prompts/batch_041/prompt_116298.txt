So I accidentally became the person who runs a monthly philosophy night at my apartment — it's mostly people from my lab plus a few friends from stats, all of them smart, none of them has ever taken an epistemology course — and last month, three drinks in, I brought up the bootstrapping problem as an example of why philosophy isn't just navel-gazing, and now it's the topic for next month, and I have one hour, a whiteboard, and eight people who will definately call me out if I hand-wave. The problem is that the version everyone already sort of knows (you can't show your senses are reliable without already trusting your senses) is not the part I care about and not the part that will land; the part I care about is the corollary, and I want the hour built around it. Here it is in my own words, the way I'd say it out loud: I look at a table and it looks red to me, and on that basis I believe it's red; I notice that it's red and that it looks red, so on this occasion my color vision got it right; I do the same with a green mug, a blue chair, a yellow banana; and after enough of these I announce, on the strength of nothing but these reports, that my color vision is reliable. And the creepy part is that at no point did I assume my vision was reliable — I never used a premise about my eyes being good, I just kept looking — and out came the conclusion the skeptic said I could never reach, which I can now use to shore up every single one of the beliefs I used to get there. There's a cousin of the case that bothers me even more: just by looking at the red table, plus some trivial reasoning about how color words work, I seem to get to know that the table isn't white with a red light pointed at it, and that feels stolen rather than earned.

What I want from you isn't the explanation — I know roughly what I think about it — it's the plan for teaching it in one hour to this exact crowd. What has to be in place before the bootstrapping case even makes sense to them, in what order, and why each piece has to come before the next (my instinct is they need the closure principle first and the whole why-can't-you-just-check-your-eyes-with-your-eyes worry second, but I don't trust that instinct); which single example should carry the whole session, what it needs to have going for it, and whether it should be the color case I sketched or something else — and if it's something else, why. I'd rather not lean on the evil demon or the brain-in-a-vat, since they've heard those and they'll decide this is just that again, but tell me if I'm wrong about that. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, with the moment in the sequence where each one should be defused: I'm betting someone says this is just the problem of induction, and I know for a fact my lab friend is going to say it's the same thing as using a model's own outputs to check the model, which I'm pretty sure is a diffrent problem and maybe even the opposite one, but I couldn't tell him why at the time and I'd like to be able to. I also want one question to ask at the very end that will tell me whether they've actually got the shape of the problem or have just been nodding. And tell me what to leave out: there's obviously a whole industry of proposed fixes out there, and I don't know whether to mention that it exists and wave at it, or pretend it doesn't, and if I do mention it, what the one-line version is that won't leave them thinking the problem has been solved. Nobody's read anything and I'm not handing out papers, so it all has to work from what's in the room.
