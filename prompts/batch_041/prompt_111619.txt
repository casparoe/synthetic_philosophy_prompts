I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and I have been asked to run a single ninety-minute seminar session, for second- and third-year undergraduates, on the difference between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers. The students will have read nothing in advance and will arrive equipped only with the vocabulary of algorithmic filter bubbles. I do not need the distinction explained to me. What I need is a plan for teaching it, and specifically a plan that makes the students reconstruct the distinction for themselves rather than receive it as a definition, since a definition they can repeat back will conceal the one structural feature that does all the explanatory work.

Begin with the dependency order: which idea must be in place before the next becomes intelligible, and why each earlier step is load-bearing for the later one. I take it that the pivot is the difference between a deficit of information and a pre-emptive disqualification of sources, but I want you to tell me exactly where in the sequence that pivot should fall, and what must already have been established for it to land rather than to sound like a slogan.

Then the carrying example. Choose one case to run through the whole session, and tell me what it must have and what it must lack. In particular, tell me why the obvious example — a partisan news feed, a politically homogeneous timeline — is the wrong thing to build on, and what the example must instead be engineered to display. It needs to be a case in which the relevant outside information is not merely absent but available, and would be accepted under other circumstances, and is nevertheless already discounted by the time it arrives. Say what kind of community or situation can carry that without becoming exotic, and why.

Then the misconception map, ordered as the students will actually form the misconceptions, with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect at least these: (a) that an echo chamber is just a bubble with heavier filtering, a difference of degree rather than of mechanism; (b) that the members of an echo chamber are simply uninformed, when the point is that what they believe may be true and their deficit lies elsewhere; (c) that the remedy for both conditions is the same, namely exposure to opposing views, which is precisely what fails in one of the two; and (d) that the phenomenon is a moral failing of the members, stupidity or bad faith, rather than a structural property of how their trust has been arranged. Tell me which of these the students bring in with them and which the session itself will manufacture, and where each is defused.

Finally, give me one question I can put to them at the end that distinguishes a student who has understood the distinction from one who has merely memorized two labels. I want it to require them to sort a fresh case they have not seen, and to say not only which of the two conditions it is but what intervention would and would not work on it, and why. And tell me what I should openly decline to cover in ninety minutes, saying so to the class, so that the session does not sprawl into the general epistemology of testimony.
