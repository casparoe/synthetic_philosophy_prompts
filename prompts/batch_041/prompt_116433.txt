Compose a sestina on the question of why there is something rather than nothing. It is addressed to an interlocutor who will not stop demanding a reason for the whole of things, and its six end-words are to be drawn from the vocabulary of the question itself: something, rather, than, why, there, is. One further constraint governs the entire poem. The word "nothing" may not appear anywhere in it until the final line, where it is to appear exactly once, and it must not be smuggled in earlier by paraphrase — the absence has to be a real absence, felt as the poem goes on.

The form should carry the argument. Six words permuted by a fixed rule across six stanzas can generate almost any content, but this poem's machinery has no term for its own apparent second half, and the reader should feel that hole widen stanza by stanza until the envoi, where the missing word arrives and turns out not to name a state of affairs at all.

Along the way, give the strongest available voice to the replies that refuse this dissolution: that the totality must have a reason, and the rejoinder that the demand for a reason may lose its sense when it is turned on the totality itself; that there might be a being whose existence is necessary rather than brute; and that the universe may simply be the case, so that asking after a cause of the whole is a category mistake. The poem should also register the thought experiment in which one imagines removing things one by one and discovers that the last removal is not a further removal but a change in what the question is asking.

End where you began defending, but leave the reader able to say what was being defended: not that the question has an answer, but that its second term was never on the table — that "nothing" works as a quantifier rather than a name, and so there is no alternative to the actual world for the question to contrast it with. The accuracy bar is high; a metaphysician should recognize the arguments and not merely the vocabulary. Keep it a genuine sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, with the end-words permuted in the standard way and all six resurfacing in the envoi.
