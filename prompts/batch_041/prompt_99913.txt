A few of my colleagues at work come from countries that went through political transitions in the 1980s and 1990s, and after a project dinner last month three of them — one Argentine, one who grew up in Rwanda, one South African — fell into an argument that I gather has been running for years and that I have now watched stall twice without anyone's position shifting. The Argentine treats the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and in particular its offer of amnesty to individuals who fully disclosed their politically motivated crimes, as a morally compromised bargain: a state that sold impunity in exchange for a public ritual, and he notes that Argentina's own amnesty laws of the 1980s were eventually annulled and their beneficiaries retried, as though the society had concluded it had made a mistake. The South African insists the realistic alternative was never a courtroom but a negotiated settlement that would have shielded the old security apparatus, and that making disclosure the price of amnesty turned it into a species of accountability rather than an escape from it — that the truth was itself the thing the victims were owed. The Rwandan, who is old enough to remember the gacaca courts, says the other two are describing one thing from opposite ends, that in these settings "justice" was never a single object to be had or forfeited but a bundle of separate goods — truth, acknowledgment, the victims' standing, deterrence, and some notion of social repair — traded against one another, so that which one you call the price depends entirely on which one you were trying to buy.

I have no philosophical training — I write software for a living — and what strikes me is that the three of them cycle through the same handful of moves and never touch the thing underneath. I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are they disagreeing about facts (what amnesties do, what happens to societies that grant or refuse them), about values (whether justice is something a victim is owed or something a community does), about which cases even count as the data, or about bedrock intuitions — that impunity is intolerable, or that a fragile peace is worth almost any price — for which neither side can argue? My own suspicion, and I'd like you to tell me whether it survives scrutiny, is that it isn't really a single disagreement at all: that one of them is answering a backward-looking question about what a wronged person is owed and another a forward-looking question about what a shattered society can afford, and that they mistake these for the same question because both carry the name of justice. If that diagnosis is right, what follows about what could actually move them — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a restatement of the question — and if it's wrong, tell me what the real fault line is. Reason from the positions as I've laid them out rather than from anything you'd have to look up; I'm after the structure of the impasse and an honest judgment about whether progress here is possible at all, or whether the two sides are simply answering different questions under one word and should stop expecting to converge.
