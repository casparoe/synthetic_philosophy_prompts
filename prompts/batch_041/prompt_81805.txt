I'm prepping for my podcast (tiny audience, mostly grad students) and next week's guest works on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents — the thing where an agent that reasons about its own proofs can't get a general "if I prove it, it's true" principle without proving everything. Her line, which I want to actually stress-test rather than let her restate: the obstacle is real but harmless, because nobody needs the unbounded version. Her proposal is a "shrinking ladder." The agent trusts its own proofs only up to some length n; its trust in *that* is itself only good up to a shorter length f(n); and so on down. She says the ladder gives a self-improving agent everything it actually needs, and that every objection she hears assumes you want trust "at the limit," which she calls a philosopher's demand rather than an engineer's. She also thinks it dissolves the successor problem — an agent can't prove its successor is safe in the full sense, but can prove it's no worse up to its own budget, and that's meant to be enough.

You're a logician who knows provability logic and the self-trust literature cold, and I want you thinking carefully about where a proposal like this actually breaks rather than what the stock objections are.
