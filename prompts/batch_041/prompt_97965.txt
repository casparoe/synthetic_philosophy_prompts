Title: You Should Bet Your Life Geometrically (and that's a problem)

The Kelly criterion says: when you get to bet repeatedly and reinvest what you win, bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of your wealth. Everyone learns this as a gambling rule. I think it's actually the whole of practical rationality, and I think that's a problem.

Why the log? Because if you reinvest, your wealth after n rounds is the product of n multipliers. The growth rate per round is the average of the logs of those multipliers, so maximizing expected log wealth is maximizing your long-run growth rate. That's a theorem, not a preference. The arithmetic mean of your final wealth across "parallel copies" of you isn't what you experience; you experience one path, and the path grows at the log rate.

Then the weird part. Mean-wealth maximization and log maximization disagree. Give me $1000 and a one-shot bet: 60% chance to win $1000, 40% to lose $1000. Expected value is +$200, so a mean-maximizer takes it. But the log-maximizer looks at the bad branch, where wealth = 0, and log(0) = -infinity, so the whole expected log is -infinity and she refuses. Fine, nobody sensible bets their entire bankroll. But notice what did the work: not the 40%, not the size of the loss, but the possibility of going to exactly zero. Any bet with any chance of total ruin is infinitely bad, and gets refused no matter how good the upside.

Now generalize. Call it geometric rationality: whenever the thing you care about compounds multiplicatively, aggregate with the geometric mean, i.e. maximize the expected log. My friend says this is just risk-aversion with a math costume. I say no: it's forced on you by long-run growth, not by a taste for safety. He says the long-run growth argument only bites for literally unlimited repeated bets, and in real life you don't get unlimited rounds, so the theorem never actually applies to a finite life. I don't have a clean answer to that.

Here's the thing I actually want to figure out. Your life compounds — you keep living and get to make more decisions, and the value of your remaining life is roughly multiplicative in the same way. So geometric rationality seems to say: never take any action with a nonzero chance of death, no matter the upside, because log(0) = -infinity. That can't be right; we all drive cars and eat sushi for the upside. So either (a) geometric rationality doesn't apply to survival, (b) the infinity is an artifact of treating death as exactly zero rather than as just very bad, or (c) something in my setup is off. I lean towards (b) but I can't tell if that's just cowardice talking.

What I want from you: (1) tell me if I've got the actual math and the ergodicity stuff right, I'm half self-taught here and might be mangling it; (2) tell me if the death argument is a real objection to geometric rationality or a cheap shot, and if it's cheap, explain why without just asserting it; (3) tell me if the essay is actually interesting or if I've just rediscovered "risk aversion exists" and dressed it up in a theorem. And if the whole framing — that the theorem does all the work and the philosophy is just along for the ride — is wrong, say so.
