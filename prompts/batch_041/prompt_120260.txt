Two people who know probability and decision theory well, going at each other about the common prior assumption -- the idea that rational agents who start off with the same information should all have the same prior over how the world is, so that if two of them disagree after seeing the same evidence, at least one of them is being irrational or secretly has information the other one doesn't.

I want a genuine back-and-forth, not two people taking turns reciting positions. Make them equally sharp; neither one is the fool of the piece.

The defender: thinks the assumption is basically what makes "rational" a shared standard at all, and what makes "I should move my belief toward yours when we disagree" mean anything. Drop it and updating on other people's opinions becomes contentless -- you can't say anyone *should* change their mind, you can only report that they did. The skeptic: thinks its an unfalsifiable stipulation dressed up as a norm. Any disagreement, however stubborn, can be redescribed as "well, they just had different priors," so nothing could ever count as evidence against the assumption, and its main real-world effect is to turn every lasting disagreement into a verdict that somebody is broken or hiding something.

The specific thing I want them to fight over, because I think it's where the meat is: two people who seem to share everything -- same data, same training, same culture, same background assumptions, same arguments heard -- and still disagree, stubbornly, for years. The defender says they can't really share everything; there's always some private signal, even "I've thought about it harder" or "I noticed my own gut reaction to the argument," so the assumption survives untouched. The skeptic says that rescue is exactly what makes it vacuous: if you keep stretching "same information" until it lines up with "same prior," you havent learned anything about the world, you've just defined disagreement out of existence. Let them really press each other there, and let each one land at least one hit the other has to absorb.

Also work in, without turning it into a checklist: is having a prior a fact about someone's psychology or a norm about what they're allowed to believe? Does the defender have to bite the bullet that two perfectly rational people can never keep disagreeing? Does the assumption have any grip on messy real disputes, or only on idealized ones?
