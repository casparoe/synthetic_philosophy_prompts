I am convening a two-day adversarial-collaboration workshop between two philosophers who have spent years arguing about self-deception, and what I need from you is the document the two of them should be able to sign at the end of it. One participant is an intentionalist: she holds that self-deception is genuinely a species of deception, that the self-deceived person is in some sense an agent in the production of her own belief, and that making sense of this requires either an intention the person does not consciously entertain or something like a division of the mind into parts that can mislead one another. The other participant is a non-intentionalist, or deflationist if you prefer: he holds that the phenomena are real but exhaustively motivational — wishful thinking, selective search for evidence, asymmetrical scrutiny of welcome and unwelcome evidence, and the like — and that neither intention nor partition is needed to explain them. Both are competent, both know the standard objections to their own position, and neither is permitted to score points by attributing a weakened version of the other's view.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Aim for 1500–2500 words, organised as follows.

1. A numbered list of claims both accept: about the phenomena to be explained (include at least two carefully described cases, one drawn from ordinary life and one institutional or professional, specific enough that a reader can see what needs explaining); about what the paradox of self-deception does and does not show; about what any adequate account must accommodate; and about which parts of the old dispute the two of them now regard as merely verbal. These claims must be substantive — nothing so bland that any sane person would accept it, and nothing that quietly concedes the disputed ground to one side.

2. The residual disagreement, stated precisely. For each disputed claim, give two formulations, one for each participant, in wording that participant would accept as their own view and would not regard as a strawman. Present this section as a table with the disputed question in the left column and the two formulations side by side.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one participant. For each, record in advance what each side predicts, and name any case, argument, or empirical finding that both agree would bear on it — including what each side would count as evidence against its own position. Where a crux is empirical rather than conceptual, say so and describe what the relevant evidence would look like.

4. A closing note on what the collaboration did not settle, and on whether any part of the disagreement is better redescribed as a disagreement about which concept of belief, intention, or evidence we ought to be working with in the first place.

Two things need explicit treatment, because they are where I think the real work is. First, the assumption that the self-deceived person holds a false belief: does either account survive if the belief is true but unwarranted, or if what is distorted is not the belief at all but the policy by which the person gathers and weighs evidence? Second, the reflexive case, in which the subject's belief is about her own self-deception — someone confidently convinced that she is self-deceived about her marriage, her politics, or her health, and who may be wrong about that. Say what each side is obliged to say about such a case, and whether the case is a problem for either.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where you find yourself adjudicating rather than reporting, mark it as your own view rather than folding it into the joint statement. No citations or reading lists are needed; I care about the content, not the bibliography.
