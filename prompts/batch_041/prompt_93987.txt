I have been reading about safe Pareto improvements in the game-theoretic literature on cooperation, and there is a point that nags at me because it seems too elementary to raise. As I understand the definition, a safe Pareto improvement is a modification of a game that leaves every player better off with certainty — the transformed way of playing is meant to guarantee that no participant ends up worse off than under the default. But if that is all that is required, then surely every Pareto improvement is already safe. A Pareto improvement is, by definition, a change under which nobody is made worse off; the improvement is precisely what rules out a loss. So what work is the word safe doing here? Is it merely emphasis, or does it mark some further technical condition that the ordinary notion of a Pareto improvement does not already contain? And if the two really amount to the same thing, why would anyone bother proving theorems about when safe Pareto improvements exist and how hard they are to find — should they not exist exactly whenever ordinary Pareto improvements do?

The only hint I can extract is that the proofs seem to lean on qualitative assumptions about how different games are played — something to the effect that isomorphic games are played isomorphically, or that strictly dominated strategies can be iteratively removed — but I cannot see how those assumptions bear on my point, since they do not appear to impose any additional requirement that the outcome actually be better for anyone. I would be grateful if you could explain where my reasoning goes wrong, or else confirm that the distinction is empty.
