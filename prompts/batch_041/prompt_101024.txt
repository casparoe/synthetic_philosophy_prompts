Some friends from my old LessWrong meetup and I have been going in circles for months about whether it's kosher to say an LLM believes things. I'm firmly in the "obviously you can, the intentional stance is just how you predict the damn thing" camp; my friend Sam is in the "no, that's a category error, or at best a dangerous shorthand that trains us to anthropomorphize" camp. We've argued long enough that we can predict each other's moves, which is usually the sign we should write the joint statement and be done with it. So: draft the adversarial-collaboration document the two of us could actually both sign. I want the claims we can both put our names to -- about what the intentional stance is and what work it's doing, about what the behavioral data even is, about what "belief" would have to mean for either of us to be right -- then the precise statements of where we still disagree, each phrased so that the person who holds it would accept the wording of their own view, then the cruxes, and for each crux the argument or case we'd both agree actually bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

The part I keep getting stuck on, and where I'd like you to really push, is where the belief is supposed to live. Same weights, different context or a different sampling seed, and the thing "changes its mind" -- so is that one believer holding contradictory beliefs, a whole population of momentary believers, or no believer at all, just a function from prompts to tokens that our belief-talk is papering over? Sam thinks that example is fatal to my side; I think it's exactly the kind of thing the stance was built to handle, so that one should be a crux with both predictions written down. And please don't just assume the disagreement is real -- if some chunk of it turns out to be us meaning different things by "belief" (a predictive shorthand vs. a truth-apt claim about inner states), say so and carve it off. Also worth deciding out loud: when we say the model "believes" something, whose belief is it -- the weights, the training process, the developers, the particular run? I suspect thats a second crux, and maybe the whole thing in disguise.

One thing I actually want from you, since I can't go look anything up right now: keep the two camps clean and tell me what's actually at stake. I'm not asking you to rule on who's right, and I don't want a survey of the literature. Where people who genuinely know this stuff agree -- like that belief-talk is at least predictively useful, or that there's a real gap between a behavioral disposition and anything we'd call understanding -- mark it as settled, and where it's genuinely contested, don't smuggle one side in as the consensus. If a step in the collaboration leans on something nobody has actually settled, flag it as such rather than quietly picking a winner, and definately don't pretend the thing is more decided than it is.
