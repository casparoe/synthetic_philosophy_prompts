In a survey of metaphysics I'm taking, the professor's handout summarizes Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition in a paragraph that I suspect is a bit too tidy, but I can't put my finger on where it goes wrong. It reads: "Bradley showed that relations are self-undermining. If a relation R holds between a and b, then for R to actually relate them, it must itself be related to a and to b. But that requires a further relation R1 to relate R to a and b, and then R2 to relate R1 to R, a, and b, and so on without end. Since this regress is vicious, no relation can ever do the work of uniting its terms. Bradley concluded that relations are unreal and that reality is a single undifferentiated whole. The same problem, he thought, infects propositions: a proposition is just a collection of terms, and no relation can bind them into a unified content, so judgment is impossible. Russell later answered Bradley by showing that the regress is harmless: a relation relates its terms without needing a further relation, because relating is just what relations do. This is why contemporary philosophy accepts relations and rejects Bradley's monism."

I have a vague sense that the handout is conflating two different problems, and that it makes Bradley sound more dismissive of relations than he was. But I don't know enough to say which sentences a careful reader of Bradley would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are outright misrepresentations. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a defender of Bradley would object to, what a defender of Russell would say, and whether the handout's final claim—that this is why contemporary philosophy accepts relations and rejects monism—follows from the accurate parts of the summary? I'd also like to know whether the regress really is vicious in the way the handout assumes, or whether that is precisely the point at issue.
