I have been reading a trade book on the history of economic ideas — the author is a journalist who writes clearly about economics but is not a specialist in this particular controversy — and its chapter on the socialist calculation debate left me unsure how far to trust it. Its account of the debate, condensed and lightly rephrased, runs as follows:

The socialist calculation debate began when Ludwig von Mises argued that socialism is impossible in principle. Without private ownership of the means of production there can be no market prices for capital goods, and without such prices a planning board has no way of knowing whether a given quantity of steel is better spent on a bridge or on a factory. Hayek broadened the point: the knowledge required to coordinate an economy is dispersed, local, and often tacit — the sort of thing people act on without being able to state it — so no central body could ever gather it in one place. The debate appeared to be settled in the socialists' favour in the 1930s, when Oskar Lange showed that a central planning board could set prices by trial and error, raising them for goods in short supply and lowering them for goods piling up unsold, and thereby reproduce everything a competitive market does, including the optimal allocation of resources. Later history vindicated Lange: modern economies run on computers that solve enormous systems of simultaneous equations in seconds, and solving those equations is all Hayek's objection really came to. The collapse of the Soviet Union therefore shows that socialism fails for political reasons — authoritarianism, corruption, and the wrong incentives — rather than because of anything in the calculation argument itself.

I would like an audit rather than a summary. Take the account claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a specialist would regard it as accurate, as a defensible simplification, as contested, or as a misrepresentation — and say who would object, and on what grounds: what would a Hayekian say the paragraph gets wrong about the knowledge problem, and what would a defender of Lange say it gets wrong about Lange's scheme? Two things interest me in particular. First, the paragraph treats the debate as having been settled in the socialists' favour in the 1930s; is that a fair description of where the argument actually stood, or does it quietly adopt one side's retrospective verdict? Second, the paragraph draws a conclusion — that the Soviet collapse is evidence about politics and not about calculation — and I want to know whether that conclusion follows from the parts of the account that survive your corrections, or whether it depends on the parts that do not. Mark clearly where you are confident and where you are reporting a live disagreement among specialists, instead of smoothing everything into one verdict. And if the way I have framed this is wrong — if, say, the paragraph's errors are so woven into its accurate parts that auditing it claim by claim would mislead me more than it helps — then say so plainly rather than producing a tidy audit regardless. I should add that my own inclination is to believe the paragraph, since it matches the version of the story I hear most often; I would rather be told that the common version is the distortion than have it confirmed.
