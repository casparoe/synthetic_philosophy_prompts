I'm a grad student putting togther a handout for a reading group on panpsychism, and the whole group keeps collapsing everything into "panpsychism vs physicalism" like there's one view in there. I want a decision tree that actually seperates them. You're a philosopher of mind who's spent way too long on the combination problem, so be pedantic about the distinctions. Before you draw anything, spend a bit of space brainstorming the logical space out loud: what the independent choice points are, and which combinations of answers are even coherent. Then build the tree. Think carefully at each step — the tree is the deliverable, the brainstorming is scaffolding I want to see.

Start from a commitment I can check myself for, roughly: "there are facts about what it's like to be something that aren't fixed by the structural and relational facts physics describes, and I'm not an illusionist about experience." From there, every branch node has to be a question about a case or a commitment, phrased so I could answer it without knowing which position it leads to. No node may be "do you accept panpsychism?" or "are you a constitutive panpsychist?"

Some distinctions I'm pretty sure I want on the tree, but you tell me where they go and whether they're actually independent of each other:

- whether the fundamental experiential items are subjects (someone is home) or just qualitative character with nobody home
- whether the micro-level nature has to be experience itself or could be something merely proto-experiential
- whether the combination relation is itself something experiential — a bonding that's felt from the inside and shows up in the resulting experience — or a structural/functional relation
- constitutive vs emergent: are the micro-experiences parts of the big experience, or just its causes
- whether the direction runs bottom-up at all, or whether there's one subject at the top that decomposes, and if so what the decomposition relation is supposed to be
- what happens to the micro-subjects when combination succeeds: do they fuse and stop existing, survive as parts, or was there never a subject there

Use these cases as at least some of the branch nodes, plus whatever else you need:

1. A factory that builds a mind on a conveyor belt, adding one micro-experiencer at a time to a growing structure. At what point, if ever, does a new subject exist? Make me say what changes at that moment.
2. Split-brain: one subject or two after the cut, and does that follow from your combination story or is it bolted on?
3. Conjoined twins whose brains are bridged so each has access to the other's experience. If they share an experience, is that one subject with two bodies, or two subjects with an overlap? I want this one to separate views that both happily say "combination happens."
4. A being whose micro-level items get swapped one at a time from subject-having experiencers to subjectless qualia, with macro behaviour held fixed. Which views notice, which don't.

Aim for about six branch nodes and nine to twelve leaves. Each leaf gets the name of the position (use the standard label where there is one — constitutive panpsychism, emergent panpsychism, cosmopsychism, panqualityism, Russellian monism, panprotopsychism, illusionism, the "we don't have the concepts yet" option, and anything else that earns a place) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment, i.e. the thing its defender has to swallow.

Two things I care about more than completeness:

- Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate, and say what extra question would be needed, or why there isn't one.
- For combinations of answers that no named view occupies, say so and diagnose it: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but unattractive, or because it just hasn't been explored? Don't quietly leave cells empty.

If you need a label for a view nobody has named, call it unnamed and describe it rather than inventing a fake tradition. And no references or reading lists, I just want the instrument.
