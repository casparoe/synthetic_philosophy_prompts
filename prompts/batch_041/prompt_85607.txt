In November 2017 the race for Virginia's 94th House of Delegates district, in Newport News, ended in an exact tie: 11,608 votes for the Republican incumbent, David Yancey, 11,608 for the Democrat, Shelly Simonds, and 675 for a Libertarian, out of 23,891 cast. The sequence matters. Yancey led by 12 on election night and by 10 when the result was certified; the recount put Simonds ahead by one; a three-judge panel then counted a disputed ballot, on which both candidates appeared to be marked and the Simonds mark struck through, for Yancey, restoring the tie. Virginia law resolves a tied election by lot, and on 4 January 2018 Yancey's name was drawn, giving the Republicans a 51–49 majority in the chamber. Simonds conceded a week later.

Analyze that episode through the rational-choice paradox of voting — the turnout version of it, the calculus that weighs the probability of casting a pivotal vote against the cost of voting, and the amended version that adds a duty or expressive term. I mean the turnout paradox, not Condorcet's cyclical-majorities paradox. I know the textbook moves, so don't spend words rehearsing them. What I want is a disciplined mapping, then an honest account of where the theory runs out.

Handle the arithmetic carefully. Estimate the ex ante probability that a single vote was pivotal in this district under two assumptions: that each voter is an independent fair coin, and that the margin is a normal random variable with a spread of a few percentage points. Give orders of magnitude rather than false precision, state what each estimate rests on, and say how the two estimates scale with the size of the electorate. Then invert the calculus: how large would the benefit term have to be for a vote to be worth its cost under each estimate, and is that magnitude available to an ordinary voter who cares about which party controls the chamber?

Second, the features of the episode that the calculus has no obvious place for: that a tie is resolved by lot, so a vote pivotal for the tie is not thereby pivotal for the outcome; that the ex post fact of a one-vote margin is not evidence about the ex ante probability, though it is routinely read that way; that the decisive acts were a judicial reading of an ambiguous ballot and then a random draw, so the locus of decision left the voters altogether; and that a third candidate took 675 votes.

For contrast, take the 2014 Scottish independence referendum: 3,619,915 valid votes, turnout 84.6 per cent, 55.3 to 44.7 against independence, with the franchise extended to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds. Say what the same analysis implies there, how the two cases differ in what the theory can explain, and which features of each episode it explains, misdescribes, or passes over in silence.

Then the part I care about most. Does the episode confirm the calculus, embarrass it, or leave it untouched because the duty term makes it unfalsifiable? If an expressive or collective-action account does better, say precisely where it does better and what it has to give up. Finally, name the one further case, real or specified in enough detail to be assessed, that would discriminate between the surviving accounts, and give the verdict each account would return on it.
