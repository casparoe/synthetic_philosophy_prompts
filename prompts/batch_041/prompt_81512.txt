I'm a novelist and I need a poem to close out my book, so here's the situation. My main character is a translator and failed novelist who's read her Epicurus and is pretty much convinced death isn't bad for her -- no subject, nothing to deprive, nobody left to miss anything -- and she has just died. The last page of the book is the poem she left behind, and its a pantoum, because I want the form doing the arguing: the same lines keep coming back in new positions, saying the same words with nobody home to mean them. But I dont want a straight illustration of the Epicurean line, that would be boring and a little smug. What actually wrecks her, in the poem, isn't being dead, its that her manuscript is sitting in some editor's inbox and somebody else is going to finish it for her. So the poem has to hold two things live at once: that death can't be bad for the one who's dead, and that a person can still be harmed after they're gone, through what happens to the work. Don't have one refute the other, just let them sit there. Six quatrains, the recurring lines should shift meaning as they move down the page, and it should be good enough that a philosopher wouldn't wince but also work as verse somebody would actually want at the end of a novel.
