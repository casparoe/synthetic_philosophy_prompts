I'm a postdoc and I've somehow ended up chairing the committee that's deciding whether my department should launch a new interdisciplinary PhD track. There are five of us, and at the first meeting we agreed on a rule so we wouldn't spend three hours going in circles: the committee recommends approval exactly if there's real student demand AND we have the faculty capacity to staff it. Simple enough. We then voted on the three propositions seperately, majority wins on each, and here's what came out: demand, 3 to 2 in favour. Capacity, 3 to 2 in favour. The recommendation itself, 3 to 2 against. I've been over the ballots three times now — every member's three answers are internally consistent, nobody abstained, nobody changed their mind mid-meeting, and I redid the tallies in a spreadsheet. The breakdown was: two people yes on all three, one person thought there was demand but no capacity and voted against, one thought there was capacity but no demand and voted against, one voted no on everything. So by our own agreed rule we've ended up recommending against launching the program on the grounds that we do have demand and we do have the capacity. I keep staring at this. My best guess is that something went wrong procedurally — maybe the order we voted in, or the fact that we voted on the two premises before the conclusion, or maybe we should have balloted all three at once instead of one at a time. I even looked around online and found something called the discursive dilemma, but from what I can tell that's about groups where the individual members are themselves being illogical, which is definately not our problem here; we're all consistent, that's the whole point of the rule. One colleague says it's basically just Arrow's theorem so all voting is broken and we should stop pretending, and another says the obvious fix is to skip the premises and just vote on the bottom line.

So I'd like you to tell me what actually went wrong and how we should have run this. If the answer is that we should only vote on the conclusion, I want to know what we'd be giving up — the two premise votes were the interesting part of the discussion and I don't love throwing them away — and whether some other ordering of the votes, or a different way of cutting the question up, gets us a set of committee positions that's both consistent and actually reflects what each of us thinks. And if you think this shows that committee decisions like this are just hopeless and I should hand it to the dean, say so, but tell me why. Also, is my colleague right that this is Arrow's theorem? It doesn't feel like the same thing to me but I can't articulate why.
