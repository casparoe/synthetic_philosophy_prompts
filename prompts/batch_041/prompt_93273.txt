For some years I have been turning over Leibniz's idea that this is the best of all possible worlds — an idea I first met as the punchline of an eighteenth-century satire and have gradually come to think is more serious than its reputation — and I have arrived at an argument that seems to follow from it but whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would like help finding the fault. As I reconstruct it: (1) God is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good. (2) A perfectly good God who could create any one of the infinitely many worlds he conceives would choose the best of them. (3) Being omniscient he knows which world is best, and being omnipotent he can make it actual. (4) Therefore the world that exists is the best of all possible worlds. (5) If the world that exists is the best possible, then everything in it — every plague, every war, every betrayal — is an ingredient without which the whole would have been worse. (6) Therefore none of these things is genuinely evil, only apparently so, and no one who does them is genuinely to blame, since a perfect God settled on the arrangement in which they were required. (7) So the doctrine of the best of all possible worlds abolishes evil and moral responsibility alike.

(1) to (4) strike me as very nearly unanswerable, and (7) strikes me as absurd, so I take it that the mischief lies somewhere in (5) or (6); yet each candidate I try either leaves (7) following anyway or costs me (4), and I cannot tell whether I am missing a real distinction or quietly inventing one. I would most like to keep (1) to (4) together with the ordinary thought that people are answerable for what they do; what I am willing to surrender is whatever must go for the sake of that.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so compelling, and what rejecting it costs — above all, whether I can keep the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds while still holding people responsible for their actions. And please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who study Leibniz; I do not want a disputed reading presented to me as the consensus.
