Ok so next Thursday I present at our dept's culture/cognition workshop and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not moral support. My paper argues that the gender-equality paradox -- the finding that richer, more gender-equal countries show *bigger* sex differences in stuff like choosing STEM vs. people-centred work, not smaller -- is basically uninformative about innate sex differences, and that both of the standard readings of it ('more freedom just lets nature show through' vs. 'it's pure socialisation') get the explanandum wrong.

My actual claim is this: what rises with gender equality isn't the sex difference in preferences, it's the return to *acting on* a preference. In poor/low-equality economies occupational choice is bottlenecked by money, credentials, family pressure and just how many slots exist, so the sex composition of a field mostly tracks who's allowed in. In rich/high-equality economies choice tracks disposition much more closely and the labour market rewards specialisation, so fields sort more sharply along whatever dispositional axis is relevant. Net: the gap widens because sorting got better, not because the underlying disposition got more extreme. Two consequences I want to push: (a) you can't read the *origin* of a disposition off the size of the gap, only its *actionability*; and (b) the same widening should show up for any disposition with a small mean sex difference and a big return to sorting -- personality traits, say -- and if it does, that's the tell we're looking at a sorting effect and not a nature effect.
