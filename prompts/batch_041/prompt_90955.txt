I am a backend engineer by trade with no formal training in philosophy, and I have fallen into a rabbit hole that began as a weekend project: I wrote a small program that solves letter-string analogy puzzles of the form "abc goes to abd, so what does kji go to?" — the sort of thing Douglas Hofstadter's Copycat was built around. My toy solver has a fixed catalogue of primitives it can apply to a string: take the successor or predecessor of a letter, mark one letter as standing in the same role as another, reverse a string, treat a run of letters as a single group, and a handful of others in that vein. It handles the easy puzzles and then hits a wall on the ones where the winning move is not any of those primitives but a shift in what counts as "the same thing" from one string to the next — where the salient correspondence stops being letter-to-letter and becomes, say, first-to-last, or left-to-right-to-right-to-left, or same-letter-to-mirrored-letter. My friends (a mix of machine-learning people and one philosophy graduate student) split on what this wall means. The ML people say I simply need a larger catalogue, or a learned representation rich enough that the wall disappears on its own; the philosophy student says the wall is the entire point, and that no fixed catalogue, however large, can dissolve it, because the system has to re-conceive its own categories mid-problem rather than search harder within them.

Please make the argument for one side of this. I am most drawn to the case that the wall is real — that this kind of conceptual slippage is not reducible to search over a fixed space of representations — but argue whichever side you actually think is defensible, and say so plainly if that turns out to be the other one. I would like you to be concrete rather than gestural: take my framing apart (is "fixed catalogue" even a well-defined notion, or does any representation rich enough to be interesting quietly smuggle the slippage back in?), give me the strongest version of the opposing view and tell me where my own reasoning is most likely to fail, and explain any term you introduce rather than pointing me toward things to read — assume I cannot look anything up.

Finally, state what would change your mind: which observation about a working analogy program, or which argument, would lead you to abandon the position you take. I would rather have a verdict with its escape hatch marked than a survey that refuses to land anywhere.
