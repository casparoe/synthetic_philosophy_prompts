For the past several months I have been sitting on the ethics and policy committee of a mid-sized nonprofit that runs a twenty-four-hour hotline for people trying to leave abusive relationships, and I have been asked to draft the portion of our staff handbook that governs when, if ever, a counselor may deceive a caller. The obvious cases are not the difficulty; the hard ones involve callers who are almost certainly the abuser trying to learn where a partner has gone, or relatives phoning to apply pressure on the victim's behalf. We currently tell staff, in effect, never to lie, but that instruction is doing less work than it appears to, and I have been arguing about it with a friend who teaches moral philosophy. Her position, which she presents as a repair of Kant rather than a rejection of him, runs roughly as follows: the prohibition on lying is not fundamentally about the falsity of what we say but about the standing of the person we say it to — lying is wrong because it seizes control of another person's deliberation and turns it toward our ends, treating them as an instrument rather than as a fellow reasoner. On that reading, she argues, the murderer at the door has already left the reciprocal community of reasoners by aiming to destroy the very person he is questioning, so the duty the classic example is meant to illustrate simply does not reach him, and the same is true of an abuser pursuing that aim more slowly. She grants that her reading has to discount Kant's insistence that a lie also wrongs the liar, by degrading his own rational nature, and she treats that strand of his view as secondary rather than central. The working criterion she extracts from all this is that a questioner forfeits any claim to a truthful answer when the truthful answer could serve no innocent purpose — when the only use of the information would be to harm the person it concerns. I am drawn to the repair but suspect it is more fragile than it looks, and I want to know what it would commit us to in practice before I put anything resembling it into a document that staff will be following, under pressure, at three in the morning.

Please describe the most important implications of taking this revisionist reading seriously — both for the concrete decisions our counselors face and for the wider norms of honesty on which a hotline depends, since callers will trust us only if they believe we do not deceive them. I am especially interested in the implications that are easy to miss: those that come into view only when the reading is pushed into cases the murderer at the door was never designed to cover, such as deceiving a caller who is merely confused rather than malicious, or deceiving one party in a way that later becomes visible to another, or applying her forfeiture test to a questioner whose purpose is mixed. Where you can, indicate which of the implications are load-bearing for the rest, so that I can see where the structure actually bears weight. And please be candid about the limits of your own reasoning: tell me which single consideration, if it turned out one way rather than the other, would most change your assessment — in other words, what would lead you to conclude that the repair should be rejected rather than adopted, and where your argument is most likely to fail.
