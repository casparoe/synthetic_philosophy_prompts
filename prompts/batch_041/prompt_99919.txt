I'm in a reading group that has spent most of a term on a thought experiment that circulates under the name torture versus dust specks: either one person is tortured for fifty years, or some astronomically large number of people each receive a single dust speck in the eye that they barely register. The aggregative side holds that for a large enough number of specks the speck-option is worse, on the reasoning that each speck is a small disvalue and enough small disvalues can outweigh any fixed disvalue. The other side holds that no number of specks, however large, is worse than the torture, because torture and a speck are not the same kind of thing and the lesser cannot sum its way past the greater. We have rehearsed the obvious moves and nothing has budged in months; each side finds the other's verdict close to monstrous, and I have begun to suspect that we are not disagreeing about the case at all. The aggregative side also presses what looks like a sorites or no-difference worry — if each added speck makes the outcome a little worse, there seems to be no point at which accumulation stops counting, yet the lexical side insists it never crosses the torture line — and that exchange in particular has the feel of two people talking past each other.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis since I would like you to tell me if it is wrong, is that the impasse is at bottom a formal one about what kind of value ordering is on the table. The aggregative side seems to presuppose that the values involved can be represented by real numbers and that the resulting ordering satisfies something like an Archimedean or continuity condition, so that enough of a small bad can overtake a large one. The other side looks committed to a lexicographic ordering, in which the avoidance of torture sits lexically above the avoidance of specks and no quantity of the lower can trade against the higher — and such orderings notoriously cannot be represented by a real-valued utility function. If that is the real fault line, the dispute is neither a verbal quarrel about the word worse nor a straightforward disagreement about facts; it is a disagreement about the structural constraints a value function must satisfy, with each side's intuitions downstream of a commitment usually left implicit. But I can think of at least two rival diagnoses and want them weighed. First, that the sides are answering different questions under one heading — one about what maximizes total welfare, the other about what an agent may do or what a rights-violation is — so that both verdicts can be correct at once. Second, that the case lies so far outside the range where our moral intuitions were calibrated that any verdict is an artifact of an intuition pump, in which case no argument should move anyone and the stall is not a failure of reasoning at all.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the case. Say what kind of disagreement this actually is, grounded in what each side is committed to rather than a survey of positions, and tell me whether the formal reading I sketched is right, a partial truth, or a red herring. To keep it honest, run your diagnosis against two variants we have not yet discussed and that I think may separate the readings: (a) the specks are distributed so that each of the enormous number of people receives exactly one speck and no one receives more than one, and (b) the specks fall on people who are asleep and never notice them, so that no one's experience is worse in any respect. If the impasse is formal, these variants should leave the structure intact; if it turns on different questions or on intuitions being out of their depth, they may not. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and say why. End with a one-sentence statement of the diagnosis, then on the final line a short machine-readable label of the form \answer{...} naming the kind of disagreement (for instance a phrase such as formal/structural, verbal, different questions, or bedrock intuitions).
