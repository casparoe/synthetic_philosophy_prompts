For a reading group I am running, I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The dispute I have in mind is the long-running one between substantivalists, who hold that space, or spacetime, is itself a thing over and above the bodies and fields located in it, and relationists, who hold that it is nothing over and above the spatial and temporal relations among those bodies and fields. In recognizable form it runs from the correspondence between Leibniz and Clarke through Mach's suggestion that inertial effects are due to the distant stars, and it continues into the era of general relativity without any sign of closing.

What makes it a good candidate for the treatment I want is that the two sides agree on every observation and, as far as I can see, on every prediction. Everyone agrees that the water climbs the walls of the spinning bucket and that the bucket and the counter-rotating-stars scenario are empirically equivalent; everyone agrees that in general relativity the metric field is what one solves for. So the disagreement is not about what happens. It is about what must exist, or what must be fundamental, in order for what happens to be explained. A further wrinkle is that a third position has been staked out between the two camps: that the points of the manifold, considered apart from the metric field, are not real individuals at all, so that the argument from diffeomorphism invariance loses its sting. And there is a persistent suggestion in the literature that the whole dispute is really about surplus structure, or about the gauge status of the absolute elements, rather than about what exists.

I have a hypothesis, and I would like it tested rather than endorsed. My suspicion is that the debate has stalled because the question being asked has changed at least twice without anyone announcing the change. It began as a straightforwardly ontological question: is there, in addition to bodies and their relations, a further entity? It then became, at least for those impressed by the bucket, an explanatory question: what makes the difference between a bucket that spins relative to the fixed stars and a bucket that is spun while the stars are counter-rotated? And in the recent literature — I am thinking of the uses made of the fact that a solution of the field equations and its image under a diffeomorphism are physically indistinguishable, the argument usually called the hole argument — it looks to me as though it has become a third question, about which predicates or which ideology are the fundamental ones rather than about which objects exist. If that is right, then the two camps may no longer be answering the same question, and the apparent stalemate may be an artifact of one label, the substantivalism–relationism debate, covering three debates.

Please tell me how much of this survives scrutiny. Concretely: is the dispute verbal, in that the sides agree on all the facts and differ only over what they are willing to call a substance? Is it substantive but non-empirical — a disagreement about what stands in need of explanation, or about what could count as an explanation of inertial effects at all? Is it two questions under one heading, one about what exists and one about what is fundamental, with each side answering its own? Or is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, for instance a disagreement about the correct rule for reading ontology off a symmetry, where one side treats a transformation that leaves the physics invariant as evidence that the transformed objects are not real, and the other treats it as evidence only about which descriptions carve nature at its joints? I would like each of these options taken seriously and then set aside if it fails, rather than a survey of positions. I am not asking for a reading list.

Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal result that forces one side's hand, a change of question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why not. Please state how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and identify the single step in it that you are least sure of.
