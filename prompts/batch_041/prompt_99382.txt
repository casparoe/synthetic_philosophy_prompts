Let's use the 737 MAX crashes as a stress test for the responsibility-gap idea. Quick facts so we're on the same page: two crashes, MCAS quietly pushing the nose down off a single angle-of-attack sensor, pilots who hadn't been told the system existed trying to fight it, Boeing pointing at pilot error, certification largely delegated back to Boeing, a criminal settlement at the end of it. I want the theory read onto the case and then the case read back onto the theory. Take the responsibility-gap thesis at full strength -- when a system acts in ways its designers and operators couldn't reasonably foresee or control, the conditions for holding anyone responsible come apart and you're left with a gap -- and tell me who the candidate responsible parties are, what each of them knew and could have done, and whether the thesis gets this case right or just forces it. Then push the other way, because liability did land somewhere. So either the gap closes once you widen the net (Boeing, the FAA, the training regime), or what we're looking at isn't a gap at all but a "moral crumple zone" -- the human nearest the automation soaks up the blame for a failure they had almost no power over while the larger system stays intact. Which of those two diagnoses does the case actually support, and what does that tell you the gap theory got wrong about where the gap comes from: is it the degree of autonomy, or opacity and the diffusion of control? Dont smooth over the parts the theory can't reach -- say where it's silent.
