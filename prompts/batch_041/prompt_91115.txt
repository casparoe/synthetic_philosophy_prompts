I run the betting side of a small staking syndicate: eight backers, a shared bankroll, and me as the person who decides how much of it goes on each opportunity that comes through the door. The opportunities resist every clean treatment. Some are small and repeatable — a few hundred bets a year on markets where I have a thin but fairly stable edge. Some are large and one-shot: a single event, a position that will resolve once and then never exist again. Some arrive on the same afternoon and are correlated, because they are all really bets on the same underlying outcome wearing different names. For almost none of them do I have a probability distribution; I have a point estimate, a rough sense of how wrong I have historically been, and occasionally a market price that disagrees with me. Large positions also move the price, and there is a floor below which a bet is not worth placing at all. Our house rule has been to stake half of what the Kelly criterion recommends, capped at a fixed fraction, and none of the three partners can say whether that rule is a principled response to our ignorance or a lucky habit.

Please give me a decision procedure I can run, in order, on each opportunity: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a number, where it terminates in a refusal, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the question is not arithmetic. It needs to be runnable in about fifteen minutes by someone numerate but not a statistician, and auditable by my two partners, who have to sign off on anything above our cap.

The procedure has to survive the cases the textbook version does not. It must distinguish an edge that is repeatable from one that is genuinely one-shot, and be explicit about which part of the Kelly argument depends on the bet being rebettable many times. It must handle several correlated opportunities arriving at once, where sizing each as if it were alone would obviously overcommit us. It must say what to do with a point estimate and a sense of error instead of a distribution, and whether the standard advice to shrink toward a smaller fraction has a justification beyond folklore. It must be clear about whether the relevant quantity is the syndicate's bankroll or the backers' total wealth, given that the backers earn outside income and that a drawdown of the bankroll is not the same kind of event as a drawdown of their savings. And it must say plainly what it cannot decide, including the possibility that for the largest one-shot bets the Kelly calculation is answering a question nobody asked.

Then run it on three cases. First, a repeatable thin-edge bet of the kind we place weekly. Second, a one-shot event where my point estimate implies staking a fraction of the bankroll that would, if wrong, end the syndicate — I expect the procedure to refuse to endorse it, and I want to see exactly where and why it refuses, and what it says the decision actually turns on. Third, a single afternoon on which four correlated opportunities appear at once. Finally, tell me whether the same procedure carries over to a partner's non-financial wager — staking a large share of her time and professional reputation on a venture that will resolve once — or whether the extension is illegitimate, and if it is, what the nearest honest thing to say is. I care less about a number than about knowing which steps are real arithmetic, which are judgment wearing arithmetic's clothes, and where the procedure will produce a position I later regret.
