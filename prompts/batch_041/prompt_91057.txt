I teach high-school math and last month a kid asked me straight out whether numbers are real, and I heard myself give the canned answer -- "oh, they're just useful fictions" -- and realized I dont actually believe anything about this. Since then I've been reading up on the philosophy of math and I keep circling Benacerraf's dilemma: if numbers are abstract objects sitting outside space and time, how do we ever get in touch with them, and if they're not, how do we get to keep saying 2+2=4 is true rather than just a legal move in a game.

Then I re-read Ted Chiang's "Division by Zero," the one where a mathematician works out a proof that arithmetic is inconsistent and it basically ends her life, and now I want to teach it. Read it through Benacerraf for me. Which horn is she actually impaled on when she loses her faith in math? Does the story suggest the dilemma only bites if you insist on one uniform notion of truth across math and everything else, or does it just dramatize the access problem -- she had some kind of contact with the mathematical, and the contact is what destroyed the thing she was in contact with?

And where does the story cheat? A proof that arithmetic is inconsistent would itself be a mathematical result, so it looks self-undermining -- from a contradiction anything follows, including the negation of the proof. Does the story know that, or does it just enjoy the spookiness of the word "inconsistent"?

I also want to know where the story resists the reading. Half of it is about her marriage, and the math may just be a stand-in for something you can't share with the person you love. Tell me which scenes to teach, and what the story gets right about mathematical certainty that the Benacerraf-style argument can't reach.
