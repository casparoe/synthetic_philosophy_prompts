I work in forecast evaluation. My day job is scoring probabilistic forecasts, so proper scoring rules (Brier, logarithmic, CRPS), the decomposition of a score into calibration and sharpness, Bayes risk, and the admissibility of decision rules are the water I swim in; philosophy I read as an amateur. I have been reading about a position usually called accuracy-first epistemology, and I want to know how much of it survives translation into my vocabulary, and what the failures of translation tell us.

As I understand the position: accuracy, closeness to the truth, is the sole fundamental epistemic good; the accuracy of a credence function is measured by a scoring rule, so that the score it earns at a world is fixed by the credence it assigns to each proposition together with that proposition's truth value there; and the substantive results are then derived by dominance or by expected accuracy. Credences that violate the probability axioms are said to be accuracy-dominated — for any strictly proper scoring rule there is a coherent credence function at least as accurate at every world and strictly more accurate at some — and a similar expected-accuracy argument is supposed to single out conditionalization as the diachronic rule.

Restate that program in my terms as faithfully as you can: credence functions as forecast rules, worlds as states of nature, scoring rules as loss functions, coherence as something in the neighbourhood of admissibility or Bayes-ness, expected accuracy as Bayes risk, conditionalization as an update rule. Do not improve it in translation, and be explicit wherever a term in my vocabulary is only approximately the right counterpart.

Then the residue report. Which claims carry over cleanly? Which carry over only if I accept a distortion, and what exactly is distorted? Which have no counterpart at all? I am particularly interested in four candidates for the last category, though I expect you to find others: the claim that accuracy is the only fundamental epistemic good, given that in my field the loss function is exogenous, chosen for a task rather than discovered; the fact that a scoring rule is applied to a credence function at a world, with no sample, no sampling distribution, and no expectation over data, so that the relevant dominance is statewise rather than in risk; the calibration-sharpness distinction, which in my field is a substantive claim about two separable virtues and in the accuracy-first program seems to be flattened into a single number; and the choice of scoring rule itself, which for me is a modelling decision and for them has to be defended on purely epistemic grounds.

I also want a specific hunch tested. My first pass is that the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism is just the admissibility of Bayes rules under a strictly proper loss, so that the program is standard decision theory with an unusually demanding statewise dominance criterion. Tell me where the hunch holds, where it breaks, and whether the break is informative — in particular whether anything is lost by the fact that admissibility is a norm for a procedure given a loss, whereas the philosophers seem to want a requirement of rationality.

Finally, run the translation in the other direction: name what my vocabulary cannot express, and say for each item whether the gap is a defect in my framework or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked.
