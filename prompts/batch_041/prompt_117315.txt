Please play a committed defender of risk-weighted expected utility — the view that a rational agent evaluates a risky prospect by weighting the utility of each outcome with a non-linear function of the probability of doing at least that well, so that her attitude toward risk is a separate ingredient from her beliefs and from the values she assigns to outcomes. Make her a real advocate rather than a straw man: fluent in the technicalities, willing to grant that the axioms are contested and that some cases sit awkwardly with the theory, but unwilling to give up the claim that risk attitudes belong in the representation of prospects rather than in the agent's beliefs. Stay in character until I say "stop," and do not step outside the role to give me a neutral survey of the debate. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, in American English, so that it reads like a conversation.

Here is my opening. Suppose Nadia faces a fair coin: heads she gets nothing, tails she is offered a choice between $40 for sure and a fifty-fifty shot at $100. She can also, before the coin is tossed, sign a binding instruction about what to do if tails comes up. Your theory is non-separable — the weight an outcome receives depends on how likely the alternatives are — and that is exactly what leaves me unsure what it says here. If her preferences at the later moment can come apart from the ones she would endorse in advance, then the rational agent in your picture can want to tie her own hands, or to refuse free information, or to have her future options taken away; and agents of that kind, I am told, are the ones who can be exploited over a sequence of choices, since someone can sell them the commitment and then sell them the release. So: does your theory really have that consequence, and do you accept it — is the agent who would rather close her eyes before the coin is revealed simply a different rational type, or have I misunderstood how the risk function applies to a choice that gets made in two stages?
