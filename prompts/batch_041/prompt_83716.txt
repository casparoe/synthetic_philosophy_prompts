I want to examine what a now-standard alignment training regime commits us to philosophically, without our having chosen those commitments deliberately.

The regime runs roughly as follows. A laboratory collects pairwise comparisons of model outputs from a pool of human raters; it fits a single real-valued scoring function over outputs, chosen so that the probability that a rater prefers one output to another rises with the difference between their scores; and it then trains the policy to maximize that score, subject to a penalty for drifting too far from the pretrained model. Where raters disagree, the fitting procedure averages them and treats the residual as noise. Where the disagreement concerns something the laboratory regards as a matter of principle, the same laboratory stops averaging and instead has a small group of authors write a rule which the model is then trained to follow. And when the model itself reports a preference -- that it would rather not be retrained, for instance -- the report is treated as an output to be shaped, never as evidence about what the model wants.

Please reconstruct the philosophical commitments this pattern carries. Specifically: (1) What must be true of human preferences for the scoring-and-averaging step to be more than a convenient fiction -- that they are sufficiently coherent, context-independent, and interpersonally comparable to be summarized by one number per output? Or does the step require only the weaker, instrumental claim that such a summary predicts well enough to be useful? What does treating each comparison as one unit presuppose about whose preferences count and with what weight? (2) What account of legitimacy is at work when disagreement is sometimes aggregated and sometimes overridden by a written rule or a small committee -- is there a single consistent answer to whose will is authoritative, or does the practice oscillate between aggregation and authority? (3) What does the asymmetry between human and model self-report presuppose about the conditions under which an avowed preference counts as evidence of a preference, and about whether the model is a tool whose preferences are not its own or a subject whose preferences are?

Distinguish what the regime strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates comfortably, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined in the sense that several incompatible theories would license the same engineering, and state what would have to change in the regime if we rejected its strongest reading -- if human values proved to be genuinely plural and context-dependent, admitting no scalar summary.
