I just spent most of last night arguing with my old flatmate — now a fully paid-up neo-republican who reads every political question through non-domination — about whether republican liberty is a genuine third thing or just negative liberty in a nicer coat, and I'm presenting my paper on it at a small political theory workshop next week, so what I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk. My line is this: define freedom as the absence of a standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily and you've added a counterfactual clause that no negative account needs — a claim about what someone *could* do to you rather than what they do — and that clause does all the work, so either non-domination collapses back into negative liberty or it quietly turns into a claim about the conditions of self-mastery, i.e. positive liberty, and you can't have it both ways. The case that started the whole argument is the one I'm leaning on: someone on long-term disability benefits whose caseworker never once interferes with anything, but whose every choice is made in the shadow of an annual review that could cut them off — my friend says that's domination without interference and I say it just shows the counterfactual doing hidden work and the republican needing positive liberty to explain why it's bad. The room will be two committed neo-republicans who know the Pettit and Skinner line cold and will definately die on the counterfactual, one Hobbes guy who thinks the positive/negative distinction is a muddle that predates Berlin, and three analytic liberals who'll keep asking what any of this changes for speech codes, workplace rules, or platform moderation. Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, a two-minute reply for each, what I should concede up front, and where my reply is soft — plus the one question I'm praying nobody asks. Start with the objection that would actually sink me and why, bottom line first, then work down, no preamble.
