I'm a graduate student in philosophy of physics, and I'm putting together a handout for a reading group session on the arrow of time that I want to make an exercise in judgment rather than another survey. What I'm after is a tier list, not an essay. Here are six candidate accounts of where the temporal asymmetry comes from, as I've been formulating them for the handout; two of them are minority positions and one is my own attempt at a synthesis, so please don't assume the list is the standard one. (1) The thermodynamic account: a special low-entropy initial macrostate, together with the statistical tendency of entropy to increase, yields the thermodynamic arrow, and every other arrow — epistemic, causal, psychological — is parasitic on it. (2) The cosmological account: the orientation is fixed by boundary conditions on the universe as a whole — that it expands out of a smooth, low-gravity-entropy state — and the thermodynamic arrow is downstream of this. (3) The dynamical account: the fundamental laws are not time-symmetric — think of the T-violation seen in the weak interaction, or of an objective collapse in quantum mechanics — and the world's arrow simply inherits that asymmetry. (4) The records/epistemic account: the asymmetry lies in the direction in which records accumulate and retrodiction is possible, so the arrow is a fact about our epistemic access rather than about the dynamics. (5) The causal/agency account: the arrow is grounded in our capacity to intervene and in the means–end structure of action, so causation is prior to thermodynamics rather than derived from it. (6) My own "compatible-history multiplicity" account: at any moment the number of distinct microhistories compatible with the present macrostate is far smaller for the past than the number of futures branching off it, and the arrow just is the direction in which that multiplicity grows — entropy increase being the shadow of our losing track of which history we came from.

Rank these into tiers by how much genuine, non-circular explanatory work each one actually does, and give each a score between 0 and 1 for its share of the explanatory burden. An account should be demoted if it (a) presupposes the very asymmetry it is meant to explain, (b) merely relabels one arrow as another and then presents the relabeled arrow as the source, or (c) stops at a boundary condition without saying why that condition is special. For each item, give me one sentence of justification for its placement and one sentence on the strongest thing that can be said on its behalf. Then answer three questions: which of these are secretly the same account wearing different clothes; which one, if any, survives all three failure modes; and whether my sixth item is a genuine competitor or just the first item in a costume — be blunt about that last one, because a friend in the group has already drafted the accusation of circularity and I would rather find out now than in the room.

Finally, two of the other participants have staked out positions I need to be able to answer. One insists that the observed T-violation settles the matter outright, so that any purely statistical account is superfluous; the other thinks the whole dispute is verbal and that all six items are answering different questions. Give me a single paragraph replying to each that I can actually defend in discussion, and tell me which of the two is closer to being right.
