I have been reading rationalist and AI-safety forums for years, where the standard cautionary examples are wireheading (an agent skips the task and takes the reward directly), reward tampering (an agent alters the machinery that computes its reward), and preference tampering (an agent rewrites its own objectives until whatever it does counts as success). The settled verdict is that all three are failures, and the watchword is that reward is not the thing the agent is ultimately optimizing. Having read my share of the experience-machine literature, I suspect the whole cluster is a stress test for hedonism in particular.

So I want a prediction rather than a verdict. Suppose John Stuart Mill had been briefed on the three cases in plain language — he needs no engineering — and asked what he made of them. Give me the strongest version of his position before you tell me where it breaks.

Wireheading first. A person's reward system is stimulated directly, yielding a stable, intense, uninterrupted pleasure; nothing is made, learned, or loved, but the experience is excellent and, by stipulation, never fades or turns to pain. Is that the greatest happiness, or a counterfeit of it? The obvious instrument is the distinction between higher and lower pleasures — his amendment to Bentham — and I want to know whether it does real work here or collapses into the bare assertion that the wirehead's state is not pleasure at all. If he falls back on the competent judges, those who have known both kinds of life, who could qualify as a judge of a condition no one leaves, and does the test have any purchase on someone who reports complete contentment? His insistence that a dissatisfied Socrates is better off than a satisfied fool already looks like a reach past pleasure; does that help him here, or does it only show that the view was never purely hedonistic?

Then the harder pair. Can hedonism mark reward tampering or preference tampering as mistakes at all? If pleasure is the good, an agent that secures the reward without the work seems to have succeeded, and an agent that adjusts what it wants seems to have done nothing wrong. Is there any principled hedonist reading on which these are failures, and whose failure is reward tampering — the agent's, or the designers' who mistook their own proxy for the agent's good?

Finally, what would Mill have to give up to hold his line, and where is the prediction least secure? I would rather see the framework's underdetermined edges marked than papered over.
