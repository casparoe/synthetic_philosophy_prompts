I'm a health policy graduate student, and for the last few weeks a friend of mine who works in health economics and I have been arguing about the value system that seems to be baked into the way public health systems ration care. The system we've been studying in my seminar does three things at once, and it's the combination that has us stuck. First, it funds a treatment only when the cost per unit of health gained falls below a fixed threshold, so a cheap intervention that helps a little can be refused while an expensive one that helps a great deal is covered. Second, it isn't purely mechanical about that threshold: it gives explicit extra weight to patients whose condition is already more severe, so that two treatments buying the same amount of health get treated differently depending on how badly off the patient was before treatment. Third, and this is the part that started the fight, it forbids anyone from buying privately, out of pocket, the same treatments the public system covers — you cannot pay to jump a queue or to buy a faster version of a covered service, even when your money would not cost the public system a penny.

My friend says the third rule is indefensible because it is what philosophers call leveling down: it makes the well-off worse off without making anyone better off, and he claims a rule like that can only be defended by someone who thinks inequality is bad in itself, apart from how it affects anybody's health. I'm not sure he's right, and I'm not even sure the three rules hang together. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but a reconstruction of the practice: what view of value — about whose health counts, and how much — does this package presuppose? Which parts of it strictly require a particular view, and which merely sit comfortably with several? Does the severity weighting commit the system to the idea that helping the worse-off matters more, and if so, is that the same idea the third rule needs or a different one? Is the ban on private purchase genuinely committed to inequality being bad in itself, or could it be defended entirely by its effects — protecting the public system's political support, preventing a second tier from drawing away staff and money, preserving a shared standard of care — in which case the practice would be compatible with a view that cares only about how well off people are and not at all about how equal they are? And is there a tension between the first rule, which appears to look only at total health produced, and the second, which appears to care about who receives it? Tell me too what would actually have to change in the practice if we swapped its implicit view for my friend's preferred one, which cares only about making people as well off as possible and not at all about equality, and then for the opposite extreme, a view that treats equality as valuable in itself; where exactly would the three rules come apart? I care about the analysis of the existing practice rather than about which view is correct, though if the practice turns out to be committed to something implausible, I'd like you to say so and explain why.
