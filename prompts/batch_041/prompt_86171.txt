I teach a seminar on 18th-century theories of happiness and last week we did Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur (mid-1740s, published long after she died). The students were charmed and completely disarmed — they could see the practical advice and couldn't see how anything could count against it, which is exactly the reaction I want to fix. So I'd like you to do the hostile work for me: lay out what would count against her position, ranked from the ones that would really hurt to the ones that are merely awkward, and tell me which of them she'd be willing to concede herself (from inside her own text) and which she'd wave away.

Here's the position as I reconstruct it. Happiness is largely in our own power and consists in satisfied tastes and passions — she says at one point that one is happy only through satisfied tastes and passions. To get there you need to have shed your prejudices, be virtuous, be in good health, have strong tastes and passions, and — the striking bit — be susceptible to illusions, because, as she writes, «Il faut, pour être heureux, s'être défait des préjugés, être vertueux, se bien porter, avoir des goûts et des passions, être susceptible d'illusions, car nous devons la plupart de nos plaisirs à l'illusion, et malheureux est celui qui la perd.» Roughly: to be happy you must be free of prejudice, be virtuous, be healthy, have tastes and passions, and be capable of illusions, since we owe most of our pleasures to illusion, and wretched is the one who loses it. Her own illustration is domestic — ten happy years with Voltaire, sustained, on her telling, by the illusion of believing herself loved.

What I want is a map of the exposure, not a summary of the text. Some things I'd especially like you to press. Whether a deliberate policy of cultivating and preserving illusions is even coherent: can you will an illusion, can you keep one while knowing that's what you're doing, and what happens with illusions about one's own illusions? Whether the view is Epicurean (satisfied desires) or Stoic (self-management), since the list includes health and so concedes a lot to luck — which of the two she's actually committed to, and which has the better defences. Whether the real mechanism might be something more respectable than illusion (hope, engagement, attention, a sense of efficacy) and what evidence would seperate the two, given that by her own account the illusion is invisible to the person holding it. Whether the taste for study — the passion she treats as most within our control — is doing all the load-bearing work, and what becomes of the theory when that passion goes (a mathematician who loses her powers, or just plain anhedonia: is she committed to blaming the depressed?). And whether "happiness requires illusion" is a conceptual claim or an empirical one, and whether the slide between them is what keeps her from ever being refuted.

Take a definite side: tell me which single defeater you'd press if you had one shot, whether the position survives in a form still worth teaching, and if so what it looks like once the casualties are cleared. No reading list needed, I know the literature well enough — I want the argument.
