I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've drifted into a monthly reading group with two friends who did degrees in it, which is how I ended up working through Aristotle's ethics on my own time and getting stuck on something I can't resolve. The whole system seems to rest on durable character: you acquire a virtue the way you acquire any skill, by repeatedly doing the corresponding acts until the disposition settles in, and the practically wise person is the one who can read what a particular situation calls for. Then I came across the social-psychology literature that the philosophers in the group keep referring to as the situationist challenge — experiments in which what people actually do is predicted far better by the immediate circumstances than by anything you could call their character, the famous one being theology students hurrying across campus to give a talk, some of them on the parable of the Good Samaritan, who mostly walk past a person slumped in a doorway coughing when they are running late. The philosophers who lean on these results say the notion of a robust trait that holds across situations is empirically empty. The reply I keep getting from my friends is that Aristotle meant something closer to practical wisdom than to a score on a personality inventory, and that the situationists are attacking a strawman. That reply feels suspiciously convenient to me — a way of making the theory immune to evidence — but I don't have the background to say whether it's legitimate or a dodge.

What I want from you is a prediction of what Aristotle's own position would be if he were shown this literature and asked to respond to it, argued the way he argues rather than in the idiom of a modern virtue ethicist. Treat him as a thinker with a definite method and definite commitments, and work out where those commitments would land him on questions he never had the chance to face: whether he would even accept the experimental findings as the kind of thing that bears on an ethical theory, given his views about the precision ethics can aim at and about the difference between a well-brought-up person's judgment and a general empirical survey; whether the traits he describes were ever meant to be robust across situations in the way the psychologists test for, or whether that expectation is a modern reading imposed on him; and, if he would grant the empirical point, whether the ethics could survive intact with the weight shifted from settled dispositions onto something else. Two things I'd especially like you to press on. First, there's a puzzle I keep circling: he says we become just by doing just acts, but doing a just act seems to require already being just, so it isn't obvious what the beginner is actually doing or how the dispositions get off the ground — I suspect the answer has to do with what habituation does to a person's perception of a situation rather than to their trait-profile, and I want to know if that's a defensible reading or my own invention. Second, one of my friends insists the real target of the situationists is globalism, the claim that a trait shows up across all situations, and that a theory which only needs situation-specific dispositions is untouched; I'd like you to say whether Aristotle is committed to globalism in that sense, and whether his virtuous person, trained for years and raised well, is supposed to be the kind of case the experiments even address, or whether the studies only show what happens without habituation — and if you think that's a fair move, say so plainly, because I can't tell whether it's a genuine reply or a way of never losing.
