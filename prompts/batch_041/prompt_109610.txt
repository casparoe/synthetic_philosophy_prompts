I'm writing a feature on the Anthropocene's failure to win formal status as a geological epoch, and I'd like to hand readers a diagnostic rather than another timeline. What I want from you is a decision tree — or a taxonomy, if a tree turns out to be the wrong shape — that sorts people not by which start date they prefer but by what they think a boundary in Earth history is and who they think has the standing to draw it.

The occasion is the 2024 vote. After roughly fifteen years of work, the Anthropocene Working Group's proposal to recognize an epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century — marked by the plutonium fallout of atmospheric nuclear tests, with a reference section in the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario — was rejected by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy and then, by a near-unanimous margin, by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences. The term nonetheless survives as a descriptor, and that is precisely the tangle I want the tree to expose: everyone agrees the concept names something real, and no one agrees how to date it or who should be allowed to.

Please build the instrument so that every node is a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — phrased as a concrete case or a concrete claim, never as "do you think the Anthropocene is a geological unit?" The first fork should separate those who treat "Anthropocene" as a claim about the rock record, answerable by stratigraphic criteria, from those who treat it as a claim about human history, whose boundaries are set by values and are not the kind of thing a rock can settle. From the stratigraphic side, the next nodes should be about what makes a marker legitimate: whether it must be globally isochronous, whether it must be preserved and detectable in a single reference section, whether it must coincide with a genuine Earth-system transition, and whether the marker should be a novel material signature — plutonium, plastics, fly ash, technofossils — or may be a shift in an existing signal such as carbon dioxide or methane. From the value-laden side, the nodes should ask whether a start date should track the onset of a causal process (industrialization, capitalism, agriculture, colonization), the onset of measurable global impact, or the moment humans acquired the knowledge and therefore the responsibility.

The leaves must carry the actual positions, each named, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. At minimum I need: the late-eighteenth-century Industrial Revolution boundary associated with the term's popularizers; the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration boundary the Working Group proposed; the 1610 "Orbis spike" proposal, which ties the start to the collapse of Indigenous populations after 1492 and the resulting reforestation and dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide; the early or Neolithic Anthropocene that locates the beginning in thousands of years of farming and land clearing; the Capitalocene and its kin, which relocate the cause from a technology to a social and economic system; the view that the Anthropocene is an event rather than an epoch and need not have a single base at all; and the austere position that keeps the Holocene and grants the term no geological standing whatever. The "who decides" axis deserves its own branch and must not be folded into the dating question: decide between those who vest authority in the formal stratigraphic bodies and their procedures, those who think the broader Earth-system science community should settle it, those who think no one needs to because it is an informal descriptor, and those who think the question is political or ethical and should never have been delegated to geologists in the first place.

Finally, be honest about the instrument's limits. Where two leaves cannot really be separated by your questions, say so and explain why. Where the logical space contains a combination nobody occupies — I suspect a strict stratigraphic formalist who insists on an agricultural base, and perhaps a reader who wants a precise date but denies anyone the authority to fix it — mark the cell empty and diagnose it: is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Aim for somewhere between ten and fourteen nodes and six to nine leaves, and keep the prose tight enough to run in a magazine sidebar.
