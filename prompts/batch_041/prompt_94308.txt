I am a graduate student preparing a seminar presentation on Nietzsche's critique of moral genealogy, and I have been working carefully through the first essay of The Genealogy of Morals in the Samuel translation. There is a passage that I can parse grammatically but cannot quite make cohere philosophically, and I would be grateful for a patient, expert reconstruction of what is going on in it. The passage reads: "The judgment 'good' did not originate among those to whom goodness was shown. Much rather has it been the good themselves, that is, the aristocratic, the powerful, the high-stationed, the high-minded, who have felt that they themselves were good, and that their actions were good... It was out of this pathos of distance that they first arrogated the right to create values for their own profit, and to coin the names of such values: what had they to do with utility?" My difficulty is not with the individual sentences but with the connections between them. In particular: what exactly is the "pathos of distance", and why does Nietzsche treat it as the source of the antithesis between good and bad rather than merely as a psychological accompaniment to it? How does the act of naming — of coining names for values — relate to the "right to create values"? And why does he insist that the standpoint of utility is alien and inapplicable to this whole business, when the obvious objection is that the powerful do benefit from the values they create, so that utility of some kind seems to be present after all? I would also like help with the direction of explanation: is Nietzsche saying that the powerful simply posit values and that is the end of the matter, or is he making a subtler claim about the order of priority in our understanding of value, such that the judgment "good" is expressive of a certain mode of being rather than a response to usefulness?

Please act as a philosopher specialising in nineteenth-century European moral philosophy and in the interpretation of difficult historical texts. Begin by setting out, in plain terms, the literal claims the passage makes and the logical relations between them; then move to a charitable reconstruction of the underlying argument; then consider the most serious objection to that reconstruction and how Nietzsche might answer it. Think carefully and step by step, and avoid merely paraphrasing the passage back to me: I need the philosophical machinery made explicit.
