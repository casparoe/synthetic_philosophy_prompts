In the political philosophy reading group I go to, one member — I'll call him D. — has a single sentence he reaches for whenever distributive questions come up: "Nobody should be worse off than anyone else because of luck." He says it as though it settles the matter. Last week he used it twice in one session, and I cannot see how the same sentence does both jobs. First he argued that a colleague of his, permanently injured in a freak accident nobody could have foreseen or prevented, must be made whole by the rest of us, since the injury was pure bad luck and she did nothing to bring it on. Twenty minutes later he used the same sentence to argue that we owe nothing to a friend of his who lost his savings on a leveraged bet: the loss was the friend's own gamble and therefore his own responsibility. What gave me pause is that he conceded, when I pressed him, that the friend's taste for risk was never chosen — it had been formed long before he could have questioned it — and that this made the line between luck and choice feel less stable than he wanted. He has a name for the distinction he thinks he is drawing, between bad luck that simply befalls you and bad luck you court by taking a risk, but I noticed he only invoked it when it helped him.

I would like to know what that one sentence can actually be made to say. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, arranged from the reading that is barely more than an expression of sympathy to the ones that would commit him to serious distributive conclusions, and for each one tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or simply false, and whether it is the reading he must be relying on if his two verdicts are to be consistent with each other. I am especially interested in whether any single reading can license both the compensation verdict and the no-help verdict, or whether the slogan is quietly doing two different jobs that he has not noticed, and in what the concession about unchosen tastes does to the whole scheme. Treat it as a menu rather than a manifesto: I want the options set out side by side and judged, not one of them defended.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing summary.
