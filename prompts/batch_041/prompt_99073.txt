I would like you to construct a thought experiment for me, engineered to specification, on the theme of acts that are morally admirable but not morally required — the category usually called supererogation, in which omitting the act is entirely permissible, yet performing it is better than merely permissible.

The case is meant to isolate one question: whether the optionality of such acts can be grounded in something other than their cost to the agent. I want a case in which the act is cheap for the agent and its benefit is clear, so that the familiar explanation — that we may omit these acts because they are too demanding to require — is unavailable, and some other feature has to do the explanatory work.

Specifications:

1. The act must be genuinely optional (permissible to omit) and genuinely better than the permissible alternatives. It must not be a case where the agent has merely done something kind but morally indifferent.

2. The agent's cost must be negligible — no sacrifice of life, health, career, or significant money, and no serious ongoing burden. The act should be easy.

3. The optionality must not rest on uncertainty about whether the act will help, on the act being one of several equally good options, or on the agent's having promised nothing. It should rest on the agent's being entitled to dispose of something of their own as they see fit — a right or entitlement, rather than a burden.

4. The setting must be ordinary and realistic: an office, a team, a neighborhood, a small business. No trolleys, no emergencies, no dying strangers, no science fiction, and no appeal to information about the distant future.

5. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

After the scenario, please do the following. Identify the act precisely and say what the agent is permitted to do instead. State what an act-consequentialist must say about it, and what a Kantian-style deontology that treats supererogation as a real category must say, and show that they part ways over the entitlement rather than over the agent's cost. Then give a contrast case in which a nearly identical act is obligatory for both theories, with the entitlement as the only variable changed, so that the reader can see which feature is doing the work. Finish with an honest note on what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and on how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack the construction.

The verdicts must be generated by the theories rather than stipulated by the setup: it is not enough to declare the act optional. The optionality has to fall out of the entitlement, and I want to be able to see that it does.
