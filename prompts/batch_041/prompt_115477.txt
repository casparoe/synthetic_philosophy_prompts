I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and various rationalist blogs, and I've somehow been asked to give the "charge" at my friend's ordination. She's becoming a prison chaplain at a maximum-security prison. The room will be a mix: her family is devout evangelical, her seminary professors are mostly Reformed types who take penal substitutionary atonement as a given, and then there's a bloc of us from the old blogosphere who are atheists or agnostics and think the whole idea of one person being punished for another's crime is morally monstrous. I want to give a 10-minute charge that takes the doctrine seriously as philosophy without pretending I believe the metaphysics. The thing I keep chewing on is David Lewis's odd paper from the 90s where he asks whether we already believe in penal substitution in ordinary life. His move, as I remember it, is to distinguish a penalty from a punishment: if your friend pays your speeding fine, the penalty is paid, but your friend hasn't been punished, and you're still the one who broke the law. Lewis thinks this shows we're already committed to something like substitution of penalty, and that it makes the theological doctrine less incoherent than it looks, because what gets transferred is the debt, not the guilt or the punitive suffering.

But I want the speech to push on where that distinction gets shaky. In prison, the penalty just is the punishment — there's no clean separation between paying a debt and undergoing the thing. So my friend can help the men she works with bear consequences, she can stand in for them in some institutional ways, she can pay costs they can't, but she can't serve their time, and she can't make them innocent. I want that tension to be the spine of the speech. It needs to land for the evangelicals without turning into a sermon, and for the atheists without turning into a debate. I'm definately not a theologian, so keep the philosophy doing the work and don't let it drift into vague uplift. Can you write it? 800–1000 words, no direct quotes from Lewis, and don't cite anything — I don't have the paper in front of me and I don't want you making up quotations.
