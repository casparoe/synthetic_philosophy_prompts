I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of language; the group is sixteen students, seated at four tables, and the week's reading is the family-resemblance stretch of Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*. I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of two connected ideas before either is named: first, that a concept can hold together with no single feature common to all its instances, the strength of the thread coming from overlapping fibres rather than from one fibre running the whole length; second, that what settles the concept's extension at the disputed edges is not a hidden essence waiting to be discovered but what the community goes on doing. I do not want a lecture with a game bolted on. The rules themselves should arrange for students to build a category extension by local, pairwise decisions, to find that no global definition survives the cases, to watch two equally defensible extensions diverge, and to discover that the divergence gets resolved by precedent and communal going-on rather than by closer inspection of the items.

Here is a rough mechanic I have in mind. Improve it, repair its predictable failure modes, or replace it outright if you can design something better.

Give each table a deck of about forty index cards, each naming a candidate member of a contested category — use a category that is not itself a philosophy term, and one whose boundaries are genuinely disputed rather than merely vague. I had thought of GAME, with cards spanning paradigm cases, near-paradigm cases, and genuine borderliners: chess, solitaire, poker, Olympic wrestling, a child's game of tag, a counting-out rhyme, hide-and-seek, war games, a stock-market simulation, a business negotiation, the dating game, a bullfight, a lottery, a single-player video game, a speedrun of that game, a mating ritual in a bird species, an AI playing Go against itself, a court proceeding, a rigged carnival booth, a game of make-believe in which a stick is a horse. The task: arrange all forty cards in a single chain in which each adjacent pair must share a feature, under the constraint that no feature may serve as the linking feature more than twice. That constraint is the whole point — it forbids the broad rescuing link (involves rules, is fun, has a winner) that would otherwise let one feature carry the entire chain, and it forces the group to build the extension fibre-on-fibre. After the chain is built, each table must rule on three new borderliner cards and defend the rulings using only local comparisons to cards already in their chain, with no appeal to a definition.

Then the second pass: run the identical deck under a different scoring rule — for instance, chains are scored for how surprising their links are, or must be defensible to an outside auditor who will pay for each link they cannot follow — and have the tables compare the extensions they produced. The same cards should land differently, which is the language-game half of the lesson: what a card means in the exercise depends on the activity it is being used in, not on a property it carries in isolation.

If time allows, close with a signpost card: an item that shares a feature with two non-adjacent regions of the chain and cannot be placed by further scrutiny of the card itself. Let the tables disagree, then let them settle it by appeal to what the other tables have done, and by training a newcomer to go on the same way. Do not name the rule-following material until the debrief.

What I need from you: the complete rules, a full materials list with an actual card list I can print, the scoring scheme and the small stakes (points or tokens, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including setup and the two passes, and facilitator moves for keeping the no-feature-twice constraint honest and for preventing one fast talker per table from building the chain alone. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical points — which moment is the overlapping-fibres point, which is the open texture at the border, which is the communal going-on that settles the signpost case — and should let students describe what happened in their own words before any term of art is introduced.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the near-certain attempt by one table to smuggle in a disjunctive definition and declare victory. Be frank, in a final section, about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief: I suspect it cannot reproduce the vertigo of the rule-following problem, since the game hands the students a social resolution rather than letting them feel that no fact about anyone's past behaviour determines the continuation, and it probably cannot reproduce the dialectical stakes of why anyone would demand an essence in the first place. Confirm or correct that, and tell me what else the activity leaves on the table.
