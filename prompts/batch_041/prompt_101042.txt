OK so I spent most of last night in a three-hour argument with my friend Devi about Camus and the death penalty, we got absolutely nowhere, and now I have to give the opening remarks at our abolition coalition's annual dinner on Friday and I've realised I've never actually had to say any of this out loud to a room.

The room is the whole problem. Roughly half of it is people who've been doing this work for twenty years, have read the Camus essay on the guillotine, and will definately notice if I'm bluffing my way through it. The other half got dragged here by somebody else — a couple of donors, a family whose daughter was murdered and who are members of the coalition, and one guy I'm pretty sure still supports the death penalty and is mostly here for the chicken. Eight minutes, after the main course. I can't lecture them, I can't guilt them, and I really can't stand up in front of people who've lost someone and explain to them that their anger is the problem.

The argument I want the whole thing to sit on is the one Devi kept pushing at me: that what's wrong with the death penalty isn't mainly the pain, it's the demand it makes. The state needs the condemned man to walk in on his own feet, hold himself together, be brave about it — and we call that dignity. It asks every single person involved to become something, and "every single person" includes the room. I think that's roughly what Camus is doing, though honestly Devi might have invented half of it, and I don't really care as long as the speech has a spine that holds the weight.

Two constraints. First, the coalition is about to endorse a bill that swaps death row for life without parole, so the speech has to be able to land there without pretending life without parole is humane — Devi's counter was that if the objection is really about what we become, then LWOP just makes jailers of us instead of executioners, and I want that answered on the page rather than dodged. Second, the man whose case is moving here has been on the row for something like thirty years; he can't just be a symbol, or the whole thing stops being about a person. Also please don't hand me any quotations from Camus — I'm not going to be the guy who misquotes him at a dinner.

Write the remarks. Last line should be a single sentence starting with "Takeaway:" that sums up the argument the speech actually makes.
