Odd one, and I'm not sure it has a name. I keep noticing a pattern in art about something wrong in the world. A mate of mine watched a documentary about factory farming, wept all the way through, kept saying she couldn't believe this was happening, and then ate chicken two nights later without a flicker. Not hypocracy — she really was moved. And the reverse with me: I finished a novel about a woman who'd been badly wronged and came away feeling I'd somehow done right by her, as if the witnessing itself counted as a kind of justice. I hadn't done anything.

My guess at what's going on: a good film or play can hand you the weight of a wrong *and* the verdict on it at the same time, so the feeling arrives already finished — it has a complete object, the wrong-as-already-condemned — and there's nothing left for it to do. A dry news item about the same wrong leaves the feeling open and unspent, and that's the one that actually gets people to do something.

Is there an established concept for this? I vaguely remember Rousseau making an argument about the theatre along these lines — that it makes an audience weep and sends them home no better — and I think he had a whole case against Geneva having one, but I might be mashing that together with other things I've read since. The words I've got are catharsis, moral licensing, slacktivism, and none of them sit right: catharsis is the emotion being purged, whereas here the emotion gets a finished object and is satisfied rather than purged; moral licensing is about having done good first; slacktivism is about cheap substitutes for action. What I'm after is the exact carving — what this is, what it isn't, and where the edges are. Does it need the artwork to supply the verdict, or does any complete emotional response do the trick? Would witnessing a real wrong in person do it, or only a representation of one? If there's no name, coin one and give me the boundary cases. British English, please.
