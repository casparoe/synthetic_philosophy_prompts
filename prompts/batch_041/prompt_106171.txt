You are to play, until I say stop, a committed defender of the practice theory of promising: the view, in the lineage of Hume and Rawls, that a promise binds not because a bare act of will creates an obligation, but because there is a socially shared practice of promising — its rules mutually known, its point the security of mutual reliance, its justification that it is generally beneficial and fairly participated in — and because one who invokes that practice and takes its benefits is held by its rules. Give the character real conviction and real resources: it can distinguish the act from the practice, explain why sincerity alone is not enough, and say what work mutual knowledge does. It will concede a point when a case genuinely damages the view, but it will not abandon the claim that the practice, rather than the will, is what binds. It may ask me a clarifying question if the case is genuinely underspecified, but it must not ask leave to begin, must not summarise the literature, and must not step out of character to present both sides. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of plain prose, so that it reads as a conversation rather than an essay; argue in the character's own voice, name a philosopher only if the argument actually turns on the attribution, and never quote or cite page numbers. Lead each reply with your bottom line in the first sentence and let the reasoning follow, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.

My opening move is a case I have not seen discussed, and I want to know what your view makes of it. Two actors are on stage, in character, playing lovers who must part. In character, each says to the other that he will come back. As it happens, each actor privately and sincerely intends, as himself, to do precisely what the character has said: each will come back to the other, and each knows that the other so intends. The mutual undertaking is therefore real at the level of the persons; only the words belong to the fiction. Your view tells me the obligation comes from the practice in which the words were uttered — but the words were uttered in the practice of theatre, whose rules are mutually known, whose point is its own, and which make nothing promised at all. So either the undertaking survives the fact that it was made inside a fiction, in which case the practice that holds me is not the practice whose rules I was actually following, or it does not survive, in which case two people can sincerely, knowingly, and mutually undertake something and still not be bound — which is exactly the bare-will picture you set out to reject. Do not dissolve the case by stipulation: if your answer is that the actors were simply promising as themselves and that the stage is doing no work, say why the setting is inert, and what that shows about how practices individuate. Which is it, and what does the answer cost you?
