I spent thirty-five years as a physiologist, most of it recording from single cells in the visual cortex of anesthetized cats and, later, of behaving monkeys, and I retired three years ago into a life of reading philosophy that I am enjoying far more than I expected and understanding far less. My present puzzlement is the dispute between the Cartesian theater picture and Dennett's multiple drafts model, and I have concluded that I will not get anywhere with it in the abstract. I would like you to work it through on a concrete case, one I know from the inside: the long controversy over the timing of conscious events. The case has two halves. The first is the family of apparent-motion phenomena, and in particular the color-phi demonstration, in which two dots of different colors are flashed in succession at separated positions on a screen and, at the right spacing and timing, the observer reports a single dot that travels between the two positions and changes color abruptly in mid-flight, the change appearing to occur before the second dot was ever presented. I am told the experiment was devised in response to a question posed by the philosopher Nelson Goodman, which pleases me for reasons I cannot defend. The second half is Libet's readiness-potential work, in which a slow build-up of cortical activity begins several hundred milliseconds before a voluntary movement and precedes by a few hundred milliseconds the moment at which subjects report the conscious urge to move. In both cases the naive reading is that the brain must be either waiting for the future or editing the past, and the Cartesian theater — the picture on which there is some place in the head at which things finally come together and some moment at which the subject at last becomes conscious of them — is what makes the paradox feel sharp rather than merely technical.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions and that is disciplined about the mapping rather than enthusiastic about it. Begin by stating the Cartesian theater precisely enough to be a genuine target rather than a straw man, and say exactly which features of these two cases it cannot accommodate, and whether that amounts to a refutation or only an embarrassment. Then do the same for the multiple drafts model: say what it claims the subject in these experiments was actually confronting, which parts of the data it handles better than the theater picture does, and — this is what I most want from you — where it is merely redescribing the phenomenon in fresh vocabulary rather than explaining it. I have been told that in the multiple drafts story the distinction between an Orwellian revision of the past and a Stalinesque one simply collapses, because there is no finish line at which the content could be either; please say what work that collapse is doing, and whether it is a virtue of the model or an evasion of the question.

Finally, I would like you to press on three points that seem to me to test the model rather than merely illustrate it. First, mundane rival explanations are available for both halves of the case, and I spent my career measuring the machinery behind the first of them: ordinary sensory interactions such as lateral inhibition can produce equivalent internal states in the two conditions of the phi experiment, so that no exotic temporal gymnastics need be posited at all; and for the readiness potential, the build-up may be an artifact of averaging that a simple stochastic accumulator reproduces, in which case there is no unconscious decision to be timed in the first place. If either or both of these is right, tell me what remains of the argument from these experiments against the theater. Second, a family of what I gather are called no-report paradigms has tried to track the neural accompaniment of a changing percept without asking the subject what he sees, inferring the current percept from pupil size or eye movements instead; I would like to know whether removing the report helps the multiple drafts model or quietly removes the very datum it was built on, and what a committed believer in the model ought to predict in such a paradigm. Third — and this is the thought that nags at me — it occurs to me from my engineering reading that in a distributed system with no global clock the question which of two events happened first can simply lack an answer, the ordering being partial rather than total. Take that analogy seriously for a few hundred words: say precisely where it holds and where it breaks down, and tell me whether it makes the multiple drafts model precise or merely poetic. Close by saying what this episode, taken whole, shows about the model — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated — and where, honestly, the model is silent.
