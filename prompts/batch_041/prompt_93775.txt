In 2003 a joint Indonesian–Australian team excavating Liang Bua cave on the island of Flores recovered the skeleton of a small hominin, LB1: an adult female, about 1.1 metres tall, with an endocranial volume in the chimpanzee range, roughly a third of the modern human mean. The hominin skeletal material at the site is now dated to roughly 60,000–100,000 years ago, the stone tools recovered alongside it to horizons as old as about 190,000 years, and the remains of dwarfed elephants occur in the same deposits. The species is generally interpreted as a case of insular dwarfism, though whether it descends from Javanese Homo erectus or from an earlier, smaller-bodied lineage is unsettled. Treat this as a test case for the view that moral status is grounded in cognitive capacities — sentience, self-awareness, the capacity to plan for and care about one's own future — rather than in species membership or in the size of a brain.

Run the analysis in both directions.

First, the theory on the case. On the capacity view, LB1's cranial capacity is by itself morally inert; what matters is which capacities were realized in that skull. Say precisely what the view needs to know, what it would conclude if these hominins had the minds of chimpanzees, and what it would conclude if they had the minds of eight-year-old humans. Then say what the view cannot decide here, and why the difficulty is not a borderline capacity but evidence that is permanently out of reach.

Second, the case on the theory. Whatever the view says about what grounds status, we in fact infer the capacities of extinct hominins from exactly the morphological and archaeological evidence the view downgrades: endocranial volume, toolkit complexity, cut-marked bone. Distinguish as sharply as you can between brain size as a ground of moral status and brain size as evidence for the capacities that ground it, and say whether that distinction rescues the capacity view or merely relocates the problem — in particular, whether a view that cannot be applied to a fossil without help from the very feature it declares irrelevant can still guide judgment.

Third, the part the theory is silent about. The capacity view is a view about individuals; this is a case about a population, a lineage, and eventually a set of bones that belong to the Republic of Indonesia and that were, at one point in the affair, removed from their repository by a rival researcher and returned damaged. Does anything follow from the capacity view about how such remains should be treated — as research material, as national patrimony, or as something closer to the remains of the dead? If nothing follows, say so plainly, and say what does the work instead.

Be explicit about which capacities you are invoking and why, do not let brain size stand in for a capacity without flagging that you are doing so, and where the view returns no verdict, say that rather than manufacturing one.
