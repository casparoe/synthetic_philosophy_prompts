I am a graduate student in philosophy, and for the past term I have been sitting in on a small reading group on formal epistemology; we have been working through the question of how a rational agent should revise her credences when the new information arrives in the form of a conditional rather than a categorical proposition. One of the regulars, Dr. Ellery, is a retired logician with strong and consistent intuitions about these cases, and we have taken to using her verdicts as a kind of touchstone. So far she has ruled on four cases. (1) You are certain you are in the Central zone; you are then told, “If you are in Central, the cache is under the palm tree.” She says you should become certain the cache is under the palm tree. (2) You are unsure whether you are in Central or North, but you already know that if you are in Central the cache is under the palm tree and if you are in North it is not; in other words, the material conditional “if Central then palm” is already certain for you. You are then told that very conditional. She says your credences should not change at all. (3) You know you are not in Central; you are told, “If you are in Central, the cache is under the palm tree.” She says no change. (4) You are certain you are in Central; you are told, “If you are in Central, then either the cache is under the palm tree or it is under the banyan tree.” She says you should conditionalize on the disjunction. In each case her verdict is the one you would get if you treated the conditional as a material conditional and conditionalized on it; but it is also the verdict you would get if you adopted what is sometimes called Adams conditioning, on which you leave the probability of the antecedent untouched and merely set the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent to one. Her four verdicts do not yet distinguish these two rules, because in each case the antecedent is either certain, impossible, or the material conditional is already certain.

Now we have a new case, and I want to predict what she will say before I present it to the group. You are lost in a territory divided into three zones: North, Central, and South. Your prior credences are P(North) = 1/4, P(Central) = 1/2, P(South) = 1/4. You know that within Central the terrain is either rocky or sandy, and your prior is that these are equally likely: P(Rocky | Central) = 1/2, P(Sandy | Central) = 1/2. You also know that rocky and sandy terrain occur only in Central. You ask a local, “If I am in Central, am I on rocky terrain?” The local answers, “If you are in Central, then you are on rocky terrain.” What will Dr. Ellery say your new credence in Central should be? What does each of the two rules predict for P(Central)? If her four earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the choice, which rule is the better extrapolation from them, and what further case — one she has not yet considered — would most cleanly force the two rules apart? I am less interested in the number than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rule that fits her verdicts just as well, and the case that would settle it.
