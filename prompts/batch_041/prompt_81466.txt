I am preparing a one-page handout for a graduate seminar on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than a summary. Build the tree out of the logical space before you name anything. The root question should be what the objection is a complaint about: the criterion of rightness itself, the decision procedure a conscientious agent could actually follow, or the overridingness of moral reasons. Let every other node be a question that separates one branch from its siblings — for instance, whether the cost falls on the agent's own projects, on a relationship, or on their standing to be blamed for not doing more; whether the objection concerns a single extreme sacrifice or the standing requirement to keep giving for as long as more good could be done; and whether its force depends on others' failure to comply. Phrase each node so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to.

The leaves should be positions rather than labels, and must include: a maximising act-consequentialism that simply accepts the demands; a version granting the agent a prerogative to weight their own projects more heavily; a satisficing version; a scalar version that abandons ought altogether; a version on which what is required of me is relaxed because others are not doing their share; a view that treats the demandingness intuition as a bias to be debunked rather than a datum; and a view that agrees consequentialism is too demanding but concludes only that morality is not overriding. Use concrete cases at the nodes rather than generalities: a marginal contribution to a collective rescue, an ongoing small cost against a single ruinous one, a demand that would cost the agent a relationship, and an agent who is already badly off relative to others. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Write in British English.
