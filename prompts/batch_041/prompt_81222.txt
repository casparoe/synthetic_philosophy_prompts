I'm closing out a reading group on Frank Ramsey this term and I want to end with a poem instead of a summary. The passage I keep coming back to is the one where he says his picture of the world is drawn in perspective and not like a model to scale, human beings filling the foreground and the stars shrunk to threepenny bits, and then he goes on to say the fact of the matter isn't in itself good or bad, it just thrills him and depresses someone else, and that he pities the other guy with reason, because being thrilled is better for all ones activities. I want a poem that takes that seriously without collapsing into either cosmic nihilism or a carpe diem pep talk, since the whole point is that the cosmic scale isn't a scale and the asymmetry between the two attitudes is practical rather than factual.

Form: five stanzas, each one line shorter than the last (5, 4, 3, 2, 1), then a final stanza that snaps back to five lines at roughly the length of the first stanza's lines. The shrinking should feel like the pull-back to cosmic scale, and the snap-back like the foreground reasserting itself — the form should be doing the argument, not decorating it. No rhyme, plain diction, and don't use the words perspective or scale in the poem itself, thats the trap. Someone who's read Ramsey should be able to tell which claim is being made, and someone who hasn't should still feel that a claim is being made. Give it a title of four words or fewer.
