Six months ago my younger brother was diagnosed with a degenerative neurological condition that will steadily erode his ability to form and to act on long-term plans, and it has unsettled the way I think about the boundary between misfortune and choice. Before I work through any more of the literature on distributive justice, I want a clear specification of what a theory in this area has to deliver.

Please write the desiderata for a luck-egalitarian theory of distributive justice — a theory that treats unchosen disadvantage as the central case of injustice. I have started a list of my own, but I want you to produce the full specification and to tell me which of my entries are genuine requirements and which are merely commitments of one camp: that the theory distinguish inequalities that are unjust from inequalities that are merely unequal; that it preserve a distinction between chosen and unchosen disadvantage even after we notice that every choice is itself shaped by unchosen circumstances and dispositions; that it not license leaving the imprudent to their fate; that it guarantee everyone some floor, or explain why it need not; that it respect the separateness of persons; that it say something defensible about people whose tastes or needs are unusually expensive to satisfy; and that it avoid treating the prudent and the talented as a resource for others.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then add the requirement I care about most: the theory must accommodate what I will call capacity luck — disadvantage that consists not in a bad outcome but in a diminished capacity to form or to act on long-term plans at all — and it must say whether this is a third category alongside the chosen/unchosen divide or whether it dissolves that divide entirely.

Finally, tell me which of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied and which single requirement does the most to rule out otherwise attractive theories. A sentence each on how the most familiar families fare against the list — the view that only unchosen inequality is unjust, the view that justice requires only a sufficient floor, and the view that justice concerns equal standing rather than equal shares — would help, but keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them; I do not need a reading list.
