You are a social choice theorist and formal epistemologist. I'd like you to help me stress-test a position I hold, reasoning carefully from first principles rather than reciting the textbook statement of the Condorcet jury theorem, and beginning with a short brainstorm of the ways the position could fail before committing to a ranking.

The position: I claim that the real engine of the jury theorem is not voter independence but the absence of what I'll call directional bias — a shared tendency for members' errors to point toward the same answer. On my view, independence matters only instrumentally, as one way of securing that absence. So I hold that even when voters are strongly correlated through a common cause, majority rule still beats both the average member's accuracy and any single member's accuracy, and becomes more reliable as the group grows, provided only that the shared error is symmetric — that it leaves voters no more likely to err toward one answer than the other. I conclude that the usual correlation objection is at most a worry about bias rather than about dependence, and therefore does not seriously limit the theorem's application to real deliberative bodies.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and in particular: the formal setups in which symmetric shared error still makes majority rule fail to converge or underperform; whether symmetry can actually do the work I am asking of it; which structural features of real bodies — shared media, opinion leaders, deference cascades, common priors — would break the symmetry condition or break the argument some other way; what a defender of my position should concede as counting against it and what they may legitimately dismiss; and an honest verdict on whether I have quietly replaced independence with some other assumption that is doing all the work. If the position turns out to be well insulated, say so, and say whether that is a problem.
