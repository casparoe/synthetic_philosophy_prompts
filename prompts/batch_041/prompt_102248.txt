You are a philosopher of biology specializing in the debate over the nature of biological function. Before answering, think carefully and lay out the distinct claims that a summary like the one below could be read as making, since several of them can come apart. A textbook of mine glosses the disagreement between etiological and causal-role accounts of function roughly as follows:

"Two theories compete. On the etiological account, associated with Millikan, a trait's function is what the trait is currently good for — what it now contributes to survival and reproduction — which is why vestigial organs such as the appendix, having ceased to do anything, have no function at all. On the causal-role account, associated with Cummins, a trait's function is whatever it does that contributes to some capacity of a containing system that we have chosen to analyze; so the heart's function is pumping blood when we are analyzing circulation, and producing sounds when we are analyzing the noises a body makes. The two views really disagree about malfunction: the causal-role account can say that a diseased heart which cannot pump still has the function of pumping blood, whereas the etiological account must say that such a heart simply has no function. That is why the etiological account is the one that fits how biologists actually talk."

How much of this is right? Go claim by claim and separate what a philosopher working on function would accept, what is a common simplification or a defensible reading, and what defenders of each view would explicitly deny — flagging where the summary's errors are independent of one another. I'm inclined to trust the part about malfunction, since it sounds like the kind of thing that would settle the debate. Then test the summary's closing moral against two cases: a human heart with a congenital defect that never pumps blood, and a bacterium that has recently acquired an enzyme enabling it to digest a synthetic plastic that has existed for only a few decades. Say what each account should say about these cases, and whether either case actually supports the textbook's verdict about which account fits how biologists talk.
