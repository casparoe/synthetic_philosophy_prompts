I spent thirty-five years doing experimental work — designing assays, worrying about operational definitions, learning to distrust my own introspection when it disagreed with the data — and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in earnest. The question I have been circling is what makes a life go well for the person whose life it is, and I have tried to write down where I have landed in as plain a form as I can manage. Before I inflict this on anyone else, I would like an audit.

Here is the position set.

1. Hedonism, in a stripped-down form: a person's life goes well for them exactly to the extent that the balance of their experience over its course is positive, and badly to the extent that it is negative. I treat "experience" as whatever occupies the stream of consciousness, so the view is not hostage to any particular theory of pleasure or of its measurement.

2. The experience machine. Offered the standard arrangement — a tank in which I live out an existence at least as rich in experience as the one I would otherwise have, with the ordinary world continuing behind me as a fiction I never discover — I would decline, and I do not regard my declining as a lapse or an irrationality; I think it is the response the situation calls for. Offered a narrower machine that leaves the world and my relations in it exactly as they are and merely subtracts the suffering — the illness, the grief, the dread — I would accept immediately.

3. Deception. Someone whose closest relationships are staged, whose friends and partner are paid, and who never learns of it, is worse off than she would have been had those relationships been real — worse off, not merely living a life less worth admiring — even though her experience is stipulated to be identical either way.

4. Undisclosed benefit. A patient who is unconscious and will never wake is run on the machine for thirty years and then dies; I take this to be a benefit conferred on her, and a benefit made no smaller by her never knowing about it.

5. Method. I regard the anti-machine intuition as evidence about human psychology rather than about well-being. It looks to me like loss aversion plus a lingering attachment to the notion that reality is more than a symbol, and I do not take it at face value when I am trying to work out what is good for someone.

6. Unwitnessed achievement. A mathematician who gets the difficult proof right and dies before telling anyone, having enjoyed the work, has fared better than a twin who does the same work, enjoys it equally, and gets it subtly wrong — better off, I mean, not just more estimable.

7. Death. I think death can be bad for the person who dies, and bad in a way that has nothing to do with anyone's experience of it, the dead person's included.

8. Scope. I think well-being is the only thing that is good for a person, while granting that well-being is not the only thing that makes a life valuable or worth admiring. I assume this one is unproblematic, but I include it so that you can tell me if I am wrong.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature — I have no use for reading lists, and you should not assume you can look anything up. Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension with which, ordered by severity, and which pairs only look like they are and why. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, which of the two commitments is the cheaper to abandon, and what abandoning it costs elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most trouble at the least expense, and what it leaves behind. Finally, say how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
