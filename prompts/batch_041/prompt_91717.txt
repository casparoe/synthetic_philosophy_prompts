I am presenting at next month's departmental colloquium a paper arguing that in the Platonia Dilemma — twenty anonymous participants, a prize paid only if exactly one of them sends in the required telegram, no communication or side-payment possible — each participant is rationally required to send it, and that this requirement is not a covert premise about team agency but falls out of treating one's own practical reasoning as evidence about what the other nineteen will do. My main new contribution is a variant I call Asymmetric Platonia: the twenty participants are known to be drawn from two populations, ten who have worked through the superrational argument and ten who are ordinary expected-utility maximizers, and nobody knows which ten are which. I claim the superrational answer in the symmetric case does not survive this asymmetry, and that this tells against the team-reasoning reading rather than against the evidentialist one. The audience is a philosophy department that is mostly ethicists and action theorists, with two decision theorists who work on causal versus evidential choice, one colleague writing on collective intentionality and team reasoning, a Kantian who will hear an old universalizability test in new clothes, and a visitor from the economics department who will say the whole literature is armchair and that the Luring Lottery variant, where the prize is divided among everyone who enters instead of being voided when more than one does, shows that people simply do not reason this way. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased in each group's own vocabulary, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, plus the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Give it to me as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way I would want it in an email from a trusted reader.
