In my school's philosophy club we are preparing for a debate, and I have been assigned the opening speech for the proposition that consequentialism cannot be put into practice because we can never know the consequences of our actions. The argument my teammate handed me rests on an analogy I have heard all my life: a butterfly's wingbeat on one continent sets off a tornado on another, so a difference too small to notice can be followed by a difference as large as you like. Our version runs: every choice I make — which charity I give to, whether I give at all, whether I buy coffee on the way to school — begins an unbounded chain of consequences I cannot trace. The child saved from malaria may grow up to be a tyrant, or may have grandchildren whose lives turn on that donation; so I cannot know whether my donation did more good than harm, and the donation and the concert ticket are equally defensible. Before I commit to the speech, I would like you to audit the analogy, because I have started to suspect that it proves too much.

Please do not assume the argument is either obviously sound or obviously silly. Work through the following:

- What is supposed to map onto what? Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the part of the wingbeat, of the air, of the tornado, of the forecaster who cannot say what the weather will do.
- The engine of the analogy is a claim about sensitivity to initial conditions in a chaotic system. Is that the same as the claim that the difference between my acting and my not acting is large? These seem to come apart: a system may be wildly sensitive to its exact state while barely responding to any particular small intervention, and someone who cannot say whether it will rain next Tuesday may still say a great deal about how much rain a season brings. Which claim does our argument need, and which one does the analogy actually deliver?
- Our argument needs the conclusion that the downstream consequences are not merely unknown to me but unknowable in principle. Does the analogy support that stronger reading or only the weaker one, and would the weaker one be enough to sink the consequentialist instruction to maximize expected good?
- There is a further distinction I only half understand: between my lacking information that more research could in principle supply, and the counterfactual of what would have happened had I chosen otherwise having no determinate answer at all. Which of these does the butterfly effect support, and does our argument need it?
- I have also been told that the wingbeat's energy is negligible and dissipates almost at once, so that the famous claim, if it holds at all, concerns divergence within an idealized model rather than a real transfer of energy across a continent. If that is right, what is left of the analogy, and does the argument survive the translation?
- Finally, the argument looks to me as though it cuts down everything in its path, itself included. If consequences wash out the difference between my donation and my concert ticket, do they not equally wash out the difference between the wingbeat and no wingbeat? And if the conclusion is that I cannot know which choice is better, why does that favour abandoning consequences rather than following rules or my gut — or does the argument leave me with no basis for any of the three?

Please write the response in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. The claim in play — about 150 words, stating the analogy and exactly what conclusion it is being used to license in our debate.
2. The correspondence table — a table of six to ten rows, one column naming the element of the butterfly picture and the next naming what it is supposed to stand for in the moral case, with a brief note where the mapping is strained.
3. Where the analogy holds — about 300 words.
4. Where it breaks — about 700 words, ordered with the breaks that matter for our conclusion first, and each one labelled as mattering for the argument or not mattering.
5. Does our argument survive? — about 300 words, giving your verdict on which part survives and what our side would have to give up to keep the rest.
6. A better analogy — about 200 words, if you think there is one that would carry the part of the argument that survives, and an explanation of what it would have to get right.
