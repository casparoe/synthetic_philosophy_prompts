I need a thought experiment built to order about Taurek's thing -- you can save one stranger or five strangers, not all six, and he says flip a coin, since each of them just loses her life and there's no "five" that suffers anything. Give me an ordinary emergency: six strangers, one rescuer, and the rescuer can get either the lone one or the other five, not both. Realistic please -- no trolleys, no fat man, no villain, nobody at fault -- and nothing that makes the five worth more to the world than the one (no third parties affected either way, no doctor who'd go on to cure something). The whole thing has to hang on the claims alone, so it's cheating to stipulate that the five are somehow a single claimant, or that the one's claim is weaker. Keep the badness of dying identical for all six.

Then run three procedures through it, because I suspect all three are defensible readings of "everybody's claim counts the same" and I want to know if I'm wrong:
1. save the five, because there are more of them;
2. Taurek's coin flip between the one and the five;
3. one ticket in the hat per person, so the lone person lives 1 time in 6 -- which is what you get if you take equal claims literally and don't let the group count as a single claimant.
For each: what you do, why, and what each of the six's odds of surviving work out to if you don't know which of them you'd be. I have a hunch about how those three sets of numbers line up and I'd like to see it spelled out.

Then the part I actually care about. People defend the coin flip by saying it gives everyone the same chance -- but I think that slogan fits the other two just as well, so it doesnt pick out the coin flip at all. Does the coin flip follow from equal claims, or does it smuggle in an extra premise about what the units are? And can a defender of saving the five argue that the coin flip is worse for every single person than saving the five is -- which would make it a strange thing to do to people in the name of fairness -- and is that move legit, or does it cheat by pretending the lone person isnt actually the lone person?

Last thing: make the lone person's death much worse for her than the others' deaths would be for them (she goes slowly, they don't). What do the three procedures say now, does the coin flip survive, and which single feature of the original case was doing all the work? Then tell me how a Taurekian rescues the coin flip once that feature is gone.
