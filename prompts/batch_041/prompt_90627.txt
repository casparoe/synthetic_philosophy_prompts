For the past two years I have been translating German hymn texts into English for a small congregation that insists on singing them to the original tunes, and I have run up against a pattern I cannot find a word for. Because an English line has to match the melody's syllable count and stress pattern, I can never render a line one-for-one; a phrase the German puts at the end of stanza two ends up at the beginning of stanza three, a subordinate clause gets promoted to the main clause because that is where the tune's high note falls, an image that in the original is a passing aside becomes, in English, the line the congregation holds longest. What surprises me is what happens afterwards. After a year of singing my versions, the congregation has absorbed a reading of each hymn — which line is the emotional climax, which image the author "really cared about," where the argument turns — that is a product of where the tune forced me to put things, not of where the German put them. A colleague who reads German fluently told me flatly that the author's whole point lands in the first stanza; for everyone who knows only my version, it lands in the third, because that is where the melody made me put it. Nobody experiences this as a mistake, since line by line the translation is faithful; the distortion lives entirely in the arrangement.

I keep reaching for terms and finding each of them slightly wrong. Compensation was my first thought, but compensation is about offsetting a loss in one place with a gain in another, whereas what I am describing is a wholesale relocation of emphasis that the reader then credits back to the original. Adaptation sounds too free — my text is not a loose rewrite. And the familiar complaint that translators ought to be invisible names a norm about reception, not this mechanical drift. Is there an established concept for the specific phenomenon in which a formal constraint inherited from outside the text — a pre-existing melody, a metre, an acrostic, a rhyme scheme — silently re-weights a translation so that the shape of the constraint is misread as the shape of the author's intention? If there is a term, I want to know what it covers and what it leaves out, and how it differs from compensation, from adaptation, and from ordinary translation shift. If there is not, I would rather have a properly bounded coinage than a slogan. In particular I want the edges marked: is this a special case of something broader, does it require that the constraint be imposed from outside rather than chosen by the translator, and is the reader's misattribution essential to the phenomenon, or does the drift count even when no monolingual reader ever notices it?
