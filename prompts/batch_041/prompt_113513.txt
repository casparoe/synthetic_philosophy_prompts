Two advisers are briefing the organizers of a citizens' assembly of five hundred randomly selected participants who will deliberate for a weekend and then vote yes or no on a single question. Both know the Condorcet jury theorem and accept its mathematics; they disagree about what it implies here and about what the theorem is really a theorem about. The first holds that the assembly's majority verdict is about as reliable as a verdict can be: hundreds of voters, each even slightly better than chance and voting independently, make a mistaken majority vanishingly unlikely. The second holds that the theorem is nearly vacuous in this setting, because the deliberation that raises individual competence is also what correlates individual error, and aggregation cancels only idiosyncratic error, not a bias shared by the whole group; and if the group's competence on this question is at or below chance, larger size makes a wrong majority more likely rather than less. Write their dialogue. Each should press the other's weakest assumption rather than the strongest, and each should make at least one genuine concession. The optimist should say what independence would have to mean for the theorem to bite, and whether deliberation can preserve it; the pessimist should say whether any realistic institution could meet the theorem's conditions and, if none can, what the theorem still explains. Neither should win by caricature or by reciting the theorem; the disagreement should turn on mechanism, not algebra.
