I'm in a graduate historiography seminar on comparative economic history, and a disagreement with a classmate has stalled out. We both accept the standard framing of the "Great Divergence" — that around the turn of the nineteenth century sustained industrial growth took off in Britain while the most plausible comparandum, the Yangzi delta, did not — and we both know the long list of candidate causes people have offered: coal, colonies, the price of labour and the wage premium, property rights and institutional form, the structure of the family and of markets, the timing of New World silver, the character of the imperial state. What we cannot agree on is whether the question everyone is answering is actually well posed, and I would like your credence in one specific proposition rather than a survey of the literature.

Take the proposition P: the contrastive question "why Britain rather than China?" — posed roughly as this literature poses it — has a determinate answer that a historical explanation can be assessed against, rather than being an artifact of an arbitrarily chosen contrast class. Let "well posed" mean that the answer satisfies three conditions: (i) contrast-class stability — the factors it cites do not flip or dissolve when the second term is swapped for an equally plausible comparandum (the Netherlands, Japan, or the Yangzi delta at an earlier date); (ii) differential presence — the factors cited are genuinely present on one side and absent on the other, rather than features common to both that merely get relabelled; and (iii) non-triviality — the answer is not a redescription of the explanandum. My own credence in P is about 0.35; my classmate's is about 0.75.

Give me your own number for P, but what I really want is the decomposition. Break P into the sub-questions it actually rests on — for instance, whether a single canonical contrast class can be defended at all; whether the candidate causes survive the symmetry test (does the mirror-image question, "why China rather than Britain?", have to be answered by the negation of the same factors?); whether the "China" side of the comparison is specified independently of the outcome being explained; and whether determinacy is even the right demand to make of a historical explanation, or whether well-posedness here should be cashed out statistically, or in terms of counterfactuals, rather than as a unique cause — assign each a rough probability, give one line of justification for each, and show how they combine into your figure. Then tell me which sub-question my classmate's 0.75 and my 0.35 most likely come apart on, and which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Finally, say how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the one step you are least sure of; if the honest answer is that P is a poor thing to assign a probability to at all, say so and tell me what the nearest well-posed proposition would be.
