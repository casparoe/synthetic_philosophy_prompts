I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am preparing a short talk on provenance and watermarking for machine-generated text. I would like to open it with a joke, and I would prefer a joke that lands for people who actually know the philosophical material over a generic one about robots or runaway artificial intelligence.

The material I have in mind is an argument by the philosopher Daniel Dennett, which I will paraphrase from memory and therefore may have somewhat wrong. Dennett observes that generative systems now produce speech, text, and images that pass for the work of a human being, and he calls the entities responsible for such output counterfeit people: not people, but engineered to be taken for people. His central analogy is to counterfeit currency. Counterfeit money does not do its damage chiefly by deceiving one merchant; it does its damage by making it impossible for anyone to tell good money from bad, so that trust in the medium itself collapses. Dennett's claim is that counterfeit people threaten human interaction the same way: once you cannot tell whether the voice on the phone or the message in your inbox was produced by a person, the presumption of sincerity that ordinary life depends on is no longer safe, and the loss is to the medium rather than to any single exchange. From this he draws a policy conclusion, namely that machine-generated output should be marked so that its provenance can be detected, and he is willing to say that producing unmarked counterfeit people should be treated as a serious wrong, on the model of counterfeiting currency.

Two further commitments of his seem relevant, though again I may be misremembering. First, he argued at length that the idea of a philosophical zombie, a being physically and behaviorally identical to a person but with no inner experience, is either incoherent or idle, which sits oddly beside a warning that we are now surrounded by things that imitate people without being them. Second, he holds that we should take the intentional stance toward a system, treating it as an agent with beliefs and desires, when doing so lets us predict it well, which is how we already treat one another.

What I want is a joke, not an essay. It should be short, and its humor should turn on the actual content of the position I have described, in the way a good joke about a technical subject turns on a real property of that subject: a reader who knows the argument should find it funny because of what it gets right about the argument, not merely because artificial intelligence is an amusing topic. It should not be a joke that would work just as well for any discussion of artificial intelligence whatsoever. The seam I find most promising, if it helps, is the contrast between his treatment of the philosophical zombie and his treatment of the counterfeit person, but you should use whatever feature of the view actually supports the joke. If my paraphrase contains a distortion serious enough to change what the joke should be, note it in a sentence and then write the joke against the corrected view. Please do not quote Dennett or cite any of his works; I want the joke itself, not a reference.
