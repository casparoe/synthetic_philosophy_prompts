I've been arguing with a friend about Mencius and Xunzi and I want to actually write up where I land, so write me an essay.

Here's whats bugging me. Everyone states the disagreement as Mencius saying our nature is good and Xunzi saying it's bad, and then somebody in the group chat says the whole fight is verbal, because they agree that anyone can become a sage through effort — so all that's left is two senses of the word nature: Mencius meaning the distinctive thing we're for, Xunzi meaning the raw material we start with before we've shaped it. (Some people also point out that the Chinese word we keep translating as nature isn't really about biology; it's closer to what you're born with.) I don't buy the verbal-dispute reading, but I need to say why not, and that means getting specific about where they actually cross.

So the essay should do three things. First, pin down the real point of contact. My suspicion is it's not about whether we can become good but about what does the work in that becoming, and about whether our starting motivational furniture contains anything that already cares about other people — some little shoot of compassion, like Mencius thinks — or only stuff that can be bent that way by ritual and habit, like Xunzi thinks. Second, test both views against the awkward cases for each: for Mencius, that people plainly do go bad without being forced to, and for Xunzi, that if nothing in us already loves the good, then become good becuase it pays has a hard time turning into the kind of person who stays good when it stops paying. Third, end with a verdict rather than a shrug. If your answer is something like Mencius is right about the raw material and Xunzi is right about the process, fine, defend that — but defend it. No both-sides sandwich. And don't lean on the famous examples as if they settle anything; I've seen the child at the well thing a hundred times.
