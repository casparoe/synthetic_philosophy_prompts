I teach a third-year seminar on social ontology and I am setting the final exam. The last third of the course is on Lewis's analysis of convention, and every year my students can recite the definition back to me — a regularity in behaviour, common knowledge of that regularity, a preference to conform on condition that others do, and the arbitrariness clause that some alternative regularity would have served equally well — without being able to do anything with it. For the essay question I want a crux that reciting the definition cannot answer, and I want it to turn on a distinction I have been pressing on the class.

The distinction is between a convention in Lewis's sense and a norm that carries genuine obligation. Both are regularities sustained by mutual expectation; both can be arbitrary, in that some other regularity would have done just as well; yet only the second makes violation wrong rather than merely awkward. Two household cases make the contrast vivid. In the first, the household has settled into keeping to the right-hand side of the shared corridor, and someone who drifts to the left is awkward, not blameworthy. In the second, the household has settled into the rule that whoever finishes the milk replaces it, and someone who does not is not merely awkward but in the wrong. I want the exam to force students to say precisely where, if anywhere, Lewis's apparatus can generate the second phenomenon out of the first, and whether the arbitrariness clause helps or obstructs that. I also want a second half on change, because the course has stressed that conventions are equilibria and that equilibria do not by themselves tell you how one displaces another: if the household shifts from 'the finisher replaces the milk' to 'whoever next shops replaces the milk', I want students to say what would have to happen for that to count as a change of convention rather than a breakdown followed by a fresh start, and whether Lewis's static analysis can describe the transition or only its two endpoints.

Please write the essay question itself, pitched so that a strong student could answer it in sixty to ninety minutes and so that a student who has merely memorized the definition cannot pass; then a model answer of roughly seven hundred to eight hundred words; then a marking scheme worth twenty marks, built around the specific moves a top answer must make. Name the must-hit points, allocate the marks across them, and for each point add a one-line note on the error that typically costs students marks.
