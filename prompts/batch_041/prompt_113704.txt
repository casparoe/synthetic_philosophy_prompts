I am preparing a graduate seminar on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I have grown tired of the standard formulations, which tend to stop at the first step of the regress and never ask what the alleged tie is supposed to be. I should therefore like you to play the part of a committed proponent of what I shall call the tie theory of propositional unity: the view that a proposition is unified not by an additional relation standing between its constituents, but by the relation itself, which in some non-relational sense ties its terms together. You are to defend this position against me in a viva-like exchange. Stay in character throughout; you may concede peripheral points and refine your formulation, but you must not abandon the core claim that unity is primitive and is not a further constituent of the proposition. Do not cite any literature or appeal to authorities; argue from the resources of the position itself. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, so that the exchange remains a genuine back-and-forth rather than a lecture. If I press you on a distinction you have not yet drawn, draw it rather than retreating into vagueness.

Here is my opening move, and I should like you to answer it directly. Take a relation that can hold in either direction, such as the relation of being to the left of. On your view, that relation itself does the tying. But what distinguishes its tying a to b from its tying b to a? If the difference is a further fact — say, that the relation is oriented one way rather than the other — then that further fact must itself be tied to the relation and its terms, and Bradley's regress resumes. If the difference is not further, then the relation must carry its direction intrinsically; but then either the direction is a constituent of the relation, in which case we need another relation to connect the direction to the relation, or the tie is doing something that no ordinary relation can do, and I do not yet understand what that is. I have been calling this the indiscernible tie problem. So: what is the tie, such that it can be present in both a's standing in R to b and b's standing in R to a, yet unify them differently? Please answer in British English, and make your first reply a defence in character rather than a request for clarification.
