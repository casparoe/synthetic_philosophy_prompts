I write about philosophy for a general-readership ideas magazine, and I've spent the better part of a month circling a thought experiment I keep being told is settled: the one Max Black published in Mind in 1952, in which we are asked to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, alike in diameter, colour, temperature, and every other intrinsic respect, sitting a fixed distance apart in the void. The moral usually drawn is that the principle of the identity of indiscernibles — nothing can be two unless it differs in how it is, not merely in which one it is — is refuted, since the spheres appear to be two while differing in no way at all. I've come round to thinking that the usual moral is wrong, and I'd like you to tell me what would count against my view rather than simply restating the case for the other side. What I want is a map of my exposure: which considerations would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which of them a defender of my line should concede are genuinely embarrassing rather than dismiss as question-begging.

My position, stated as carefully as I can manage it. The spheres are not indiscernible, because each stands to the other in a relation — being exactly that distance from — that it does not stand in to itself: the relation is irreflexible in the sense that nothing is a given distance from itself, and yet each sphere bears it to the other. That is a grade of discernibility weaker than differing in a property, but stronger than merely being two; I've seen the term weak discernibility used for it, and I'm persuaded it does real work. Crucially, I don't think this is the disreputable move of letting in properties like being identical with the sphere on the left, which would make the principle true by definition and therefore empty. The principle as I want it still has content: it forbids two objects whose complete profiles of properties and relations coincide and which stand in no irreflexive relation to one another. So the pressure points I can see, and want you to develop, are these. First, my own test case, which is the one that actually keeps me up: two Cartesian minds, non-spatial, causally sealed off from each other, with exactly similar streams of thought, so that the only candidate relation between them is bare distinctness — which is of course irreflexive. Either I count that relation, and the principle starts looking like a triviality of logic again, or I don't, and I've got a counterexample to my own rescue. I suspect the honest answer is that one of those two horns has to be taken, and I want you to say which and at what cost. Second, the quantum-mechanical case, where I'm out of my depth: I'm told that two electrons in the same spatial orbital but with opposite spins are still weakly discernible, whereas two bosons in the same state are not discernible even weakly, and that this is either a counterexample to the principle or evidence that such particles were never two objects to begin with. I've seen the matter asserted in both directions and I don't trust my grasp of it; tell me whether it's a genuine defeater or a distraction dressed up in formalism. Third, tell me whether my rescue commits me to the bundle theory, in which objects are nothing over and above their properties, or whether a philosopher who believes in bare particulars underneath the properties can help herself to it too — and if it does commit me, say what that costs. And fourth, whether the whole issue is entangled with the separate question of whether there could be two possible worlds differing only over which of two indiscernible individuals each contains, or whether that is a different fight that I can leave alone.

Don't give me citations, names, dates, or reading lists — I'm not asking you to attribute views to anyone, and I'd rather you reason it out from scratch than gesture at who said what. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, be explicit about which ones I would have to concede and which ones I can reasonably bat away, and finish with an honest verdict on whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether, if so, that is actually a problem for a principle of this kind or simply what happens to any metaphysical thesis worth having. On the final line, in the form \answer{...}, give me a short label for the single defeater you rank most decisive, followed by a vertical bar, followed by one word — standing, qualified, or sunk — for the condition you judge the position to be in.
