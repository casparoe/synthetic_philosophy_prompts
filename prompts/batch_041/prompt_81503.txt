I've been going down a rabbit hole on the Reflection principle — van Fraassen's, I'm pretty sure it's his, the Bayesian one rather than the physics one — and I've ended up converting to the strong reading of it, which is what I want you to attack. The principle as I understand it: my credence in A right now, given that my credence in A at some later time will be x, should be x. Which sounds almost trivial until you notice it's a constraint on what I can currently think about my own future self, not just a piece of bookkeeping. What I've talked myself into is that this is a genuine requirement of rationality, on all fours with probabilism, not a defeasible heuristic that careful thinkers happen to satisfy when they're well-rested. So give me the map of its exposure. What would count against it — which cases, arguments, formal results, empirical findings — sorted by how decisive they'd be, with an honest note on which ones a committed defender would concede actually count against them versus which they'd wave off as confusion? I already know the shape of the drunk case (I know that tonight I'll be drunk and sitting at 0.9 on A, and the principle seems to tell me to be at 0.9 now, which is nuts) and I know the standard defender move (my evidence already contains the fact that drunk-me is unreliable, so it isn't a counterexample, it's just a case where I should predict my own error). That move seems right as far as it goes, but I want to know how far it goes, because it smells like the kind of defense that can absorb literally anything. If every apparent counterexample gets redescribed as a case where the agent's total evidence was misdescribed, then the principle never risks anything, and I want you to say plainly whether that's what's happening, and whether that's fatal for a principle of this kind or whether diachronic coherence constraints are allowed to be that elastic in a way that a claim about what causes what isn't.

A few specific things I keep circling, which I'd like you to either kill or vindicate. First, I've never seen an accuracy-dominance or calibration-style argument that gets you to Reflection the way it gets you to probabilism — every reconstruction I've tried smuggles in a premise that my future self is at least as good a judge as I am, which is exactly what's at issue in the drunk case, so if the real justification is a diachronic Dutch book I want to know whether that argument survives the obvious objection that I'm not being coerced by a bet I can't take, and whether it gives you anything that isn't already in the probabilism story. Second, the disanalogy that bugs me: nobody believes in a preference-reflection principle. You don't defer to your future tastes; that's Ulysses, the heroin, the whole reason precommitment is a thing we care about. So what exactly is different about credence? If the answer is that credences are truth-directed and tastes aren't, tell me what that buys, and then tell me what happens to a future self who is truth-directed but demonstrably worse at it: drunk, drugged, sleep-deprived, emotionally compromised, or just running a systematically biased heuristic I can name in advance. Is that where the principle has to be restricted, and if so restricted how — to future selves I'd trust as experts, to future selves I'm certain are at least as reliable? And if it gets restricted, is the restricted version still doing any work, or has it quietly collapsed into the claim that I should believe what I have good reason to believe? Third, and this is the one that might be the real killer: tell me what Reflection forbids that its rivals forbid too. What does it rule out that a picture with only synchronic coherence plus conditionalization permits? If the answer is nothing, that's a collapse result and I want to hear it announced as one. And if you want to make the problem harder, say what happens when credences are imprecise — I suspect the whole thing falls apart there, but I'd like to know if some version survives.

No citations or reading list please (I'm offline and would only be guessing at them anyway), and I don't want the received wisdom rehashed — I want the exposure map: defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, what a friend of the principle concedes versus dismisses, what the principle actually predicts that its rivals don't, and your honest read on whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that should bother me. If any of this has a moral analogue that's actually load-bearing — deferring to your future values, trading with your future self — I'd be curious, but that's a bonus, not the main thing. I argued about this with a friend who does forecasting for a living and we both got nowhere, so don't worry about hurting my feelings; I'd rather find out the view is well insulated than keep defending something that's already dead.
