I am trying to get clear on what it would even take to solve the old-evidence problem in Bayesian confirmation theory, before I evaluate any particular proposed solution. Write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate treatment must satisfy, the case or consideration that motivates each, and a verdict on which requirements are genuine constraints and which are merely prejudices of a particular camp.

Set the scene as follows. A hypothesis H is introduced and turns out to entail, or to make very probable, a fact E that was already known and accepted well before H was formulated — the textbook instance being the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, observed long before the theory that accounted for it existed. Intuitively, E is strong evidence for H; the old fact is counted among H's successes. But if the agent already assigns E probability 1, conditionalizing on E changes nothing, and on the standard Bayesian account the known fact cannot raise credence in H at all. The problem is to say what, if anything, is wrong with that, and what a principled repair looks like.

At a minimum, I want requirements covering the following, each with its motivating case:

- The core datum: the account must allow known evidence to confirm in the cases where we are confident that confirmation genuinely occurred, and must do so without denying the probability calculus.

- Non-omniscience: if a logically omniscient agent would never be in this predicament — for her the entailment is already seen and P(E|H) = P(E) = 1 — the account must say whether the confirmation is a fact about an agent's limited reasoning or about an objective evidential relation, and must not silently presuppose the very ignorance of a logical implication that it is supposed to be tracking.

- The counterfactual-ignorance maneuver: many repairs say the confirmation should be measured by the credence the agent would have had in H had she not known E. State what this requires to be well-defined — whose counterfactual, at what time, against what background knowledge — and whether it survives the case of a scientist who has known E since childhood and never once lacked it.

- Trivial consequences: not every known fact entailed by H should confirm it, or H is confirmed by every tautology it entails. Say what filter does the work (surprise, independence, explanatory unification) and whether that filter can be specified in advance rather than read off after we already know which hypothesis deserves credit.

- Recalcitrant evidence: the account should say whether known evidence can also disconfirm, and whether confirmation and disconfirmation by old evidence are handled symmetrically.

- Novelty and the new-theory problem: how the account bears on the separate demand that a brand-new theory, with no track record, still receive enough prior probability for any evidence to move it. Flag whether the treatment of old evidence makes that harder.

- Framework: whether the solution stays within subjective Bayesianism, revises it — say, by admitting a non-probabilistic notion of explanation or a second, non-conditional credence — or abandons it, and what must be given up in each case.

Then flag any pair of these you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate otherwise popular solutions. Close by sketching, in a few lines each, how the main families of response — the denial that there is a genuine problem at all, the counterfactual-credence approach, and the appeal to explanatory power or unification — fare against your list. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the criteria, the cases behind them, and the tensions among them.
