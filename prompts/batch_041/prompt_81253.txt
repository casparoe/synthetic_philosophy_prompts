I am a high school student, and I would like a document I can take back to my biology class. Our textbook hands us a checklist for deciding whether something is alive — movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition — and then, a few pages later, calls viruses non-living while describing a viral life cycle in which they reproduce inside host cells. A classmate pointed out that the same checklist fits a campfire, which grows, consumes fuel, spreads, and reacts to the wind; our teacher answered that we should not take the checklist literally, which left the question exactly where it started.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two camps I keep running into whenever this comes up. The first camp holds that the mark of life is a self-maintaining metabolism: a system that keeps itself running, repairs itself, and stays far from equilibrium by drawing energy from its surroundings. The second holds that the mark of life is evolution: any system with heredity and variation on which selection acts, whatever it is made of and whether or not it metabolizes. I suspect these camps are partly answering different questions, so I want the constructive document a good-faith collaboration between them would produce: the claims both could sign; the points where they still genuinely differ, each written in wording that the side holding it would accept; the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than about the world, say which part and why.

Please make the document work through these cases rather than gesturing at them: a virus with several hundred genes, some of which build its own components instead of merely hijacking the host; a viroid, which is bare infectious RNA with no coat and no proteins of its own; a prion; a mitochondrion, both inside a cell and extracted from it; a transposon; a healthy but sterile mule; a bacterial spore that stays metabolically almost silent for thousands of years and then revives; and a sterile worker ant in a colony that reproduces. Add two cases I invented to try to force the camps apart, because I could not find anything quite like them: a sealed vessel of chemicals, powered from outside, that continuously rebuilds and repairs its own components and stays far from equilibrium for years but has no heredity and cannot reproduce, vary, or evolve; and a program on a computer that replicates with variation, undergoes selection, and adapts over thousands of generations but is purely digital. I also want the statement to say what both camps agree excludes a campfire and a growing crystal — and, if they cannot agree on one principle that does this, to say so plainly and explain why.

Close with a short section on whether the two definitions would ever lead to different decisions in practice, for instance in how people search for life elsewhere or in what they regulate in a laboratory; if they never differ there, tell me whether the disagreement is only verbal.

Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims, then a table of the remaining disagreements, then the cruxes with their cases and advance predictions. Please do not cite papers, books, or the names of researchers, and do not send me to a reading list; I have no way to check any of it, and what I need is the substance in your own words. Tell me how confident you are in each part, and single out the one claim you are least sure both camps would actually sign.
