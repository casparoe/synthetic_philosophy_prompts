My mother was diagnosed with dementia this spring, and the symptom I cannot stop turning over is not the forgetting but the fluctuation. On some afternoons she reports the people in the room with such specificity that the rest of us check the doorway; the next morning she denies it ever happened, and each time the report arrives with the same unshakeable sense of being a direct description of what is in front of her. I had read Dennett's Consciousness Explained (1991) some years ago, and the fact that the vision and the denial carry an identical phenomenology of immediacy has made an abstract dispute the most urgent thing in my house.

I would like two things, held together. First, a reading of Being John Malkovich (Jonze and Kaufman, 1999) through the debate between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model. The film supplies the theater almost literally: a half-floor between the seventh and eighth, a hidden door, a visitor who looks out through another man's eyes for fifteen minutes, a puppeteer who discovers he can pull the strings, and a plot in which bodies are vessels and the self is whatever moves between them. It also stages the regress that Dennett says dissolves the metaphor, when Malkovich enters his own portal and finds a world in which everyone is him and nothing else is said. Does the film know it has done this, or does it need a homunculus in order to remain watchable? I am especially interested in the ending, where the character who most wanted to be the controlling observer is left as a viewer who can no longer act.

Second, bring the reading back to my mother's case. My own way of drawing the distinction is this: the theater metaphor can be read thinly, as the claim that for every content there is a single moment at which it becomes a content for someone, even if no place is ever located; the drafts model denies the guarantee, not the place. If that is the right line, which model does the film dramatize, and which better predicts the pattern I am living with — the same scene reported twice, once as present and once as never having happened, with equal conviction both times?

I do not need citations or a survey of the literature. I need the reading itself, faithful to the film and to the positions, an honest account of where the film resists it, and what the film shows that the argument alone does not.
