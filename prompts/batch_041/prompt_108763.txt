My flatmate Mira and her friend Dan got into it at our kitchen table last week over a bylaw our city council is actually considering, and I've been chewing on it since. The proposal would ban commercial "grief services" — companies that, for a fee, write and send condolence messages on your behalf, or send a professional mourner to sit at the back of a funeral you don't want to attend. There's a startup in town doing exactly this, Kind Words, and they've been advertising on the sides of buses. Mira wants it banned. Dan thinks the council has lost its mind. I should mention that Mira is about as far from a churchy person as you can get — she's an actuary, she thinks most of what people call sacred is sentimentality, and she'd never argue that grief is holy or that the dead are watching. That's part of why I couldn't referee: her case didn't sound like the usual one.

What she said was this. A condolence note only means anything because it costs the sender something — time, awkwardness, the risk of saying the wrong thing, the fact that they had to stop and think about you. The words themselves are nothing: my best friend writing "sorry for your loss" and a stranger writing it are the same words and completely different acts. Once there's a market, the cheap version is available to everyone, and receivers can no longer tell which one they've got. So condolence erodes for everybody, including the many people who would never hire Kind Words — that's a third-party effect, not hurt feelings, and that's what makes it the law's business. Dan came back with three things. One: Hallmark. Pre-printed sympathy cards, singing telegrams, copy-pasted "so sorry" texts, wedding registries — markets have been sitting inside our expressions of regard forever, and if the signaling story were right, condolence should be dead already. It isn't. Two: if cost is what carries the meaning, then buying flowers instead of growing them, or ordering a cake instead of baking one, degrades regard just as much, which is absurd. Three: if deception is the real worry, pass a disclosure rule — "this note was written by Kind Words on behalf of Dan" — don't ban the service. A ban is Mira imposing her ideal of sincere mourning on people who are too busy or too awkward to manage it themselves.

Mira took the Hallmark point seriously and refined her view: a card is blank inside and the sender writes the part that matters, so the card is a frame, not the content; with flowers it's the choosing and the showing up that carries the weight, not the horticulture. Her claim, she said, was narrower — what can't be outsourced without changing its meaning is attention. And the sticker only shows that Dan agrees something goes wrong when receivers can't tell, he just thinks a label fixes it. But a labeled condolence note, she said, is worse than an unlabeled one: "written on behalf of Dan by Kind Words Ltd" turns a note about my father into an ad. Then she put the deeper thing plainly: some practices are worth more than the convenience of the people who'd like to buy their way out of them, and law is one of the ways a community declares what it refuses to put a price on. Dan said that last sentence is what scared him — "the community declares" is the exact move every majority has made before banning things that she and I both think are nobody's business, and she's given him no stopping point. And anyway, he said, look at what her argument actually is: if the injury is that receivers can't distinguish a costly signal from a purchased one, she's argued for a harm-based ban, not for the law enforcing morality as such — so either she's quietly switched sides or she's lost on her own terms.

They've asked me to settle it and I honestly can't. I want a move-by-move adjudication — who's right at each step, not who's nicer, plus what they're both missing. I'm especially stuck on whether the cost story really works as a third-party harm or whether the Hallmark case sinks it; whether a labeling rule is the proportionate fix or whether labeling makes things worse; and whether Dan's last point is a genuine dilemma for Mira or just a cheap trick. Then, if you had a vote on the bylaw, how would you vote — and does that verdict follow from the same reasoning you used on the individual moves, or did you have to bring in something else to get there?
