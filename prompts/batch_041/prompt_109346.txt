Two colleagues — one an orthodox backward-induction theorist, the other a critic who holds that the centipede argument collapses once its assumptions are made fully explicit — have argued for a year without producing anything. Write the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have yielded.

For reference, the game: two players alternate; at each of finitely many nodes the player to move may Take, ending the game with a payoff slightly larger than what that player would receive if they passed and the other then took, or Pass, which enlarges the pot and hands the choice over; the last player to move must Take.

Set out the claims both can sign — about the game's structure, about what a solution concept is for, and about what the observed tendency of subjects to pass does and does not show — then the residual disagreements, each rendered in wording its own side would accept as its own position, neither so hedged that either could sign it by quietly reinterpreting itself. Identify the cruxes; I suspect the deepest is whether "rational" functions here as a normative standard for choices or as a prediction of play, and that another is whether the argument is entitled to assign determinate truth-values to counterfactuals about what the other player would do at nodes the argument itself proves unreached. For each crux, supply the case, argument, or formal result both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

Finish with a single table, one row per item, with these columns: Item type (agreed claim / disagreement / crux / verbal dispute); Statement; Side A's position in its own wording; Side B's position in its own wording; Evidence or argument both accept as relevant; Side A's prediction; Side B's prediction. Leave a cell blank where the column does not apply.
