I'm the lay member on the ethics committee of a fairly big fertility clinic — IVF, pre-implantation genetic testing, donor gametes, the lot — and roughly once a month a consult lands that boils down to the same thing: a couple wants to go ahead, and if they do, the child will quite probably have a life that's hard. Not necessarily a life that's "not worth living" in the medical sense, but one with a lot of suffering in it: a severe condition, invasive treatment from infancy, sometimes a shortened life. Around the table we're mostly consequentialists of some stripe, and the phrase everyone reaches for to break the deadlock is the procreation asymmetry — that it's bad to bring into existence someone whose life goes badly, but it isn't thereby good, or required, to bring into existence someone whose life goes well, and the good of a happy life doesn't cancel out or outweigh the bad of a miserable one; it just doesn't figure in the same way. In the abstract we all nod. In the room we can't do anything with it, and we end up voting on vibes. Our chair's pet idea, which nobody can make precise, is that the asymmetry is essentially a no-cancellation rule: keep the suffering ledger and the flourishing ledger seperate and never net one off against the other. I think there's something in that but I can't turn it into anything we can use.

So what I'd like from you is a procedure we can actually run, in the twenty minutes or so we get per case, by people who are clinicians and administrators and one philosopher-for-an-afternoon (me) rather than professional ethicists. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point where the procedure is entitled to stop with a no, and the points where it has to hand the decision back to a person because the question has gone over into values, or into facts we simply can't get. Then run it on three cases: (i) a couple whose prospective child would likely have a condition causing severe pain but no cognitive impairment, and would likely live a normal length of life; (ii) a case where the child would likely be profoundly impaired and die in early childhood; and (iii) one where the child would be perfectly healthy and the parents are just anxious — where I suspect the procedure should have almost nothing to say.

Be honest about where it will misfire, and about where it's smuggling in a substantive ethical view — totalism, or a threshold for a life worth living, or the non-identity problem — under the guise of mere procedure. I'd also like to know which parts of the asymmetry can't survive the translation into a checklist without being distorted, and whether the no-cancellation reading our chair keeps pushing is actually a fair gloss on it or a different position wearing its clothes. The committee's recommendation goes to the clinical lead, who signs off, and there's a lawyer in the room, so flag anything that would make her nervous.
