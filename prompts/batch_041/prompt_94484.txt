I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on conditionals and probability — twelve students, mostly MA philosophers with one logic course and some Bayesian epistemology behind them — and I want a session in which they do something rather than hear about it. The target is the equation everyone reaches for when asked how likely an indicative conditional is: that its probability is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, the suppositional rule that textbooks call the Ramsey test. I want them to arrive at that rule themselves, by trading, and then to feel the floor move: the very tickets they have been pricing have to be settled in a way that makes their price equal the plain probability of the consequent, so that on their own posted numbers every antecedent comes out independent of every consequent, which is absurd on its face. I know that this manoeuvre is the simple shape of the triviality results and that the equation's collapse is what those results are about; I do not need you to re-prove anything, I need the activity. So: a market — desks, chips, a leaderboard, no real money — in which the prices are forced out of them by the payoff structure rather than announced by me, plus a second stage in which the settlement or buy-back rule forces the second valuation. One scenario I have half-worked out is a rare disease with a sensitive test, where the conditional from having the disease to testing positive is worth nearly everything to them and the branch-by-branch settlement prices it near the base rate of positive tests; invent your own set rather than reusing that one, but it needs at least one case where the gap between the two prices is wide enough to hurt. Give me rules, materials, roles, a timeline for the ninety minutes, the payout scheme, and the resolution procedure they must agree to before play — the resolution procedure is the part I care most about, since the whole session lives or dies on whether the second valuation feels like something the rules did to them rather than something I told them. No lecture up front, and no naming of any view until the debrief.

Then the debrief, which is where the session has to earn its keep. Map the moments of play onto the pieces of the argument — where they used the conditional probability, where the branch settlement bit, and why the two together force the conclusion — and then lay out the ways out: conditionals without truth values, the material reading with the divergence blamed on pragmatics, a restriction of the equation to some class of cases, a non-classical valuation, or something else you prefer, with a definite verdict from you on which of these the activity actually supports and which it merely fails to rule out. I also want an honest account of the ceiling. This is not a refutation of anything: tell me precisely what the activity cannot reproduce — the generality of the result over a class of credence functions closed under conditionalisation, the fact that the students never held the equation as a universal thesis to begin with, the semantic question the market never touches, whatever else you think is missing — and how I should flag that to the group so they do not leave thinking they have seen a proof.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: which student objections will turn out to be correct, which will be the material reading in disguise, which piece of the mechanics will eat the clock, and what to do when a desk refuses to price the ticket at all because it insists the conditional has no truth value — which, I take it, is the right instinct arriving at the wrong moment. A colleague tells me this is material for a lecture; I would rather they trip over it. Take definite positions throughout: one payout scheme, one settlement rule, one diagnosis in the debrief, one verdict on what the activity shows and what it leaves open, rather than a menu of alternatives for me to choose from. And if you think my two-stage structure is the wrong shape for this, say so and design the version you would run instead.
