I am not a philosopher or a neuroscientist. I am a retired postal worker, and I have been thinking about this on my own, without any academic training in the relevant debates. Next Saturday I am giving the eulogy at the memorial service for my older brother, Daniel. Daniel had a progressive neurological disease. Over about eighteen months, he underwent a gradual replacement of his brain tissue with a prosthetic system that copied the connections of each neuron before the biological one failed. He was awake for the whole process. For most of it, he was still himself in every way that mattered to us. His last biological neuron was replaced on a Tuesday in March. Since then, the prosthetic system has continued to talk in his voice, remember his memories, and ask about his children. His wife, who is a nurse, believes Daniel died on that Tuesday. His two teenage children are not sure. His lab colleagues, who worked with him on the prosthetic, believe he is still with us and that the Tuesday story is a kind of superstition. The memorial will be in a Unitarian church hall. About half the room will be family and friends who share his wife's view, and about half will be colleagues and friends who share the lab's view. The prosthetic system has said it will not attend, but it has sent a letter to be read by someone else. I do not want to give a eulogy that pretends to settle the question of when Daniel died. I also do not want to give a eulogy that is so vague it says nothing. I want to speak about the man I grew up with, the man who taught me to fish, the man who could be impossible and generous in the same hour, in a way that does not require the room to agree on whether he is dead or alive. Please write the eulogy. It should be about five minutes long. It should be formal enough for a memorial but not stiff. It should not use academic terms like personal identity or continuity of consciousness. It should not tell the audience what to believe about the prosthesis. It should find a way to be honest and comforting at the same time, and it should let both halves of the room hear their own grief acknowledged. The philosophy here is not decoration: the fact that the replacement was gradual, neuron by neuron, is what makes the question of when he died so hard, and the eulogy has to be built around that difficulty rather than avoiding it.
