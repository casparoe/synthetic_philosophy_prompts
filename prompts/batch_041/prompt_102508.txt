I am circulating a short statement of my commitments about chance and credence before a workshop session, and I would like an audit of it before it goes out. This is where I have landed.

(1) Chance facts are objective features of the world, not facts about anyone's information. In a deterministic world the only chances are 0 and 1; in an indeterministic world there are non-trivial chances.

(2) Chances are to be analysed in the Humean, best-system way: they are the numbers that appear in the probabilistic laws of the system which best balances simplicity, strength and fit with the complete history of occurrent fact. Chance facts therefore supervene on the whole mosaic, past and future alike.

(3) An agent who does not know the laws of nature or the complete state of the world can rationally be uncertain about the future even if determinism is true; I would not tell such an agent to be certain of everything that happens.

(4) I accept the Principal Principle in something like its classical form: where the chance at t of A is x, a rational agent's credence in A, conditional on that fact together with any admissible evidence, is x.

(5) Admissibility should be characterised by content rather than by provenance. The only evidence I would exclude is evidence that entails whether A occurs; a proposition should not be ruled out merely because it concerns the future, and I would want a principled reason before accepting any exclusion of that kind.

(6) Reliable advance knowledge of the outcome of a genuinely indeterministic event seems to me possible — an instrument or an informant might tell me which way a chancy measurement will go — and in that position I would be certain of the outcome.

(7) Conditionalization is the only rule of updating I accept; whatever deference to chance comes to, it should fall out of conditionalizing on facts about chance rather than being a further, independent requirement. I also take it as a constraint on any account of chance that it explain why chance constrains credence at all: an account on which the Principal Principle held by accident would be a failure.

I do not know which of these is doing the work and which is freeloading, and I would rather not simply be told to abandon whichever one I happen to like least. Please tell me which of the commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ordered by severity; for each genuine tension, which commitment has to give and what the cheapest repair would cost; and which single revision would clean up the most. I want the steps of the arguments rather than a list of general worries, and I do not need references.
