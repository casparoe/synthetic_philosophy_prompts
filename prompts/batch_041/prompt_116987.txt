The boundary problem in democratic theory is usually presented as a trilemma: a decision is legitimate only if it's made by the demos, but the demos has to be constituted by some decision, and that constituting decision can't itself be authorized by the demos it creates. I've read enough survey treatments of this to know the standard menu, so what I want is an essay that actually takes a position. Set out the strongest version of the problem in your own words — in particular, say whether you think it's a genuine incoherence in democratic theory or just a local instance of the general fact that no decision rule can apply itself (the reference-class problem in anthropics, where the class of observers you're supposed to conditionalize on seems chosen rather than discovered, may or may not be a useful analogy; use it only if it earns its keep). Then argue for a criterion rather than cataloguing one: (a) everyone subject to the decision's coercion, (b) everyone causally affected by it, (c) everyone residing in the territory, or (d) something else you're prepared to defend.

To keep this from turning into a recital, I want you to work through a specific proposal I've been turning over, which I'll call the residual-cost rule. It says: a person belongs in the demos for a given decision just in case (i) if the decision goes against them, they bear a loss they cannot recover through exit, contract, or later political reversal, and (ii) the loss is not one they could reasonably have been expected to insure against. The motivating thought is that we don't need to settle who counts as affected in the abstract; we only need to locate the people who are stuck with an outcome and can't price it. Argue for the rule, against it, or for a repair of it. Two cases seem to be the hard ones, and I want them treated at length: climate policy, where nearly everyone alive and everyone not yet born is residually exposed, so that the rule threatens to make the demos boundless; and the enfranchisement of long-term resident non-citizens, where the rule may say include while any actual electorate will predictably vote to exclude. If you end up defending the rule, say which of the standard alternatives it beats and on what grounds, and what it costs you elsewhere. If you reject it, say what it gets right that your preferred criterion also has to capture.

Please reason in your own voice and don't furnish citations, a reading list, or a survey of who has said what — I care about the argument, not the bibliography. Close by stating how confident you are in your verdict, with a rough number, and identify the single step in the argument you're least sure of.
