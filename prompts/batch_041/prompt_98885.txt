I spent thirty years as a field geologist and have lately been reading philosophy of probability, which has been humbling. I now chair the planning group for a volunteer search-and-rescue unit, and our briefings keep arriving in a form I have no procedure for. A team will report something like: "We can't tell you which sector he's in, but if he's in the north sector, he's almost certainly staying near water." The report rules nothing out — he may well be south — and yet it plainly ought to change how we allocate searchers. When I try to handle it with the only tool I trust, conditioning on what the report excludes, there is nothing to condition on; when I do it by hand I find I am quietly assuming something about whether the report should move my probability that he is in the north at all. I have since learned that this shape of problem has a name in the literature, and that the respectable answers disagree: some hold that such a report should leave the probability of its antecedent exactly where it was, others that the same evidence forces it upward, and the dispute is not arithmetic but about which rule for revising a distribution is the right one.

Please write me a procedure our planning group can run on a briefing of this kind: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and declares the report unusable as stated, and where it must be handed back to a person because the choice is a judgment about what we are entitled to assume rather than something the evidence settles. It has to be usable in about fifteen minutes by people who are not probabilists; it must name the updating rule it uses and be candid about how much of the answer that choice, rather than the evidence, is doing; and it should include a small worked table over four sectors with a flat prior so we can see what the competing rules do to the same report. Our unit leader signs off on the final allocation, so mark clearly which outputs are recommendations and which are decisions he has to own.

Then run the procedure on three briefings: the near-water conditional above; a report that says only that the subject is probably in the north, with no conditional attached; and a conditional report whose antecedent is something we were already nearly certain of, so that the procedure returns an answer I will not like, because it tells me the report barely matters. End by telling me what the procedure cannot do — which briefings will remain judgment calls no matter how carefully we run the checklist, and whether a group like ours has any way to discover that it has chosen the wrong rule rather than merely an unpopular one.
