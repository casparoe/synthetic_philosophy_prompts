I chair the acquisitions and ethics committee of a small university art museum. Our collections policy requires that any proposed acquisition whose production involved significant harm to an identifiable person be reviewed by this committee before the director acts. The work before us is a video installation made in the late 1990s, and most critics treat it as one of the defining pieces of that decade. Its power does not seem incidental to the wrong: the artist recruited a woman, then a student, to be filmed over several weeks in a series of increasingly degrading situations, and the finished piece works, on most accounts, precisely because the degradation is real and was consented to only in the thinnest sense. The subject is now in her sixties; she has said publicly that she does not regard herself as a victim and would be glad to see the work preserved. The artist has never expressed regret, and would receive both the purchase price and a continuing share of exhibition fees. Half the committee holds that our task is to preserve important art and that the subject's own attitude settles the moral question; the other half holds that we would be laundering a wrong and rewarding its author. Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before the vote in a fortnight: a concrete recommendation, with the reasoning behind it, and a fair statement of the strongest case against the recommendation you give. I especially want your own judgement on where the wrong, if there is one, actually lies — in the making, in the keeping, or in the showing — and on whether the work's value is separable from what makes it troubling or depends on it, since that is what will decide the conditions, if any, we attach. Write in British English.
