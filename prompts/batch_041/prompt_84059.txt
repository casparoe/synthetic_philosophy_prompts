I am a retired field ecologist. For thirty-five years I studied population cycles in northern forests; since retirement I have been reading moral philosophy with more energy than method, and I have become interested in Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility. I now sit on the Research Integrity Committee of the institute where I spent my career, and a case has arrived that turns the seminar question into an institutional one.

A postdoctoral researcher in the department falsified several microscopy images in a paper published in a reputable journal. The facts are not in dispute; the researcher has admitted them in writing. But the researcher's statement also argues that the committee's blame presupposes a kind of responsibility that no one can have. The argument, as the researcher presents it, runs roughly: to be morally responsible for an action, one must be responsible for the character and motives from which it issued; but one did not choose one's genes, upbringing, training, or the pressures of one's situation; to be responsible for those, one would need to be responsible for the causes that formed them, and so on without end; therefore no one is ultimately responsible for anything. The researcher concludes that the committee should correct the record and take whatever forward-looking steps protect science, but should not condemn him, because condemnation attributes a desert that no one can earn.

The committee is split. Some members regard this as a clever way of avoiding responsibility and want to apply the misconduct policy as written. Others think that if the argument is sound, the committee should at least change the language of its finding. I have been asked to draft the committee's written opinion for next week's meeting. My own instinct, as a scientist, is that causes run all the way down, which makes me sympathetic to the researcher; but I also know that a research community cannot function if every finding of misconduct is met with a regress argument. I need a memo that faces that tension rather than dissolving it.

Please draft that opinion. It should do the following:

- Recommend a concrete disposition: what finding to enter, what sanction or conditions to impose, what the researcher must do, and by when.
- Engage the researcher's argument at its strongest. Do not treat it as a debating trick, and do not simply assert that we must hold people responsible because society requires it. Explain where the argument has force and where it may overreach.
- Distinguish, if you think the distinction can be made, between being ultimately responsible for one's character and being accountable to an institution for one's conduct. Consider whether the committee's action is retributive, deterrent, rehabilitative, protective, or a mixture, and whether any of those purposes can survive the argument.
- Consider a proposal from two committee members to create a third finding category—something like serious error without blame—between no finding and research misconduct. Say whether the committee should adopt it, and what difference it would make in this case.
- Address the possibility that the Basic Argument is sound. If it is, would a blame-free finding be more honest? Would it be unfair to the researcher to omit blame, or unfair to the scientific community to include it? What would the committee owe to the paper's readers, the researcher's coauthors, and future whistleblowers?
- End with a clear bottom line the committee can vote on, and a short paragraph acknowledging the strongest consideration on the other side.

The document should be formal, around 900–1,100 words, written in the voice of the committee (I will sign it as chair of the subcommittee). No citations or literature review are needed; the committee needs reasons it can act on, not a survey. I am not asking you to settle the free will debate. I am asking for a defensible institutional position that does not pretend the metaphysical question is settled.
