During a seminar discussion of the French Revolution, a classmate ended an argument about why the Revolution turned on its own leaders with the old saying that revolutions devour their children, as if that settled the matter. I want to know what that sentence could actually be claiming, laid out as a menu of precise readings rather than a single interpretation.

Begin by separating the moving parts. What is the devouring supposed to be: the literal killing of the Revolution's own leading figures, the discrediting and marginalizing of one faction by another, the absorption of an earlier radicalism into a later and more moderate settlement, or the destruction of the institutions and ideals the Revolution itself brought into being? And who or what are the children: the individual leaders it elevates, the political generation it creates, the new institutions and factions it spawns, or the successor movements that inherit its name? Distinguish also the scope and the modality of the claim — whether it is offered as a lawlike generalization about revolutions as a class, as a claim about this revolution in particular, or only about one phase such as the Terror, and whether the devouring is presented as inevitable or merely frequent.

For each resulting reading, say whether it is a platitude almost no one would deny, a substantive claim that could be defended by evidence or by a mechanism, or something false or confused; and specify what would have to be true — what mechanism, selection effect, or institutional dynamic — for that reading to hold. Consider at least these candidate mechanisms: a psychological claim about the kind of temperament that makes a revolutionary; a structural claim about the power vacuum and the absence of legitimate authority that follows a collapsed regime; a selection or radicalization effect in which the center of gravity shifts so that yesterday's radical becomes today's moderate; a claim about the internal logic of an ideology that demands purity; and a near-tautological claim that any coalition which overthrows a regime will eventually quarrel.

Then tell me which of these readings my classmate most plausibly had in mind, and, more importantly, which reading would actually do the work his remark was doing — namely, making the Revolution's internal destruction look like something other than a contingent string of personal and political accidents, so that it bears on the broader dispute over whether the Revolution is best explained by class struggle, by ideology, or by contingency. If no single reading accomplishes that, say which combination would, and identify the reading that a defender of contingency would most need to resist. Write the whole answer in American English.
