ok so I run a political theory reading group and we've done Burke, and next month we're doing the Chesterton's fence thing — the rule about not tearing a fence down until you know why somebody put it up — and I want to close the term with a film night and a discussion, and I've picked Fiddler on the Roof. Everyone in the group has seen it, which is the point and also the problem, because the lazy reading is just "it's about tradition getting steamrolled by history, Tevye is a Burkean, pass the popcorn." I don't think that's right and I want a real reading that takes the film and the ideas equally seriously.

Heres the thing I keep tripping over. Chesterton's fence as it's usually stated is an *epistemic* rule: don't remove it till you know what it was for. Burke is doing something different — he's making a normative claim, that inherited arrangements have a claim on us simply because they're inherited, whether or not anyone alive could now give a reason for them. Those two come apart, and I think Fiddler lives in the gap. So: is Tevye a Chestertonian, a Burkean, or neither? He bends for Tzeitel and Motel (no matchmaker, no Lazar Wolf), bends again for Hodel and Perchik, and then breaks over Chava. Is that inconsistency principled or just where his nerve ran out? And does he actually know why the fences were there — the matchmaker, the rabbi's ruling, the Sabbath — or is he defending something he can't give an account of? The film is pretty good about showing what the fences are *for* (a small people staying a people) and also how much they cost, especially the women.

Two more things I want in the reading. First, the fences here are the community's own, not the state's — the constable and the pogrom are what comes in from outside to smash them, so it's not the usual reformer-vs-tradition setup. Second, the ending: everyone scattered, the place gone, the tradition carried off in suitcases. Does the exile vindicate the fence or show it failed? Is a fence you carry with you still a fence? Burke has that image of society as a partnership between the dead, the living, and the not-yet-born, and I want to know if the film is dramatizing that or quietly refuting it.

So give me a reading I can open the discussion with: which scenes you'd actually put in front of the group, where Burke and Chesterton's fence each illuminate something the other misses, and where the film flatly refuses the conservative reading and the honest critic has to admit it. I don't need citations or a reading list, and please dont just tell me it's about tradition — that's the thing I'm trying to get past.
