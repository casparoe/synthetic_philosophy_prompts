I'm running a 90-minute session at a science writers' conference this fall — about 20 people, mostly reporters and podcast folks, nobody signed up to be lectured at — and the thing I want them to walk out having felt in their gut is the unilateralist's curse. Not understood, felt: the situation where a bunch of actors each independently check whether the thing they could do is a good idea, most of them get it right, and the thing gets done anyway in exactly the cases where it shouldn't be — not because anyone's reckless or greedy or defecting, but because that's just what happens when you have a lot of independent, individually reasonable decision-makers.

Here's my rough sketch, gut it if it doesn't work. Around 18 players, each one runs a team that can unilaterally "release" something. Each round there's a hidden state of the world: the thing is genuinely worth doing about 1 time in 6, and the rest of the time it's a dud or worse. Every player gets a private reading of the state that's right 9 times out of 10. So a green light only means the thing is good about two times in three — that gap between how confident the reading feels and how confident it should actually make you is half the point. In a bad round each player still has a 1-in-10 shot at a green light, so with 18 of them the chance that at least one releases is 1 - 0.9^18, about 85%, nobody can see anybody else's reading, and everyone decides at the same instant. The world only needs the thing done once — one good release and everyone's fine — but every bad release does damage, and the damage is per release. Everyone shares one score, so I definately don't want it to be a freeloading or selfishness story: I want the trap to be that each of them, honestly reading their own card, is making a defensible call.

What I want the sequence to do: first rounds with no talking, so they walk into it; then rounds where they can talk before deciding but can't show each other their readings; then rounds where they get to write themselves a rule; then a phase where some outside labs (sealed envelopes I've filled in advance) start releasing too, so whatever rule they wrote can't bind everyone. Trap, talk your way out, rule your way out, discover the rule only covers the people who agreed to it.

What I need from you: the runnable version. Rules, materials and prep I can do the night before with no technical skills, the payoff sheet with the arithmetic spelled out, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90, a way to pick the hidden state each round without leaking it, the debrief that maps what happened onto the idea, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each of those, and an honest paragraph on what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to just say out loud. And I want them to leave with the arithmetic stuck in their heads — that the group's odds of avoiding a bad release is each of them being right raised to the power of how many of them there are, so it's the number of actors that kills you, not their quality.

Two things I'm worried about. One: some clever person will notice that if everyone else is going to release anyway, holding back costs them nothing — I want that to be a moment in the debrief, not a way for the room to squirm out of the first round, so tell me how you'd handle it. Two: don't let me pretend the game is realistic about how easy it is to coordinate; be straight with me about that in the wrap-up.

Don't let them hear the name of the thing until the debrief, and don't have me explain anything before we start.
