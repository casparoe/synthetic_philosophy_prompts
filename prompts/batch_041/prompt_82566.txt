I'm in a small working group on AI governance and policy, and one of the more technically minded members keeps returning to an argument I can't decide whether to accept. Her position is that an AI system is a tool in the same sense that a hammer is a tool: it has no ends of its own, it does nothing unless a user sets it going, and whatever it produces is attributable to the user rather than to the artifact. From that she draws a design prescription — build tools, not agents; question-answering systems, calculators, search-and-summarise pipelines and the like are fine, while anything that acts over time, pursues goals, or would have to be argued out of a course of action is a category mistake as well as a hazard — and a deflationary conclusion about safety: alignment, on her view, is a problem for agents; for tools it collapses into ordinary quality control of the sort we already have for bridges and pacemakers. The whole apparatus of AI-risk discourse, she says, is anthropomorphic projection onto something that is, in the end, a lever. I've noticed that the same analogy does different work elsewhere — "it's just a tool" is also the line companies use when they want to disclaim responsibility for what their systems do — and I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it admits.

What I want from you is not a verdict on her politics but an audit of the analogy. Start by brainstorming what the analogy is even claiming, since "tool" is doing at least three jobs here (a metaphysical job about ends and agency, an engineering job about how a system's behaviour is determined, and an ethical/legal job about who is answerable for outcomes), and tell me which of them the hammer comparison can actually reach. Then lay out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if you like: what plays the role of the user's ends, the carpenter's skill, the hammer's head and handle, the material worked on, and the finished product — and identify what the analogy quietly imports from the hammer case, such as a narrow and fully specified behavioural repertoire, a one-to-one pairing of tool with user, an absence of any internal process that selects between courses of action, and a clean temporal boundary between the user's decision and the artifact's effect. Where does the mapping break for a system that is general-purpose, that is produced by an optimisation process rather than by a designer's blueprint, that serves many users with incompatible ends at once, and that supplies framing and omissions the user never asked for? Separate the disanalogies that matter for her two conclusions from the ones that are merely picturesque, say plainly whether either conclusion survives once the load-bearing ones are on the table, and if the analogy is worth keeping in some repaired form, say what the repaired version looks like and what it would and wouldn't license. I'd especially like you to be honest about the possibility that "tool" is not a natural kind at all but a status one can claim or withhold, in which case the argument may be doing rhetorical work in the guise of conceptual analysis.

Please approach this as a philosopher of technology with a good grasp of the tool-versus-agent distinction in AI safety and of the philosophy of artifacts, reason carefully and in stages, and don't smooth over the places where you have to choose between a tidy answer and a defensible one.
