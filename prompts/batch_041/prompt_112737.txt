A friend of mine has a take I half agree with, and an argument for it that I'm pretty sure proves to much. I'd like to know which step is doing the damage. Here it is:

(1) A change in how people live is a mistake if the people living after it are worse off, on average, than the people living before it.
(2) Take the shift from foraging to farming. On just about every measure we have -- height, teeth, anemia and infection in the bones, hours of grinding labor, disease from crowding and from keeping animals -- the average early farmer was worse off than the average forager.
(3) So the agricultural revolution was a mistake.
(4) Nothing in (1)-(3) is special to farming, though. Any change that lets more people live while pushing the average down gets the same verdict: famine relief, curing a disease that mostly kills the poor, the industrial revolution, the demographic transition. All mistakes.
(5) That's obviously wrong.

I want to keep (3) -- I really do think farming was a raw deal for the people who lived through it -- and I want to keep (5). So (1) probably has to go, but every replacement I try costs me something and I can't tell what. Which step should I give up, and why did it look fine in the first place? Then price the alternatives for me: if I swap (1) for something that only counts harms to people who'd exist either way, what happens to (3)? If I keep an impersonal version instead, what do I have to swallow about adding lives that are worth living? And is (4) actually a fair application of (1), or is there a difference between those cases that I'm papering over?
