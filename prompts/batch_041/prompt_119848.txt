My two roommates got into it last night over one of those puzzles where you get six boxes on the left and six on the right and you have to say what all the left ones have in common that none of the right ones do (I think they're called Bongard problems, after whoever came up with them). Iris had made one up herself, so it was all her own drawings, no book involved.

Left: a triangle with a dot at each corner and the lines all joined up; a square the same way; a pentagon the same way. All of them upright, each a bit taller than it is wide. Right: three dots in a row with a line between the first two and a line between the last two; four dots in a zigzag with three lines; five dots in a longer zigzag with four lines. All lying down, each wider than tall.

Iris says the answer is closed vs. open: on the left the dots are joined into a loop, on the right they're a chain with two loose ends. Sam says no, the answer is the one staring at you — left boxes are taller than wide, right boxes are wider than tall. That fits all six pairs too, and it's one clause instead of a story about loops.

Iris: any rule that fits is a rule; you can fit twelve boxes with a million rules, including "closed unless the box was drawn on a Tuesday." If fitting were the standard the puzzle would have no answer. The closed/open thing is why those particular figures ended up on those sides, and the aspect ratio is an accident of how you drew them.

Sam: you don't know that — you drew them, so you know. Take away your intentions and all I've got is the boxes. And "closed vs. open" isn't even defined for everything: is a spiral closed? a figure eight? Taller-than-wide always has an answer. Your rule isn't simpler or better defined, it just sounds deeper.

Iris: fine, test it. Wide closed figure, which side? Sam: right, under mine. Left under yours. Iris: and a tall open chain? Sam: left under mine, right under yours. And notice neither of us thinks those are hard calls — you think the puzzle-maker would draw them your way, which is a fact about her, not about the boxes. So "correct" just means "matches the intent," and intent is whatever's in somebody's head. In a textbook the boxes come with an author; here they came with you. I'll take the cheap rule and you can't stop me.

Iris: then why is the cheap rule cheap? Because it latches onto a property that has nothing to do with why the shapes look the way they do. Somebody solving with your rule would be useless on the next puzzle. The right concept is the one that keeps working.

Sam: keeps working for whom? You need a prior about what kind of generator makes these things, and simplicity is a prior too, so we're both guessing — yours just feels more like a concept because it's the kind of thing people happen to notice.

Iris: the prior isn't arbitrary, it's the one that's held up on every puzzle anyone's ever solved, and in perception generally. Sam: that's circular, since "held up" gets scored by us. They went to bed annoyed and I never got a straight answer.

Can you go through their moves one by one and say who's right at each step — not who wins overall, but what each of them has hold of and where each one overreaches? And at the end, tell me whether there's even a fact of the matter about which of the two rules is the answer, or whether they're just talking past each other.
