I'm grading for a mid-level ethics course (the unit on aggregation and the trolley literature), and I want a second opinion on one exam answer before I return the scripts. The question was worth twenty points and read as follows: "Taurek's 'Should the Numbers Count?' argues that when you can save either one person or five, you are not morally required to save the five. Reconstruct his argument for that claim, and then assess what you take to be the most serious objection to it." My rubric gives roughly half the credit for a faithful reconstruction (the target of the argument, the reasoning, and the positive proposal) and half for the critical assessment (correctly identifying an objection, stating it in the objector's terms, and reaching a defensible verdict about whether it lands). A hasty pass would see a well-organized answer that names the right article, the right opponent, and the right conclusion and would mark it high; I am less sure.

The answer I'm unsure about runs as follows. "Taurek's target is the assumption that moral reasons can be added up across persons — that five lives saved is a greater good than one, and that this greater good gives a stronger reason. He sets up a case where you can save either one stranger or five strangers and cannot save all six. The utilitarian, and most non-utilitarians too, say you must save the five. Taurek denies it. Each of the six has a claim to be saved, and each claim is the claim of a distinct person to go on living. There is no further entity, 'the five', whose claim could outweigh the one's; to say the five's claims outweigh the one's is to treat the five as a single claimant, which we are not entitled to do. From the standpoint of each individual nothing distinguishes the one from any of the five: each can say that if you save the others instead of me, I have been sacrificed for a reason that does not apply to me. That is why he says the numbers do not count. The positive proposal is that where claims are equal and cannot be summed, the choice should be made by a random device giving each person an equal chance — not a concession to utility but a way of respecting each person equally, since no one's survival should be weighted more heavily than anyone else's in the procedure that settles the matter. The strongest objection is Parfit's, whose essay treats Taurek's position as proving too much: if claims cannot be aggregated then we also cannot prefer five to four, or a million to one, and Taurek must say the agent who saves one person rather than a million does nothing wrong. Parfit also charges that the randomizing procedure is itself aggregation in disguise, since flipping a coin between the one and the five weights the one equally with the whole group. I think the first charge is serious and the second is not, since the coin flip is meant to express equal concern for persons rather than to sum their claims; so I conclude that Taurek survives the second objection but not the first, and that the numbers probably do count after all."
