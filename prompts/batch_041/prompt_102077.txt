Last term, in a graduate seminar on the foundations of computability, a visiting speaker shut down a question about whether a physical system could compute a function that no Turing machine can compute — the questioner had in mind a machine performing a supertask, completing infinitely many steps in finite time — by saying flatly that the Church–Turing thesis is not a theorem but a definition, as if that disposed of the matter. I could not tell whether the remark was a deep point or a dodge, and I have to write a short reply for the seminar. Help me work out what that one-liner could actually be asserting.

Set out the distinct precise claims that the sentence "the Church–Turing thesis is not a theorem, it's a definition" might be making about the status of the thesis: from the near-trivial, through the substantive and defensible, to any that are simply false. Consider at least the readings on which the thesis is (a) a stipulative definition of "effective computability," (b) an analytic or conceptual truth about the notion of an algorithm, (c) an empirical hypothesis about which functions physical systems can compute, (d) a mathematical theorem about the equivalence of particular formalisms — Turing machines, lambda calculus, recursive functions — and (e) a claim about what an idealized human could compute in principle. For each reading, say what it commits its holder to, whether it is plausible, and — the point I most need — whether it would actually license the speaker's dismissal of the supertask question or leaves that question entirely untouched. Where two readings are often run together, separate them.

Keep what is settled distinct from what is genuinely contested among people who work on computability, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I am inclined to argue in my reply that the thesis is best read as a claim about the limits of any step-by-step procedure rather than about physical substrates; tell me whether any of the readings above support that and where it breaks. Answer in no more than 800 words, as a numbered list of readings, each with a one-line verdict.
