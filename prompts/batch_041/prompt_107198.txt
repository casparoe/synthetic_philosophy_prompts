I'm a science journalist — features, mostly, on how research actually gets made — and I keep walking into the same argument in different rooms. Someone defends a model stuffed with assumptions everybody knows are false: frictionless planes, well-mixed populations, voters who are perfectly selfish calculators, cows as spheres. The defense is always that the assumptions don't matter, only what the model predicts does. Someone else says you can't get truth out of falshood, or that a false assumption is harmless right up until it turns out to be the one doing all the work. Everybody nods along to the line that models are all wrong somewhere and some of them are still useful, and then nobody can tell me which wrong ones are the useful ones, or why. Before I start calling people for quotes I want the actual spec sheet, because I think the interesting part is the criteria, not who wins the fight.

So: write me the desiderata a theory of legitimate idealization would have to meet — what it would take for a theory to actually be the thing that tells us when a model's false assumptions are acceptable and when they poison the result. Give me the cases it has to get right, each with the case behind it: the ideal gas law, the frictionless plane, rational-actor economics, the SIR-style epidemic models that assume everyone mixes with everyone, the climate models that just parameterize clouds instead of resolving them, the fisheries and predator-prey models, whatever else you think earns a place. Give me the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse — idealization versus approximation versus plain omission, false-but-harmless versus false-and-load-bearing, a model that's a useful predictor versus one that's an explanation, the as-if reading versus the literal one, and the difference between an assumption being wrong in a way that stays put and an assumption being wrong in a way that grows with scale or time. And give me the results it has to stay consistent with — that some false models predict beautifully, that some true mechanisms predict terribly once you add back the factors you left out, that robustness and fragility are not the same thing as truth and falsity.

Then the part I actually care about: tell me which of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once, and which single one does the most to throw candidates out. I have a suspicion there's a real conflict between wanting a model to be predictively accurate in the regime you care about and wanting its central mechanism to be true rather than merely convenient — if a model can score well while its engine is fake, and score badly while its engine is real, then any criterion that leans on one of those is going to fight the other. Convince me I'm right or show me I'm not.

One thing I'd like you to fold in, because it's how I'd actually interrogate a model out in the field: a test I've started calling the flip test. You take the assumption you're worried about, relax it, and see whether the model's headline conclusion survives or flips sign. If it survives, the assumption was scaffolding you can probably get away with. If it flips, the assumption was load-bearing, and then the burden is on the modeler to argue that the real system actually lives in the regime where the false assumption holds. Tell me where this test is a genuine requirement and where it's a trap — I can already see it would flunk some models that are fine and pass some that aren't, depending on how you pick the perturbation.

Just write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. An email to me is the register I want, not a white paper.
