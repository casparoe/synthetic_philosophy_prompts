I sit on the ethics committee of a regional neuro-rehabilitation hospital, and this Thursday the committee votes on a question that stopped being academic for me the day my father was admitted to our prolonged disorders of consciousness unit after a stroke. Watching the awareness-assessment protocol from the family side of the table has made me doubt that we know what we are measuring.

The case before us is a thirty-four-year-old man, fourteen months after cardiac arrest. At the bedside he shows no command-following, no visual pursuit, no response of any kind that two examiners can agree on; under our protocol's existing trigger, which treats behavioral responsiveness as the mark of a morally considerable mind, he stays on the uncertain-prognosis pathway. But two EEG sessions a month apart show that when he is asked to imagine squeezing his right hand, or to count backwards from nine, the sensorimotor rhythm over his left hemisphere shifts in the predicted direction, on most trials, reliably enough that the neurologists have begun calling it covert command-following, or cognitive motor dissociation. He cannot tell us anything. The signal is there, and as far as the analysis goes it is under his control. His mother has asked the committee to move him onto the unit's presumed-sentient pathway.

Two amendments are on the table. The first would extend that pathway -- scheduled analgesia, comfort rounds, no painful procedure without documented justification, family treated as surrogate decision-makers for comfort -- to any patient showing covert command-following. The second would set the bar higher: not merely that a signal can be produced on command, but that the patient's own subsequent processing depends on it, that the signal is one he can use in guiding his later behavior, which the EEG evidence by itself does not establish.

I have to circulate the committee's written opinion before the meeting. Please draft it: a clear recommendation for one amendment, the other, or neither, with a specified evidentiary bar the clinical team could actually apply at the bedside and a time frame for reassessment. It should state what the committee takes the morally relevant capacity to be here -- whether what matters is the presence of a certain kind of information processing or the presence of someone who can take up his own state as his -- and it should give the strongest case for the position it rejects its due, since the committee is split and the losing side will be in the room. Do not rest the argument on legal definitions of death or on professional society guidelines; the family has heard those, and they are not what is at issue.
