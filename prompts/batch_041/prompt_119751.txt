Write the desiderata for a theory of the limits of toleration — the specification a theory must meet before I trust it to tell me when a tolerant society may stop tolerating. I do not want a survey of positions; I want the requirements themselves, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and I want the list sharp enough that a theory can visibly fail it.

The following are the requirements I would start from. Add, split, or reject them as the argument demands, but engage with each:

The theory must preserve the difference between toleration and indifference. Toleration is exercised toward what one disapproves of; where there is no disapproval there is nothing to tolerate. The theory therefore owes an account of what the disapproval attaches to — a doctrine, a practice, a person, or a form of life — and of why that choice of object governs the limits.

It must make toleration self-limiting without making it self-contradictory. A principle that permits the destruction of the conditions of its own exercise defeats itself, but a principle that permits suppressing only those who would destroy it must say, non-arbitrarily, when an objectionable view has become an existential threat. Call this the collapse threshold; the theory is only as good as its account of it.

It must avoid both degenerate positions: tolerating everything, which tolerates its own gravediggers, and tolerating only the already tolerant, which never requires tolerating anything one objects to.

It must keep apart the virtue of toleration in persons and the principle of tolerance in the state. My refusal to associate with a view is not the state's entitlement to suppress it, and the state's forbearance toward a view is not my obligation to forbear.

It must be action-guiding for actual institutions — party bans, hate-speech law, the withdrawal of public platforms — and so must specify the trigger: a threat to whom, at what threshold, assessed by whom, and on what evidence.

It must accommodate the fact that intolerance is often itself a response to perceived intolerance, so that the relation between the tolerators and the tolerated is not a one-way arrow.

Then do two things. First, mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are the parochial preferences of a particular camp, with your reason. Second — the part I most want — say whether some subset cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect at least two conflicts: between requiring the state to stay neutral among conceptions of the good and permitting it to suppress intolerant movements, since the suppression is itself a stance; and between a principle that applies uniformly to all views and a collapse threshold that turns on a case-by-case empirical assessment of danger. If a genuine impossibility falls out of the list, that is the answer to the paradox, and I would rather have it than a tidy resolution. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a table is fine for the marking.
