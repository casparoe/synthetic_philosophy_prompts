I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-one years in precision measurement, the last decade of it running a metrology group — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with considerably more enthusiasm than method. My former institute publishes a review journal that has lately begun to carry conceptual and methodological essays, and its editor, who knew me when I was still useful, has put me on the panel that referees them, on the reasoning that a working scientist will notice when the science is being misdescribed. She is right about that. It has not occurred to either of us that I have no method at all for the other half of the job. She decides; I only recommend.

Two or three times a quarter I now receive a manuscript whose central move is a claim of the form: you cannot do X — measure, report an uncertainty, reproduce a result, doubt your own memory, communicate a finding, apply a statistic — without presupposing Y; therefore Y. The Y is usually something large: the uniformity of nature, a mind-independent world, the reliability of induction, the reality of other minds, the objectivity of evidential standards. I can usually tell whether the science has been described correctly. I cannot tell whether the argument is any good, and my reports have settled into a pattern of praising the science and hedging on everything else, which is no service to anyone.

What I want is a procedure: an ordered set of questions with a stopping rule, something I can run in an afternoon with the manuscript open beside me, writing the answer to each step in the margin. Specifically, the questions in the order I should ask them; for each, what a sufficient answer looks like and what an evasive one looks like; the points at which the procedure stops and returns the verdict that the argument does not establish its conclusion; and the points at which it stops and hands the matter to me, because what remains is a question of judgment rather than of anything I can check. I would also like it to say which of the distinctions in this area must survive being made usable by someone without the theory and which can safely be dropped — and, since I will be tempted, which ones I will be tempted to collapse.

At a minimum the procedure should force four things I keep losing track of. What kind of necessity is being claimed, and by what right — conceptual, metaphysical, epistemic, normative, psychological, or merely the pragmatic sort that means we would find it very hard to do otherwise. Who the we in the argument is: all possible thinkers, all human beings, all competent speakers of a language, or the members of one research tradition at one moment in its history. What would count as a counterexample, and what it means for the argument if nothing would. And what follows for practice if the conclusion is granted — whether anything actually changes in what anyone should do. I have now seen three manuscripts make the slide from cannot to we could not bring ourselves to somewhere in the middle, and I would like the procedure to catch that.

Run it on three cases. First, a manuscript arguing that global doubt about memory is self-undermining: to entertain the hypothesis that all my apparent memories were implanted last Tuesday, I must rely on my memory of what the words mean and of what I was doing a moment ago, so the doubt consumes the ground it stands on, and trust in memory is vindicated. Second, a manuscript arguing that a purely operational reading of uncertainty intervals is incoherent: to report an interval at all is to commit oneself to a determinate fact of the matter about the quantity, independent of the measurement, so the operationalist's disclaimer is a pretence. Third, an argument of my own, drafted for a chapter I am writing and now distrusted: that the reproducibility of results presupposes that nature is regular, or at least that it does not rearrange itself between a calibration and the reading that follows it. I would like my own case run last, and I would like the verdict to go against me if that is where it goes; it is the one I am least able to judge.

Close with a short account of what the procedure cannot do — the manuscripts it will pass that it should not, and the ones it will fail unfairly — and be explicit about your own confidence throughout. For each substantive claim you make about how the procedure should work, say how confident you are, and single out the one step you are least sure of, with a brief note on what would change your mind. I don't need a reading list; I have one, and it is already longer than I can finish.
