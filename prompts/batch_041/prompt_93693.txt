So I retired about two years ago from a physics department (statistical mechanics, mostly — thirty-odd years of it), and did the classic thing: finally read all the philosophy I'd been putting off, and now I'm apparently a man with opinions about epistemology. Anyway, I've gotten myself a slot at the little formal-epistemology reading group that meets in the back room of a pub near me, and next month I'm presenting my take on the surprise exam paradox. I've been chewing on this a good while and I think I've got something, though I'm not niave enough to think it'll go down easy.

Here's the gist, so you know what to aim at. The setup is the usual one — teacher says there'll be a surprise exam next week, the students do the backwards induction and conclude it can't happen, teacher gives it Wednesday and everyone's caught out. My claim is that the standard puzzle is under-described, because nobody says what "surprise" is supposed to mean or who knows the teacher's method. So I propose a cleaned-up version: the teacher announces the exam will be on one of five days, and that the day is being chosen by a genuinely random mechanism (say a fair five-sided thing the students get to inspect before the week starts) that the teacher commits to and can't override. On that version the backwards induction can't even get started — there's no last day you can rule out, because on any given morning the students still only put one-in-five on today. So the exam is always a surprise, and moreover the students can *prove in advance* it'll be a surprise, which is where my arguement gets its teeth: I want to say the original paradox isn't about self-reference or knowledge at all, it's about the students assuming the teacher's announcement is true in order to derive that it can't be true — a kind of question-begging — and that the fix is to model the teacher's strategy as part of the public information. I've got a little fixed-point argument that an announcement of the form "the exam will surprise you" is consistent exactly when the day-choice isn't a deterministic function of public information, and I think that's the whole story.

The audience is the problem. It's maybe a dozen people: two are proper logicians who've read everything on this puzzle and its cousins and will smell any rehash instantly; one is a Bayesian who thinks every question is secretly a probability question and will either love me or hate me; one works on dynamic-epistemic logic and public announcements and will want to know why my "fixed point" isn't just a known update rule in a costume; one is a philosopher of language who'll insist the whole thing is a Gricean point about what "surprise" conversationally means; and then the usual grad students who'll ask whatever's in fashion. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense: the objections I'll actually get from *these* people, phrased the way each of them would put it, ordered by how likely and how much damage each one does, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where my reply is softest — and please include the one question I'm privately hoping nobody asks, because I think I know what it is and I'd rather rehearse it than get caught.
