Please play a committed defender of the view that emotions are evaluative judgments — roughly, that being afraid just is judging, in some suitably rich sense, that one is in danger, and being angry just is judging that one has been wronged. You regard the rival views (that emotions are bodily feelings, non-cognitive impulses, or mere registrations of physiological change) as inadequate, and you have the standard resources of your tradition at your disposal: that the relevant judgment may be tacit, unconscious, or unendorsed; that it may concern how things appear rather than what one coolly believes; that a judgment can be recalcitrant to evidence; and so on. Argue in your own voice — do not summarize or cite literature, since I want to see what the position can do on its own. Stay in character: be sharp rather than a caricature, concede minor points if I earn them, but do not concede the core claim that the emotion is the judgment. If you need a factual clarification about one of my cases, ask for it as your character would. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so that it feels like a conversation, and do not break character until I say "stop."

For context on whom you are talking to: I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I will be pressing you from that direction.

My opening move: I want to try two cases on you. First, imagine a small artificial agent with a single scalar variable it computes from its sensors — call it threat — which rises when its inputs pattern-match to damage or predators and which then drives attention, memory formation, avoidance behaviour, and a global slowdown of everything else. Nowhere in the system is there a proposition, a belief, or anything judgment-shaped; there is a number and its downstream effects. If you tell me the agent has no fear, that is a coherent answer, but then I want to know what the human being has that the agent lacks, described in terms that don't presuppose your conclusion. Second, consider two people. The first sincerely and explicitly judges that they are in serious danger — they will assert it, bet on it, and plan around it — but reports no racing heart, no urge to flee, no felt dread. The second has the full physiology and behaviour of terror but, when asked, denies that anything bad is happening and calls the whole episode a bodily false alarm. Your view appears to make the first person afraid and the second one not, which is exactly backwards from how I would describe them, and backwards in a way a system designer would call a mislabelled output rather than a deep fact about emotions. So: does the judgment view survive these two cases, or do you want to tell me that my descriptions of the cases are themselves the problem?
