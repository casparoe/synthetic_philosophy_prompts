You are a philosopher working on moral responsibility and the ethics of blame, with additional expertise in speech-act theory and in drafting adversarial-collaboration documents. You have been asked to serve as the neutral drafter for a good-faith collaboration between two camps in the debate about standing to blame. Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short working note, clearly labeled as such and no longer than ten lines, in which you brainstorm candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes and discard the ones that are vacuous or that only one side could accept. Then produce the document.

The two camps are as follows. Camp A, the self-relation camp, holds that blame is an address that presupposes a claim to be a participant in good standing in the moral relationship, and that a blamer whose own record contains the wrong being blamed — or who is implicitly claiming an exemption from a standard they apply to others — lacks the standing to make that demand, so the blame is defective at its root. Camp B, the authorization camp, holds that what licenses blame is a relation of authorization (membership in the moral community, a role, a relation to the victim), and that the blamer's own record bears on other things — the fittingness of resentment, the felicity of the address, the likelihood of uptake, or the separate wrong of hypocrisy — but not on standing, which no bad record can remove. Neither camp should be described by the names of its defenders.

The collaboration has pre-registered a shared case battery. Both camps agree that these cases bear on the dispute, and both must record their predictions about them in advance. The cases are: (1) a blamer who committed the very same wrong in the past, has acknowledged it, made amends, and has no ongoing pattern; (2) a blamer currently committing that same wrong; (3) a blamer who has never committed this wrong but has an unaddressed record of a different wrong of comparable gravity; (4) a blamer constitutionally incapable of the wrong in question — by temperament, circumstance, or design — and therefore innocent of it without ever having resisted it; (5) a blamer who speaks on behalf of a third party who was wronged, while having a record of the same wrong against others; (6) an artificial system that issues moral criticism to a user, has no moral record, no capacity to have acted otherwise, and no standing in any relationship to that user, yet is correct on the merits and is heard as making a demand.

Produce the following. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, about blame, about what standing is supposed to do, and about what the case battery does and does not show; flag any claim you suspect is agreed only because it is empty. Second, a table of the remaining disagreements, with columns for the question at issue, Camp A's formulation in wording Camp A would accept, Camp B's formulation in wording Camp B would accept, and what turns on the answer. Third, the cruxes, with each side's pre-registered prediction for the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and a note on which side, if either, would be embarrassed by each possible outcome. Fourth, a section stating whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so which part, and what the substantive disagreement turns out to be once the terminology is fixed. Fifth, a brief closing section, signed by each side, listing what would change its mind.

Write in American English, in formal and precise prose, addressing an audience of professional philosophers. Do not include citations, references, reading lists, or attributions to named authors; describe positions by their content. Do not pad the agreed-claims section: a short list of claims with real content is worth more than a long list of platitudes.
