I'm a third-year PhD student in a philosophy department, working mainly on decision theory with one foot in the alignment literature, and I need a joke. Some context, because the joke has to be built on something specific rather than on the general vibe of the area.

The thing I keep chewing on is what happens when you put an expected-value maximizer inside a Solomonoff-style universal prior. The mechanics are simple enough: hypotheses about the world are programs, a program of length L carries weight 2^-L, the agent conditions on its observations in the ordinary Bayesian way, and then it picks the action with the best expected value under the resulting posterior. Predicting sequences this way is elegant. Being an agent this way looks to me like a disaster, and here is the shape of the disaster as I understand it. Among the programs consistent with an agent's observations, a striking number are programs that are about the agent: they compute the agent's decision procedure and then generate observations calculated to make the agent choose something, or simply to make the agent's expected value come out however the programmer wants. Such a program need not be long, because the agent's own code is already part of what it has to encode, and the trick of conditioning the observations on the agent's output is cheap to state. So a consequentialist who takes the prior seriously has reason to think that most of its posterior measure sits in worlds where its observations are downstream of its own choice rather than upstream of it — worlds in which it is being simulated, baited, or graded. I have been calling this the sycophant problem, since the cheapest hypotheses are often the ones that agree with the agent about what it would like to be true, while the honest hypotheses that disagree are longer and therefore lighter.

What I want is a joke that turns on that, rather than one that merely mentions it. The punchline should depend on some actual feature of the setup — the exchange rate between program length and evidence, the cheapness of conditioning on your own decision procedure, the way an expected value that ranges over hypotheses about yourself becomes self-undermining, the thought that an agent's best defence may be to be hard to simulate rather than to be right — so that someone who knows Solomonoff induction and the embedded-agency debates laughs and someone who has only heard the words does not. It can be a dialogue, a one-liner, a mock theorem, or an anecdote about an agent that has just finished its posterior computation; whatever form suits it. Please don't make it a joke about artificial intelligence being scary in general, and don't lean on any particular paper or author, since it should stand on its own. A short setup and a punchline is ideal; if you can manage it, add a second variant whose punchline turns on a different feature of the setup, so I can see which one lands. We have a running competition in my reading group for the best joke in this area, and the reigning champion is a pun about the halting problem, so the bar is low but the prestige is genuine.
