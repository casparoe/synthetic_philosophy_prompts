I've been meeting with a reading group on echo chambers and epistemic bubbles for about eighteen months now, and I've started to suspect we aren't actually disagreeing about anything — which is strange, because we disagree constantly. Half the group reckons the whole framing is empirically hollow: the data on who talks to whom doesn't show the sort of segregation the term implies, and where you do find clustering it's concentrated in a small slice of the population, so the concept is being asked to carry more than the evidence will bear. The other half thinks that's a daft standard of evidence: the phenomenon is about the *quality* of the epistemic environment, not about whether the average person is sealed off, and demanding population-level effect sizes is a category error. We've been round that loop maybe six times and nobody has moved an inch.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. And there are a few things I've noticed that I think are where the real action is, so please factor them in:

First, the distinction people usually draw between an echo chamber (contrary views are present but actively discredited) and a bubble (contrary views simply never come up) seems to do no work at all in our group. Whenever anyone presses on it, the two collapse into each other — the bubble turns out to be maintained by something, and the discrediting turns out to depend on people not having heard the other side properly. I don't know if that means the distinction is unstable or just that our cases are mixed.

Second, my own experience running a first-year seminar. I had students write down anonymously, at the start and end of term, what they thought about a couple of charged political questions, and I also logged what they were willing to say out loud. The written beliefs barely moved. What they'd say in the room moved a lot. So maybe what we're tracking isn't belief formation at all but the conditions of expression — something closer to preference falsification or a spiral of silence than to a filter.

Third, a supply/demand split. Some of us explain the thing top-down (recommendation systems, media incentives, platform architecture) and some bottom-up (what people want to hear, what they're motivated to seek out). Ask the top-down lot for evidence and you get platform-level data; ask the bottom-up lot and you get psychology experiments. Neither counts as evidence by the other's lights, and I can't tell whether that's a genuine clash of explanatory targets or two halves of one story that we're wrongly treating as rivals.

My working hypothesis is that it isn't one disagreement but three stacked on top of each other: an empirical one about what the world is like, a conceptual one about what counts as an echo chamber and whether the term is doing any explanatory work or is just a pejorative, and a normative one about what's supposedly bad about it — is the harm epistemic (people have bad evidence and false beliefs) or political and affective (people don't trust each other, or can't act together)? And I suspect nobody moves because each side keeps demanding evidence of the wrong kind. Tell me if that's right, and if it isn't, what the actual fault line is.

What I want from you: the diagnosis, grounded in what each side would actually say; whether the sides are answering different questions under a single name; whether any part of this is a hidden empirical question a study could settle, and if so what it would have to measure; and what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or just the admission that it bottoms out in normative starting points. Don't give me a reading list, I don't need citations, and I'd rather you reason from the structure of the thing than from who said what.

Please write in British English. Finish with a final line of exactly the form Diagnosis: <short label>, naming in a few words what kind of disagreement you think this is, and nothing after it.
