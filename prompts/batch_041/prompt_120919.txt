The case: the modern hunt for a historical exodus out of Egypt. Here's the record I want you to work with, so we don't waste words on it. The Merneptah stele, about 1208 BCE, is the only Egyptian text that names Israel, and it's a victory boast that Israel is laid waste and its seed is no more — and Israel there carries the determinative for a people, not for a city, so by that date there's already an Israel in Canaan, roughly when the Bible has them arriving. Then nothing. No Egyptian text ever mentions the sojourn, the plagues, or the departure, in a civilization that recorded its labour gangs. Meanwhile the highland villages that look like earliest Israel are culturally continuous with Canaanite ones, the numbers in the story (600,000 fighting men) are impossible, the store city named Rameses points to a 19th-dynasty delta capital, the Pentateuch as we have it is usually dated to the Persian period though Amos and Hosea already allude to the exodus in the 8th century, the Sinai shows nothing, the Ipuwer papyrus gets trotted out every few years as plague evidence, and Manetho's third-century BCE Egyptian history — as Josephus quotes it and then furiously rebuts it in Against Apion — already has the ancestors of the Jews emerging from the Hyksos expelled from the Delta by Ahmose.

Now the lens, and it's mine, so take it seriously enough to break it if it deserves breaking. Split the standard criteria of authenticity from historical-Jesus work (embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, contextual plausibility) into two kinds. Attestation criteria tell you what a community was willing to say — they measure the tradents' commitments and the pressures on them. Occurrence criteria would tell you something about what happened on the ground. My claim is that embarrassment is nearly always doing attestation work while being sold as occurrence work, and that this is exactly the muddle in the exodus debate: that a tradition preserves what's inconvenient for it (Israel as slaves, an Egyptian name on the founding hero, that ambush in Exodus 4 where Yahweh jumps Moses at a night camp and Zipporah has to circumcise the boy and touch his feet with the foreskin to save him, the mixed multitude, the fact that the whole founding story happens in the wrong country) is real evidence about the people who told it and close to nothing about events.

So: run the case through the split. Which features are attestation-only, and what do they actually license? Where does the split do work the unsplit criteria can't? Then turn it around — does any occurrence work survive here (the people-determinative on the stele, the Rameses toponym, Egyptian silence, the sheer oddity of inventing an origin in slavery abroad)? Is silence evidence or just a shifted burden? And the reverse test I care most about: does this case show the split is too clean, i.e. are there attestation facts that do constrain occurrence — or is the distinction just we can't know dressed up in two columns? Where do the criteria, split or not, go silent, and what's your honest verdict on what the historicity question can and can't deliver?
