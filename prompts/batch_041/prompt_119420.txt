I teach ethics, so I'm allowed to be an amatuer about causation and historiography, but I've been reading my way through the Cold War origins literature for fun (orthodox, revisionist, post-revisionist, the whole train set) and there's a move I keep running into that I don't have a word for.

Here's the shape of it. A historian wants to tell you that the question of who started the Cold War is the wrong question. The outcome was overdetermined, both sides were caught in a secruity dilemma, it was a collision of two systems, nobody started it in the sense the question assumes. Fine, I believe it about half the time. But then two chapters later the same book asks, quite comfortably, whose decision it was to do some particular thing, and answers without hesitation: his. Agency gets switched back on the moment the actor is on the other side. And you get the mirror image from the other camp, where American moves are explained structurally (the bomb, the open door, the security dilemma) and Soviet moves are explained by ideology and one paranoid man's temperament. I get the same feeling from the 1914 literature, though I know it less well. The part that really bugs me is that each of these explanations is individually pretty good. Nobody is lying, and if you read either chapter on its own you'd never catch it.

Is there a name for this? I don't mean just 'bias' — that's the lazy answer and it carves nothing, because the move isn't an error in any single application, and I don't think it's the old cause-versus-condition distinction either. The closest I can get on my own is that whether you're allowed to say someone caused something seems to depend on what you're implicitly comparing it to: caused it as opposed to what? And so which side comes out looking guilty is fixed by which comparison you set up, and the comparison looks like it can be set up after the fact, defended by a levels-of-explanation argument — structure here, agency there.

So: does that have a name, or a family of nearby names, and if the honest answer is that there isn't one, coin me one and tell me where its edges are. I want the boundaries, not the label. What separates the guilty version from a legitimate one? Presumably the shift is fine when the two cases really do differ in the evidence, or really do sit at different scales — so what distinguishes that from the shift that's doing ideological work? And how is this different from plain special pleading, from the actor/observer stuff in social psychology, and from whataboutism (which I take to be a different animal, since whataboutism changes the subject whereas this one keeps the subject and turns the dial)?

And don't hand me a reading list, I'm not going to chase down six articles — I want the concept itself, plus the near misses and why they don't quite fit.
