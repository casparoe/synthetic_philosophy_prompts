I'm a science and ideas journalist — features, not academia — and I've somehow agreed to run a 70-minute session at an ideas festival in the autumn. Mixed audience, maybe 35 people, no philosophy background, and it's a festival so they'll have had a drink. The subject is insect sentience, or more precisely the argument that does the heavy lifting in that whole debate: there are something like a billion billion insects alive at any one time (I've seen a figure, I can't remember it exactly, and I don't want the session to depend on it), our evidence that any of them can suffer is very thin, but if you multiply a thin probability by an astronomical number you can end up with a moral quantity that dwarfs the farmed animals everyone actually worries about.

I don't want to stand there and explain expected value to a room of strangers. I want them to walk into it. So design me the session. A game, a staged decision, a role-play, whatever you think actually works on a general audience — rules, what I need to bring or print, how it's scored if it's scored, and a running order that fits 70 minutes including questions. Payoffs fake, obviously; no real money, no real stakes. They shouldn't hear the name of the argument, or the phrase expected value, until the debrief.

The thing I want them to feel is the moment the intuition and the arithmetic come apart — ideally that they've committed to a position, in public, before the arithmetic arrives, and then have to watch it move. My hunch is that this means making them put a number on it first (bet on whether there's anyone home in there) and only then running their own number out across the population. But you may have a better idea, and if my framing is wrong, say so.

The debrief matters more than the game here. Map what happened onto the actual structure of the argument: what in the session plays the role of the probability, what plays the role of the number, which move in the argument each stage was standing in for, and where the participants will have supplied a premise themselves without noticing. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with a festival crowd rather than a seminar. And tell me which part of the argument the activity can't reproduce and that I'll just have to say out loud.

Two things on accuracy. No citations or reading lists — I can't use them in a festival session anyway, and I'd rather you didn't guess at them. Same with figures: if you're confident about an order of magnitude, use it, otherwise keep it vague ('astronomically many', 'a very small probability') rather than inventing a number. I'd rather the activity work with any number of the right size then rest on one you've half-remembered.

And push back if this is a bad idea. If the honest answer is that you can't get an audience to feel a judgement about probability of sentience in 70 minutes without them just taking my inputs on trust, tell me that instead of designing politely around it. If the whole thing is arithmetic dressed up as a moral discovery, I want to know that too, because I'll be taking questions from people in the room who know more than I do.
