I'm a retired geologist — 35 years of ash layers and ice cores — and since I packed it in I've been reading alot of history and philosophy of science, mostly out of my depth. Here's what's been bugging me. There's a recent line of work arguing that the collapse of indigenous populations in the Americas after 1492 was so enormous that farmland went back to forest, which pulled enough CO2 out of the air to show up in Antarctic ice cores and maybe helped tip things toward the Little Ice Age. If that's right, smallpox and trees end up as major actors in early modern history, right alongside the conquistadors. Same story with Tambora blowing in 1815 and the year without a summer that followed it.

Humboldt died in 1859, before germ theory was even settled, but he spent his life insisting that nature is one interconnected system of forces, and he was one of the first to write about humans wrecking the landscapes they settle (he linked deforestation around Lake Valencia to the lake shrinking). So what would he have made of all this? Would he have welcomed germs and volcanoes as historical actors, as the obvious extension of his holism? Or would he have balked, on the grounds that explaining human history with bugs, ash and CO2 is exactly the environmental determinism trap? And the political side of it: he was a loud critic of slavery and colonialism, so would he have read the "the germs did it" version as letting the conquerors off the hook?

Don't give me a menu of readings. Take a definite position on what he'd have thought and give me your reasons, even if it means going out on a limb. If your answer rests on a guess about what he actually believed, say which guess it is and how much weight its carrying.
