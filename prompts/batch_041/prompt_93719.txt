I am assembling a two-year working group on the metaphysics of intentionality — two postdoctoral fellows, a modest workshop series, and enough budget to keep the group focused — and I need to decide which questions to build it around. The organizing problem is the distinction between original and derived intentionality: the thought that some states are intrinsically about their objects, while others (words, pictures, the internal states of artifacts) are about things only because some intrinsically intentional agent conferred that aboutness on them. The case I keep returning to is the two-bitser, the coin-detecting device whose behavior can be predicted and explained by attributing to it something like the belief that a coin is a genuine quarter, even though we are confident it believes nothing at all. Dennett treats the case as showing that the intentional stance is all there is to aboutness, or at least that the difference between the device and us is one of degree and complexity rather than kind; Searle treats it as showing the opposite, that derived intentionality presupposes original intentionality and that the intentional stance quietly borrows the very thing it claims to explain away. I want the group to make progress on that dispute rather than rehearse it.

So: within that neighborhood, which open questions should we prioritize? I am less interested in a list than in a defended ordering. For each candidate question, I want to know whether it is upstream of the others — whether settling it dissolves or reshapes the rest — how tractable it is for philosophers working with conceptual tools rather than empirical data, how neglected it currently is, and what would have to happen (a new argument, a counterexample, a formal result) for it to move up or down the ranking. Candidates I have in mind, though I expect you to add or drop some: whether derived intentionality can be defined without circular appeal to original intentionality; whether the intentional stance is a predictive strategy, a metaphysical thesis, or both at once; whether the two-bitser is genuinely analogous to a contemporary language model or whether the analogy breaks in a way that matters for the dispute; and whether the original/derived distinction is itself theoretically load-bearing or a relic of a pre-naturalistic picture of mind. Please also tell me which of these I should refuse to fund, and why.
