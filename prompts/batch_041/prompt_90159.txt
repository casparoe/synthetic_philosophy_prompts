I am a statistician who builds probabilistic forecasts for a living: my working vocabulary is filtrations, conditional expectations, martingales, proper scoring rules, calibration diagnostics, likelihood ratios, and Bayesian model averaging over a set of candidate data-generating processes. My economics is at the level of slogans. I want the cluster of results about markets and information — the claim that rational agents who share a prior cannot profitably trade on purely informational differences, the claim that a price already reflects the information available to the market, and the accompanying epistemology in which the market price is treated as an authoritative aggregator of dispersed private knowledge — restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as it can be.

Treat this as a translation exercise, not as an explanation pitched at a layperson. Say what each of those claims becomes when it is written in terms of probability spaces, filtrations, conditional expectations, and scoring rules. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I expect the interesting cases to be those in which the source vocabulary is about equilibrium among several agents while mine is about a single exogenous data-generating process, and those in which my habitual assumption that the process generating the outcomes is fixed and unaffected by my forecast is exactly what the source claims deny.

Two things I would like worked through rather than gestured at. First: suppose I hold a proprietary model that has predicted an asset's returns better than the market's implied forecast on every sample I possess. State the no-trade reasoning that concludes this cannot be a genuine informational advantage, and say precisely which premises that reasoning requires — where in my vocabulary each premise would have to live, and whether any of them can be stated as a condition on a joint distribution alone. Second: forecast combination. In my practice, pooling several forecasts usually improves accuracy, and I get to choose the pooling weights; in the market setting the pooling is not performed by anyone. Tell me what the market's aggregation is, if it is not a weighted average, and whether the properties I rely on when I combine forecasts survive the translation.

Finish with a verdict on the residue. For each piece that did not survive, say whether it is a gap in my vocabulary — something a statistician ought to be able to state and cannot — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than its phrasing suggests. Do not let the translation quietly import my framework's assumptions (a fixed data-generating process, exogenous sampling, a single decision-maker, no endowments) and present them as though they were neutral; where you must import one, flag it as an import.
