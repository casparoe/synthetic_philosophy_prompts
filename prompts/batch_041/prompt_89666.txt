Next month I am presenting to a philosophy of physics reading group, and what I need from you is a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a summary of my argument. I know what I think; what I do not know is what this particular room will do to it.

Some background, so that you know what to aim at. I am a software engineer by trade and have no formal training in philosophy; I came to this from an interest in consciousness rather than from a degree. My thesis is that some version of panpsychism — specifically the version, sometimes labelled Russellian monism, on which physics describes only the structural and relational features of matter while leaving its intrinsic nature uncharacterised — is not merely a theory of mind but an ethical thesis. If it is true, then fundamental physics is itself a domain of moral concern, and the question of how much suffering exists in the universe is settled by physics rather than by biology. I further want to argue that the standard panpsychist way of doing the moral arithmetic — one subject of experience per fundamental entity, then count the entities — is both unnecessary and probably incoherent, and that we should begin instead from valenced states and their distribution rather than from subjects. Two corollaries I hold and will have to defend: I am not much moved by the combination problem as it is usually stated, because I think it assumes experience must be unified and reportable, and I see no reason a fundamental entity could not have a momentary tone with no self behind it; and I suspect the valence might not attach to entities at all, but be more like a field than a set of little minds.

The room is about a dozen people, friendly but unsentimental. Five or six work on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and two of those are committed Everettians who spend their time on how to count branches and how to recover probabilities from the wavefunction. One works on quantum field theory and regards particle talk as a convenient fiction. One is a Kant scholar. One is a philosopher of biology who works on animal welfare and on the markers of sentience. One is a statistical mechanic who cares about measures, coarse-graining, and what counts as a natural unit. They have sat through too many consciousness talks to be impressed by enthusiasm.

I want the objections I will actually get from this room — not the generic ones a philosopher of mind would raise — set out in roughly the order they will arrive and marked for how much damage each would do. Phrase each one the way the relevant member would phrase it, in their own vocabulary. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, say plainly what I should concede rather than fight, and flag where the reply is soft or where I am leaning on something this room will not grant. Finish with the question I am most hoping nobody asks, and explain why that is the one I should fear.

Two things to keep in view. The pressure points are where the physics and the moral arithmetic meet: what counts as a subject when the fundamental entities are not countable, what supplies the measure over which suffering is supposed to be distributed, and whether anything can be valenced without an interest that can be set back. And do not spare me: if a line of attack shows that the view has no determinate content, say so rather than offering reassurance. I do not need citations or further reading — the room will supply those.

Please write throughout in British English.
