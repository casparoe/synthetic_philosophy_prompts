I run a small reading group — lesswrong-adjacent, mostly people who've read the Sequences and at least one metaethics textbook — and we keep circling the same argument about idealized preferences without ever getting anywhere, because everyone is answering a different question while using the same words. I've read the coherent-extrapolated-volition writeups more often than is healthy, and I've noticed that the machinery gets deployed for at least three different jobs: telling a person what is good for them, telling them what they really will, and telling a group what it should do together. Most disputes I've seen are really disputes about which job is being done, plus an unexamined choice about what "idealized" is supposed to add. So what I want from you is a classification instrument rather than an essay: please act as a philosopher who works on desire-based accounts of welfare and on collective decision procedures, comfortable with both the formal apparatus (utility functions, reflective stability, aggregation) and the standard metaethical objections to idealization, and build me a decision tree over the positions in this neighborhood — where every branch node is a question someone could answer about a concrete case without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf is a named position or is explicitly marked as one nobody defends. Think it through carefully before you commit to a structure, and start by brainstorming, because I would much rather see the distinctions you considered and discarded than get a tidy tree with no seams in it.

The angle I care about is the one above, so let the tree do some work that a textbook summary wouldn't. Force every leaf to declare which job it is doing — a theory of the person's own good, a theory of their own will or autonomy, or a procedure for collective choice — and let at least one branch node separate positions that agree about the mechanics but disagree about the job. Make the tree distinguish idealization by more information from idealization by more time and cognitive power from idealization by better character, and separately distinguish the view that idealization discovers a preference already there from the view that it transforms the person into someone whose preference counts; I want the cases at those nodes to be ones where the two idealizations actually diverge, not a case that fits both. The tree must also locate aggregation in the right place: extrapolating each person and then combining, combining first and extrapolating a single merged subject, and treating the whole thing as one shared volition with a convergence assumption all need to be distinguishable, and I want at least one question about what the theory says when two people's idealized volitions flatly conflict. Please include leaves for, at minimum: full-information desire satisfaction in its plain form; the ideal-advisor picture where a better-informed counterpart of you sets the standard; a proceduralist view on which the authority comes from the process of reflective endorsement rather than from any output; the structure where authority lies in a desire about your own desires; the CEV-style shared extrapolation; the objection that the idealized counterpart is simply a different person whose preferences have no claim on you; and a deflationary view that treats idealization as a formalization of ordinary practical reasoning with no separate facts behind it. I also want at least one question about whether unbounded idealization is even determinate — whether there is a fact of the matter about what the fully idealized agent wants, or whether the extrapolation fails to converge and the whole account inherits that indeterminacy. Two constraints on the questions themselves: nobody should be able to answer a node correctly by pattern-matching a label, so no question of the form "do you think idealization discovers or transforms?", and every node should be a case or a plain-English claim that a smart person outside the literature could answer from their own intuitions. Aim for nine to twelve questions and eight to twelve leaves; I'll be photocopying this as a handout people fill in themselves, so it needs to work without me in the room. Finally, resist the temptation to give each question exactly one leaf — if two questions turn out to do the same separating work, say so.
