I do causal inference for a living — public health epi, mostly — so the way I think about causes is the sufficent-component-cause picture: each sufficient cause is a pie, the slices are component causes, the effect happens when any one pie gets all its slices in place. A component cause can be indispensable for its own pie and still not be necessary for the effect, because some other pie might do the job, and I'm fluent in the potential-outcomes/counterfactual stuff that sits next to it.

On the side I've been reading about historical explanation, and I keep running into the historians' and philosophers-of-history's worry about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation: a war, a revolution, a regime collapse seems to have several independent causal stories, each of which on its own would have been enough, so no single factor is 'the' cause — and yet historians go right on naming one. Take the role of an expert who can hold both the formal causal-modelling apparatus and the historians' actual practice in view at once. Before you write anything, brainstorm: list the main things historians and philosophers of history say about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation, list the main moving parts of the sufficient-component-cause framework, then do the translation. Think it through carefully; I'd rather have the working-out than the tidy version.

The task: restate the historical debate as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — pies, component causes, sufficient sets, necessary causes, attributable fractions, potential outcomes and contrasts — position by position, not as one blended 'standard view' (and don't bother with citations, I'm not going to check them; what I care about is whether the translation is honest).

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which of the historians' claims go over cleanly, which only go over if I distort them, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all? For each residue item, say whether it looks like a defect of the pie picture — something the framework genuinely can't say — or a sign that the historians' formulation was carrying weight the causal talk was never meant to carry. For instance, if some supposed problem about overdetermination just dissolves once you write the pies down, I want to know that, and why.

Push especially on the places I suspect the failures live. (a) Ranking causes: everyone wants to say one cause was the big one, while the pie picture treats every slice of a completed pie as equally indispensable — what survives of degrees of causation? (b) Explaining one particular event rather than a rate or a class, and contrastive questions like why this outcome rather than that one. (c) The cause/condition, cause/background distinction and causal selection — why we single out one background condition as the explanation when the structure looks symmetric. (d) The counterfactual 'it would have happened anyway' claim, which looks like the historians' real test for overdetermination, and how that reads as potential outcomes with backup causes in play.

Don't smuggle my frameworks assumptions in and call the result a translation — if the pie picture handles their claims only by redefining them, say so plainly. And keep the two registers separate: be explicit about what's settled among people who actually know this material versus what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. I'd rather get 'this is my call, here's the counterargument' than false confidence.
