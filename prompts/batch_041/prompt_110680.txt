A friend of mine — Dutch, raised in Rotterdam, two years in Kyoto — and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about politeness and white lies, and I came away with the uncomfortable suspicion that I hold a set of opinions no single principle can hold together. Her position has two parts. First, she distinguishes, in her own vocabulary, cushioning falsehoods, which absorb a blow that has already landed, from scaffolding falsehoods, which hold up something the listener is going to lean on later, and she says only the second kind matters. Her test for telling them apart is consent: a false statement is permissible exactly when the person you say it to, in full possession of the truth, would be glad you said what you said rather than nothing. On that test, the food was delicious and you two seem good together are innocent, while the report is fine is not, because only the last leaves the listener worse off down the road. Second, she thinks the moral category is fixed by culture: Dutch bluntness and Japanese indirectness are two local conventions for the same job, so calling my colleague rude is just provincially preferring the other convention. I told her both halves seemed wrong to me, but I could not say why without contradicting myself somewhere.

Here is what I actually think, case by case, going by my gut. A host asks how the meal was and it was mediocre; I say it was lovely. Fine, no hesitation. A close friend asks, privately, whether I think her partner is right for her, and I have doubts I cannot articulate; I say they seem good together. Fine. A student asks whether her draft chapter is ready to go to her supervisor, and I think it is not; I say it is fine. Not fine — and I notice this is the same shape as the previous case, a solicited opinion where I give the reassuring answer, and it is the pair I cannot reconcile. A colleague gives a muddled conference talk; afterwards I tell him it was good. Fine, since the audience has already made up its mind and my opinion cannot change anything — though I would feel differently if he were giving the same talk again next week, and I do not know what to do with that. A Dutch colleague, asked for feedback on my proposal in front of the whole group, says flatly that the second section is confused; it was, and I had asked. I think saying it was right and saying it there was wrong, and I think that would be true in Amsterdam as much as in Osaka, which is where I part company with my friend. My Japanese host asks whether I liked the natto; I say I did; I did not. Fine, and I would think it faintly monstrous to say otherwise. My father asks whether I still think he should be driving, and I say yes. Not fine, and I cannot imagine a culture in which it becomes fine. Then there is a small one I keep going back and forth on: to get out of a dinner I said I already had plans, when what I wanted was an evening alone. I said it was fine at the time and now think it was not, because it told her she ranked below something, whereas saying I was exhausted would have been fine. I have a tentative principle of my own — a false statement is innocent when it spares a feeling and guilty when it protects a decision — but it does not survive the partner case, where the decision is real and I still think the lie is fine.

What I want is the principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible without turning into a pile of exceptions. Please state it precisely enough that I can apply it, say which clause is doing the work for each case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Then apply it to at least two cases I have not mentioned, and tell me which of my friend's claims survive: is the cushioning and scaffolding line the real distinction, is consent the right test for sorting them, and is the directness of my colleague and the indirection of my host a matter of local convention or of something that holds everywhere?
