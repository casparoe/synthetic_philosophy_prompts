I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and I keep a reading journal in which, for every view I encounter, I write down the best one-paragraph statement of it I've heard and then my own audit of that statement. A guest lecturer in our laws-of-nature seminar opened with a précis of the Humean/anti-Humean debate that several people in my cohort have since been repeating as settled, and I'd like to check it before it hardens into something I cite. Here is the précis as faithfully as I can reproduce it: "The Humean thinks the world is nothing over and above the particular matters of fact — where things are, what they're doing — and that laws are just summaries of those facts, a compact way of recording what has happened so far. So on the Humean picture, the fact that every electron repels every other is a grand coincidence rather than something that had to be so. The anti-Humean, by contrast, thinks laws are entities in their own right that govern the world — the cement holding the regularities together — the paradigm being the Armstrong-style view on which a law is a relation of necessitation between universals, which is what supplies the modal force. Because the Humean's law is only a summary of the actual, the Humean has to deny that laws explain their instances or support counterfactuals: a summary can't tell you what would have happened had things gone differently, nor why this electron repelled that one. That last point is what most philosophers take to be fatal — a mere summary can't do explanatory work — which is why the field has largely gone anti-Humean." I have the vague sense that this is unfair to the Humeans in some places and unfair to the anti-Humeans in others, but I can't yet say where, and I'd rather not keep repeating it until I can.

Please audit it for me clause by clause.
