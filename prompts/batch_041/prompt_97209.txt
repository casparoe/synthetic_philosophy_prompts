I'm putting together the final for my third-year philosophy of mind seminar, and the essay section is giving me trouble. I want it to be on the Malcolm vs. Dennett thing about whether dreams are experiences, but I don't want students just reciting the standard line on each and stopping. So the question has to make them apply both views to a case neither of them wrote about.

Here's the case. In a sleep lab, subjects are trained to make a distinctive pattern of eye movements — call it the signal — and a different pattern — call it the tone-signal — when they hear a tone. During REM sleep they produce the signal. Then a tone is played, and they produce the tone-signal. Woken, they report a dream in which they were walking down a corridor, heard a tone, and decided to signal. In a seperate condition, subjects produce the signal during REM but on waking report no dream at all, or a dream with nothing about signalling in it. The eye movements happen in real time, during sleep, and the tone-signal only shows up after the tone.

It's a 60-minute question, 25 marks. Write me the essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The question should ask whether this case refutes Malcolm, refutes Dennett, both, or neither — and which view handles it better, and what the dissociation condition does to each. It should not be answerable by reciting the two positions; students have to say what each view must say about the eye movements and whether that's a cost or a feature.

Some things to bake in. Malcolm, as I read him, says dreaming isn't an experience at all: the concept of a dream is anchored in the waking report, and the question of whether the dreamer really had images while asleep is confused rather than an empirical question with a hidden answer. Dennett doesn't go that way; he offers the cassette theory, where the brain composes a dream narrative during sleep and stores it like a memory, and the dream you seem to remember is played back or reconstructed at waking, so again no experience during sleep, but for a different reason and with different commitments. Both deny dreams are experiences, but they're not the same denial, and the eye-signal case bites them differently. I want the marking scheme to separate an A from a B by naming the exact moves: an A has to say whether the real-time, content-sensitive eye movements count as a report or just as behaviour, and why that matters for Malcolm; an A has to say whether the cassette theory can absorb the tone-signal as part of the composition or whether it needs the sleeper to be aware during sleep; an A has to say what the dissociation condition does — does it undercut the signal as evidence of experience, or does it just show that memory for the dream is fragile? A B answer will probably say this shows dreams are experiences, or that Malcolm and Dennett both deny dreams are experiences so neither is refuted, and stop there. If there's a move that saves both views at a price — say, treating the eye movements as trained reflexes with no awareness behind them — the scheme should note it and say what the price is.

No citations, no reading list, and don't put any quotations from Malcolm or Dennett into the question. Paraphrase if you need to.
