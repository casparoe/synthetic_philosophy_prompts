I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and I am trying to design an exercise that forces my students out of the usual dialectical rut. The two camps we spend the term on are the conciliationists — roughly, those who hold that learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you about some proposition, on the basis of evidence you regard as shared, obliges you to move your credence toward theirs (at least absent an independent reason to think you are the better judge) — and the steadfasters, who hold that you may in some such cases keep your credence where it is, on the ground that the fact that you have weighed the evidence and arrived where you did can itself count in favour of the peer having misjudged it. I would like you to act as a philosopher with expertise in both social epistemology and the methodology of expert judgement, and to write the joint statement that a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfaster could both put their names to after a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a short, clearly marked brainstorming section in which you lay out in your own words the distinctions the document will need — epistemic peer, evidential parity, independence, the first-order versus higher-order evidence distinction, and the difference between a revision of credence and a revision of what one takes to be admissible evidence — and note where you suspect the two camps have been talking past one another because of terminology rather than substance. Then produce the document proper: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, followed by a disagreements table.

I want the collaboration anchored to a concrete case rather than to abstractions, so use the following. Two specialists in the same subfield disagree about whether a particular industrial solvent is a human carcinogen at the low doses found in ordinary occupational exposure. They share the same evidence base: several epidemiology cohorts with wide confidence intervals, a contested animal study, and a mechanistic argument that the dose-response curve has a threshold below which no effect occurs. One of them reads the totality as probably carcinogenic; the other reads it as roughly balanced. They also disagree at a level above that: whether the mechanistic threshold argument is admissible at all as evidence bearing on low-dose risk, or whether admissibility is itself something that the epidemiological evidence should decide. Their track records are good but asymmetric: over roughly a decade of disputes in this subfield, the first has turned out right about seven times in ten, but the second observes that most of those disputes were resolved by methods the first favoured, so the track record may be partly an artefact of which questions got asked. Each can also recall specific cases where the other was plainly wrong and they were right. Finally, a third specialist whom both regard as a peer has publicly taken the first one's side, and the two of them disagree about whether that agreement is independent evidence or merely a restatement of evidence they both already possess.

The disagreements table should have one row per disputed claim, with columns for the question at issue; the wording of the conciliationist position that the conciliationist would accept as her own; the wording of the steadfast position that the steadfaster would accept as his own; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; a concrete case, datum, or argument that both would agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, written down in advance. Two things I especially want the document to handle: the diachronic problem that arises if the conciliationist has already moved twice toward the steadfaster, who has not moved at all, and whether continued conciliation in that situation is required or absurd; and whether conciliationism, if it always halves the difference, collapses into a general scepticism about expertise that neither party wants. I also want a closing section identifying any part of the dispute that you judge to be merely verbal and saying why, and a short appendix recording what each side would count as a consideration against its own view. Do not let the agreed-claims section degenerate into platitudes — both agree the truth matters and that evidence is relevant is not worth writing down. Please think carefully and at length before drafting, phrase each side's position in wording its own adherent could endorse without wincing, and refer to the positions rather than to their famous proponents: no citations, no reading list, and no appeals to what some well-known paper is supposed to have established. Aim for something in the region of fifteen hundred words, though let the structure dictate the length.
