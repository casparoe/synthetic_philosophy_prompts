I'm on the historical commission in my town (I'm not a historian — I run a hardware store, I got put on the commission because I'm on the preservation society) and next month we have to vote on a new interpretive plaque for the statue of one of our founders on the town common. The draft text was written by a grad student the town hired. The gist of it is that he was a man of his time, but that the fortune he built the town with came from shipping and insuring slaving voyages. Two of us want the plaque to just say plainly that it was wrong and that he got rich off it. Two of us say it's not our job to moralize about the 1840s, we should describe what he did in context and let people decide. Our staff historian keeps telling us we have to avoid "presentism," and when I asked her what that meant she said it means don't judge the past by the standards of today.

Heres what's been bugging me and I cant find anybody to explain it to me. Everybody says dont judge the past by the standards of today. But don't we always judge by the standards of our own time, whatever those happen to be? People in 1840 were judging by the standards of 1840. So when the historian tells me not to be presentist, she's telling me to use some standard other than the one I've got — but the only standard I've got is the one I've got. Is that even possible for a person? Or does "don't be presentist" secretly just mean "go adopt what you imagine their standards were," which is still me, here and now, deciding what to imagine?

And the other thing: people say "he was a man of his time" as if everybody back then agreed. But there were people back then who thought it was wrong, weren't there? So which one counts as "his time" — the majority, or everyone who was alive? If even one person objected, was that standard already present in his time, and does that mean he wasn't really a man of his time at all, just a man on the winning side of it?

I have to write up my recommendation for the commission before the vote, and I want it to be something we can actually act on, not just hand-wringing.

Can you help me? I'd like you to take on the role of someone who really knows the philosophy of history and historiography — the kind of person who has thought hard about whether historians can ever escape the standards of their own time — but write it so a regular person on a town commission can follow it. Please think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming out the considerations on each side for me before you commit to anything.

Then give me the memo itself, written the way I'd circulate it to the other commissioners. It needs a clear bottom line: tell me which way to vote — approve the draft as written, amend it to specific language, or reject it and start over — and dont waffle. Give the reasoning for the recommendation, and then honestly give the strongest argument for the position I'm NOT taking, the one that could actually change my mind if I believed it, instead of a strawman.

Don't worry about citations, sources, or a reading list, I'm not going to look anything up, I just want the argument.

Please write in American English.
