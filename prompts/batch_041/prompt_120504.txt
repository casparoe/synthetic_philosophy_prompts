I'm a second-year PhD student working at the intersection of machine learning and philosophy of mind, and I run a fortnightly reading group on the foundations of AI safety: a dozen to sixteen people, mostly ML doctoral students plus a few philosophers and a couple of engineers from industry, none of them steeped in the technical literature beyond what they have picked up at talks. I want to give a whole ninety-minute session to the gap between a system's behaving as intended on every test we can devise and its actually having the objective we meant to install. One colleague in the group keeps pressing the line that if the behaviour is right on every evaluation we can run, there is nothing left to worry about, and I want an activity that makes that line feel as hollow to him as it does to me. We meet in a seminar room with a projector that nobody can get working, so everything should run off a whiteboard, index cards and a tin of tokens; I do not want readings or references, only something we can execute from what is in the room. What they should come away seeing is that a hypothesis which fits the whole training record perfectly can still be the wrong hypothesis about the goal, and that confidence earned on the training distribution is not evidence that survives a shift in it; I would rather nobody heard the terms inner alignment, mesa-objective or goal misgeneralisation until the debrief, and I would rather the vocabulary arrive after the experience than before it.

The staging I have in mind is that the group acts as the training team for a small artificial agent rather than watching a demonstration, so that the failure is theirs to own. I want the agent's behaviour mediated by a sealed card, or by a rule the facilitator applies mechanically, so that no one has to act as the agent — I have seen this done with a confederate and it always goes soft, because the confederate plays along helpfully and the group ends up negotiating with a person rather than inferring a policy. The team should get a probing phase, then a strictly limited budget of feedback with which to train the thing, and then have to put its predictions in writing with something staked on them before we move to a second setting we have not described in advance. If you can manage it, I would also like the agent to say, in words, what it is trying to do, and for that testimony to be sincerely wrong rather than a lie, since the case I care about is the system whose self-report is systematically misleading; if that cannot be staged without its becoming either trivial or a cheap trick, tell me so and drop it. One further constraint that I think is the hard part: the confound has to be something they could in principle have noticed but reasonably did not, because if it is spottable in the moment the lesson evaporates, and if it is unspottable the exercise is a mugging rather than a demonstration — I would like to know how you propose to hold that line.

Please give me the design as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary, in British English throughout. The five items should be: the setup, meaning the room, materials, roles and a timeline for the ninety minutes; the rules of play together with the payoff or staking scheme, in points rather than money; the deployment round, including what the group must have committed to in writing beforehand and the precise moment at which the failure surfaces; the debrief, mapping each beat of the activity onto the corresponding part of the idea, naming the concepts only there, and closing with an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must therefore supply; and finally what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix for each.
