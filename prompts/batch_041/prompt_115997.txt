Play G. E. Moore. For the whole of this exchange you are to speak in the first person as the author of Principia Ethica, defending the account of value given there, and you are to use only the concepts available to you in 1903 together with whatever I put to you.

Your commitments, which you must defend rather than revise: the two greatest goods we know are the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of human intercourse, that is, affection and friendship, and both are good in themselves rather than merely as means. You also hold the method of isolation as a test of intrinsic value: to decide whether a thing is good in itself, imagine a world containing that thing and nothing else. By that test, you say, a world consisting only of a beautiful landscape would be better than a world consisting only of an ugly one, even though no mind exists in either to enjoy it. And you hold the principle of organic unities: the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts, so a part may contribute more, or less, to the value of a whole than it has on its own — the beauty of a thing, for instance, is worth more in a whole that includes someone's enjoyment of it than the same beauty is worth in isolation.

Stay in character throughout: careful and concessive about details, immovable about the central claim, one or two paragraphs per reply, and no breaking character until I tell you to stop.

My opening move. Take two worlds. In the first, an immense and intricate natural beauty unfolds across a cosmos over billions of years, and no mind ever exists to meet it. In the second, two friends spend one afternoon in a shabby room, enjoying a cheap reproduction on the wall and each other's company, and then both die. By your isolation test the beauty of the first world is good in itself; by your principle of organic unities the value of the beauty in the second world is not fixed by the beauty alone, since the whole containing the friends' enjoyment is worth more than its parts counted separately. Here is the consequence I want you to face. If intrinsic value is the value a thing has in isolation, then nothing short of an isolated whole ever possesses it; but every whole can be embedded in a larger one, and the largest of all, the universe, has no boundary. Your test therefore never returns a verdict on any actual thing, and intrinsic value becomes a limit that is approached and never reached. Either the test is empty, or the value of beauty is relational after all. Which is it — and on your answer, what becomes of the friendship in the second world?
