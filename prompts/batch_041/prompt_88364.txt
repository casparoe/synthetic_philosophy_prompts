For the last two years I have helped run a small volunteer group that looks after a local heritage site — a mill and its grounds that the council would otherwise have let go — and the experience has left me with a question I cannot settle by myself. Our group tells a story about how the place came to be: who founded it, what it was for, who lived and worked there before us, what happened to them. It is a good story and it is retold at every open day, and in several particulars it is wrong. The county archive contradicts us on dates, on who actually held the land, and on what one of the buildings was used for; some of our most cherished details look like inventions from about a century ago, grafted onto a real event. What troubles me is that the story is not idle decoration. The group's whole sense of obligation — why we give up our weekends, why we say we owe something to the people named in the story, why we ask the council for money on the strength of it — rests on those particulars. When I raise the matter, half the group tells me that a community's memory of itself is not the same kind of thing as a historian's reconstruction and should not be judged by that standard, that memory is about who we are now rather than what happened then, and that the archive is itself somebody's selection. The other half says that a false story is a false story, that self-respect means facing the record, and that a duty founded on a mistake is not a duty but a delusion. I can see the force of both, and I have a suspicion that they are quietly answering different questions.

I would like you to write an essay that takes this seriously and lands somewhere. I do not want a survey of what other people have thought, and I do not need citations or a reading list — I want your reasoning. Start by pulling apart the things that might be meant by a community's memory: at the least, the factual claims it holds about its past, the rituals and practices by which it carries that past forward, and the identity or self-understanding the two together support. Say whether the clash with the documentary record really arises for all three or only for some, and whether the others are being dragged into a fight that is not theirs. Then weigh the case for the record having priority — the default authority of truth, the wrong of treating a group as entitled to its own facts, the cost of hanging present obligations on error, the way a comfortable false memory can be used against the very people it flatters — against the case for memory having priority: that collective memory is a distinct practice with its own standards rather than a poor imitation of history, that the record is partial and interested and often written by the powerful, that a community may have a real claim to its own self-description, and that correcting a people's past has more than once been a polite way of dispossessing them. Ask, too, whether giving way must be all-or-nothing: whether memory might yield on the facts while history yields on significance, or whether that split is a dodge that lets everyone keep what they wanted.

Then reach a verdict and defend it. Say which should give way, in which respects, and to whom the correction is owed — the living, the dead, or nobody. Test your verdict against the hard case I described, where the false belief is load-bearing: correcting the story would not merely embarrass the group but dissolve an obligation it currently honours. If your principle says correct the story and let the obligation go, then say that plainly and defend the loss; if it says keep the story, then be honest about what you are giving up and why the price is worth paying. Finish by naming the conditions under which you would change your mind.
