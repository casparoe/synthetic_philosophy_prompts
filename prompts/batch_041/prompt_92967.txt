I am a philosopher who serves on the governing board of a state historical society, and I need to circulate a written opinion before our next meeting, which is ten days away. The matter is as follows.

In 1953 a fire destroyed the records room of a state institution that had, for three decades, confined and sterilized people the law of that era classified as mentally deficient, with documented consent in almost no case. The fire was ruled accidental after a two-week inquiry. Last year a retired archivist at the society published a book arguing that the fire was set to destroy the sterilization files, that the inquiry was a whitewash, and that the surviving inventory sheets list files no one can account for. The book has travelled well beyond the state. A senator has written to us asking the society to settle the question; a group of descendants has asked us to endorse the claim and press for an investigation; two trustees have told me privately that we should announce that this is a conspiracy theory and move on. There is an awkward complication: the 1953 inquiry was run by this society's predecessor body, and its report was signed by a man who later served as our president.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate to the board. It should recommend one concrete course of action, something the board can actually do within thirty days, and give the reasons for it. I want it to state plainly what standard of evidence the board would have to meet before it could responsibly call the question settled in either direction, and to say whether the board is well placed to apply that standard at all. It should confront the fact that the claim belongs to a recognizable family of claims about destroyed records and whitewashed inquiries: say whether we are entitled to assess this claim by the general character of claims like it, or whether we must assess it on its own evidence, and what we give up on either route. It should distinguish, and not run together, the claim's being false, its being unsupported by what survives, its being framed so that nothing could count against it, and its being likely to do harm — and it should say which of these, if any, would justify which response on our part. It should be candid about how our own entanglement with the 1953 inquiry bears on what we say and on who should say it. And it should treat the descendants' request as a serious claim on us rather than an inconvenience to be managed.

State the strongest case against your own recommendation rather than softening it; I have not made up my mind, and I would rather read the best objection now than hear it for the first time in the meeting. Keep it to a page or two, and no citations or reading list, please — I know the literature and will supply references myself.
