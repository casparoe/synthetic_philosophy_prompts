I'm in a reading group that's been plowing through the Dutch book literature and I'm trying to turn my notes into a short essay, and I'd really like your comments before I inflict it on anyone else. The gist: I think the standard Dutch book argument against an agent whose price for A and price for not-A sum to more than one quietly assumes the agent is a price-taker with a fixed price list, i.e. that their willingness to sell a bet on A for 60 cents and a bet on not-A for 60 cents doesn't depend on the fact that those two offers are arriving together. Drop that assumption and the argument loses its teeth: an agent can hold 0.6 for A and 0.6 for not-A, sell you either bet on its own, and simply decline the pair, and no sequence of trades ever drains their wallet. So the sure-loss result starts to look like a theorem about static price lists rather than about credences, and the legitimacy of the price-list idealization is doing more work than the literature usually admits.

Then the repair I attempt: drop betting talk entirely and compare preferences over gambles directly, where a gamble pays a fixed prize on A and nothing otherwise, and treat it as a requirement of rationality that the agent prefers a sure prize to a bundle of gambles costing more than that prize. This gets me a dominance argument with no bookie and no price list, which is nice, but I worry it isn't cheap — it seems to need preferences to be complete and to satisfy something like the independence condition, which is alot to assume about an agent who might be indifferent, or who has non-linear attitudes toward the prizes themselves. My tentative conclusion is that the argument is either modest and only applies to price-takers, or general and no longer modest. A friend insists the second horn is fine because you can get away with just transitivity plus dominance, but I don't see how.

Comments please, and be blunt. I'm most interested in (a) whether the price-taker point is genuinely distinct from the old complaint that betting behavior isn't credence in disguise, (b) whether the preference-based repair really smuggles in more than dominance and transitivity, and (c) whether there's an obvious way to Dutch book someone whose prices depend on what else is on offer — say by offering the bets separately, or wrapping them in a single package the agent can't unbundle. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no concluding paragraph.
