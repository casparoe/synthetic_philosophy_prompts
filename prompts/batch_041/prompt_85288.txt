I'm a third-year Politics and Philosophy student, and this term I'm taking a module on political corruption and clientelism. As part of the coursework, four of us have been asked to write a mock exam paper for our revision sessions: each of us drafts one essay question, plus a model answer and a marking scheme so we can grade each other's practice attempts. I've been given the corruption-and-universalism topic, and I want my question to be genuinely difficult rather than one that rewards reciting the standard definition of corruption (abuse of entrusted power for private gain) and then summarising the familiar universalism-versus-relativism debate. Please act as an experienced examiner in comparative political theory and produce the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme.

Everything the question needs must be contained in the question itself, because our revision sessions happen in a room with no library access and no internet, and the module handbook forbids us from setting anything that requires a reading list. So: no citations, no author names, no dates, no titles of works, and nothing in quotation marks that would have to be an actual published quotation. If a theoretical framework is useful, describe it in plain prose and give it a neutral label of your own.

Here is the material I want the question built around, so that it can't be answered from memory of a textbook case. A donor agency conditions aid to a district on the council adopting open competitive tendering and mandatory asset declarations for councillors. One councillor, Amina, has used her discretionary constituency development fund to pay the secondary-school fees of children in her ward; part of the fund came from a fifteen per cent markup on a road contract awarded to a firm owned by her brother-in-law, which also employs about forty people from the ward, and she was re-elected with seventy per cent of the vote. She says the donor's conditions are a foreign imposition, because where she is from, looking after your own people is precisely what a representative is for, and a rule that turns ordinary decency into an offence has no legitimacy there. In my module we were given a constraint my lecturer calls the symmetry requirement: any argument that a practice counts as corruption in a poor, clientelist setting but not in a comparable rich-country setting must identify a difference between the two cases that is not merely the fact that one practice is foreign or unfamiliar, not merely the fact that one is illegal where the other is legal, and is defensible on grounds independent of which country we happen to be talking about. For comparison, the question should also involve a rich-country counterpart: a mayor who steers city contracts to a firm whose owner later donates to her re-election campaign and funds a local charity, all of it lawful. Two further things the module stressed, which the question should force students to use rather than merely mention: first, that in settings of dense reciprocal obligation, refusing to favour kin and neighbours can itself be experienced as a betrayal of a duty, so the person we call corrupt may sincerely see the act as required rather than as a perk; second, that the argument that informal payments grease the wheels of a slow bureaucracy has a standard structural reply, namely that actors who profit from the grease acquire a reason to keep the wheels slow, which turns the efficiency claim into an empirical dispute rather than a knock-down point either way.

Now the deliverables. First, one essay question, pitched so that a good student could answer it in about sixty minutes of writing and roughly 1,200 words, and constructed so that it cannot be answered by defining corruption, stating that standards are culturally relative, and concluding that judgment is difficult. It should require the candidate to say what the councillor's objection would have to establish in order to succeed, whether it does succeed, and what follows for what the donor may legitimately do. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that actually makes the moves rather than describing them. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty marks, built around five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong; please also add a short closing note distinguishing an answer that merely names the symmetry requirement from one that can apply it, and say what a long but weak answer would look like.

Before you draft, spend a few sentences working out where the real crux of the question lies and what a competent-but-unremarkable answer would look like, so that the marking scheme can separate the two; then write the deliverables under the headings Exam question, Model answer, and Marking scheme. Think carefully about the strongest version of Amina's position before you write anything that dismisses it, and make sure the model answer treats it as a serious argument rather than a confusion.
