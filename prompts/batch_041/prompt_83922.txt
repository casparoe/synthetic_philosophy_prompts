I am drafting a reply to a philosopher's account of meaning and rule-following, and I want to anticipate her verdict on a new case before I commit an argument to it. Her verdicts on the following earlier cases are settled.

(i) A speaker has only ever added numbers below 40. Asked for 63 + 29, he answers 8. Her verdict: there is no fact of the matter as to whether he meant addition, which yields 92, or a deviant rule that agrees with addition whenever both addends are below 40 and yields 8 for 63 and 29. She insists this is not our ignorance. Nothing about him settles it, and nothing he could consult — his past answers, his training, his memory of what he intended — would settle it either.

(ii) Someone proposes that what he meant is fixed by his dispositions, by what he would say if asked. She rejects the proposal. His dispositions are finite and mostly silent about this case, and where they are not silent they include the answers he would give when tired, distracted, or careless. A rule you are disposed to break is still the rule you meant.

(iii) A community in which everyone has always answered 8 for 63 + 29, and which trains its children to answer that way. Her verdict: it is correct to say that a speaker raised in this community means the deviant rule. She is careful to add that this does not restore the fact of the matter that was missing in (i); what has changed is only what we are entitled to say. She also dismisses the inference that anything goes, or that every answer is as good as any other, as a misreading of her position.

(iv) A member of that community who repeatedly answers 92, and who, after correction, agrees that he has made a mistake and regards his answers as slips. Her verdict: he means the deviant rule, and his answers are errors.

The case I want to press her on is this. A man raised in an ordinary community — one that answers 92 and corrects children who answer otherwise — has for thirty years answered 8. He keeps written records of his calculations, checks new answers against them, re-derives those that conflict, and treats departures from his own practice as errors of his to repair. He does not accept the community's correction; he regards his answers as right and would correct others.

What will she say he means: addition, with his answers mistaken; the deviant rule, with the community mistaken about him; or something else again? Which rule is she actually running, and which other rules fit her verdicts on (i)–(iv) just as well? I am especially interested in whether the community is doing any work in her licensing condition or whether something about the speaker alone would do just as much. Say what each candidate rule predicts about the case above, which prediction you would bet on and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the candidates. If her verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and tell me what she would have to be committed to on either reading.
