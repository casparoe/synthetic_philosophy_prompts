For a long-form piece I'm writing for a magazine of science and ideas, on the recent revival of hedonism in ethics, I want to commission a staged dialogue — an imaginative reconstruction, not a transcript — between two philosophers who agree that well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain, but who cannot agree on what makes an experience a pleasure. Please write it, at roughly fifteen hundred words.

The first, whom I'll call the feeling theorist, holds that every pleasant experience contains a distinctive felt quality — a hedonic tone — the same in kind whether one is tasting a peach, feeling a headache lift, or finishing a proof, and varying only in intensity. Where others see a motley collection, she sees one ingredient in many dishes; she attributes the impression of heterogeneity to our habit of attending to what an experience is about rather than to how it feels.

The second, the attitude theorist, denies that any such common tone can be found. For him, what unites pleasures is not a sensation but a stance: an experience is a pleasure when it is welcome, when the subject is pleased by it, when she wants it to continue. He treats the feeling theorist's tone as a posit no one has ever managed to point to, and takes the attitude account to be the only one that does justice to the range of things we actually call pleasures.

I want them to collide over cases rather than trade generalities, and to work through at least these three, which I have been collecting:

1. A woman who, after surgery, reports that she still enjoys music and company but can no longer locate any bodily sensation of pleasure. She insists she is enjoying herself, and she behaves as though she is.
2. A man who is pleased that a rival was passed over for a job, though the news produces no felt sensation of any kind.
3. The pleasure of a sad piece of music, or of grief, where what is enjoyed and what hurts seem to be a single experience rather than two side by side.

Put two questions to them directly. To the attitude theorist: if what makes an experience a pleasure is a stance rather than a feeling, is he still offering a theory of well-being distinct from desire-satisfaction, or has he quietly changed the subject? To the feeling theorist: when she introspects the tone, what exactly is she attending to, and what does she say about the woman in the first case, who reports finding nothing there?

Finally, I would like them to fight about a device of my own, and about whether it even makes sense. I call it the transplant test. Suppose we could lift the pleasure out of one experience and set it into another — the contentment of a warm bath poured into the experience of a funeral. The feeling theorist should say that something is being transplanted; the attitude theorist should say the test is incoherent, because there is nothing there to move.

Neither philosopher should be a strawman, and neither should carry the day by fiat. Each should answer the other's actual points, concede where concession is warranted, and press where the other is weakest — and I would like the exchange to make clear whether the two are disagreeing about the nature of pleasure or only about which experiences deserve the name, if those can be separated at all. I want to finish the piece genuinely unsure which of them I believe.
