I recently came across a short encyclopedia-style entry on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I would like a careful audit of it before I rely on it in anything. The entry runs roughly as follows.

"Fitch's paradox starts from an innocent-looking premise: every truth is knowable — that is, for any true proposition, it is possible for someone to know it. Fitch showed that this premise, together with two uncontroversial features of knowledge, yields a startling result: there are no unknown truths. For suppose some truth p were unknown; then the proposition that p is true and that no one knows it would itself be a truth, and hence knowable; but whoever knew it would know both that p and that no one knows p, and since knowing that p is true implies that someone knows p, that combination is impossible. The paradox belongs to the same family as the liar and the knower: it is generated by a self-referential construction, a sentence that says of itself that it is not known. The derivation also leans on the KK principle — that knowing something brings with it knowing that one knows it — and it collapses once that principle is given up. Fitch published the result in a brief note in the 1960s. Anti-realists had held that truth in a domain cannot outrun what could in principle be known there, and the paradox seemed to show that this commitment has a consequence they cannot accept. The received verdict is that the knowability principle must simply be abandoned, which is why verificationism is now widely regarded as untenable. A few writers have tried to rescue the principle by restricting it to truths that do not themselves mention knowledge, but this manoeuvre is generally considered ad hoc; in any case, knowability is a matter of degree, since the more complex a truth is, the less knowable it is."
