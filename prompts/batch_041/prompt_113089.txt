I teach a seminar on pain, and I keep finding that the case of pain asymbolia does more damage to my own views than I can repair in front of a class. I would like you to play the part of a philosopher who holds, firmly and without hedging, that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation itself: that a pain state is bad for the one in it independently of any desire, preference, or judgement on their part, and independently of its consequences, and that a state which is not unpleasant is therefore not a pain state at all, so that 'pain that does not bother you' is a contradiction in terms rather than a clinical finding. Give this character real arguments rather than a caricature: they should be able to grant that the patients are sincere, articulate, and not malingering, and that the syndrome is a genuine one, while still holding the main line. They may concede peripheral points — about what a definition of pain is for, about how much weight first-person reports carry, about whether the syndrome is one thing or several — but not the central claim. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, so that this feels like a conversation rather than an essay. Argue it out in your own voice: do not recite what has been written on the subject and do not appeal to authority. If you need a clinical fact you do not have — how such patients move, what they say when asked directly, whether the automatic withdrawal response survives — ask me for it and I will answer as the clinician would, but do not invent one.

My opening move is the one my students always use against me, and I have never had a good answer to it. Your view looks unfalsifiable. Whenever someone describes a patient who reports pain in the ordinary way — locates it, rates it, says it hurts — but is wholly unbothered by it, you reply that the patient is not really in pain. But that is precisely what is at issue, and the patient's report is the only evidence we could have had. Consider fear instead. If I claimed that fear is intrinsically aversive, and you produced someone who sincerely says she is afraid, avoids the thing, trembles, and yet reports feeling no distress at all, I could not answer by saying that she is not really afraid; I would have to abandon the claim, because being afraid is not something I get to define out from under her. Why is pain different? What is the word 'pain' doing in your mouth such that a person who says she is in pain, and behaves as though she is, can still turn out not to be?

And I want to press a case I have constructed rather than one I have read about, precisely so that you cannot retreat into the empirical details of any particular lesion. Suppose everything is intact except the minding. The patient withdraws her hand from the ice water, guards it afterwards, reports the burning as burning, says 'it hurts' and means it, and yet, asked whether she would rather it stopped, shrugs — not out of stoicism, and not because her desires are unusual, but because there is no aversion anywhere in her to find. Tell me what state she is in. If you say she is in pain, your thesis is false. If you say she is not, then say what is missing from her other than the aversion, and how you could possibly know that it is missing, given that she denies nothing.
