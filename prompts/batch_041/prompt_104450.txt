So I'm a science journalist — cosmology and the philosophy-of-physics beat, mostly — and a philosopher I interviewed a few months back emailed me this argument after we got to talking about Gödel's rotating universe. He swears its airtight and that it proves time isn't real, and I've been staring at it for a week and can't find the exit. Cleaned up, it goes like this:

(1) Gödel's rotating universe is a genuine solution to Einstein's field equations — nothing in general relativity rules it out.
(2) In that universe there are closed timelike curves, so a rocket can come back to where it started before it left, and the whole thing can't be sliced into a single global 'now' that ticks once, everywhere, from past to future.
(3) Real time — the flowing now, the thing that makes the past gone and the future open — just is that global ticking.
(4) So in the Gödel universe time in that sense doesn't exist. There's still order, earlier and later along a given worldline, but no passage.
(5) Whether our universe is rotating in the relevant global sense is a contingent fact about how much total angular momentum everything happens to have — and it's not even something an observer inside could ever measure, since rotation of the whole is only ever rotation relative to something else.
(6) So ours could have been, or might already be, a Gödel-type universe.
(7) But if something as fundamental as the passage of time can be present or absent depending on a contingent global fact that nobody inside the universe could ever detect, then it was never a feature of reality to begin with — it was a parochial artifact of our local viewpoint, like 'up'.
(8) Therefore the passage of time isn't real, not here and not anywhere. Time is ideal — a thing we project.

He calls (7) his 'no free lunch for the now' principle and says it's the whole ballgame. I feel like something slipped but I can't name it, and I keep wondering whether the same move would also just condemn plain special relativity, which also has no single global now. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd really like to keep (2) and (4) — the physics and the diagnosis of the Gödel universe — because those are the parts I can report on with a straight face. If I have to give up (7), tell me what work it was quietly doing and whether Gödel needed anything like it or could get to 'time is unreal' cheaper. And if the thing is actually valid and I just have to swallow (8), say so, and tell me what it does to a sentence I've written a hundred times: 'the universe is 13.8 billion years old.'
