Here is my opening move, and I would like you to answer it directly.

I am a software engineer, and part of my job is deciding which of our customer support tickets count as the same kind of issue. The scheme I have built is this: embed each ticket as a vector, define a similarity score between any two tickets, choose a threshold, and then say that two tickets belong to the same kind whenever there is a finite chain of tickets running from one to the other in which every neighbouring pair scores above the threshold. No single feature has to be shared by every member of a kind. Membership is just connectedness through a chain of pairwise likenesses, and that looks to me like family resemblance, implemented. If I am right, two things follow that I want you to reckon with. First, family resemblance turns out to be a rule after all: the chain condition is a precise, checkable necessary-and-sufficient condition, so the anti-essentialist point is either a mundane observation about features or simply mistaken. Second, the scheme makes a prediction: the extension of a kind will drift with the threshold, and long chains will bridge things we would rather not see bridged, so that with enough intermediate tickets the complaints about a phishing email and the complaints about a slow login end up as one kind. Tell me which of these two consequences you accept, which you reject, and where exactly my chain picture misdescribes what you think is going on when we call things by the same name.
