I am trying to work out what the institutions of direct democracy take for granted, because their rules strike me as inconsistent, and I suspect the inconsistency is revealing rather than accidental.

Consider Switzerland. A change to the federal constitution passes only if it wins a majority of the votes cast and, separately, a majority of the cantons, with each canton counting equally and a few counting as half-cantons; an ordinary statute, once passed by parliament, can be overturned by a simple majority of voters in an optional referendum. Neither vote carries a minimum turnout. A popular initiative may propose only a constitutional amendment, and the electorate accepts or rejects it whole, with no power in parliament to revise it, only to offer a counter-proposal.

Now consider California. A bare majority of voters can amend the state constitution by initiative. But the legislature needs two-thirds of each house to raise a tax, and local governments need two-thirds of their voters to levy a special tax, and those requirements were themselves adopted by a simple-majority initiative. Each measure must address a single subject. Every voter receives a pamphlet containing arguments for and against and an official fiscal analysis.

So the same electorate, sometimes on the same ballot, is treated as competent to bind the whole polity by a bare majority on one question and as unworthy of a bare majority on another; a majority that cannot be trusted with a tax rate is trusted with the text of a constitution. I am not asking whether any of this is wise. I want the implicit theory.

Reconstruct it. What does the practice assume a vote to be: a preference to be aggregated, or a judgment to be weighed? Does it assume a determinate popular will that the tally merely records, existing before and independently of the procedure? Does it treat legitimacy as consent or as getting the answer right, and if the latter, why the silence about turnout, information, and the reasons voters hold? What theory of the polity does the double majority presuppose, given that it makes the electorate count twice over, once as a sum of persons and once as a union of member units? And what does the take-it-or-leave-it, one-issue-per-ballot framing presuppose about the separability of issues, namely that issue-wise majorities compose into a coherent whole?

Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates. Say where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of legitimacy would license the same rules, and where it is not. Name the views it quietly rules out, and judge whether its parts presuppose consistent things or whether the tax supermajority and the initiative majority are genuinely in tension. Finally, take one rival view, whether deliberative, purely aggregative, or epistemic, and say what would have to change in these rules if it were adopted in place of the presupposed one.
