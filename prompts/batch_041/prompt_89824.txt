I am putting together materials for a seminar on self-reference and paradox, and I want to close the unit on Escher with a poem rather than another expository handout. The idea I want the poem to carry is the one Hofstadter develops in Gödel, Escher, Bach around Escher's Drawing Hands: two hands, each drawing the other, form a tangled hierarchy, a loop that runs through levels and returns to where it started, and yet the loop cannot be its own ground. For the drawing to exist at all, there has to be a level outside it, the level of the artist or the viewer, which Hofstadter calls the inviolate level. The poem should not merely mention this; it should enact it.

Write a pantoum of four or five quatrains. The pantoum's interlocking repetitions, where the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third of the next, and the final stanza folds back into the first, should do the work of the tangled hierarchy: the poem should feel like two hands drawing each other, each line returning as both cause and effect. The subject can be the hands themselves, or two voices, or two propositions, or anything else that can draw and be drawn, but the loop must be real rather than asserted. The poem should close by returning to its opening line, so that the reader ends where they began.

The harder part is the inviolate level. A pantoum that simply loops is a closed system, and the point of the inviolate level is that the loop cannot close on its own. So the poem must also mark, structurally, the level from which the loop is drawn, the level that is not inside it. You can do this with the title, an epigraph, a stage direction, a marginal note, a bracketed line, or a single line that breaks the pantoum's form; whatever you choose, the break should be motivated by the idea rather than decorative. The reader should be able to see both the tangle and the outside that makes the tangle possible. A specialist in Hofstadter should recognize the distinction; a reader who has never heard of him should still feel the poem's structure doing something it cannot quite contain.
