I work as a software engineer, and I keep running into a pattern in how my industry allocates effort to safety that seems to me to have a philosophical shape I can't quite name. We will spend very large sums and years of engineering time to drive down the probability of a catastrophic failure — a regional outage, a launch failure, a defect that silently corrupts customer data — even when the best available estimate of that probability is far below one in a million. Our internal safety standards are usually written as thresholds rather than expected-value calculations: below some failure rate per hour of operation we stop treating the risk as something to act on, and above it we act largely regardless of cost. Governments behave the same way at a larger scale when they fund asteroid-deflection missions or prepare for pandemics that may never arrive. Yet the very people who fund asteroid deflection will dismiss as absurd the argument that you should hand over your wallet to a stranger who claims, with a probability of, say, one in a googol, that he will otherwise cause an amount of suffering so large it dwarfs every human life that will ever exist — the argument I have seen called Pascal's mugging. The expected-value arithmetic seems to say you should hand over the wallet. Almost everyone says the arithmetic has gone wrong somewhere.

I would like you to reconstruct the view of practical rationality and of value that this pair of attitudes presupposes, and then tell me whether the two halves can be made consistent. Specifically:

1. Does spending real resources on extremely improbable catastrophes actually presuppose that we maximize expected value with no upper bound on how much value can be at stake? Or can the whole practice be explained by ordinary expected-value reasoning in which both the probabilities and the harms are finite and the products stay modest? Be specific about which of our practices requires the exotic commitment and which does not.

2. Is there a principled line between the asteroid case and the mugging case? If so, where does it fall — in the magnitude of the harm, in the size of the probability, in whether the harm is finite or unbounded, in the requirement that the threat run through a mechanism we can investigate and act on, or in the fact that the same form of argument would license an unlimited number of conflicting demands? If no line can be drawn, say why the intuition that the two cases differ is an illusion.

3. Suppose we adopted whatever view makes the mugging argument come out correct. What would have to change in how we budget for reliability and safety, and would the resulting practice be tolerable to live with?

4. Suppose instead we adopted a view that makes the mugging argument fail. Does that view also undercut the asteroid-deflection spending, or can it license it? If it licenses one and not the other, what is doing the work?

Do not simply survey the options. Take a position: tell me which view you think is right, defend the line you draw (or explain why there is none), and state plainly what your position forces you to give up. If your answer turns on a particular way of measuring probability or value — for instance, whether utilities are bounded, or whether very small probabilities may be discounted or ignored — commit to a specific choice and say why you made it.
