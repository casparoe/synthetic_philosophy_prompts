I am preparing a one-page handout for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science, and I want it to be a decision tree for the reduction/emergence debate rather than a list of positions. Build the tree from the logical space first and only then locate the named views in it. Fix the starting point so that the tree does not sort physicalism: every student is assumed to accept a single physical substrate, and the questions should instead sort what they take "levels" and higher-level explanation to be doing. The branch nodes should be questions about concrete cases, each answerable without knowing where it leads, and between them they should probe at least the following: whether "levels" are individuated by scale (length and time), by energy, by compositional hierarchy, or only by the usefulness of a vocabulary; whether a higher-level kind maps onto a unique lower-level kind or only onto heterogeneous realizers; whether the lower-level derivation is in principle available or blocked by bridge principles that are not themselves derivable; and, where it is blocked, whether that is an epistemic limitation or an ontological fact with causal consequences of its own. Give the leaves names where a name is standard (Nagelian reduction, eliminativism, non-reductive physicalism, strong emergence, weak emergence or explanatory pluralism, the autonomy-of-the-special-sciences view) and mark them "unnamed" otherwise. Include a leaf for the effective-field-theory and renormalization-group case, phrased as a question about whether the systematic insensitivity of a lower-energy description to microscopic detail (universality at a fixed point) should count as reduction, as emergence, or as a third thing, and place it explicitly on the tree. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and say why, and note any branch of the logical space that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the whole thing to something a student could work through in a single seminar.
