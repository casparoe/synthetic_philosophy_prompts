You are a philosopher of law and technology with a strong background in the philosophy of privacy. Think carefully before you answer: begin by brainstorming the candidate theories of privacy that could be in play — secrecy, control, access, contextual integrity, and any others you think are relevant — so that your eventual position is a considered one rather than the first framing that comes to mind.

Treat the following cluster of practices as a single object of interpretation.

First, in American surveillance law the content of a communication has long been treated as more private than the information surrounding it. Reading the body of an email or listening to a call requires the highest showing, whereas the numbers dialed, the addresses on an envelope, the timing and duration of a connection, and the identity of the parties can be obtained on a much weaker showing, on the reasoning that a person who voluntarily conveys this surrounding information to a service provider retains no reasonable expectation of privacy in it.

Second, contemporary messaging products reproduce the same split as a design choice. A service may encrypt the message body end-to-end so that no one but the correspondents can read it, while storing in the clear a complete interaction graph — who talks to whom, when, how often, and from where — and monetizing aggregate patterns drawn from it. Users are told that their messages are private; the graph is treated as a business asset.

Third, the same legal system that licenses the first practice has repeatedly conceded that when the surrounding information becomes dense enough, aggregating it can reveal more than any single item and therefore deserves protection — so the very facts that are individually unprotected are said to become protected in bulk.

What theory of privacy does this cluster presuppose? Reconstruct it as precisely as you can. Say whether the cluster presupposes a representational view, on which privacy protects the meaning or content of what is communicated, or instead a relational or associative view, on which what matters is who stands in what relation to whom. Say which rival theories it rules out and which it merely happens to fit: does it require a secrecy view, a control view, or an access view, or is it compatible with any of them? Then assess whether the cluster is internally consistent. The third practice looks like a concession that cuts against the first two; decide whether it does, and if so, whether the inconsistency is a defect in the doctrine and the design or evidence that they were never committed to a single theory in the first place. Be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several distinct theories would license exactly the same rules and the same product.

Finally, take a definite position. Tell me what would have to change, in both the law and the product design, if we replaced the presupposed theory with a contextual-integrity view, on which privacy is a matter of information flowing appropriately given the norms of the context in which it was shared. Which of the three practices would survive intact, which would have to be abandoned, and what concrete rule or design decision would you put in their place?

Write in American English. Give me a committed answer — a definite reconstruction and a definite recommendation, with your reasons — rather than a neutral survey of the options.
