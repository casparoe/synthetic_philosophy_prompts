I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy and our reading group on repugnant markets has me stuck on something. A friend sketched this argument and I cant find the bad step, even though I'm sure the conclusion is wrong. It runs:

(1) An exchange is wrong only if it wrongs somebody: it harms a party, violates someone's rights, or isn't genuinely voluntary (coerced, uninformed, or made by someone who can't consent).
(2) A voluntary exchange between two competent adults that leaves both of them better off and harms nobody else wrongs nobody.
(3) Selling a kidney is voluntary, leaves the seller better off (cash) and the buyer better off (a kidney), and harms no third party.
(4) The same holds for selling my vote, for paying a stranger to sit on a jury in my place, and for paying someone to argue my side in a fight with my partner.
(5) So none of those are wrong in themselves. The revulsion is disgust, and disgust isn't an argument.
(6) And in fact there's nothing you can't sell, so long as everyone says yes and nobody else gets hurt.

I think (6) is false. I'm fairly sure selling my vote is wrong even if I'd have voted the same way anyway, and even if the buyer is a saint, and I don't think that's only because of some diffuse 'harm to democracy'. But every repair I reach for does something worse. The obvious repair is that some things are wrong because pricing them changes what they are: a citizen's judgement becomes a token, a body part becomes a spare, a juror's duty becomes a chore you can outsource. Then the repair eats itself, because by that test paying a nurse changes nursing (her motive is now the wage), paying a teacher changes teaching, paying a therapist changes therapy — and I will not say paid care is corrupt. It's work. A world where nobody is paid to care is worse, not purer. My friend even offered a sharper version of the repair: ask whether the good survives being done for the money. Paid jurors are fine, he says, because a juror paid by the court is still judging; but a vote cast for a price isn't a judgement at all, and neither is an expert who says whatever the person paying wants. Except we do pay expert witnesses, and lawyers argue for whoever hires them, and I think paid advocacy is fine, so now the sharp version condemns that too.

So tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. If I keep (1) I need a wrong that isn't a harm and isn't 'corruption', or I need to show that corruption doesn't generalize to nursing and to advocacy. If I drop (1) I have to say what's wrong with the vote sale without it. I'd rather keep paid care and paid advocacy than keep (6), but I want to know exactly what I'm buying. And say whether the fix also has to let price gouging through, since thats the case I'm most sure is wrong. Don't just tell me it's complicated — point at the line and show me why it doesn't slide to wherever I happen to want it.
