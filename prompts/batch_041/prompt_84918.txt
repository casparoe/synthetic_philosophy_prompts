Six weeks ago I took a job doing policy analysis for the budget office in a mid-sized city, and it has dropped me into the middle of the reform / defund / abolish fight in a way I was not expecting when I applied. Here is the thing that's actually eating at me. Our Community Safety Office — the civilian crisis-response pilot the last council set up: six unarmed responders, a van, a dispatch protocol, a small evaluation contract — came up for renewal, and all three camps read the same evaluation report as confirming their position. The reformers point at the big drop in police contacts on mental-health calls and say, see, it works, scale it. The defunders say, see, the cops were never needed for those calls, and every dollar you leave sitting in the police budget is a dollar you chose not to move. The abolitionists say, see, you built a small unarmed agency that still calls the cops the moment things get hard, and now you want to call that a victory. Nobody's numbers are cooked. Nobody is lying about the data. And the working group has not moved an inch in five meetings — everyone restates, nobody shifts, and my roommate, who organizes with the local abolition group, has stopped discussing it with me at dinner.

I keep thinking the three camps are not actually disagreeing about the pilot, and I'm trying to work out what they are disagreeing about, because I have to write the options memo and I can't write they have different vibes. My best guesses, and tell me which are real: (a) a disagreement about institutions, i.e. whether an institution has a function it will always revert to, so that a police department with a nicer mission statement and a new name is still going to produce police violence because that's what it's for, versus a contingent bundle of practices you can subtract from one at a time; (b) a disagreement about the state itself, where the phrase 'monopoly on violence' is quietly doing three jobs at once — a description of what states in fact manage to do, a definition of what a state is, and a claim about what states are entitled to do — and each camp is answering a different one and hearing the others as answering theirs; (c) a hidden empirical dispute about whether state violence generates the order it claims to protect or just feeds off it. I'd add a fourth that someone at a conference put to me and I can't stop chewing on, which is that states are basically protection rackets that won, so the question of whether the police are legitimate never had a clean answer to begin with — but I don't know if that's a serious claim or a bumper sticker.

So what I want from you is the diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right: is this one disagreement or three wearing a single coat, are the camps differing about facts, about which facts count, about what an institution is, or about starting moral intuitions nobody can argue for, and is the 'monopoly on violence' bit doing the load-bearing work or is it just rhetorical wallpaper? Then tell me what would actually move it — a study, a case, a distinction, a change in the question — or whether the honest answer is that nothing will, because the room is arguing about three different things and only one of them is the pilot. I don't need a literature survey or a reading list, I need the map and the honest read on whether the map helps, because I go into a council meeting on the 14th and I'd like to at least be able to say why the room is stuck.
