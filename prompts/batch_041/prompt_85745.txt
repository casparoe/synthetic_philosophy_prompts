My mate Dan and I have just spent three hours on the sofa going round in circles about Hofstadter — the thing where your self is a pattern rather than a lump of meat, and where a bit of the people you love carries on running inside your head after they've gone. I'll admit I'm not neutral here: my dad died two years ago and I like thinking there's a version of him still doing the crossword in my head.

Dan's not having it. He built this at me, in millisouls (his invention — 1000 millisouls = one full self, apparently):

(1) What I am is a pattern, not a lump of matter.
(2) A pattern is the sort of thing that can be copied, and a good copy of a pattern just is the pattern — two copies of the same tune are both the tune.
(3) When I'm close to someone, my head runs bits of their pattern: their turns of phrase, their reactions, sometimes their voice when I'm deciding something.
(4) A bit of a pattern is a bit of the pattern. There's no cut-off below which a partial copy stops counting as a copy; it's just degrees.
(5) So when someone dies, whatever fraction of them is still running in the heads of the people who knew them is still running, full stop. That's not a metaphor, that's what they were.
(6) Same reasoning the other way: the bit of me in my own head and the bits of me scattered round other people's heads are all just millisouls, and nothing says the bit in my own head has to be the biggest.
(7) If it needn't be the biggest, there's no fact of the matter about where I stop and other people start.
(8) So there's definately no fact of the matter about how many selves are sat in this kitchen. It isn't two. It isn't any number.
(9) Which is daft. So Hofstadter's wrong.

I'm not giving up (1) or (3) — drop (1) and the whole thing about my dad evaporates. But I can't see where (9) stops following. Which step is dodgy, and what am I paying if I bin it?
