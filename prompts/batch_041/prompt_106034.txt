I teach a graduate seminar on Aquinas's political thought, and every year the same objection comes up when we reach the sedition question. Here is the passage that provokes it, from the reply to the third objection in the Summa's II-II, q. 42, a. 2: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

Set against this is II-II, q. 64, a. 3, on whether a private individual may kill a man who has sinned, whose corpus runs: "it is lawful to kill an evildoer in so far as it is directed to the welfare of the whole community, so that it belongs to him alone who has charge of the community's welfare." Its reply to the third objection adds that a private individual may do anything for the common good "provided it harm nobody," but where it harms another it may not be done "except by virtue of the judgment of the person to whom it pertains to decide what is to be taken from the parts for the welfare of the whole."

Help me give my students a coherent account of how these two texts hang together. In particular: (1) what does Aquinas mean by "disturbing" a tyrannical government in q. 42, and who is the subject of that disturbance — the whole people, public officials, or any private person? (2) Does the "unless" clause make the permissibility of resistance turn on a consequentialist comparison of harms, and if so, is that comparison to be made by the resister, or does it instead function as a limit on what counts as sedition at all? (3) Given q. 64's restriction of lethal force to those who hold charge of the community, is the q. 42 permission to disturb a tyrant confined to non-lethal resistance, or does Aquinas leave room for killing a tyrant by someone lacking public authority — and how should I relate this to his treatment of tyranny in the De regno?

Say where your reading is most likely to fail: which textual or doctrinal consideration would push you to a different verdict on the q. 42/q. 64 relation, and what you take to be the weakest link in the interpretation you offer.
