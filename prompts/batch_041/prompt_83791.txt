I am a working statistician, fluent in the tools of my trade — likelihoods and priors, identifiability and weak identification, regularization, model-selection criteria, and the causal-discovery machinery in which a joint distribution pins down only a Markov equivalence class of graphs rather than a single directed acyclic graph — but with essentially no formal training in philosophy. I would like you to take the philosophical debate over the underdetermination of theory by evidence and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use.

The debate, as I understand it, has three pieces. First, the Duhem–Quine thesis: no hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, only as part of a web of auxiliary assumptions, so recalcitrant data can always be accommodated by revising something else. Second, the existence of empirically equivalent theories: rivals that make exactly the same observable predictions while disagreeing about what the world is like — much as the loadings in a factor model are identified only up to rotation. Third, the constructive empiricist's proposal that science aims at empirical adequacy rather than truth, and the scientific realist's reply that we should nonetheless believe the best explanation.

Restate each piece in my terms — likelihoods, identifiability, observational equivalence, priors and regularization as stand-ins for the web of belief, model selection, and the gap between the true model and the best model in a chosen class — and be exact about what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what my vocabulary cannot say at all.

Then give me the residue report, and be candid. Can I express the realist's notion of truth as anything more than 'the true model,' when in my world even the true model is merely a member of a hypothesis class I selected? Is the observable/unobservable distinction the constructive empiricist leans on even statable for me, given that 'unobservable' reduces to 'latent variable' and the choice is a modeling convenience? And when I say that more data will eventually identify a weakly identified parameter, am I capturing the difference between underdetermination that further evidence could in principle resolve and underdetermination that no possible evidence could — or am I quietly changing the subject? For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a defect in my framework or a sign that the philosophers were less clear than they appeared.
