I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of action, at work on a chapter about diachronic commitment, and for some months I have been keeping a ledger of my own verdicts on cases in which a person continues a course of action because of what they have already put into it. I do not need the standard statement of the sunk cost fallacy or its history. What I want is a principle: whether one principle captures the pattern in these verdicts, and, if none does, which of the verdicts I should surrender.

The ledger:

1. Finishing a mediocre meal because it is paid for and half eaten: irrational.
2. A city completing a half-built transit extension whose costs have tripled and whose ridership projections have collapsed, on the ground that the tunnels are already dug: irrational.
3. A colleague who has spent three years on a paper that will not come together, and who continues because of the three years rather than for any new reason: irrational.
4. A friend who stays in a ten-year relationship that has gone flat, partly because of the ten years: irrational.
5. Someone who finishes a marathon after a year of training, on a day when they feel wretched and would rather stop at mile twenty: not irrational; I find it admirable.
6. A reader who finishes a nine-hundred-page novel they stopped enjoying three hundred pages ago, because they have come this far: irrational.
7. Someone who keeps adding to a collection they no longer care about, because of what is already assembled: irrational.
8. Someone who keeps up a family recipe, or a craft, or a holiday ritual that gives them no pleasure, because a grandparent did it and they have done it for twenty years: not irrational. Here the history appears to be part of what makes continuing worthwhile, rather than a fact about the past that ought to be ignored.
9. A government continuing a war because of the soldiers already lost: irrational.

I cannot reconcile 5 with 6, or 8 with 4. Each pair looks alike, a course of action continued because of what has been put into it, and I want opposite verdicts. The test I have been applying, and distrusting, is this: would I begin this today? If not, stopping is licensed. Cases 5 and 8 fail that test, since I would not begin training for a marathon today and would not take up my grandmother's recipe today, and yet I do not think continuing in them is irrational, so either the test is wrong or those verdicts are.

An economist friend tells me the matter is simple: in 5 and 8 continuing has forward-looking value, an achievement or a ritual or a relationship maintained, so the past is doing no work and the apparent exception dissolves. What I want to know is whether that is the whole story, or whether the history can itself be a reason, not a cause that enables a forward-looking good and not evidence of one, but a reason in its own right; and, if it cannot, which of my verdicts are really cases in which the history merely coincides with a forward-looking good.

So: state the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures the ledger; give the case behind each clause; say what the account predicts for two cases I have not listed, namely someone finishing a degree they would not begin today and someone keeping a monthly standing engagement they have come to dread; and tell me plainly which verdicts the account cannot save. If a single principle costs me 5 or 8, I would rather be told that than be offered a complicated story that keeps everything.
